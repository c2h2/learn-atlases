On Monday morning, Zhou Min's manager says to her:

> *"I'm going to Singapore tomorrow. Can you send me the sales figures before I leave? And don't forget the meeting with the designers — it's been moved to Thursday."*

On Wednesday, Zhou Min tells a colleague what the manager said. In Chinese she can keep most of the words: 经理说他明天去新加坡…… — except that 明天 is now wrong, and she would change it to 星期二. In English much more changes:

> *She said **she was going** to Singapore **the next day** and asked **if I could send her** the sales figures before **she left**. She also reminded me **that** the meeting with the designers **had been moved** to Thursday.*

*I* became *she*, *me* became *I* and *her*; *am going* became *was going*; *tomorrow* became *the next day*; a question became *asked if…*; *don't forget* became *reminded me that*. None of these changes is mechanical. Each follows from the fact that the speaker, the time and the place of the report are different from those of the original words. A good report also chooses its reporting verb — *said*, *asked*, *reminded* — to show what kind of speech act the original was.

This lesson covers reporting statements, questions and commands, the shift of tenses and when it is not needed, the changes in pronouns and time and place words, and the large family of reporting verbs (*claim*, *deny*, *suggest*, *insist*, *admit* …) with their patterns. It relies on the noun clauses of [[complex-sentences/noun-clauses]] and the verb + object + infinitive pattern of [[verb-patterns/object-and-verb]].

## Direct and reported speech

::: definition Direct and reported speech {#def-reported}
**Direct speech** gives the speaker's exact words, in quotation marks: *"I'm tired," she said.*

**Reported speech** (also **indirect speech**) gives the content of what was said, in the reporter's own words and from the reporter's point of view, usually in a subordinate clause after a **reporting verb**: *She said (that) she was tired.*

The **reporting verb** (*say*, *tell*, *ask*, *explain*, *claim* …) describes the act of speaking; the **reported clause** gives the content.
:::

Direct speech is used in stories, in quotations from sources, and in conversation when a speaker wants to act out what someone said. Reported speech is more usual in summaries, minutes, news reports and academic writing, where the content matters more than the exact words. The punctuation of direct speech — single quotation marks in most British publishing, double in American — is covered in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/punctuation#rule-quotes]].

### *Say* and *tell*

- ***say*** + (*that*) clause; the listener, if mentioned, needs *to*: *She **said** (**to me**) that she was tired.* (✗ *She said me*)
- ***tell*** + listener + (*that*) clause; the listener is required: *She **told me** that she was tired.* (✗ *She told that she was tired*)
- *Tell* without a listener is used only in fixed phrases: *tell the truth*, *tell a lie*, *tell a story*, *tell the time*, *tell the difference*.

## Tenses in reported speech

::: theorem Backshift {#rule-backshift}
When the reporting verb is in the **past** (*said*, *told*, *asked*), the verbs in the reported clause are often moved one step back in time, because the original present is now past:

| original words | reported after *said* |
|---|---|
| present simple: *"I **work** in Xi'an."* | past simple: *She said she **worked** in Xi'an.* |
| present progressive: *"I'**m** **leaving**."* | past progressive: *She said she **was leaving**.* |
| present perfect: *"I'**ve finished**."* | past perfect: *She said she **had finished**.* |
| past simple: *"I **finished** at six."* | past perfect or past simple: *She said she **had finished** / **finished** at six.* |
| *will*: *"I'**ll** call you."* | *would*: *She said she **would** call me.* |
| *can*: *"I **can** help."* | *could*: *She said she **could** help.* |
| *may*: *"I **may** be late."* | *might*: *She said she **might** be late.* |
| *must*: *"I **must** go."* | *must* or *had to*: *She said she **must** / **had to** go.* |

The past perfect and *would*, *could*, *should*, *might* and *ought to* do not change: *"I'd love to come"* → *She said she'**d** love to come.*
:::

::: theorem When the tense need not change {#rule-no-backshift}
Backshift is **optional** when the reported situation is **still true** or **still in the future** at the time of reporting:

- *"I live in Xi'an."* → *She told me she **lives** in Xi'an.* (she still does) or *… she **lived** in Xi'an.*
- *"The exam will be on Friday."* → (reported on Wednesday) *The teacher said the exam **will be** on Friday.* or *… **would be** on Friday.*
- *"Water boils at 100 °C."* → *He explained that water **boils** at 100 °C.*

Backshift is **not** used when the reporting verb is in the **present**, present perfect or future: *She **says** she**'s** tired.* *He**'s told** me he **can't** come.* *I'**ll tell** them you**'re** busy.*

Keep backshift when the situation is over, or when the reporter wants to show that it is the speaker's claim, not a fact: *He said he **was** innocent* (neutral, or doubtful) — *He said he **is** innocent* (the reporter accepts that it is still true, or at least still claimed).
:::

::: proof
Quirk et al. (1985), Huddleston and Pullum (2002) and Swan (2016) treat backshift in the same way: it is not an automatic transformation but the normal consequence of reporting from a later time point. If the original *present* is still present when the report is made, the present tense is equally correct, and in conversation it is common. Huddleston and Pullum point out that the backshifted and non-backshifted versions differ slightly in what the reporter takes responsibility for: the present tense implies that the reporter also regards the content as currently true. The past simple → past perfect shift is the most often omitted, because the order of events is usually clear without it (Swan 2016). Rules of the kind "always change *will* into *would*" are teaching simplifications, and examiners who mark a correct unshifted present as wrong are applying a rule that grammars do not support.
:::

:::

::: example Shift or no shift? {#ex-shift}
Report each sentence after *said*, giving both versions where both are possible, and explain. (a) Yesterday, Ali said: *"I'm learning Chinese."* (He still is.) (b) Last year, Ali said: *"I'm learning Chinese."* (He gave up in the summer.) (c) This morning, the forecaster said: *"It'll rain this afternoon."* (It is now noon.) (d) Your friend said: *"The Earth is about 4.5 billion years old."* (e) Ten minutes ago, your sister said: *"I've lost my keys."* (She is still looking.)
::: solution
(a) *Ali said he **is** learning Chinese* or *… he **was** learning Chinese.* Both: it is still true.

(b) *Ali said he **was** learning Chinese.* Only backshift: the situation is over.

(c) *The forecaster said it **will** rain this afternoon* or *… it **would** rain this afternoon.* The afternoon is still in the future.

(d) *My friend said the Earth **is** about 4.5 billion years old.* A general fact: backshift would sound as if it had changed. (*was* is also found, but the present is better.)

(e) *My sister said she **has** lost her keys* or *… she **had** lost her keys.* Both, since the situation is still current; the present perfect sounds more immediate.
:::
:::

## Pronouns, time and place

::: theorem Changing the point of view {#rule-deixis}
Words that depend on **who** is speaking, **when** and **where** change to fit the new situation — not automatically, but only if the situation has changed:

| original | reported (if the time or place has changed) |
|---|---|
| *I*, *me*, *my*, *we*, *you* | *he*, *she*, *they*, *I* … (whoever is meant) |
| *now* | *then*, *at that time* |
| *today*, *tonight* | *that day*, *that night* |
| *yesterday* | *the day before*, *the previous day* |
| *tomorrow* | *the next day*, *the following day* |
| *last week*, *next week* | *the week before*, *the following week* |
| *ago* | *before*, *earlier* |
| *here* | *there* (or the name of the place) |
| *this*, *these* | *that*, *those*, *the* |
| *come*, *bring* | *go*, *take* (if the place is different) |

If the report is made on the same day and in the same place, *today*, *tomorrow* and *here* stay: *She said she'd see me **tomorrow*** (reported the same day).
:::

::: proof
These words are called **deictic** expressions: their meaning depends on the situation of speaking. Swan (2016) and Quirk et al. (1985) give the usual changes, and both stress that they follow from the facts, not from a rule of reported speech. The table is a list of what is *often* needed, and the reporter should always ask: from where I am now, which day, place or person is meant? *The next day* and *the following day* are neutral; in conversation, a speaker usually names the day instead: *She said she was going on **Tuesday***.
:::

::: example Reporting from a different time and place {#ex-deixis}
On Friday 3 May, in Chengdu, Ruth said to you: *"I arrived here yesterday, and I'm flying back to London tomorrow. Can you meet me at this hotel tonight?"* Report her words (a) the same evening, in the hotel lobby, and (b) two weeks later, in Shanghai.
::: solution
1. Find the deictic words: *I*, *here*, *yesterday*, *tomorrow*, *you*, *this hotel*, *tonight*.
2. For each report, ask: is the day the same? is the place the same?

(a) Same day, same place: *Ruth said she **arrived here yesterday** and **is flying** back to London **tomorrow**, and asked if I **could** meet her at **this hotel tonight**.* — Only the pronouns change. The tenses can stay, because the flight is still in the future; *could* reports a request either way.

(b) Two weeks later, elsewhere: *Ruth said she **had arrived** in Chengdu **the day before** and **was flying** back to London **the next day**, and asked if I **could** meet her at **her hotel that night**.* — *here* becomes the name of the place, *this hotel* becomes *her hotel* (or *the hotel*), and the times are moved to the past. In conversation you would probably name the days: *… had arrived on Thursday and was flying back on Saturday.*
:::
:::

::: quiz
On Monday your friend said: *"I'll finish the essay by Thursday."* It is now Tuesday. Which report is **not** acceptable?
- [ ] *She said she'll finish the essay by Thursday.*
- [ ] *She said she'd finish the essay by Thursday.*
- [x] *She said she'll have finished the essay yesterday.*
- [ ] *She told me she would finish the essay by Thursday.*
::: solution
Thursday is still in the future, so both *will* and *would* are correct; *told me* is also fine. The third sentence changes the content and mixes a future form with *yesterday*.
:::
:::

## Reporting questions

Reported questions are indirect questions, and they follow the rules of [[complex-sentences/noun-clauses#rule-word-order]]: statement word order, no *do*, and *if* or *whether* for yes/no questions.

- *"Where do you live?"* → *He asked me **where I lived**.* (✗ *where did I live*, ✗ *where do I live*)
- *"Are you coming?"* → *She asked **if / whether I was coming**.*
- *"Who told you?"* → *They wanted to know **who had told me**.*
- *"Shall I open the window?"* → *He asked **if he should open** the window.* (an offer) or *He offered to open the window.*

Reporting verbs for questions are *ask*, *want to know*, *wonder* and, in formal style, *enquire* (BrE) or *inquire* (AmE and BrE). *Ask* can take the listener as object (*She asked **me** whether…*); *wonder* and *want to know* cannot.

## Reporting commands, requests and advice

Commands, requests, advice and warnings are usually reported with **verb + object + *to*-infinitive** ([[verb-patterns/object-and-verb#rule-obj-to]]). The negative is *not to*.

- *"Sit down, please."* → *She asked us **to sit** down.*
- *"Don't touch it!"* → *He told the children **not to touch** it.*
- *"You should see a lawyer."* → *My friend advised me **to see** a lawyer.*
- *"Remember to lock the door."* → *She reminded me **to lock** the door.*
- *"Don't go out alone at night."* → *They warned us **not to go** out alone at night.*

A request can also be reported with a *that*-clause and a modal: *She asked **if we could** sit down.* *Tell* + *that*-clause reports information, not a command: *She told us **that** we **should** sit down* reports advice; *She told us **to** sit down* reports an order.

::: quiz
Which is the correct report of *"Please don't tell anyone," Kenji said to me*?
- [ ] *Kenji said me not to tell anyone.*
- [ ] *Kenji asked me don't tell anyone.*
- [x] *Kenji asked me not to tell anyone.*
- [ ] *Kenji told to me not to tell anyone.*
::: solution
*Ask* + object + *not to* reports a negative request. *Say* cannot take a listener without *to* and is not used with object + infinitive; *don't* cannot follow *asked me*; and *tell* takes the listener directly, without *to*.
:::
:::

## Choosing the reporting verb

*Say*, *tell* and *ask* report the words. Other reporting verbs report what the speaker was **doing** with the words — promising, refusing, admitting, complaining — and often make the report shorter and more precise. Each has its own pattern.

::: theorem Patterns of reporting verbs {#rule-reporting-verbs}
| pattern | verbs | example |
|---|---|---|
| verb + *that*-clause | *add*, *admit*, *agree*, *announce*, *argue*, *claim*, *complain*, *confirm*, *deny*, *explain*, *insist*, *mention*, *point out*, *promise*, *reply*, *state* | *She **admitted that** she had made a mistake.* |
| verb + listener + *that*-clause | *tell*, *inform*, *assure*, *remind*, *warn*, *convince*, *persuade* | *He **assured us that** everything was fine.* |
| verb + *to*-infinitive | *agree*, *offer*, *promise*, *refuse*, *threaten*, *claim*, *decide* | *He **refused to** answer.* *She **offered to** help.* |
| verb + object + *to*-infinitive | *ask*, *tell*, *advise*, *encourage*, *invite*, *order*, *persuade*, *remind*, *urge*, *warn* | *They **urged me to** apply.* |
| verb + *-ing* | *admit*, *deny*, *mention*, *recommend*, *suggest* | *He **denied taking** the money.* |
| verb + (object) + preposition + *-ing* | *apologise for*, *insist on*, *object to*; *accuse* sb *of*, *blame* sb *for*, *congratulate* sb *on*, *thank* sb *for*, *warn* sb *against* | *She **accused him of lying**.* *He **apologised for being** late.* |
| verb + *that* + subjunctive / *should* | *suggest*, *recommend*, *propose*, *insist*, *demand* | *She **suggested that** we **leave** early.* |

Common errors: ✗ *suggest someone to do*, ✗ *explain someone that*, ✗ *deny to do*, ✗ *accuse someone for*. See [[verb-patterns/object-and-verb#rule-that-clause]].
:::

::: proof
The patterns are those given in Swan (2016) and in the learner's dictionaries, which list the pattern of each verb in its entry; the COBUILD *Grammar Patterns* volume on verbs (Francis, Hunston & Manning 1996) groups the verbs by pattern from corpus evidence. The choice of reporting verb is never neutral. *Admit* and *confess* present the content as true and unwelcome to the speaker; *claim* and *allege* report it without the reporter accepting it; *point out*, *show* and *demonstrate* commit the reporter to its truth (they are factive, [[complex-sentences/noun-clauses]]); *insist* suggests that others disagreed. In news and academic writing this choice is one of the main ways in which a writer shows stance ([[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]]).
:::

::: example Choosing the reporting verb {#ex-verbs}
Report each sentence with a suitable verb from [[#rule-reporting-verbs]], without using *say*, *tell* or *ask*. (a) *"I'll pay you back on Friday, I promise," said Raj.* (b) *"I didn't take your phone!" said Lucy.* (c) *"OK, yes, I broke the vase," said my son.* (d) *"Why don't we take the train?" said Mei.* (e) *"Congratulations on your new job!" Grandma said to me.* (f) *"If you don't pay, I'll call the police," the shopkeeper said to the man.*
::: solution
(a) *Raj **promised to pay** me back on Friday.* / *Raj promised that he would pay me back on Friday.*

(b) *Lucy **denied taking** my phone.* / *Lucy denied that she had taken my phone.* (✗ *denied to take*)

(c) *My son **admitted breaking** the vase.* / *… admitted that he had broken the vase.*

(d) *Mei **suggested taking** the train.* / *Mei suggested that we (should) take the train.* (✗ *suggested us to take*)

(e) *Grandma **congratulated me on** my new job.*

(f) *The shopkeeper **threatened to call** the police if the man didn't pay.*
:::
:::

::: warning Reporting verbs that do not take an object + *to*
✗ *He suggested me to see a doctor.* ✓ *He suggested (that) I (should) see a doctor.* ✓ *He advised me to see a doctor.*

✗ *She explained me that the office was closed.* ✓ *She explained (to me) that the office was closed.*

✗ *He denied to have stolen the money.* ✓ *He denied stealing / having stolen the money.* ✓ *He denied that he had stolen the money.*

✗ *They accused him for cheating.* ✓ *They accused him **of** cheating.*
:::

## Reporting in speech, news and fiction

**Conversation.** Speakers often report in direct speech, and often switch to the present tense to make a story vivid ([[tense-and-aspect/tenses-in-texts]]): *So she **says** to me, "Where have you been?"* In informal English, especially among younger speakers, *be like* and *go* introduce direct speech or even a gesture: *And I **was like**, "No way!"* *Then he **goes**, "Are you serious?"* These quotatives are well established in British, American and other varieties of informal speech, but are not used in formal writing.

**News.** News reports use *say* far more than any other reporting verb, because it is neutral, and add precise verbs where the act matters (*announced*, *denied*, *warned*). Reports that the writer has not confirmed are marked: *according to*, *reportedly*, *is said to*, *is thought to*, *allegedly*. The passive patterns *It is said that…* and *X is said to…* are in [[verb-patterns/the-passive]].

**Fiction.** Novels often use **free indirect speech**, which has the backshifted tenses and changed pronouns of reported speech but no reporting verb, and keeps the character's own words and questions: *She looked at the clock. Where **was** he? He **had** promised to be home by six — and now it **was** nearly nine.* The reader hears the character's thoughts through the narrator's voice. This is discussed in [[reading-literature/narrative]].

::: example A meeting reported in minutes {#ex-minutes}
Write formal minutes (reported speech, no direct speech) for this exchange. **Chair:** *"Let's start. Has everyone read the report?"* **Dr Liang:** *"Yes, and I think the budget is too small. We can't finish the project on time without two more staff."* **Ms Brown:** *"I disagree. We've finished similar projects with fewer people."* **Chair:** *"Could you both send me your figures by Friday? We'll decide next week."*
::: solution
1. Choose reporting verbs that show what each speaker did: opening, asking, arguing, disagreeing, requesting.
2. Backshift (the minutes are read later), change pronouns and time words, and use statement order in questions.

*The Chair opened the meeting and **asked whether** everyone **had read** the report. Dr Liang **confirmed that** she had and **argued that** the budget **was** too small: in her view, the project **could not** be finished on time without two additional staff. Ms Brown **disagreed**, **pointing out that** similar projects **had been** completed with fewer people. The Chair **asked** both members **to send** their figures by Friday and **said that** a decision **would be** taken the following week.*

Note *the following week* (minutes are read later), the passives (*had been completed*, *would be taken*) typical of minutes, and the neutral *said* for the final announcement.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **说 for *say* and *tell*.** Chinese 说 and 告诉 do not map neatly onto *say* and *tell*: 他跟我说 is *he told me* or *he said to me*. ✗ *He said me* comes from treating *say* like 告诉. Remember: *tell* someone, *say* something (*to* someone).
- **No backshift in Chinese.** Chinese verbs have no tense, so a report keeps the verb unchanged and adjusts only time words: 他说他明天来 can be reported later as 他说他第二天来. English adds a tense change, and also requires a choice: backshift if the situation is over, optional if it is still true.
- **Direct and indirect merge in Chinese speech.** In spoken Chinese, 他说我明天来 is ambiguous: it can mean ‘he said, "I'll come tomorrow"’ (direct) or ‘he said that I would come tomorrow’ (indirect). Chinese writing marks direct speech with quotation marks (“……”) and a colon after 说, but in speech the two often blend. English keeps them apart by pronouns, tense and word order, so a reporter must decide which one is meant.
- **Questions.** Chinese reported questions keep the order of direct questions (他问我住在哪儿), which English does not ([[complex-sentences/noun-clauses]]). Chinese 问 + 是不是 / 吗 corresponds to *ask if / whether*.
- **Commands with 让 and 叫.** 老师让我们明天交作业 is a reported command: *The teacher told us to hand in our homework the next day* — *tell* + object + *to*-infinitive.
:::

::: history
The reporting of speech has long been part of school grammar, and the "rules" of tense change were already a fixture of English teaching in the nineteenth century, taught in parallel with the much stricter sequence of tenses of Latin. In 1912 the Swiss linguist Charles Bally described *le style indirect libre* — free indirect speech — in French literature, and Otto Jespersen (*The Philosophy of Grammar*, 1924) discussed the same technique in English under the name "represented speech". Corpus grammars later showed how differently reporting works across registers: Biber et al. (1999) found *say* to be by far the most common reporting verb in conversation and news. And in 1990 Carl Blyth, Sigrid Recktenwald and Jenny Wang published the first study of the new quotative *be like* in American speech, which has since spread through informal English around the world.
:::

## Where this leads

Reporting the words of sources in academic writing — with *argue*, *claim*, *suggest*, *find* — is taught in [[academic-reading/paraphrase-and-quotation]] and [[academic-writing/citation-referencing]]. Free indirect speech in novels is in [[reading-literature/narrative]], and quotation as a skill in conversation in [[listening-and-conversation/storytelling]]. The passive reporting patterns *It is said that…* are in [[verb-patterns/the-passive]].

::: summary
- **Reported speech** gives the content of what was said from the reporter's point of view; direct speech gives the exact words ([[#def-reported]]).
- *Say* (*to* someone) + clause; *tell* + someone + clause.
- After a past reporting verb, tenses usually **shift back** one step; past perfect and *would/could/might/should* do not change ([[#rule-backshift]]).
- Backshift is **optional** when the content is still true or still in the future, and is not used after a present reporting verb ([[#rule-no-backshift]]).
- Pronouns, time and place words change only if the speaker, time or place has changed ([[#rule-deixis]]).
- Reported questions use *if*/*whether* or a *wh*-word and **statement order**; commands and requests use *tell/ask* + object + (*not*) *to*.
- Reporting verbs have fixed patterns (*deny* + *-ing*, *refuse* + *to*, *accuse* sb *of*, *suggest* + *that*/*-ing*) and show stance (*claim* versus *point out*) ([[#rule-reporting-verbs]]).
:::

::: exercise Correct the report {level=2}
Correct the mistakes. (a) *She said me that she will call later.* (yesterday) (b) *He asked me where did I buy my shoes.* (c) *The doctor suggested me to do more exercise.* (d) *My teacher explained us how the system works.* (e) *They told that the concert was cancelled.*
::: solution
(a) *She told me (that) she would call later.* (or *said to me*) (b) *He asked me where I had bought (bought) my shoes.* (c) *The doctor suggested (that) I (should) do more exercise.* / *The doctor advised me to do more exercise.* (d) *My teacher explained to us how the system works* (still true, so *works* can stay). (e) *They told us / They said that the concert had been cancelled.*
:::

::: exercise Report a conversation {level=3}
Turn this dialogue into a paragraph of reported speech, as if you were telling a friend about it a week later. Use at least four different reporting verbs. **Landlord:** *"I'm putting the rent up next month."* **Wang Lei:** *"But you raised it in January! Why?"* **Landlord:** *"Costs have gone up. If you don't like it, you can leave."* **Wang Lei:** *"I'll pay the old rent until you fix the heating."* **Landlord:** *"All right, I'll send someone tomorrow."*
::: hint
Think about what each speaker was doing: announcing, protesting, explaining, threatening, promising.
:::
::: solution
A model:

*Wang Lei's landlord **announced** that he **was putting** the rent up the following month. Wang Lei **protested that** he **had** already **raised** it in January and **asked** him **why**. The landlord **explained that** costs **had gone** up and **said** that if Wang Lei didn't like it, he could leave. Wang Lei **refused to pay** more than the old rent until the heating was fixed, and in the end the landlord **promised to send** someone the next day.*

Note *the following month* and *the next day* (reported a week later), and the question reduced to *asked him why*.
:::

::: exercise Stance in reporting {level=3}
A news report can present the same words in different lights. Report this statement three times: neutrally, in a way that suggests the reporter doubts it, and in a way that suggests the reporter accepts it. *The company spokesperson: "Our products are completely safe."*
::: solution
Models:

Neutral: *A company spokesperson **said** that its products **were** completely safe.*

Doubtful: *A company spokesperson **claimed** that its products **were** completely safe.* / *The products are, **according to** the company, completely safe.*

Accepting: *A company spokesperson **confirmed** that its products **are** completely safe.* / *… **pointed out** that the products are completely safe.*

*Claim* and *according to* keep the reporter at a distance; *confirm* and *point out* present the content as fact. Using the present *are* rather than the backshifted *were* also suggests that the reporter accepts the claim as true now.
:::
