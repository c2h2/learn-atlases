Two versions of the same paragraph from a student's report on a volunteering project:

> *Because we had no money, we asked local shops for help. Because they agreed, we bought paint. When we finished the walls, we painted the doors. Although it rained, we worked outside. Although we were tired, we finished on time.*

> *As we had no money, we asked local shops for help, and once they had agreed, we could buy paint. By the time we had finished the walls, the doors were the only thing left. We worked outside even though it rained — tired as we were, we finished on time.*

Both are correct. The first uses three subordinators, each twice, always at the start of the sentence. The second uses six, places them at the start, in the middle and at the end, and chooses each one for a precise meaning: *as* for a reason the reader can accept without discussion, *once* for a condition that must be completed first, *by the time* for a point before which something had happened, *even though* for a surprising fact, *tired as we were* for a formal concession.

The basic subordinators — *because*, *although*, *when*, *if*, *so that* — and the commas that go with them were taught in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/subordination]]. This lesson widens the range: it sets out the main meanings of adverbial clauses, the less common but important conjunctions for each, the differences between near-synonyms (*even if* and *even though*, *in case* and *if*, *while* and *whereas*), the tenses that time clauses require, and the reduced clauses used in formal writing. Conditional clauses have their own lesson, [[complex-sentences/conditionals]].

## The meanings of adverbial clauses

::: definition Adverbial clause {#def-adverbial-clause}
An **adverbial clause** is a subordinate clause that works as an adverbial in its superordinate clause: it tells us when, where, why, on what condition, in spite of what, for what purpose, with what result or in what manner the main situation holds. It is introduced by a **subordinator** (a subordinating conjunction such as *because*, *although*, *once*, *in case*), and it can be finite, non-finite or verbless:

- finite: ***Once the guests had left**, we cleared up.*
- non-finite: ***Having cleared up**, we went to bed.*
- verbless: ***Once inside**, she locked the door.*
:::

| meaning | common subordinators | more formal or less common |
|---|---|---|
| time | *when*, *while*, *as*, *before*, *after*, *until*, *since*, *as soon as*, *once* | *by the time*, *the moment*, *whenever*, *now that*, *no sooner … than* |
| place | *where*, *wherever* | |
| reason | *because*, *since*, *as* | *given that*, *seeing that* (informal), *in that*, *now that* |
| condition | *if*, *unless*, *as long as* | *provided (that)*, *on condition that* → [[complex-sentences/conditionals]] |
| concession and contrast | *although*, *though*, *even though*, *while*, *even if* | *whereas*, *much as*, *however* + adj., *no matter* + *wh*-, *adj./adv. + as* |
| purpose | *so that*, *so* (informal) | *in order that*, *lest*, *for fear that*; precaution: *in case* |
| result | *so … that*, *such … that*, *so* | |
| manner and comparison | *as*, *like* (informal), *the way* | *as if*, *as though* → [[complex-sentences/unreal-past]] |

Many subordinators have more than one meaning: *since* (time or reason), *as* (time, reason or manner), *while* (time or contrast), *so that* (purpose or result). The tense and the context usually decide, but where a sentence could be read two ways, choose a word with one meaning (*because*, *whereas*, *in order that*).

## Time

::: theorem Tenses in time clauses {#rule-time-tense}
1. A time clause about the **future** uses a **present** tense, not *will*: *I'll call you **when I arrive**.* (✗ *when I will arrive*)
2. To show that one future action will be **complete** before the other, use the **present perfect**: *You can go out **when you've finished** your homework.* *I'll tell him **as soon as I've spoken** to the manager.*
3. In the past, the **past perfect** shows that one action was complete before another: ***After she had read** the letter, she burnt it.* With *after* and *before*, which already show the order, the past simple is also correct: ***After she read** the letter, she burnt it.*
4. ***Since*** (time) takes a past simple for a point in time, with a perfect in the main clause: *I've lived here **since I was ten**.* *It's two years **since we last met**.*
5. ***By the time*** looks back from a point: main clause in a perfect: ***By the time** we arrived, the film **had started**.* ***By the time** you read this, I'**ll have left**.*
:::

::: proof
Rules 1–2 apply to all time and conditional subordinators that introduce a clause about the future (*when*, *as soon as*, *before*, *after*, *until*, *once*, *by the time*, *if*, *unless*); they are given in [[tense-and-aspect/future-forms#rule-time-clauses]] and [[sentences-and-paragraphs/subordination#rule-present-future]]. The future meaning is already supplied by the main clause, and the time clause presents its event as a fixed point from which the main event is located; English then uses the present (Quirk et al. 1985; Swan 2016). *Will* is possible after *when* only when the *when*-clause is not adverbial — in a noun clause (*I don't know **when** she **will** arrive*) or a relative clause (*the day **when** you **will** finally understand*).
:::

The less common time subordinators are worth learning as pairs of meaning:

- ***once*** ‘after, and as a result of, something being complete’: ***Once** you've paid, you'll get a receipt.*
- ***the moment*** / ***the minute*** / ***as soon as*** ‘immediately when’: *Call me **the moment** you hear anything.*
- ***until*** ‘up to the time when’ (the main situation continues and then stops) versus ***by the time*** ‘before or at the time when’: *I waited **until** he came.* — *I had eaten **by the time** he came.*
- ***whenever*** ‘every time that’ or ‘at any time that’: ***Whenever** I see that photo, I smile.*
- ***now that*** ‘because now’ — time and reason together: ***Now that** the exams are over, we can relax.*
- ***as*** ‘while, during, at the same moment as’, often for two changes happening together: ***As** the sun rose, the temperature climbed.*

::: example Tenses in time clauses {#ex-time}
Put the verbs in brackets into the right form, and explain. (a) *I'll lend you the book once I (finish) it.* (b) *By the time the guests (arrive), we (cook) everything.* — a past situation. (c) *We (not see) each other since we (leave) school.* (d) *Can you wait until I (find) my keys?* (e) *By the time you (get) this message, I (land) in Nairobi.*
::: solution
(a) *have finished* — future completion before the lending: present perfect in the time clause, not *will have finished*.

(b) *arrived … had cooked* — *by the time* in the past: the cooking was complete before the arrival.

(c) *haven't seen … left* — *since* + past simple for the starting point; present perfect in the main clause for the period up to now.

(d) *find* (or *'ve found*) — future meaning, present tense after *until*.

(e) *get … will have landed* — *by the time* in the future: present in the time clause, future perfect in the main clause.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *I'll wait here until you will come back.*
- [ ] *Since I have moved to Xi'an, I made many friends.*
- [x] *Once you've tried it, you'll never go back.*
- [ ] *By the time we got there, the shop closed.*
::: solution
*Once* + present perfect describes a future completion, and the main clause has *will*. The others need *until you come back*; *Since I moved to Xi'an, I have made many friends*; and *the shop had closed* (or *had already closed*).

## Reason

*Because*, *since* and *as* are compared in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/subordination]]: *because* states the reason as new, important information; *since* and *as* give a reason the reader probably accepts already, and usually come first. At this level three more are useful.

- ***given that*** (neutral to formal) ‘considering the fact that’: ***Given that** the budget is limited, we should prioritise.*
- ***seeing that*** / ***seeing as*** (informal) ‘because, as you can see’: ***Seeing as** you're here, you could help me.*
- ***in that*** (formal) ‘in the sense that, because’: *The study is unusual **in that** it follows the same people for forty years.*

*For* as a conjunction (*She was tired, **for** she had worked all night*) is literary or old-fashioned; it joins main clauses like *and* and cannot start a sentence's first clause.

## Concession and contrast

::: theorem Concession and contrast {#rule-concession}
1. ***Although***, ***though*** and ***even though*** introduce a fact that makes the main clause surprising. *Though* is more informal; *even though* is stronger. ***Although** it was late, she kept working.*
2. ***Even if*** introduces a **possibility**, not a fact: the main clause is true whether or not the possibility happens. ***Even if** it rains, the match will go ahead.* (It may or may not rain.) Compare ***Even though** it's raining, the match will go ahead.* (It is raining.)
3. ***While*** and ***whereas*** compare two facts that differ, without surprise: *My brother loves football, **whereas** I prefer tennis.* *Whereas* is more formal, and is common in academic writing.
4. ***However*** + adjective/adverb, ***no matter*** + *wh*-word and ***whatever***, ***whoever***, ***wherever*** say that the main clause is true whatever the value: ***However hard** I try, I can't remember her name.* ***No matter what** you say, I won't change my mind.*
5. In formal and literary English, an adjective, adverb or noun can come before ***as*** or ***though***: ***Tired as** she was, she kept going.* (= Although she was very tired …) ***Much as** I like him, I can't agree.*
6. *Despite* and *in spite of* are **prepositions**, followed by a noun phrase or *-ing*, not a clause: ***Despite** the rain* / ***Despite** feeling ill* / ***Despite the fact that** it rained*.
:::

::: proof
Quirk et al. (1985) distinguish **concession** (one situation is surprising in the light of the other: *although*) from **contrast** (two situations are simply compared: *whereas*), and Swan (2016) gives the same distinctions for *even if* and *even though*. Rule 5 — sometimes called concessive inversion, though the subject and verb are not inverted — is described as formal in both grammars; the American variant *As tired as she was, …* is also found. Rule 6 is a frequent source of errors, because *despite* means the same as *although* but belongs to a different word class ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/subordination]] has the basic warning). Note also that *while* is ambiguous between time and contrast: *While I was in Paris, my sister was in Rome* can be read either way, and both readings are true at once.
:::

::: warning *Although … but* and *despite* + clause
✗ *Although the hotel was expensive, but it was very dirty.* ✓ *Although the hotel was expensive, it was very dirty.* ✓ *The hotel was expensive, but it was very dirty.*

✗ *Despite it was raining, we went out.* ✓ *Despite the rain, we went out.* ✓ ***Although** it was raining, we went out.* ✓ *Despite **the fact that** it was raining, we went out.*

✗ *Even it was late, he called me.* ✓ ***Even though** it was late, he called me.* — *even* alone is an adverb, not a conjunction.
:::

::: example Choosing the concessive {#ex-concession}
Choose the best word and explain. (a) *(Even if / Even though) I had a map, I got lost.* (b) *I wouldn't sell this house (even if / even though) you offered me a million.* (c) *The north of the country is dry, (whereas / although) the south gets heavy rain.* (d) *(However / Whatever) tired you are, you must finish the report.* (e) *(Despite / Although) her injury, she won the race.*
::: solution
(a) *Even though* — I had a map: it is a fact.

(b) *even if* — the offer is hypothetical (note the unreal *offered … wouldn't*, [[complex-sentences/conditionals]]).

(c) *whereas* — two facts compared, neither surprising given the other. (*although* would suggest that a dry north makes a wet south surprising.)

(d) *However* — *however* + adjective. (*Whatever* needs a noun or a clause: *Whatever happens, …*)

(e) *Despite* — followed by a noun phrase. (*Although she was injured, …*)
:::
:::

## Purpose and precaution

::: theorem Clauses of purpose {#rule-purpose}
1. ***So that*** + a modal verb (*can*, *could*, *will*, *would*, *may*, *might*) expresses purpose: *I'll write it down **so that** I **don't** forget.* *We left early **so that** we **would** miss the traffic.* In informal English, *that* is often left out: *… **so** we'd miss the traffic.*
2. ***In order that*** is formal: *Dates are announced early **in order that** students **may** plan ahead.*
3. When the subject of the purpose is the same as the main subject, a ***to*-infinitive** is more usual: *She left early **(in order) to** catch the train.* The negative is ***so as not to*** or ***in order not to***: *We spoke quietly **so as not to** wake the baby.* (Plain *not to* is possible but less common for purpose.)
4. ***In case*** gives a **precaution** against something that might happen: *Take an umbrella **in case** it rains.* (= because it might rain). It does not mean *if*: *Take an umbrella **if** it rains* means take one only when it is raining.
5. ***Lest*** (formal or literary) and ***for fear that*** mean ‘so that … not’: *He spoke softly **lest** he (should) wake the child.*
:::

::: proof
Swan (2016) and Quirk et al. (1985) describe the modal verbs in purpose clauses and the use of the infinitive when the subjects are the same. On *in case*, British usage keeps the precaution meaning strictly; Swan notes that in American English *in case* is also used to mean *if* (*In case you need me, I'll be in my office*), and *in case of* + noun (*In case of fire, break the glass*) means ‘if there is’ in both varieties. *Lest* is rare in British English and more common in American English, where it is often followed by a subjunctive (*lest he wake*); Biber et al. (1999) and Swan class it as formal everywhere.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence means ‘Take some cash because the card machine might not work’?
- [ ] *Take some cash if the card machine doesn't work.*
- [x] *Take some cash in case the card machine doesn't work.*
- [ ] *Take some cash so that the card machine doesn't work.*
- [ ] *Take some cash unless the card machine works.*
::: solution
*In case* gives a precaution: take the cash now, before you know whether the machine works. *If* would mean ‘only when you find that it doesn't work’ — too late to take cash. *So that* gives an absurd purpose, and *unless* means ‘if … not’ (take cash only if the machine doesn't work), which has the same problem as *if*.
:::
:::

## Result

::: theorem Clauses of result {#rule-result}
1. ***so*** + adjective/adverb + ***that***: *The film was **so** long **that** I fell asleep.* *She spoke **so** quickly **that** nobody understood.*
2. ***such*** (+ *a/an*) (+ adjective) + noun + ***that***: *It was **such a** long film **that** I fell asleep.* *They were **such** good friends **that** …*
3. ***so much / so many / so few / so little*** + noun + ***that***: *There were **so many** people **that** we couldn't get in.*
4. ***so*** (no *that*), after a comma, for a result in informal and neutral style: *The shop was closed, **so** we went home.* This *so* is a coordinator, like *and* ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/coordination]]).

*That* is often left out after *so* and *such* in informal English: *I was so tired __ I fell asleep.*
:::

The difference between *so* and *such* is one of word class: *so* goes with an adjective or adverb, *such* with a noun phrase. ✗ *It was so a long film.* ✗ *It was such long.* The formal inverted forms *So long was the film that…* and *Such was the noise that…* are covered in [[complex-sentences/emphasis-inversion]].

::: example *So that*: purpose or result? {#ex-so-that}
Is the clause in bold a purpose or a result? How do you know? (a) *She saved for two years **so that she could travel**.* (b) *The bridge was closed, **so that we had to drive round**.* (c) *Put the milk in the fridge **so that it doesn't go off**.* (d) *He trained every day, **so that by June he was ready**.*
::: solution
Purpose clauses with *so that* usually contain a modal verb and no comma, and describe something wanted. Result clauses usually follow a comma and describe something that happened.

(a) Purpose — *could*, no comma, something wanted.

(b) Result — comma, past tense of a fact; *so that* = *and so*. (Plain *so* is more usual here.)

(c) Purpose — present tense with future meaning, a desired outcome. (With present tense, *so that* for purpose is informal; *won't go off* is also possible.)

(d) Result — comma and a fact; it might also have been his purpose, but the sentence states what happened.
:::
:::

## Manner and comparison

*As* introduces a clause of manner or comparison: *Do it **as** I showed you.* *As I expected, she was late.* In informal English, especially American, ***like*** is used in the same way: *Do it **like** I showed you.* *Like I said, she was late.* Usage guides have long objected to *like* as a conjunction, and it is still avoided in formal writing, but dictionaries record it as standard in speech and informal writing (Swan 2016 notes it as informal in British English and very common in American English). In formal writing, use *as* or *the way*: *Do it **the way** I showed you.*

*As if* and *as though* introduce a comparison with something that may be unreal: *He talks **as if** he knew everything.* The choice of tense after them is part of [[complex-sentences/unreal-past]].

## Place

*Where* and *wherever* introduce clauses of place: *Sit **where** you like.* ***Wherever** she goes, people recognise her.* They are less common than other adverbial clauses, and in descriptions English usually prefers a prepositional phrase (*by the window*) or a relative clause (*the place where…*).

## Reduced adverbial clauses

In formal English, especially writing, an adverbial clause can be shortened when its subject is the same as the main subject and its verb is *be* or can be made non-finite:

- ***When** in Rome, do as the Romans do.* (= When you are in Rome)
- ***Although** tired, the team played well.* (= Although they were tired)
- ***If** necessary, we can change the date.* (= If it is necessary)
- ***Once** opened, the jar should be kept in the fridge.*
- ***While** waiting for the bus, I read the paper.*
- ***Before** leaving, please switch off the lights.*

The understood subject must be the subject of the main clause. ✗ *Once opened, you should keep the jar in the fridge* suggests that *you* are opened. This is the problem of the dangling modifier, treated fully with participle clauses in [[complex-sentences/participle-infinitive-clauses]].

::: example Upgrading a paragraph {#ex-upgrade}
Rewrite the paragraph using at least five different subordinators from this lesson, with no subordinator used twice. *Because the train was late, we missed the first talk. When we arrived, the hall was full. Although we had tickets, we had to stand. Because the second speaker was very good, we stayed. When the conference ended, we were exhausted.*
::: solution
1. Decide the relation in each sentence: reason, time (with completion), concession, reason, time.
2. Choose a subordinator that expresses it precisely, and vary positions.
3. Combine where the relation crosses sentences.

A model:

*As the train was late, we missed the first talk, and **by the time** we arrived the hall was full. **Even though** we had tickets, we had to stand. The second speaker was **so** good **that** we stayed to the end, and **once** the conference was over, we were exhausted.*

Subordinators: *as* (reason the reader can accept), *by the time* (completion before a point — note *was full* is a state), *even though* (surprising fact), *so … that* (result, which replaces the second *because*), *once* (after completion).
:::
:::

## Position of the clause

An adverbial clause can usually come before or after its main clause. The choice is not free: it depends on what the sentence is about and what is new.

- An initial clause sets up a **frame** — a time, a condition, a known reason — for the main statement: ***When the war ended**, my grandfather returned to Shanghai.* This is common at the start of a paragraph and links to what has gone before.
- A final clause gives **new information**, often the point of the sentence: *My grandfather returned to Shanghai **when the war ended**.* (answering "When did he return?"). *Because*-clauses are more often final than *since*- and *as*-clauses, because the reason is usually the new information.
- Purpose and result clauses are almost always final, because they follow the action.

::: note English and Chinese
- **Paired connectives.** Chinese uses pairs — 虽然……但是, 因为……所以, 既然……就, 即使……也 — where English uses only one conjunction ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/subordination#rule-one-link]]). ✗ *Although … but*, ✗ *Because … so* are the classic results. Choose *either* the subordinator *or* the coordinator.
- **Connectives left out (意合).** Chinese often leaves the relation between clauses unmarked: 下雨了，我们就不去了 (reason), 他来了我就走 (time or condition), 天再冷也得去 (concession). English normally needs a subordinator, and the translator must choose the right one: *Since it's raining, we won't go*; *I'll leave when (as soon as) he comes*; *However cold it is, we have to go* (连淑能 1993 discusses many such cases).
- **Order.** In Chinese, a subordinate clause of time, reason, condition or concession normally comes **before** the main clause: 因为下雨，我们没去 is far more usual than 我们没去，因为下雨, which sounds like an afterthought. Li and Thompson (1981) describe this as the usual Chinese order of background before foreground. English allows both orders freely and often puts reason clauses last. Learners writing English can vary the order to put new information at the end.
- **即使 and 虽然.** 虽然 (fact) corresponds to *although* / *even though*; 即使 / 就算 (possibility) to *even if*. Chinese speakers sometimes use *even though* for 即使: ✗ *Even though it rains tomorrow, we'll go* ✓ *Even if it rains tomorrow, we'll go.*
- **以便 and 以免.** 以便 corresponds to *so that*, 以免 / 免得 to *so as not to*, *in case* or *lest*: 带把伞，以免下雨 — *Take an umbrella in case it rains.*
:::

::: history
The traditional classification of adverbial clauses by meaning — time, place, cause, condition, concession, purpose, result, manner, comparison — comes from the grammars of Latin and passed into English school grammars in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries; the major modern grammars keep it, with refinements (Quirk et al. 1985 separate concession from contrast). Linguists have since compared adverbial clauses across languages. Sandra Thompson and Robert Longacre's survey "Adverbial clauses" (1985) found that most languages have special forms for clauses of time, condition, purpose and reason, and that conditional clauses tend to come first; Joseph Greenberg had already proposed in 1963 that in conditional statements the condition normally precedes the conclusion in all languages. In 1975 the philosopher H. P. Grice used *but* and *therefore* to show that some words add a meaning — a contrast, a consequence — that is not part of the truth of the sentence, which he called a conventional implicature: the same idea as the truth table for *even if* in this lesson.
:::

::: summary
- An adverbial clause tells when, where, why, on what condition, in spite of what, for what purpose, with what result or how the main situation holds ([[#def-adverbial-clause]]).
- Future time clauses use the present (or present perfect for completion); *by the time* and *since* need a perfect in the main clause ([[#rule-time-tense]]).
- *Even though* introduces a fact, *even if* a possibility; *whereas* compares two facts; *however*/*no matter*/*whatever* mean ‘it doesn't matter how/what’; *despite* takes a noun or *-ing*, not a clause ([[#rule-concession]]).
- *So that* + modal for purpose; *to*-infinitive when the subject is the same; *in case* means ‘as a precaution’, not ‘if’ (in British English) ([[#rule-purpose]]).
- *So* + adjective/adverb + *that*, *such* + noun phrase + *that* for result ([[#rule-result]]).
- *Like* as a conjunction is informal; use *as* or *the way* in formal writing.
- In formal writing, clauses can be reduced (*Although tired, …*, *If necessary, …*) when the subject is the same as the main subject.
- Use one connective per link: ✗ *although … but*, ✗ *because … so*. Chinese puts subordinate clauses first; English lets you put new information at the end.
:::

(a) *Although she's very rich, she isn't happy.* (b) *I'll give you my number in case you need to contact me.* (c) *He left the light on so that the house wouldn't look empty.* (d) *However much I eat, I never put on weight.* (e) *Much as I like Paris, I couldn't live there.*
:::

::: exercise Reduced clauses {level=2}
Shorten the adverbial clause where possible. If it is not possible, say why. (a) *When you are in doubt, ask a member of staff.* (b) *Although he was exhausted, he finished the race.* (c) *Although the road was icy, the bus kept going.* (d) *If it is necessary, we can meet on Saturday.* (e) *While I was waiting, I phoned my sister.*
::: solution
(a) *When in doubt, ask a member of staff.* (b) *Although exhausted, he finished the race.* (c) Not possible as a simple reduction: the subject of the clause (*the road*) is not the subject of the main clause (*the bus*). ✗ *Although icy, the bus kept going* would mean the bus was icy. (d) *If necessary, we can meet on Saturday.* (e) *While waiting, I phoned my sister.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Translate the connectives {level=3}
Translate into English. Choose the subordinator that best expresses the relation, which the Chinese may leave implicit. (a) 你不早点说，我已经买好票了。 (b) 再忙也要吃早饭。 (c) 他一到家就给我打了电话。 (d) 既然你来了，就留下来吃饭吧。 (e) 带上手机，万一迷路了可以打电话。
::: solution
Models:

(a) *I wish you'd said so earlier — I've already bought the tickets.* or *Why didn't you say so earlier? I've already bought the tickets.* (The Chinese is a reproach; a subordinator may not be the best choice.)

(b) ***However** busy you are, you should have breakfast.* / *Even if you're busy, …*

(c) *He called me **as soon as** (**the moment**) he got home.*

(d) ***Since** (**Now that**) you're here, stay for dinner.*

(e) *Take your phone **in case** you get lost — then you can call.*
:::

::: exercise Write a short report {level=3}
Write a paragraph of 100–130 words comparing two places you know (two cities, two schools, two workplaces). Use at least six different adverbial clauses: at least one each of time, reason, concession or contrast, purpose and result, and one reduced clause. Vary the position of the clauses.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

*I grew up in Harbin, but I have lived in Guangzhou **since I started university**. The two cities could hardly be more different. Harbin is cold and dry, **whereas** Guangzhou is hot and humid for most of the year. **Although I love the snow**, I do not miss Harbin's winters, which are **so** long **that** spring seems to arrive in June. **Because Guangzhou is a trading city**, its streets are busy at all hours, and I have learned to leave home early **so that I don't get stuck in traffic**. **When asked which I prefer**, I say Guangzhou for work and Harbin for holidays.*

Check: time (*since*), contrast (*whereas*), concession (*although*), result (*so … that*), reason (*because*), purpose (*so that*), reduced (*When asked*).
