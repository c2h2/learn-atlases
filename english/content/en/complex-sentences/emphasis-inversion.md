A Chinese sentence with 直到……才 is a frequent puzzle in translation:

> 直到半夜他才回家。

A word-for-word version gives ✗ *Until midnight he just came home*, which is not English. *He didn't come home until midnight* is correct but loses the emphasis on how late it was. English has two structures that keep it:

- *It was **not until midnight that** he came home.* (a cleft sentence)
- ***Not until midnight did he** come home.* (inversion — formal)

Both move one part of the sentence into a position where it stands out. English word order is fairly fixed — subject, verb, object, adverbial — and the last important word of a clause normally carries the main stress and the new information. To give special weight to something else, English has a set of special structures: **cleft sentences** with *it* and *what*, **fronting**, and **inversion** of subject and verb after certain words. These are features of careful speech and especially of writing, where there is no voice to show emphasis. This lesson explains how they are built, what they do, and the register each belongs to.

## Given and new information

::: definition Given and new information, end-focus {#def-focus}
In a clause, **given** information is what the listener or reader already knows from the context; **new** information is what the speaker is adding. English tends to place given information early and new information late. The main stress in speech normally falls on the last important word of the clause, the **focus**: *I bought a new **bike**.* This tendency is called **end-focus**.

A related tendency, **end-weight**, places long and complex elements towards the end ([[complex-sentences/clause-structure#rule-main-visible]]).
:::

Ordinary English gives emphasis in speech by moving the stress: *I bought a new bike* (not someone else), *I bought a **new** bike* (not a second-hand one). In writing this is impossible, and the structures in this lesson do the same job by changing the order of the words instead. The passive is one such tool, putting the doer last ([[verb-patterns/the-passive]]); clefts and inversion are others.

## *It*-clefts

::: definition *It*-cleft {#def-it-cleft}
An ***it*-cleft** sentence divides a clause into two parts:

*It* + *be* + **focus** + *that*/*who* + the rest of the clause

*Mei broke the vase yesterday.* →

- *It was **Mei** who broke the vase yesterday.* (not Tom)
- *It was **the vase** that Mei broke yesterday.* (not the bowl)
- *It was **yesterday** that Mei broke the vase.* (not today)

The focus is the part that is new or contrasted; the *that*-clause contains what is treated as already known (here: someone broke something at some time).
:::

::: theorem Forming *it*-clefts {#rule-it-cleft}
1. The focus can be a noun phrase (subject or object), a prepositional phrase or an adverbial, including an adverbial clause: *It was **because he was ill** that he missed the exam.* It cannot normally be the verb: to focus an action, use a *wh*-cleft ([[#rule-wh-cleft]]).
2. *Be* is usually *is* or *was* (matching the time of the clause), and can include a modal: *It **might have been** Mei who broke it.*
3. Use ***who*** or ***that*** after a person, ***that*** after other focuses. *That* can be left out informally when the focus is not the subject: *It was the vase __ she broke.*
4. **Agreement**: the verb in the *that*-clause agrees with the focus: *It is **the children** who **are** suffering.* With pronouns, *It's **me** who's wrong* is normal; *It is **I** who **am** wrong* is very formal.
5. ***It was not until** X **that** …* puts a time in focus and says that the event happened only then: *It was not until 2010 that the bridge was finished.*
:::

::: proof
Quirk et al. (1985), Biber et al. (1999) and Huddleston and Pullum (2002) describe the *it*-cleft in these terms. Its job is to mark a single part of a clause as the focus — usually in contrast with an alternative — while presenting the rest as known or assumed. That is why *It was Mei who broke the vase* implies that someone broke it (a presupposition) and says who. Biber et al. found *it*-clefts more common in writing, especially news and academic prose, than in conversation, where the same contrast is usually made with stress alone. Clefts with *not until* are a common and natural way to express 直到……才 in English.
:::

::: example Choosing the focus {#ex-it-cleft}
Respond to each statement with an *it*-cleft that corrects it. (a) *"Li Na won the prize, didn't she?"* (No — Chen Jie.) (b) *"You lost your keys on the bus?"* (No — on the train.) (c) *"Shakespeare wrote Don Quixote."* (No — Cervantes.) (d) *"You decided to leave because of the money?"* (No — because of the people.) (e) *"The museum opened in 1990?"* (No — not until 2005.)
::: solution
1. The wrong information becomes the focus, after *It was*.
2. The rest of the sentence, which both speakers accept, goes into the *that*/*who*-clause.

(a) *No, it was **Chen Jie** who won the prize.*

(b) *No, it was **on the train** that I lost them.*

(c) *No, it was **Cervantes** who wrote Don Quixote.* (Shakespeare and Cervantes were contemporaries — the confusion is understandable.)

(d) *No, it was **because of the people** that I decided to leave.*

(e) *No, it **wasn't until 2005** that it opened.*

In speech, the focused words carry the main stress; the clause after *who* or *that* is said more quietly, because it is known.
:::
:::

## *Wh*-clefts

::: theorem *Wh*-clefts {#rule-wh-cleft}
A ***wh*-cleft** (also called a **pseudo-cleft**) puts the known part first, in a *what*-clause, and the new part after *be*:

*What* + clause + *be* + **focus**

- *What I need **is a holiday**.*
- *What surprised me **was his reaction**.*
- *What she did **was (to) call** the police.* — focus on an action: *what … do* + *be* + infinitive (with or without *to*).
- *What happened **was that** the bridge collapsed.* — focus on a whole event.

Variants: the **reversed** *wh*-cleft (*A holiday is **what I need**.*), *all* (*All I want is some sleep* = the only thing), and *the thing*, *the reason*, *the place*, *the person*, *the one* in place of *what*: *The thing I don't understand is why she left.* *The reason I'm calling is to ask about the job.*
:::

::: proof
Huddleston and Pullum (2002) call these **pseudo-clefts**, and Quirk et al. (1985) describe them alongside *it*-clefts. Because the *what*-clause comes first, the *wh*-cleft builds up expectation: the listener knows something is coming and waits for it. This makes it frequent in spoken explanations and presentations (*What we're going to look at today is…*), and in corpus counts *wh*-clefts are relatively more frequent in conversation than *it*-clefts (Biber et al. 1999). The pattern with *do* is the usual way to focus an action. *What* refers to things, not people: for a person, use *the person who* or an *it*-cleft (*It was the doctor who…*, not ✗ *What told me was the doctor*).
:::

::: warning *What* + clause + *is* … and agreement
✗ *What I need it is a holiday.* ✓ *What I need is a holiday.* (no extra subject)

✗ *The thing what I don't like is the noise.* ✓ *The thing (that) I don't like is the noise.* ✓ *What I don't like is the noise.*

? *What we need are more teachers.* — *is* or *are* are both found when the focus is plural; *is* is common, and *are* is usual in writing when the focus is clearly plural (Huddleston & Pullum 2002). ✓ *What we need is more teachers* and ✓ *What we need are more teachers* are both acceptable.
:::

::: quiz
Which cleft answers the question *"Why are you so happy?"* best?
- [ ] *It's my exam that I passed.*
- [x] *What happened is that I passed my exam.*
- [ ] *It's happy that I am because I passed.*
- [ ] *What I passed is my exam.*
::: solution
The whole event is new, so the focus is a whole clause: *What happened is that…* (or simply *I passed my exam!*). The first and fourth focus *my exam*, as if the listener already knew I had passed something; the third tries to focus an adjective, which *it*-clefts do not allow.
:::

::: example From plain sentence to *wh*-cleft {#ex-wh-cleft}
Rewrite each answer as a *wh*-cleft, choosing the right kind (*What … is*, *What … did was*, *What happened was*, *All …*, *The reason …*). (a) *"Why do you look so worried?" — "I don't understand the instructions."* (b) *"How did you get in?" — "I climbed through the window."* (c) *"Why was the road closed?" — "A lorry overturned."* (d) *"Do you want anything else?" — "No, just a glass of water."* (e) *"Why did you choose this university?" — "Its library is excellent."*
::: solution
1. Decide what the focus is: a thing, an action, a whole event, a limit, or a reason.
2. Put the known part in the *what*-clause (or after *the reason*, *all*), then *be*, then the focus.

(a) A thing: ***What I don't understand is** the instructions.* (*are* is also possible with the plural focus.)

(b) An action: ***What I did was** climb through the window.* — *what … did* + *be* + bare or *to*-infinitive.

(c) A whole event: ***What happened was that** a lorry overturned.*

(d) A limit: ***All I want is** a glass of water.*

(e) A reason: ***The reason I chose it is that** its library is excellent.* (Informally also *The reason is because…*, which careful writers avoid as repetitive.)
:::
:::

Which sentence is **not** correct?
- [ ] *Down the hill ran the children.*
- [x] *Down the hill ran they.*
- [ ] *Down the hill they ran.*
- [ ] *There goes the last train.*
::: solution
Full inversion after a place or direction adverbial does not happen with a pronoun subject: *Down the hill they ran*. With a noun subject (*the children*, *the last train*) the inversion is correct.
:::

## Fronting

**Fronting** moves an element that normally follows the verb to the beginning of the clause, without any other change.

- **Objects and complements**, for contrast or to link to what came before: *Some of the questions I could answer; **the others** I had never seen before.* ***That** I can't believe.* — This is uncommon and expressive; in most contexts, the normal order is better.
- **Adverbials** of time, place and manner, very commonly, to set a scene or link sentences: ***In 1998**, the company moved to Shenzhen.* ***Slowly**, she opened the box.*
- **Comparatives and *more/most* + adjective**, with inversion in formal writing: ***More important** is the question of cost.* ***Most surprising of all** was his reaction.*

Fronting a whole clause is a way of making it the topic: *Why she left, nobody knows.* As [[complex-sentences/noun-clauses]] noted, this is common in Chinese and marked in English.

## Inversion after negative and restrictive words

::: theorem Negative inversion {#rule-negative-inversion}
When a **negative** or **restrictive** word or phrase is placed at the start of a clause for emphasis, the subject and the auxiliary are **inverted**, as in a question. If there is no auxiliary, *do* is added.

| fronted element | example | normal order |
|---|---|---|
| *never*, *rarely*, *seldom* | ***Never have I** seen such a mess.* | *I have never seen …* |
| *hardly … when*, *scarcely … when*, *barely … when* | ***Hardly had we** sat down **when** the phone rang.* | *We had hardly sat down when …* |
| *no sooner … than* | ***No sooner had she** arrived **than** she had to leave.* | *She had no sooner arrived than …* |
| *not only … (but also)* | ***Not only did he** forget my birthday, **but** he **also** …* | *He not only forgot …* |
| *not until* | ***Not until** the next morning **did we** realise …* | *We didn't realise … until …* |
| *only* + time/condition/manner | ***Only then did I** understand.* ***Only by** working together **can we** succeed.* | *I understood only then.* |
| *at no time*, *on no account*, *under no circumstances*, *in no way* | ***Under no circumstances should you** open this door.* | *You should not open this door under any circumstances.* |
| *little* (= not at all) | ***Little did she** know what was waiting for her.* | *She had no idea …* |
| *nowhere* | ***Nowhere** else **will you** find such prices.* | |

With ***not until*** and ***only*** + a clause, the inversion is in the **main** clause, not in the fronted one: ***Only when** the rain stopped **did we** leave.* ***Not until** I got home **did I** notice the damage.*
:::

::: proof
Quirk et al. (1985), Huddleston and Pullum (2002) and Swan (2016) describe negative inversion in the same terms, and all mark it as formal or rhetorical, typical of writing and of speeches. It is triggered only when the negative or restrictive meaning applies to the whole clause. Huddleston and Pullum contrast *In no time **he had** finished* (no inversion: *in no time* means ‘very quickly’, and the clause is positive) with *At no time **did he** complain* (inversion: he never complained). The same test explains why *Not far away **stood** a hotel* has a different kind of inversion (see below) and *Not long ago, **I** met her* has none: *not long ago* means ‘recently’. In conversation, the uninverted versions are usual; *Hardly had we…* and *No sooner had she…* are literary. Note the fixed partners: *hardly/scarcely … **when*** (or *before*) and *no sooner … **than*** (✗ *no sooner … when* is sometimes found, but is considered an error).
:::

::: example Writing with negative inversion {#ex-inversion}
Rewrite each sentence beginning with the words given. (a) *I had never felt so nervous.* (Never …) (b) *We had just got home when it started to snow.* (Hardly …) (c) *She didn't only win the race; she broke the record.* (Not only …) (d) *You must not leave your luggage unattended at any time.* (At no time …) (e) *I didn't realise how ill he was until I saw him.* (Not until …) (f) *He had no idea that he was being followed.* (Little …)
::: solution
1. Put the negative or restrictive phrase first.
2. Invert the first auxiliary and the subject; if there is none, use *do/does/did*.
3. With *not until* and *only* + clause, invert in the main clause.

(a) ***Never had I** felt so nervous.*

(b) ***Hardly had we** got home **when** it started to snow.*

(c) ***Not only did she** win the race, **but** she **also** broke the record.* (*did* is added; the second clause is not inverted.)

(d) ***At no time should you** leave your luggage unattended.* (or *must you*)

(e) ***Not until** I saw him **did I** realise how ill he was.* — inversion in the main clause.

(f) ***Little did he** know that he was being followed.*
:::
:::

::: warning *Not only* with and without inversion
✗ *Not only she speaks French but also German.* ✓ *She speaks **not only** French **but also** German.* (no inversion: *not only* is not at the start of the clause) ✓ ***Not only does she** speak French, **but** she **also** speaks German.*

✗ *Only when I got home I realised.* ✓ ***Only when** I got home **did I** realise.* ✓ *I realised **only when** I got home.*

✗ *Never I have seen such a thing.* ✓ ***Never have I** seen such a thing.* ✓ *I have **never** seen such a thing.*
:::

### *So* and *such* at the front

With ***so*** + adjective/adverb or ***such*** + *be* at the start, the subject and verb are inverted, and a *that*-clause of result follows. Both are formal or literary:

- ***So strong was** the wind **that** trees were blown down.* (= The wind was so strong that …)
- ***Such was** the demand **that** the tickets sold out in an hour.* (= The demand was so great that …)

## Inversion after place and direction

::: theorem Full inversion after adverbials of place {#rule-locative}
After an adverbial of **place** or **direction** at the start of a clause, an intransitive verb of position or motion (*be*, *stand*, *lie*, *sit*, *come*, *go*, *run*, *fly*, *walk*) can come **before** the subject. The whole verb moves, without *do*:

- ***On the hill stood** an old castle.*
- ***Into the room walked** a tall woman in a grey coat.*
- ***Here comes** the bus.* ***There goes** our last chance.*
- ***Down came** the rain.* ***Off went** the lights.*

If the subject is a **pronoun**, there is no inversion: ***Here it comes**.* ***Off they went**.* (✗ *Here comes it.*)
:::

This inversion introduces a new participant at the end of the sentence, where new information belongs: it is common in descriptions and stories, and *Here comes…* / *There goes…* are everyday spoken English. A similar inversion follows a fronted participle or adjective, linking a description to what came before: ***Standing** at the door **was** a man I had never seen.* ***Far more serious was** the damage to the roof.*

Other inversions are covered elsewhere: ***So do I*** / ***Neither can she*** in [[complex-sentences/ellipsis-substitution]]; conditional ***Had I known…*** in [[complex-sentences/conditionals]]; and the optional inversion after direct speech in stories, *"Stop!" **shouted the guard***, which does not happen with a pronoun (*"Stop!" **he shouted***).

::: example Recognising the kind of inversion {#ex-kinds}
Name the kind of inversion in each sentence and say whether it is formal or neutral. (a) *Rarely does the river flood in summer.* (b) *Here's your coffee.* (c) *Under the bridge lay a small boat.* (d) *Had we known, we would have helped.* (e) *"I'm Chen," said the new student.* (f) *I've never been to Tibet, and neither has my sister.*
::: solution
(a) Negative inversion after *rarely*, with *do* added — formal.

(b) Full inversion after *here* with *be* — neutral, everyday speech.

(c) Full inversion after a fronted place adverbial — written, descriptive.

(d) Conditional inversion without *if* — formal.

(e) Inversion after direct speech — usual in stories (*said the student* or *the student said*); not with a pronoun.

(f) Inversion after *neither* in an elliptical response — neutral, very common in speech.
:::
:::

## Other ways to add emphasis

- **Emphatic *do***: *I **do** like your new haircut.* *She **did** tell you — I heard her.* *Do sit down.* (polite insistence). The auxiliary is stressed in speech.
- **Stress** in speech: *I didn't say **she** took it.* (but someone else did) — see [[connected-speech/sentence-stress]].
- **Intensifiers and emphatic words**: *the **very** person I wanted to see*; *no reason **whatsoever***; *not **at all***; *What **on earth** are you doing?*; *I **myself** have never seen it* (emphatic reflexive, [[verb-patterns/reflexive-reciprocal]]).
- **Repetition and parallelism** for rhetorical effect, discussed in [[style-and-rhetoric/figures-of-speech]].

::: note English and Chinese
- **Topic-prominent Chinese.** Li and Thompson (1976) classified Chinese as a **topic-prominent** language, in which putting a topic first (这本书我看过; 那个人我认识) is a normal, neutral structure. English is **subject-prominent**: fronting an object is unusual and strongly contrastive. Translating 这本书我看过, use the normal order *I've read this book*, or *I have read **that** one* with stress, not ✗ *This book I have read* (which sounds contrastive or poetic).
- **是……的 and *it*-clefts.** Chinese 是……的 often focuses a time, place or manner of a past event: 我是昨天到的 ‘it was yesterday that I arrived’; 他是坐火车来的 ‘he came by train’. An English *it*-cleft fits only when the focus is contrastive; usually the English is a plain sentence with the stress on the focus: *I arrived **yesterday**.* *He came **by train**.*
- **直到……才 and 只有……才.** 直到……才 corresponds to *not until* (*He didn't come home until midnight*; *It was not until midnight that…*; formal *Not until midnight did he…*). 只有……才 corresponds to *only if* / *only when*, and when the *only*-phrase comes first, the main clause inverts: 只有努力才能成功 — *Only by working hard **can you** succeed.*
- **一……就 and 不但……而且.** 一……就 can be *as soon as*, or in formal writing *No sooner had … than* / *Hardly had … when*. 不但……而且 is *not only … but also*, with inversion only when *Not only* begins the clause.
- **连……都/也.** 连他都不知道 — emphasis on an unexpected case — is *Even he doesn't know*; English uses *even* where Chinese uses 连.
:::

::: history
The term **cleft sentence** was introduced by Otto Jespersen in the 1930s, in his description of how English splits a sentence into two clauses to give one part prominence. In Prague, Vilém Mathesius and the linguists who followed him studied how languages order given and new information, calling it the **functional sentence perspective**, and in 1967 M. A. K. Halliday's "Notes on transitivity and theme in English" set out the analysis of given and new information and of theme that later grammars of English use. Charles Li and Sandra Thompson's paper "Subject and topic: a new typology of language" (1976) contrasted subject-prominent languages such as English with topic-prominent languages such as Chinese, which explains why English needs special structures for what Chinese does by simply putting the topic first.
:::

Information order in paragraphs — keeping the topic at the start and the new point at the end — is taught in [[style-and-rhetoric/cohesion-emphasis]]. ::: summary
- English places **new** information last (**end-focus**); special structures move the focus when needed ([[#def-focus]]).
- ***It*-clefts** — *It was Mei who broke the vase* — focus a noun phrase or adverbial, present the rest as known, and are common in writing; *It was not until … that* = 直到……才 ([[#def-it-cleft]], [[#rule-it-cleft]]).
- ***Wh*-clefts** — *What I need is a holiday*, *What she did was call the police* — put the known part first and can focus an action ([[#rule-wh-cleft]]).
- **Fronting** objects is strongly contrastive in English; fronting adverbials is common.
- **Negative inversion** after *never*, *rarely*, *hardly … when*, *no sooner … than*, *not only*, *not until*, *only* + phrase, *under no circumstances*, *little*: auxiliary + subject, with *do* if needed; formal ([[#rule-negative-inversion]]).
- With *only when* and *not until* + clause, invert the **main** clause.
- **Full inversion** after place and direction adverbials (*On the hill stood a castle*, *Here comes the bus*), but not with pronoun subjects ([[#rule-locative]]).
- Chinese topics and 是……的 are neutral in Chinese; English equivalents are often plain sentences with stress.
:::

::: exercise Rewrite for emphasis {level=3}
Rewrite this paragraph from a speech so that it is more emphatic, using one *it*-cleft, one *wh*-cleft, one negative inversion and one *so … that* inversion. Keep the meaning. *The volunteers made this festival possible. They gave up their weekends. We have never had so much support. The response was very strong, and we have already started planning next year's festival. We need your ideas now.*
::: solution
A model:

*It was the volunteers who made this festival possible — they gave up their weekends for it. Never have we had so much support. So strong was the response that we have already started planning next year's festival. And what we need now is your ideas.*

Check: *it*-cleft (*It was the volunteers who…*), negative inversion (*Never have we had…*), *so … that* inversion (*So strong was the response that…*), *wh*-cleft (*what we need now is…*). In a real speech one or two of these would be enough; four in a row is very rhetorical.

::: exercise Topic, cleft or plain? {level=3}
Translate into natural English. For each, decide whether English needs a cleft, an inversion, or just a plain sentence. (a) 这部电影我看过三遍了。 (b) 我是在网上买的。 (c) 连小孩子都知道这个道理。 (d) 只有在周末他才有时间陪孩子。 (e) 他一进门就开始抱怨。
::: solution
(a) Plain: *I've seen this film three times.* (The Chinese topic is neutral; fronting *this film* in English would sound contrastive.)

(b) Plain, with stress on the focus: *I bought it **online**.* An *it*-cleft (*It was online that I bought it*) is possible only if correcting someone.

(c) *Even* + plain order: ***Even** children know that.* / *Even a child knows that.*

(d) Inversion or plain: *Only at weekends **does he have** time for his children.* (formal) / *He only has time for his children at weekends.* (neutral; AmE *on weekends*)

(e) Plain or formal inversion: *He started complaining **as soon as** he came in.* / *No sooner had he come in than he started complaining.* (literary)
