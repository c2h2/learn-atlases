Two sentences from a travel story:

> *Having spent three days on the train, we arrived in Lhasa exhausted.*
>
> ✗ *Having spent three days on the train, the city looked wonderful.*

The first is good written English: *Having spent three days on the train* is a clause without a subject or a tense, and the reader understands its subject from the main clause — *we* had spent three days on the train. The second has exactly the same opening, but its main clause begins with *the city*, so grammar says that the city spent three days on the train. Readers notice this kind of slip, called a **dangling participle**, and in careful writing it is a mistake, even though the meaning is usually clear.

Clauses built on *-ing* forms, *-ed* participles and *to*-infinitives are among the most useful tools of written English. They make a text shorter and more connected than a series of finite clauses, they let a writer put background information in the background, and they are typical of good narrative and academic style. This lesson shows how to use them: as reduced relative clauses (*the man **sitting by the door***, *a bridge **built in 1890***, *the first person **to arrive***), as adverbial clauses (*Arriving late, she…*), with their own subject (*The weather being fine, …*), and in other infinitive patterns. It also explains when they go wrong. The finite relative and adverbial clauses that these forms reduce were treated in [[complex-sentences/relative-clauses]] and [[complex-sentences/adverbial-clauses]].

## The forms

::: definition Participle and infinitive clauses {#def-nonfinite}
A **participle clause** is a non-finite clause ([[complex-sentences/clause-structure#def-finite]]) built on a participle:

| form | active | passive |
|---|---|---|
| *-ing* participle | *taking* | *being taken* |
| perfect participle | *having taken* | *having been taken* |
| *-ed* participle | — | *taken* |

An **infinitive clause** is built on a *to*-infinitive: *to take*, *to be taken*, *to have taken*.

Neither kind has a tense, and normally neither has a subject: the time and the subject are understood from the main clause.
:::

The *-ing* participle is the same form as the gerund (*Swimming is good for you*); the difference lies in the job of the clause. Clauses that work as nouns (*I enjoy swimming*) belong to [[complex-sentences/noun-clauses]] and [[verb-patterns/ing-or-infinitive]]. This lesson is about clauses that work like relative and adverbial clauses.

## Reduced relative clauses

::: theorem Participles after a noun {#rule-reduced-relative}
A participle clause after a noun can do the job of a defining or non-defining relative clause:

1. ***-ing*** = active, often continuous or permanent: *the man **sitting** by the door* (= who is sitting); *people **living** near the airport* (= who live); *a road **leading** to the sea*.
2. ***-ed*** = passive: *the cars **made** in this factory* (= which are made); *a bridge **built** in 1890* (= which was built); *the people **injured** in the accident*.
3. ***being* + *-ed*** = passive, in progress: *the building **being repaired** across the road* (= which is being repaired).

Non-defining reduced clauses take commas: *The palace, **built** in the fifteenth century, is now a museum.*
:::

::: proof
Quirk et al. (1985) and Biber et al. (1999) describe these clauses as the non-finite equivalents of relative clauses. Biber et al. found them far more frequent in news and academic prose than in conversation: they pack information into noun phrases, which is what informational writing needs. The *-ing* form does not have to correspond to a progressive: *Anyone **knowing** the answer should raise their hand* (= who knows) is correct, although *know* is rarely progressive. The *-ed* participle is always passive in meaning when it modifies a noun, except with a few intransitive verbs: *a **retired** teacher*, *the train **just arrived** at platform 4* (rare and formal).
:::

There are limits. An *-ing* clause after a noun describes a situation at the same time as the main clause, or a lasting one; it is not used for a **single completed action** before the time of the main clause, or for a future action.

- ✗ *The man **breaking** the window has been arrested.* ✓ *The man **who broke** the window has been arrested.*
- ✗ *The students **arriving** tomorrow will stay in Block C.* — possible in informal planning language, but ✓ *The students **who arrive** / **are arriving** tomorrow …* is safer.
- ✓ *The man **standing** at the bus stop is my uncle.* (now)
- ✓ *Thousands of people **living** in the valley had to leave.* (a lasting situation at that time)

::: theorem Infinitive clauses after a noun {#rule-infinitive-relative}
A *to*-infinitive can also follow a noun:

1. after ***the first***, ***the last***, ***the only***, ***the next*** and superlatives, with the noun as subject: *She was **the first woman to climb** the mountain.* (= the first woman who climbed) *He is **the only person to have read** the whole report.*
2. to say what can or should be done with the noun, which is the object or complement of a preposition: *a book **to read** on the train*, *something **to eat***, *a place **to stay***, *a pen **to write with***, *the man **to ask***. A different subject is introduced by ***for***: *a book **for children to read***.
3. in the passive, for something that must be done: *the problems **to be solved***, *the next decision **to be made***.
:::

In 2, the object or prepositional gap must not be filled: ✗ *a pen to write with it*, ✗ *something to eat it*. The rule is the same as the gap rule of [[complex-sentences/relative-clauses#rule-gap]].

::: example Reducing relative clauses {#ex-reduce}
Shorten the relative clause where possible. If it cannot be shortened, say why. (a) *Passengers who are travelling to Guangzhou should change at Changsha.* (b) *The paintings which were stolen last year have been found.* (c) *The woman who sold me the ticket gave me the wrong change.* (d) *She was the youngest player who had ever won the title.* (e) *I need somebody who can help me with the move.*
::: solution
1. Active verb → *-ing*; passive → *-ed*; after *first*/*only*/superlative → *to*-infinitive.
2. Check the limits: no single completed earlier action with *-ing*.

(a) *Passengers **travelling** to Guangzhou should change at Changsha.*

(b) *The paintings **stolen** last year have been found.*

(c) Not with *-ing*: selling the ticket was a single completed action before the main event. ✗ *The woman selling me the ticket gave me…* would mean she was still selling it as she gave the change, which is just possible, but the finite clause is better.

(d) *She was the youngest player **ever to win** the title* (or *to have won*).

(e) *I need somebody **to help** me with the move.* — the infinitive includes the meaning of *can*.
:::
:::

## Participle clauses as adverbials

A participle clause before or after the main clause can express time, reason, result, condition or accompanying circumstance. It is shorter and more formal than a finite adverbial clause, and often leaves the exact relation to the reader.

| meaning | participle clause | finite equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| time (same time) | ***Walking** home, I met an old friend.* | *While I was walking home, …* |
| time (just before) | ***Opening** the letter, she began to read.* | *When she had opened the letter, …* |
| time (earlier) | ***Having finished** the report, he went home.* | *After he had finished the report, …* |
| reason | ***Not knowing** the way, we took a taxi.* | *Because we didn't know the way, …* |
| reason (passive) | ***Written** in simple English, the book is easy to follow.* | *Because it is written in simple English, …* |
| condition | ***Used** carefully, this knife will last for years.* | *If it is used carefully, …* |
| result | *The storm closed the airport, **causing** long delays.* | *…, and this caused long delays.* |
| accompanying circumstance | *She sat by the window, **reading** a newspaper.* | *…, and she was reading a newspaper.* |

::: theorem Adverbial participle clauses {#rule-adverbial-participle}
1. The understood subject of the participle clause is the **subject of the main clause**.
2. The simple *-ing* form describes an action at the **same time** as the main action, or just before it. The **perfect participle** (*having done*) shows that the action was **complete before** the main one: ***Having lived** in Japan for years, she spoke Japanese well.*
3. The *-ed* participle and *being* / *having been* + *-ed* are **passive**: ***Shocked** by the news, he sat down.* ***Having been warned** about the traffic, we left early.*
4. The negative puts ***not*** before the participle: ***Not having** a car, I rarely leave the city.*
5. Subordinators can be kept to make the meaning clear: ***After** leaving school, …*, ***While** waiting, …*, ***When** asked, …*, ***Although** knowing the risks, …*, ***If** used correctly, …*, ***Since** joining the company, …*
6. A result clause with *-ing* comes **after** the main clause, separated by a comma: *Prices rose sharply, **forcing** many families to cut back.*
:::

::: proof
Quirk et al. (1985) and Swan (2016) describe these clauses and the use of the perfect participle for earlier actions; Swan notes that the simple *-ing* form is used for an earlier action only when the two actions follow each other immediately (*Taking a key from her bag, she opened the door*). Biber et al. (1999) found adverbial participle clauses to be rare in conversation and common in fiction and academic writing, so in speech a finite clause is usually more natural. The result clause in rule 6 is especially frequent in news and academic writing. Its *-ing* refers to the whole previous clause, not to its subject: in *Prices rose sharply, forcing families to cut back*, it is the rise, not the prices, that forced them.
:::

::: example From finite to participle clauses {#ex-participle}
Rewrite each sentence with a participle clause. (a) *Because she felt ill, Amina left the meeting early.* (b) *After he had read the instructions, Diego assembled the desk.* (c) *The house was built on a hill, so it has wonderful views.* (d) *Because I didn't want to wake anyone, I took my shoes off.* (e) *The company closed two factories, and this put 800 people out of work.*
::: solution
(a) ***Feeling** ill, Amina left the meeting early.* — reason, same time.

(b) ***Having read** the instructions, Diego assembled the desk.* — earlier and complete: perfect participle. (*After reading the instructions, …* also works.)

(c) ***Built** on a hill, the house has wonderful views.* — passive, reason.

(d) ***Not wanting** to wake anyone, I took my shoes off.* — negative before the participle.

(e) *The company closed two factories, **putting** 800 people out of work.* — result, after a comma.

In each case the subject of the main clause is the understood subject of the participle clause: Amina felt ill, Diego read the instructions, the house was built, I didn't want to wake anyone, and the closures put people out of work.
:::
:::

## Dangling participles

::: warning The dangling participle
✗ *Walking into the room, the lights suddenly went out.* (The lights were not walking.) ✓ *As I walked into the room, the lights suddenly went out.* ✓ *Walking into the room, **I** noticed that the lights had gone out.*

✗ *Having finished dinner, the dishes were washed.* ✓ *Having finished dinner, **we** washed the dishes.*

✗ *Born in Hangzhou in 1990, her first novel was published at 21.* ✓ ***Born** in Hangzhou in 1990, **she** published her first novel at 21.*

✗ *Once opened, you should keep the jar in the fridge.* ✓ *Once opened, **the jar** should be kept in the fridge.*
:::

::: theorem Keeping participles attached {#rule-dangling}
In careful writing, the understood subject of an adverbial participle clause (or a reduced clause such as *Once opened*) must be the **subject of the main clause**. If it is not, change the main clause so that its subject is the doer, or turn the participle clause into a finite clause with its own subject.

Some participles have become **prepositions or conjunctions** and are used without an attached subject: *generally speaking*, *strictly speaking*, *considering*, *given*, *judging by*, *assuming (that)*, *provided (that)*, *regarding*, *according to*, *owing to*, *including*. ***Considering** the weather, the turnout was good.* ***Judging by** his accent, he's from Scotland.*
:::

::: proof
Usage guides since Fowler's *Modern English Usage* (1926), which called them "unattached participles", have warned against danglers, and both Quirk et al. (1985) and Huddleston and Pullum (2002) describe the requirement that the understood subject be the main-clause subject. Huddleston and Pullum also point out that danglers are frequent in published writing and that many cause no difficulty; what makes one unacceptable is that the reader first attaches the participle to the wrong noun and gets an absurd picture. A common case in scientific writing is a participle with a passive main clause: *Using this method, the samples were analysed.* Here the doer (the researchers) is not mentioned at all. This pattern is widespread and often tolerated in science, but many editors and style guides prefer *Using this method, we analysed the samples*, now that the active *we* is accepted in most academic fields ([[academic-writing/academic-style]]). The fixed forms in the rule are listed as prepositions or conjunctions in dictionaries; *based on* is used in the same way, but some editors still object to it.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence has a dangling participle?
- [ ] *Feeling hungry, I made a sandwich.*
- [x] *Looking out of the plane window, the mountains seemed very small.*
- [ ] *Generally speaking, the trains here are punctual.*
- [ ] *Built in 1912, the station is the oldest in the city.*
::: solution
The understood subject of *Looking out of the plane window* is a person, but the main clause's subject is *the mountains*. Correct: *Looking out of the plane window, I thought the mountains seemed very small*, or *From the plane window, the mountains seemed very small.* *Generally speaking* is a fixed expression, and in the last sentence the station was built.
:::

::: example Attached or dangling? {#ex-dangling}
Decide whether each participle is correctly attached, and correct the ones that are not. (a) *Arriving at the hotel, our room was not ready.* (b) *Arriving at the hotel, we found that our room was not ready.* (c) *Considering the price, the food was excellent.* (d) *Having been repaired twice, I decided to sell the car.* (e) *Once finished, the report should be sent to the manager.*
::: solution
For each sentence, ask who or what does the action of the participle, and compare it with the subject of the main clause.

(a) Dangling: the room did not arrive. → *When we arrived at the hotel, our room was not ready.*

(b) Correct: *we* arrived.

(c) Correct: *considering* is a fixed preposition-like form that needs no attached subject.

(d) Dangling: the car had been repaired, not *I*. → *The car had been repaired twice, so I decided to sell it.* / *Having been repaired twice, the car was still unreliable, so I sold it.*

(e) Correct: the report is finished and is sent. (✗ *Once finished, you should send the report…* would dangle.)
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence uses a non-finite clause correctly?
- [ ] *The woman winning the race last Sunday is my cousin.*
- [ ] *This exercise is too hard to do it without help.*
- [x] *Not wanting to be late, she took a taxi.*
- [ ] *Having been born in Harbin, the cold doesn't bother me.*
::: solution
*Not wanting to be late* is negative with *not* before the participle, and its subject is *she*. The others need *who won the race last Sunday* (a single completed past action), *too hard to do without help* (no *it*), and *Having been born in Harbin, I'm not bothered by the cold*.

## Clauses with their own subject

A participle clause can also have its own subject. This is called an **absolute** clause (Quirk et al. call it a supplementive clause with a subject).

- *The weather **being** fine, we ate outside.* (formal, rather old-fashioned)
- ***All things considered**, the trip was a success.* (fixed expression)
- *She stood at the door, **her heart pounding**.* (literary)
- ***With prices rising** so fast, many families are struggling.* (neutral; *with* is common)
- ***With** the children asleep, the house was quiet.* (verbless, with *with*)

Absolute clauses avoid the dangling problem, because their subject is stated. The *with* construction is the most natural in modern English, in speech as well as writing. The absolute clause without *with* is mainly formal or literary, and in descriptions of people it often describes a part of the body or a feeling (*her hands shaking*, *his voice trembling*).

## More infinitive clauses

Infinitive clauses do many jobs besides modifying nouns. The most important at this level:

- **purpose**: *She went to Shanghai **to look** for work.* (see [[complex-sentences/adverbial-clauses#rule-purpose]])
- **result after *too* and *enough***: *The box was **too heavy to lift**.* *He's **old enough to vote**.*
- **an unexpected result**, with *only to*: *We hurried to the station, **only to find** that the train had gone.*
- **after adjectives**, with the subject of the sentence as the understood object: *This book is **easy to read**.* *The instructions were **difficult to follow**.* *She is **hard to please**.*
- **with a subject introduced by *for***: ***For a child to travel** alone is risky.* → *It's risky **for a child to travel** alone.* *I'm waiting **for the shop to open**.*
- **as a comment on the whole sentence**, in fixed phrases: ***To be honest**, …*, ***To tell the truth**, …*, ***To begin with**, …*, ***To sum up**, …*

::: warning *Easy to read it*
✗ *This question is difficult to answer it.* ✓ *This question is difficult to answer.* ✓ ***It** is difficult to answer this question.*

✗ *The film was too long to watch it in one evening.* ✓ *The film was too long to watch in one evening.*

In these patterns the subject of the sentence is also the object of the infinitive, so the object position is empty. Adding *it* fills a gap that must stay empty — like the resumptive pronoun in a relative clause. With anticipatory *it* at the front, the object is needed: *It is difficult to answer **this question***.
:::

::: example Combining with non-finite clauses {#ex-combine}
Combine each group into one sentence, using at least one participle or infinitive clause. (a) *Liu Yang finished university. She moved to Berlin. She wanted to study music.* (b) *The museum is closed for repairs. The repairs will take two years.* (c) *We got to the cinema. We discovered that the film had already started.* (d) *The bridge was damaged in the flood. It has been closed to traffic.* (e) *The children were tired. They fell asleep in the car.*
::: solution
(a) ***Having finished** university, Liu Yang moved to Berlin **to study** music.* — perfect participle for the earlier action, infinitive of purpose.

(b) *The museum is closed for repairs **expected to take** two years.* — a reduced relative clause (*which are expected to take*).

(c) *We got to the cinema, **only to discover** that the film had already started.* — *only to* for an unwelcome result.

(d) ***Damaged** in the flood, the bridge has been closed to traffic.* — passive participle, reason.

(e) ***Tired**, the children fell asleep in the car.* / *The children, **being** tired, fell asleep in the car.* — a verbless clause is the most natural; *Being tired, …* is correct but heavy.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **No participles in Chinese.** Chinese verbs have no participle forms, so 坐在门口的那个人 and 一九〇〇年建的桥 are 的-modifiers before the noun. English uses a participle clause after the noun: *the man **sitting** by the door*, *the bridge **built** in 1900*. The choice between *-ing* (active) and *-ed* (passive) has no Chinese parallel, and learners often reverse them: ✗ *the problems discussing in the meeting* ✓ *the problems **discussed** in the meeting*.
- **Topic chains and dangling participles.** In Chinese a series of clauses can share an implicit topic, and each clause can leave its subject out when it is clear (Li and Thompson 1981 call this zero anaphora): 到了北京，天气很热 — the topic is 我们, and 天气 is the subject of only the second clause. Carried into English as a participle clause, this produces a dangler: ✗ *Arriving in Beijing, the weather was very hot.* English needs ✓ *When we arrived in Beijing, the weather was very hot* or ✓ *Arriving in Beijing, we found the weather very hot.*
- **Easy to read.** Chinese 这本书很容易读 and English *This book is easy to read* have the same shape: the subject is understood as the object of 读 / *read*. Chinese learners rarely add *it* here, but the error appears when the sentence is longer (✗ *This kind of problem is not easy for students to solve it*).
- **为了 and 结果.** 为了 + verb corresponds to the purpose infinitive (*to*, *in order to*), and a 结果 / 导致 clause often to a result *-ing* clause: 工厂关闭了，导致八百人失业 → *The factory closed, **putting** 800 people out of work.*
:::

::: history
Absolute participle clauses came into English partly by imitation of Latin, whose **ablative absolute** (*urbe capta* ‘the city having been taken’) was a standard way of giving circumstances. Old English translators rendered it with a dative absolute, and later writers formed English absolutes in the nominative (*the city taken*, *this done*). H. W. Fowler's *Modern English Usage* (1926) gave the dangling participle the name "unattached participle" and made it a fixture of usage advice. Corpus linguistics later showed the register profile of these clauses: Douglas Biber's *Variation across Speech and Writing* (1988) used participle clauses as one of the features that distinguish carefully planned, informational writing from conversation, and the *Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English* (1999) confirmed that they are rare in speech.
:::

## Where this leads

Participle clauses are a key feature of dense academic sentences: see [[academic-reading/dense-sentences]] and [[style-and-rhetoric/concision]]. Fronted participle clauses with inversion (*Standing at the door was a tall man*) are in [[complex-sentences/emphasis-inversion]]. Leaving out words more generally is the topic of [[complex-sentences/ellipsis-substitution]].

::: summary
- Participle and infinitive clauses are non-finite: no tense and normally no subject; both are understood from the main clause ([[#def-nonfinite]]).
- After a noun, *-ing* = active (*the man sitting there*), *-ed* = passive (*the cars made here*); *-ing* is not used for a single earlier completed action ([[#rule-reduced-relative]]).
- *The first / last / only / next* + noun + *to*-infinitive; *something to eat*, *a pen to write with* — with the gap left empty ([[#rule-infinitive-relative]]).
- Adverbial participle clauses express time, reason, condition, result and circumstance; *having done* for an earlier action; *not* before the participle ([[#rule-adverbial-participle]]).
- The understood subject must be the main-clause subject; otherwise the participle **dangles** (✗ *Walking into the room, the lights went out*). Fixed forms such as *generally speaking* and *considering* are exceptions ([[#rule-dangling]]).
- Absolute clauses have their own subject: *With prices rising, …*; *The weather being fine, …* (formal).
- Infinitive clauses also express purpose, *too/enough* results, *only to* results, and follow adjectives (*easy to read* — no *it*).
:::

::: solution
*Having prepared the solutions, **we raised** the temperature to 60 °C. Using a digital thermometer, **we checked** the temperature every minute. After **we had stirred the mixture** for ten minutes, the colour changed to blue.* (or *After ten minutes of stirring, the colour changed…*) *The results **obtained** are shown in Table 1.*

The first two danglers are fixed by making *we* the subject. In the third, the colour did not stir, so a finite clause or a noun phrase is needed. *Obtaining* should be the passive participle *obtained* (= which were obtained).
:::

::: exercise Write a short narrative {level=3}
Write a paragraph (80–120 words) about a journey that went wrong. Use at least five non-finite clauses of different kinds: a perfect participle, a passive participle, a result clause after a comma, an *only to* clause, and an absolute clause with *with*. Check that none of your participles dangles.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

***Having booked** the cheapest flight we could find, my brother and I set off for Kunming at four in the morning. **With the roads still empty**, we reached the airport early, **only to discover** that the flight had been cancelled. **Offered** a seat on a plane the next day, we decided to take the train instead, **arriving** in Kunming thirty hours later. **Exhausted** but happy, we agreed that it had been the best part of the trip.*

Check: *Having booked* (we), *With the roads still empty* (absolute), *only to discover* (unexpected result), *Offered* (passive; we were offered), *arriving* (result/sequence; we arrived), *Exhausted* (verbless; we were exhausted).
:::
