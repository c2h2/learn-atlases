A learner wants to say 我真希望我会飞. She knows that 希望 is *hope*, and writes:

✗ *I really hope I can fly.*

An English reader understands, but finds it odd: *hope* is for things that may well happen (*I hope you can come*), and flying is not one of them. The English verb for an impossible or unlikely wish is *wish*, and it changes the tense of the clause that follows: *I wish I **could** fly.* The past form *could* says nothing about past time; it marks the wish as unreal, exactly as in the unreal conditionals of [[complex-sentences/conditionals]].

The same "unreal past" appears after *if only*, *it's time*, *would rather*, *as if* and *suppose*. This lesson collects these patterns and shows that they follow one principle: a past form for an unreal present or future, a past perfect for an unreal past. The second half of the lesson is about a related but different form, the **subjunctive** of formal English (*It is essential that every candidate **be** informed*), which uses the base form of the verb and is far more common in American than in British English.

## The unreal past

::: definition The unreal past {#def-unreal-past}
The **unreal past** is the use of a past tense form to refer to a situation in the present or future that the speaker presents as **not real**: false, imagined, wished for or unlikely. *If I **had** a car…* *I wish I **knew**.* *It's time we **went**.* For a situation in the **past** that did not happen, the **past perfect** is used: *I wish I **had known**.*

In this use, *were* can replace *was* with *I*, *he*, *she* and *it*: *I wish I **were** taller.* Huddleston and Pullum (2002) call this form **irrealis *were*** (Quirk et al. 1985 call it the *were*-subjunctive).
:::

::: theorem The unreal past after wishes and similar expressions {#rule-unreal-past}
After *wish*, *if only*, *would rather* (with a new subject), *it's time*, *as if* and *as though* (for unreal comparisons), and *suppose* / *imagine* (for imagined situations):

| time of the unreal situation | form | example |
|---|---|---|
| present or future | past simple / past progressive | *I wish I **lived** nearer.* *I wish it **wasn't raining**.* |
| past | past perfect | *I wish I **hadn't said** that.* |
| ability, present | *could* + verb | *I wish I **could** swim.* |
| ability, past | *could have* + participle | *I wish I **could have** helped.* |

Use *were* or *was* for *be*: *I wish I **were** / **was** on holiday.* *Were* is more formal.
:::

::: proof
Quirk et al. (1985), Huddleston and Pullum (2002) and Swan (2016) all describe these expressions together, because the verb form after them is the same as in an unreal *if*-clause, and for the same reason: the past tense expresses distance from reality. Huddleston and Pullum's term **modal preterite** captures the idea that the past form is here a marker of modality, not of time. The past perfect for the unreal past is needed because the simple past is already used for the unreal present. Like the *if*-clause, these expressions do not normally take *would* for the same subject: ✗ *I wish I would know*.
:::

## *Wish* and *if only*

::: theorem Three patterns after *wish* {#rule-wish}
1. ***wish* + past** — a wish about the **present** that cannot or probably will not come true: *I wish I **had** more time.* *She wishes she **were** / **was** taller.* *I wish I **could** speak Arabic.*
2. ***wish* + past perfect** — a regret about the **past**: *I wish I **had studied** harder.* *He wishes he **hadn't sold** his old bike.*
3. ***wish* + *would*** — a wish that **someone or something else** would change their behaviour or act, often a complaint: *I wish you **would** stop interrupting.* *I wish it **would** stop raining.* *I wish the bus **would** come.*

*Wish* + *would* is used for actions and changes, not for states (*I wish I **had** a bigger flat*, ✗ *I wish I would have a bigger flat*), and not normally with the same subject: ✗ *I wish I would*. For your own behaviour use *could* or the past: *I wish I **could** stop smoking.*
:::

::: proof
The three patterns and the restrictions on *would* are described in Swan (2016) and Quirk et al. (1985). The restriction follows from the meaning of *would* here: willingness or insistence. One can wish that another person were willing to act, but one's own willingness is under one's own control, which is why *I wish I would* sounds strange. With weather and machines (*I wish it would stop raining*, *I wish this computer would work*), the speaker treats the thing as if it could cooperate. Because *wish* + *would* so often expresses annoyance, it can sound impolite when addressed to someone directly: *I wish you would listen* is a complaint, not a request.
:::

### *Wish* or *hope*?

*Hope* is for things the speaker thinks are possible, and it takes ordinary tenses — often the present for the future: *I **hope** you **pass** your exam.* (✗ *I wish you pass*) *I **hope** it **doesn't** rain tomorrow.* *I **hope** she **got** home safely.*

*Wish* is for things the speaker thinks are unreal or unlikely, and takes the unreal past. *Wish* has three other uses that are not about unreality:

- *wish* + object + noun phrase, for good wishes: *We **wish** you a merry Christmas.* *I **wish** you luck.* (✗ *I hope you a good holiday* — use *I hope you have a good holiday*.)
- *wish* + *to*-infinitive, a formal way of saying *want*: *I **wish to** make a complaint.*
- *wish* + object + *to*-infinitive (formal): *The manager **wishes** you **to** wait.*

***If only*** is a stronger, more emotional *I wish*, and takes the same forms: ***If only** I **had** more time!* ***If only** I **hadn't left** my phone on the train!* ***If only** he **would** listen!* It often stands as an exclamation on its own.

::: example *Wish*, *hope* or *if only*? {#ex-wish}
Express each situation with *wish* or *hope*, choosing the correct tense. (a) *I can't play the guitar, and I'd like to.* (b) *My neighbour plays loud music every night.* (c) *I didn't go to Li Wei's party. It sounds as if it was great.* (d) *My sister has a job interview tomorrow.* (e) *It's very hot, and I'm not near the sea.*
::: solution
(a) Unreal present ability: *I wish I **could** play the guitar.*

(b) Someone else's annoying behaviour: *I wish my neighbour **would** stop playing loud music* (or *wouldn't play*).

(c) Past regret: *I wish I **had gone** to Li Wei's party.* / *If only I'd gone!*

(d) A real possibility: *I **hope** she **does** well.* (✗ *I wish she did well* — that would suggest she probably won't.)

(e) Unreal present: *I wish I **were** / **was** at the seaside.*
:::
:::

## *It's time*, *would rather* and *as if*

::: theorem Other expressions with the unreal past {#rule-other}
1. ***It's time* + subject + past simple** — something should already be happening: *It's time we **went** home.* *It's **high** time you **got** a job.* (*high* or *about* make it stronger or more critical.) The alternative ***It's time* + *to*-infinitive** is more neutral: *It's time **to** go.* *It's time **for us to** go.* (✗ *It's time we had gone*.)
2. ***Would rather*** (also *would sooner*):
   - with the **same subject**, + bare infinitive: *I'**d rather stay** at home.* *I'd rather not say.*
   - with a **different subject**, + past (present/future) or past perfect (past): *I'd rather you **didn't** smoke in here.* *I'd rather you **hadn't told** her.*
3. ***As if*** / ***as though***:
   - + present tense when the comparison is **likely true**: *It looks **as if** it**'s going** to rain.* *She sounds **as if** she**'s** got a cold.*
   - + past (or past perfect) when it is **unreal** or the speaker wants to show doubt: *He talks **as if** he **owned** the company.* (He doesn't.) *She looked at me **as though** she **had** never **seen** me before.*
4. ***Suppose*** / ***supposing*** / ***imagine*** take either the real tense (a possibility) or the unreal past (more hypothetical): ***Suppose** we **miss** the train?* — ***Suppose** we **missed** the train — what would we do?*
:::

::: proof
These patterns are listed in Swan (2016) and Quirk et al. (1985). After *as if*, British and American English both allow the present for likely comparisons; the past makes the comparison more clearly unreal, but many speakers use the present even for unreal ones (*He talks as if he owns the place*), and Swan describes both as correct. In informal English, especially American, *like* replaces *as if*: *It looks **like** it's going to rain*; this is avoided in formal writing ([[complex-sentences/adverbial-clauses]]). *Had better* (*You'd better go*) looks like *would rather*, but it is followed by a bare infinitive only and expresses advice; it has nothing to do with the unreal past.
:::

::: example *Would rather* and *it's time* {#ex-rather}
Rewrite using the words in brackets. (a) *Please don't park your car in front of my gate.* (I'd rather) (b) *We should have left ten minutes ago.* (It's time) (c) *I prefer to walk.* (I'd rather) (d) *You told everyone my secret. I wasn't happy about that.* (I'd rather) (e) *The government should do something about the traffic. It's late already.* (high time)
::: solution
(a) *I'd rather you **didn't park** your car in front of my gate.* — a different subject, present/future: past simple.

(b) *It's time we **left**.* (or *It's time **to** leave.*) — *it's time* + past simple, not past perfect, even though the right moment is already past.

(c) *I'd rather **walk**.* — same subject: bare infinitive.

(d) *I'd rather you **hadn't told** everyone my secret.* — a different subject, past: past perfect.

(e) *It's high time the government **did** something about the traffic.*

## The subjunctive in formal English

::: definition The mandative subjunctive {#def-subjunctive}
The **subjunctive** in modern English is the **base form** of the verb (the infinitive without *to*), used in a finite *that*-clause for all persons, with no *-s* in the third person and no tense: *that he **go***, *that she **be** told*, *that they **not be** late*.

Its main use is the **mandative subjunctive**, after verbs, adjectives and nouns that express a demand, proposal, recommendation or necessity:

- verbs: *suggest*, *recommend*, *propose*, *insist*, *demand*, *request*, *require*, *ask*, *urge*, *order*
- adjectives: *essential*, *vital*, *important*, *necessary*, *imperative*, *crucial*, *advisable*
- nouns: *suggestion*, *proposal*, *recommendation*, *requirement*, *demand*, *insistence*
:::

::: theorem Three forms after verbs of demand and necessity {#rule-subjunctive}
After these words, the *that*-clause can take three forms:

1. **subjunctive**: *The committee recommended that she **be** promoted.* *It is essential that every child **have** a place.* — normal in American English in all styles; formal in British English.
2. ***should*** + base form: *The committee recommended that she **should be** promoted.* — mainly British.
3. **ordinary tense** (indicative): *The committee recommended that she **was** promoted.* *It is essential that every child **has** a place.* — British, informal to neutral; rare in American English.

In the negative subjunctive, *not* comes **before** the verb, with no *do*: *I insisted that he **not** pay.* *It is vital that the door **not be** left open.* (✗ *that he doesn't pay* is the indicative, which changes the meaning in some contexts.)
:::

::: proof
Swan (2016) gives the three forms and their distribution in British and American English, and Quirk et al. (1985) and Huddleston and Pullum (2002) describe the same choice. Corpus comparisons of British and American writing from the 1960s and 1990s (Leech et al. 2009) found the mandative subjunctive far more frequent in American English, and increasing again in British English, with *should* declining — a reversal of the decline that grammarians of the early twentieth century had expected. The indicative can make a sentence ambiguous: *She insisted that he **was** there* reports a claim (she said he was there), while *She insisted that he **be** there* reports a demand. The *should* and subjunctive forms keep the meaning of demand clear. The same contrast was met with *suggest* in [[verb-patterns/object-and-verb#rule-that-clause]].
:::

::: warning *Suggest* + the subjunctive
✗ *I suggest that he goes to the doctor* — not wrong in British English, but informal; in formal writing and in American English the subjunctive is expected: ✓ *I suggest that he **go** to the doctor.* ✓ *I suggest that he **should go** to the doctor* (British).

✗ *The manager requested that all staff are not late.* ✓ *The manager requested that all staff **not be** late.* ✓ *… that all staff **should not be** late.*

✗ *It is important that he must attend.* ✓ *It is important that he **attend** / **should attend** / **attends**.* — *must* repeats the idea of necessity already in *important*.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is in the subjunctive and means a **demand**, not a report of a fact?
- [ ] *She insisted that he was at home that night.*
- [x] *She insisted that he be at home by ten.*
- [ ] *She insisted that he is always at home.*
- [ ] *She insisted that he had been at home.*
::: solution
*Be* (the base form, with a third-person subject) is the subjunctive: she demanded it. The other three use ordinary tenses after *insist*, so they report what she claimed was true.
:::

### Fixed subjunctives and *were*

Some old subjunctives survive in fixed expressions, many of them formal or literary:

- wishes: *God **save** the King.* *Long **live** the King.* *Heaven **forbid**!*
- concession: ***Be** that as it may, …* ‘even if that is true’. ***Come** what may, …* ‘whatever happens’. ***Suffice** it to say that…*
- condition: *We'll work through the night, **if need be**.* *So **be** it.*

The *were* of *If I were you* and *I wish she were here* is the only other form of the old past subjunctive still in use. In formal writing it is safest after *if*, *wish*, *if only*, *as if* and *suppose*; in conversation *was* is common in all these places.

::: example Writing formal recommendations {#ex-subjunctive}
A school committee has made five decisions. Report each in formal American style with the subjunctive, then in British style with *should*. (a) *Students must wear their ID cards at all times.* (recommend) (b) *The library will open on Sundays.* (propose) (c) *Parents must be told about the change.* (essential) (d) *Visitors should not park in the staff car park.* (request) (e) *Every teacher needs to attend the training.* (require)
::: solution
(a) *The committee recommends that students **wear** their ID cards at all times.* / *… that students **should wear** …*

(b) *The committee proposes that the library **open** on Sundays.* / *… that the library **should open** …*

(c) *It is essential that parents **be told** about the change.* / *… that parents **should be told** …*

(d) *The committee requests that visitors **not park** in the staff car park.* / *… that visitors **should not park** …*

(e) *The committee requires that every teacher **attend** the training.* / *… that every teacher **should attend** …* — In (e) the third-person subject shows the subjunctive clearly: *attend*, not *attends*.
:::
:::

::: example Correcting a letter of complaint {#ex-letter}
Correct the verb forms in this extract from a letter to a hotel manager. *I wish to complain about my stay. I really wish the room is quieter — the road is very noisy. I would rather you tell me about the building work before I booked. Now I wish I chose another hotel. I hope you will give me a partial refund, and I suggest that the hotel informs future guests about the work. It is time something is done.*
::: solution
1. *I wish to complain* — correct: formal *wish* + *to* = *want*.
2. *I really wish the room is quieter* → *I wish the room **had been** quieter* (the stay is over: unreal past). If the writer is still there: *I wish the room **were** quieter.*
3. *I would rather you tell me … before I booked* → *I would rather you **had told** me … before I booked* (different subject, past).
4. *I wish I chose* → *I wish I **had chosen*** (regret about the past).
5. *I hope you will give me a partial refund* — correct: *hope* + real future.
6. *I suggest that the hotel informs* → *I suggest that the hotel **inform*** (subjunctive, formal) or ***should inform***. *Informs* is acceptable in informal British English but not in a formal letter.
7. *It is time something is done* → *It is time something **was** done.*
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **希望, 但愿 and 要是…就好了.** Chinese 希望 covers both *hope* and *wish*, and the verb after it does not change. English distinguishes by belief: 希望明天不下雨 is *I hope it doesn't rain tomorrow* (possible); 真希望我会说法语 is *I wish I could speak French* (not so). 但愿 is close to *I hope* or *let's hope*; 要是……就好了 and 真后悔…… correspond to *I wish* / *If only*: 要是我早点儿来就好了 — *If only I'd come earlier!* / *I wish I'd come earlier.*
- **No tense to step back.** Chinese has no past tense, so it cannot use one to mark unreality; it uses words instead — 要是, 就好了, 早知道, 本来 — as in conditionals ([[complex-sentences/conditionals]]). Mandarin verbs have no form corresponding to the subjunctive of European languages (Li & Thompson 1981 describe mood and modality through particles and modal verbs instead). For a Chinese speaker, the English unreal past is a form with no counterpart, and is best learned by its triggers (*wish*, *if only*, *it's time*, *would rather*, *as if*).
- **该……了.** 该走了 or 你该找工作了 corresponds to *It's time to go* / *It's (high) time you got a job*. The English past *got* surprises learners, because 该 points to the future.
- **宁愿.** 我宁愿你别告诉她 is *I'd rather you didn't tell her* — again a past form for a present request.
- **建议 and 要求.** After 建议 and 要求 the Chinese verb has no special form: 我建议他去看医生. In English, use the subjunctive or *should* (*I suggest that he **go** / **should go***), or *suggest* + *-ing* when no particular person is meant.
:::

::: history
Old English had a full subjunctive mood, with its own endings in the present and the past (for example *he singe* 'that he sing' beside the indicative *he singþ*). As the endings of English verbs wore away in Middle English, most subjunctive forms became identical to indicative ones, and the meanings they had carried passed to modal verbs such as *should*, *would*, *may* and *might*. By the early twentieth century many British grammarians regarded the subjunctive as dying, apart from *were* and a handful of fixed phrases. American English, however, kept the mandative subjunctive in ordinary use, and corpus studies of the late twentieth century — summarised in Geoffrey Leech, Marianne Hundt, Christian Mair and Nicholas Smith's *Change in Contemporary English* (2009) — showed it spreading back into British writing, probably under American influence.
:::

## Where this leads

Unreal conditionals, which use the same forms, are in [[complex-sentences/conditionals]]. Reported suggestions and demands (*She suggested that we leave*) are in [[complex-sentences/reported-speech]]. ::: summary
- The **unreal past** uses a past form for an unreal present or future and the past perfect for an unreal past; *were* can be used for all persons ([[#def-unreal-past]], [[#rule-unreal-past]]).
- ***Wish* + past** for the present, ***wish* + past perfect** for regrets, ***wish* + *would*** for wanting someone else to act or change ([[#rule-wish]]); *if only* is a stronger *wish*.
- ***Hope*** is for real possibilities and takes ordinary tenses: *I hope you pass*.
- *It's time* + past (*It's time we went*); *would rather* + past for another subject (*I'd rather you didn't*); *as if* + past for unreal comparisons ([[#rule-other]]).
- The **mandative subjunctive** is the base form after *suggest*, *insist*, *essential* etc.: *that he go*, *that she not be*. American English uses it in all styles; British English also uses *should* or the ordinary tense ([[#def-subjunctive]], [[#rule-subjunctive]]).
- Fixed subjunctives: *if need be*, *be that as it may*, *come what may*, *God save the King*.
- Chinese 希望 can be *hope* or *wish*; choose *wish* + unreal past when the situation is unreal.

Answers will vary. A model:

*I like my flat, but **I wish it were** closer to the station and **I wish the walls weren't** so thin. My neighbours are friendly, although **I wish they would** stop moving furniture at midnight.
