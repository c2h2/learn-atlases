Compare a Chinese noun phrase with its English equivalent:

> 我昨天在书店买的那本关于中国历史的书
>
> *the book about Chinese history that I bought in the bookshop yesterday*

In Chinese, everything that describes the book comes **before** 书, joined to it by 的: who bought it, when, where, and what it is about. In English, only short modifiers come before the noun (*the old book*, *the history book*); a phrase (*about Chinese history*) and a whole clause (*that I bought in the bookshop yesterday*) come **after** it. The clause that follows the noun is a **relative clause**, and it is the most common way in English of saying which person or thing we mean, or of adding something about them.

Relative clauses look simple, but they ask a learner to make several choices at once: *who*, *which*, *that*, *whose*, *where* or no word at all; commas or no commas; the preposition at the start or at the end. And some choices change the meaning. *My sister who lives in Canada is a nurse* tells the listener that I have more than one sister; *My sister, who lives in Canada, is a nurse* tells them I have one. This lesson sets out the forms, the two kinds of relative clause and their punctuation, the facts behind the *which*/*that* debate, prepositions, and the special relative words. Reduced relative clauses such as *the man sitting by the door* are in [[complex-sentences/participle-infinitive-clauses]].

## How a relative clause is built

::: definition Relative clause {#def-relative}
A **relative clause** is a subordinate clause that modifies a noun phrase, called its **antecedent** (or **head**). It usually begins with a **relative word** — *who*, *whom*, *whose*, *which*, *that*, *where*, *when*, *why* — which refers back to the antecedent and also has a job inside the relative clause: subject, object, complement of a preposition, or adverbial.

In *the book **that I bought __ yesterday***, the antecedent is *book*, and *that* is the object of *bought*. The place where the object would normally be, after *bought*, is empty: this empty place is called the **gap**. In *the people **who __ live next door***, *who* is the subject, and the gap is before *live*.
:::

The relative words and what they refer to:

| relative word | antecedent | job in the clause | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *who* | people (and pets, often) | subject; object in informal use | *the woman **who** called* |
| *whom* | people | object, or after a preposition; formal | *the candidate **whom** we interviewed* |
| *which* | things, animals, ideas | subject, object, after a preposition | *the train **which** leaves at six* |
| *that* | people or things | subject or object, defining clauses only | *the key **that** opens the shed* |
| *whose* | people or things | possessive determiner | *the girl **whose** bike was stolen* |
| *where* | places (and situations) | adverbial of place | *the town **where** I grew up* |
| *when* | times | adverbial of time | *the year **when** we met* |
| *why* | *reason* | adverbial of reason | *the reason **why** she left* |
| zero (no word) | people or things | object or after a preposition, defining clauses only | *the film __ we saw* |

::: theorem The gap rule {#rule-gap}
The relative word takes the place of one element inside the relative clause, so that element is **not repeated** with a pronoun:

✗ *the book which I bought **it** yesterday* ✓ *the book which I bought yesterday*

✗ *the man who **he** lives next door* ✓ *the man who lives next door*

✗ *the city where I was born **there*** ✓ *the city where I was born*
:::

::: proof
Standard English relative clauses have a gap, not a "resumptive" pronoun, in every position (Quirk et al. 1985; Huddleston & Pullum 2002). Resumptive pronouns do occur in unplanned speech when the gap would be deeply buried and hard to process — *a problem which I don't know whether we can solve **it*** — but even there they are judged as slips, and they are not used in writing. Many languages, including Arabic, Persian and (in some positions) Chinese, use resumptive pronouns normally, which is why this is a frequent error for learners from many backgrounds.
:::

::: theorem Leaving out the relative word {#rule-zero}
In a **defining** relative clause, the relative word can be left out when it is **not the subject** of the clause:

- *the film **(that)** we saw* — *that* is the object of *saw*: it can go.
- *the person **(who)** I was talking to* — object of the preposition *to*: it can go.
- *the film **that** won the prize* — *that* is the subject of *won*: it must stay. ✗ *the film won the prize was French*.

The relative word cannot be left out in a non-defining clause, or when a preposition comes before it (*the person **to whom** I was talking*).
:::

::: proof
Biber et al. (1999) found that the zero relative is common in conversation and fiction and much rarer in academic writing, where *which* and *that* are kept; Swan (2016) describes the same pattern. The reason the subject relative cannot normally be left out is that the sentence would then contain two finite verbs side by side with nothing to show where the clause begins — exactly the error ✗ *There are many people want to come* discussed in [[complex-sentences/clause-structure]]. Some regional and very informal speech does omit a subject relative in existential sentences (*There's a man at the door __ wants to see you*); Quirk et al. note this as informal, and it is not used in standard writing.
:::

## Defining and non-defining clauses

::: definition Defining and non-defining relative clauses {#def-defining}
A **defining** relative clause (also **restrictive**, or in Huddleston and Pullum's terms **integrated**) tells us **which** person or thing is meant. Without it, the noun phrase would not identify its referent, or would mean something different: *The students **who passed** will get a certificate* (only those students).

A **non-defining** relative clause (also **non-restrictive**, or **supplementary**) adds extra information about a referent that is already identified. Without it, the sentence still picks out the same thing: *Our head teacher, **who retires next year**, has worked here for thirty years.*
:::

::: theorem Punctuation and form of the two kinds {#rule-defining}
| | defining | non-defining |
|---|---|---|
| commas | **no** commas | commas round the clause (or dashes or brackets) |
| in speech | no pause; one intonation unit with the noun | a separate intonation unit, often lower in pitch |
| *that* | possible | **not** used in standard English |
| zero relative | possible when not the subject | **not** possible |
| proper names, *my mother*, unique things | rare | normal: *Beijing, **which** …*; *my mother, **who** …* |
| *which* referring to a whole clause | no | yes: *He passed, **which** surprised us.* |

✗ *My father, that is a doctor, works in Wuhan.* ✓ *My father, who is a doctor, works in Wuhan.*

✗ *Shanghai which is China's largest city is on the coast.* ✓ *Shanghai, which is China's largest city, is on the coast.*
:::

::: proof
The two kinds differ in how they relate to the noun, and the punctuation follows the meaning. A defining clause is part of the noun phrase: it narrows down the set the noun refers to, so no break is wanted. A non-defining clause is a comment on a referent that is already fixed, like a sentence in brackets, so it is set off. Both Quirk et al. (1985) and Huddleston and Pullum (2002) describe the restriction on *that* in non-defining clauses; Huddleston and Pullum note that it occasionally occurs in older literature and in informal writing, but it is not standard today. Biber et al. (1999) report that non-defining clauses are much more common in news and academic writing than in conversation, where speakers tend to add the extra information as a new sentence instead.
:::

The difference is easiest to see with a set of people. The class has thirty students; twelve of them passed.

::: widget venn

caption: Circle *students* is the class; circle *passed* is everyone who passed the exam. *The students **who passed** will get a certificate*: the defining clause picks out a subset, and only the shaded overlap gets a certificate. :::

::: example One sister or more? {#ex-sister}
What does each sentence tell you about the speaker's family? (a) *My sister who lives in Canada is a nurse.* (b) *My sister, who lives in Canada, is a nurse.* (c) *The passengers who had tickets were allowed on the boat.* (d) *The passengers, who had tickets, were allowed on the boat.*
::: solution
(a) Defining: the clause is needed to say *which* sister. The speaker has **more than one** sister; the one in Canada is a nurse.

(b) Non-defining: *my sister* already identifies her, so the speaker has **one** sister; the clause adds that she lives in Canada.

(c) Defining: only some passengers had tickets, and only they were allowed on.

(d) Non-defining: all the passengers had tickets, and all were allowed on.

In speech, (a) and (c) are said without a break; in (b) and (d), *who lives in Canada* and *who had tickets* are said as a separate unit. In formal writing, a missing pair of commas can change the facts — a point that matters in contracts and regulations.
:::
:::

Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *Mount Everest that is the highest mountain in the world lies on the border of Nepal and China.*
- [ ] *Mount Everest, that is the highest mountain in the world, lies on the border of Nepal and China.*
- [x] *Mount Everest, which is the highest mountain in the world, lies on the border of Nepal and China.*
- [ ] *Mount Everest, is the highest mountain in the world, lies on the border of Nepal and China.*
::: solution
*Mount Everest* is a unique name, so the clause cannot define it: it is non-defining, with commas and *which*. *That* is not used in non-defining clauses, and the relative word cannot be left out.

## *Which* or *that*?

Many learners have been told that *that* must be used in defining clauses and *which* only in non-defining ones. This is a recommendation of some style guides, not a rule of English grammar.

::: theorem *Which* and *that* in defining clauses {#rule-which-that}
1. In **non-defining** clauses, use *which* for things (never *that*).
2. In **defining** clauses, both *which* and *that* are standard for things: *the train **that** leaves at six* and *the train **which** leaves at six* are both correct in British and American English.
3. In usage, *that* is the more common choice in conversation in both varieties. In **British** writing, *which* is frequent in defining clauses. In edited **American** writing, *that* is strongly preferred, because many American style guides and editors recommend it; following that preference is a sensible choice if you write for an American publication.
4. *That* is usually preferred after *all*, *everything*, *something*, *nothing*, *anything*, *the only* and superlatives: *Everything **that** happened was my fault.* *It's the best film **that** I've seen this year.*
5. Only *which* (or *whom*) can follow a preposition: *the room **in which** we met*, ✗ *the room in that we met*.
:::

::: proof
The idea that *that* should be kept for defining clauses was proposed by H. W. Fowler in *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage* (1926) as a division of labour that would be convenient, and Fowler himself admitted that it did not describe the practice of most good writers. American style manuals later adopted it as house style. Corpus studies show the effect: comparisons of American and British written English from the 1960s and 1990s (Leech et al. 2009) found *which* in defining clauses declining, more steeply in American than in British English. Huddleston and Pullum (2002) and Swan (2016) treat *which* in defining clauses as fully standard. The preference for *that* after *all*, *everything* and superlatives is a tendency noted by Quirk et al. (1985) and Swan (2016), not a rule: *the only thing which matters* also occurs.
:::

### *Who*, *whom* and *that* for people

For people, *who* is usual in all registers; *that* is also standard in defining clauses (*the man **that** sold me the car*), more so in speech than in writing. The belief that *that* cannot refer to people is another zombie rule: it has been used for people since Old English, and dictionaries and reference grammars accept it.

*Whom* is the object form of *who*. In modern English it is formal and is used mainly in two places:

- directly after a preposition, where it is required: *the colleague **to whom** I sent the report* (✗ *to who*);
- as an object in formal writing, especially in non-defining clauses: *The applicants, **whom** we interviewed last week, …*

In everyday English an object relative is *who*, *that* or zero: *the colleague **who** I sent it to*, *the colleague **(that)** I sent it to*.

::: warning *Which* for people, *who* for things
✗ *The doctor which treated me was very kind.* ✓ *The doctor **who** (or **that**) treated me was very kind.*

✗ *The company who makes these phones is Korean.* — Not wrong: British English often treats a company or team as a group of people (*The company **who** … they*). In formal writing, especially American, *which* is safer: *The company **which** / **that** makes these phones …*
:::

## Prepositions in relative clauses

When the relative word is the complement of a preposition, the preposition can go in two places.

::: theorem Preposition first or preposition last {#rule-preposition}
1. **Preposition at the end** (informal and neutral): *the house **(that / which)** we lived **in***, *the person **(who)** I spoke **to***. All relative words, including zero, are possible.
2. **Preposition at the start** (formal): *the house **in which** we lived*, *the person **to whom** I spoke*. Only *which* and *whom* are possible: ✗ *in that*, ✗ *to who*, and the relative word cannot be omitted.
3. With phrasal verbs and fixed verb–preposition pairs, the preposition usually stays with the verb: *the problems **(that)** we came **up against*** (✗ *against which we came up*), *the children **(who)** she looks **after***.
4. Some prepositions are normally placed first: *during which*, *beyond which*, *as a result of which*, *in which case*.
:::

::: proof
The "rule" that a sentence must not end with a preposition goes back to John Dryden, who in 1672 criticised Ben Jonson for placing a preposition at the end of a clause; Dryden based his objection on Latin, where prepositions cannot be separated from their nouns. Robert Lowth's grammar (1762) mentioned the idea but called final prepositions an idiom "which our language is strongly inclined to" and suited to familiar style. Modern reference grammars (Quirk et al. 1985; Huddleston & Pullum 2002) and usage guides (Swan 2016) agree: a preposition at the end of a relative clause is normal English; the fronted preposition is a formal alternative. Choosing it can sound stiff in speech (*Who is the person to whom you were speaking?*) and forcing it with phrasal verbs produces errors.
:::

::: example Formal and informal versions {#ex-preposition}
Give a formal and an informal version of each. (a) *That's the hotel. We stayed at it last year.* (b) *She is the expert. I asked her for advice.* (c) *These are the conditions. The contract depends on them.*
::: solution
(a) Formal: *That is the hotel **at which** we stayed last year.* Informal: *That's the hotel **(that / which)** we stayed **at** last year.* Also possible: *That's the hotel **where** we stayed last year* — *where* replaces *at which*, and is the most natural in conversation.

(b) Formal: *She is the expert **whom** I asked for advice.* — here *whom* is the object of *asked*, and *for* goes with *advice*, so there is no preposition to front. Informal: *She's the expert **(who / that)** I asked for advice.*

(c) Formal: *These are the conditions **on which** the contract depends.* Informal: *These are the conditions **(that)** the contract depends **on**.*
:::
:::

### Quantifiers with *of whom* and *of which*

In non-defining clauses, a quantifier or number can come before *of whom* or *of which*: *some of whom*, *most of which*, *both of whom*, *none of which*, *two of which*, *the largest of which*.

- *She has three brothers, **all of whom** live abroad.*
- *We visited several temples, **the oldest of which** dates from the Tang dynasty.*

::: warning *Both of them* is not a relative
✗ *I have two cousins, both of them are engineers.* — this is two main clauses joined by a comma (a comma splice), because *them* is an ordinary pronoun.

✓ *I have two cousins, **both of whom** are engineers.* (relative) ✓ *I have two cousins. **Both of them** are engineers.* (two sentences) ✓ *I have two cousins, **both of them** engineers.* (verbless clause, [[complex-sentences/participle-infinitive-clauses]])
:::

## *Whose*, *where*, *when* and *why*

***Whose*** is the possessive relative. It is used for people and also for things: *a country **whose** economy depends on tourism*, *a word **whose** meaning has changed*. Some writers avoid *whose* for things and use *of which* (*a country the economy **of which** depends on tourism*), but this is heavy, and reference grammars accept *whose* for things as standard (Swan 2016; Huddleston & Pullum 2002).

***Where***, ***when*** and ***why*** stand for a preposition + *which*:

- *the town **where** I grew up* = *the town **in which** I grew up*
- *the day **when** we met* = *the day **on which** we met*; *when* can be replaced by *that* or left out: *the day **(that)** we met*
- *the reason **why** she left* = *the reason **for which** she left*; also *the reason **(that)** she left*

::: warning *Where* needs an adverbial gap
*Where* replaces an adverbial of place, not an object. If the verb in the clause needs an object, use *which*, *that* or zero.

✗ *Hangzhou is the city where I visited last year.* ✓ *Hangzhou is the city **(that)** I visited last year.* (*visit* takes an object.)

✓ *Hangzhou is the city **where** I stayed last year.* (*stay* takes an adverbial: *I stayed **there***.)

✗ *That's the reason because I left.* ✓ *That's the reason **(why / that)** I left.* ✓ *That's **why** I left.*

✗ *This is the way how I did it.* ✓ *This is **the way** I did it.* ✓ *This is **how** I did it.*
:::

*Where* is also used for abstract "places" — situations, cases, points: *a situation **where** nobody wins*, *the point **where** I gave up*. In formal academic writing, *in which* is often preferred: *a model **in which** prices are fixed*.

## Clauses about a whole sentence

::: theorem *Which* referring to a clause {#rule-sentential}
A non-defining clause with *which* can refer back to a **whole clause**, not just a noun: *The train was late, **which** meant we missed the start.* *She said she'd lost the tickets, **which** wasn't true.* Only *which* is possible, after a comma: ✗ *… , what meant we missed the start*; ✗ *… , that meant …*
:::

::: proof
Quirk et al. (1985) call these **sentential relative clauses**. They are common in speech, where they add a comment or a consequence (*… which was nice*), and in writing they are a useful way to join a fact and its result. Because *which* could also refer to the nearest noun, check for ambiguity: in *She lent me a book about Japan, which was very useful*, was the book useful, or her lending it? If it matters, rewrite: *…, and this was very useful.*
:::

::: example *Which*, *what* or *that*? {#ex-which-what}
Fill each gap with *which*, *what* or *that*, and explain. (a) *He didn't apologise, ___ annoyed everyone.* (b) *___ annoyed everyone was his attitude.* (c) *The thing ___ annoyed everyone was his attitude.* (d) *She speaks four languages, ___ is very useful in her job.*
::: solution
(a) *which* — a sentential relative referring to *He didn't apologise*.

(b) *What* — not a relative clause at all but a noun clause meaning ‘the thing that’, the subject of *was* ([[complex-sentences/noun-clauses]]). *What* has no antecedent: it contains its own.

(c) *that* (or *which*) — a defining relative clause modifying *the thing*. ✗ *the thing what* is non-standard.

(d) *which* — a sentential relative: what is useful is the fact that she speaks four languages.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is **not** standard English?
- [ ] *That's the café where we first met.*
- [ ] *That's the café we first met in.*
- [x] *That's the café where we first visited.*
- [ ] *That's the café in which we first met.*
::: solution
*Visit* takes an object, so the gap in the relative clause is an object, not an adverbial of place: *the café **(that)** we first visited*. The other three are correct; *in which* is the most formal and the stranded *in* the most informal.
:::
:::

## Stacking modifiers after the noun

English puts single-word modifiers before the noun and longer ones after it, in a fixed order: *the **two old** wooden houses **by the river** **that my grandfather built***. When several modifiers follow, phrases usually come before clauses, and a relative clause should be as close as possible to its antecedent:

- ? *I bought a phone from a market stall **that** breaks easily.* (Does the stall break?) ✓ *The phone **(that)** I bought from a market stall breaks easily.* — the clause now stands next to the noun it describes.
- Agreement follows the antecedent: *the books on the shelf **that are** dusty* (the books) — *the shelf of books **that is** dusty* (the shelf).

::: example From Chinese 的-phrases to English {#ex-chinese}
Translate the noun phrases, putting each modifier in its English position. (a) 坐在门口的那个穿红衣服的女孩 (b) 我们上个月讨论过的问题 (c) 一个父母都是医生的学生 (d) 我住了十年的城市
::: solution
1. Find the head noun (the last noun in the Chinese).
2. Short modifiers go before it; phrases and clauses go after.
3. Choose the relative word from the job of the gap.

(a) *the girl **in the red dress** **(who is) sitting by the door*** — *in the red dress* is a phrase; *sitting by the door* is a reduced relative clause.

(b) *the problem(s) **(that) we discussed last month*** — object gap: *that* or zero.

(c) *a student **whose parents are both doctors*** — 父母 belongs to the student, so *whose*.

(d) *the city **where I lived for ten years*** or *the city **(that) I lived in for ten years*** — 住 needs a place adverbial, so *where* or *in*. (✗ *the city where I lived in*.)
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Before or after the noun.** In Chinese, a relative clause precedes its head and ends in 的 (我昨天买的书); in English it follows (*the book I bought yesterday*). Li and Thompson (1981) analyse the Chinese structure as a nominalization with 的 modifying the head. Because of this, a long English noun phrase must be built in the opposite order from Chinese, and reading English, the learner has to keep the noun in mind while the description comes after it.
- **No relative pronoun.** Chinese 的 is the same whatever the role of the head in the clause, so 我认识的人 ('the person I know') and 认识我的人 ('the person who knows me') differ only in word order. English marks the difference with word order too, but also chooses among *who*, *which*, *that* and *whose* by animacy and job, and allows zero only for non-subjects.
- **Resumptive pronouns.** Chinese prepositions cannot be stranded, so when the head is the object of a preposition, Chinese often keeps a pronoun in the clause: 我跟他学过中文的那个老师 ('the teacher with whom I studied Chinese', literally 'I with him studied Chinese 的 teacher'). Carried into English, this gives ✗ *the teacher who I studied Chinese with him*. English leaves a gap: *the teacher (who) I studied Chinese **with***.
- **Non-defining meaning.** Chinese has no punctuation that marks a modifier as non-defining. Chinese writers often express extra information about a known person as a separate clause: 我哥哥是医生，住在上海. English can do the same, or use a non-defining clause: *My brother, **who lives in Shanghai**, is a doctor.* Translating into Chinese, long English non-defining clauses are usually turned into separate clauses rather than heavy 的-phrases ([[translation/english-into-chinese]]).
:::

::: history
Relative clauses have been a battleground for usage advice. In 1672 the poet John Dryden criticised Ben Jonson for ending a clause with a preposition, and the idea passed into eighteenth-century grammars, although Robert Lowth (1762) himself called the final preposition an idiom natural to English. H. W. Fowler's *Modern English Usage* (1926) suggested reserving *that* for defining and *which* for non-defining clauses, a suggestion that American editors later turned into a house rule. A different kind of research began in 1977, when Edward Keenan and Bernard Comrie compared relative clauses in about fifty languages and proposed an **accessibility hierarchy**: if a language can relativise on an object, it can relativise on a subject; if it can relativise on the object of a preposition, it can relativise on a direct object; and so on down the list. Later studies of second-language learners found that subject relatives (*the man who called*) are generally easier to learn than object and preposition relatives (*the man I called*, *the man I spoke to*).
:::

::: summary
- A relative clause follows its antecedent and begins with a relative word that fills a **gap** in the clause; do not repeat the gap with a pronoun ([[#def-relative]], [[#rule-gap]]).
- **Defining** clauses say which one is meant: no commas, *that* and zero possible. **Non-defining** clauses add information: commas, no *that*, no zero ([[#def-defining]], [[#rule-defining]]).
- *Which* and *that* are both standard in defining clauses; *that* dominates in conversation and edited American writing, *which* is common in British writing. Prefer *that* after *all*, *everything* and superlatives ([[#rule-which-that]]).
- *Whom* is formal; it is required directly after a preposition. *That* for people is standard.
- Prepositions can go first (formal: *in which*) or last (informal: *(that) we lived in*); ending a clause with a preposition is normal English ([[#rule-preposition]]).
- The relative word can be omitted only when it is not the subject, and only in defining clauses ([[#rule-zero]]).
- *Where* replaces a place adverbial, not an object (✗ *the city where I visited*); use *whose* for possession, *of whom*/*of which* after quantifiers, and *which* for a whole clause ([[#rule-sentential]]).
- Chinese puts the description before the noun with 的; English puts phrases and clauses after it.
:::

::: exercise Can the relative word go? {level=1}
In which sentences can the relative word be left out? (a) *The man **who** fixed my bike was very quick.* (b) *The man **who** I asked didn't know the way.* (c) *The money **that** you lent me is in the drawer.* (d) *My boss, **whom** you met yesterday, wants to see you.* (e) *The office **in which** she works is on the fifth floor.*
::: solution
Only (b) and (c): there the relative word is the object of the clause in a defining clause. In (a) *who* is the subject; (d) is non-defining; in (e) the preposition comes first (the informal version *the office she works in* has no relative word).
:::
:::

::: exercise Correct the mistakes {level=2}
Each sentence has one mistake. Correct it. (a) *The book which I borrowed it from the library is overdue.* (b) *Shenzhen, that is next to Hong Kong, has grown very fast.* (c) *She was born in Dali, where she has visited many times since.* (d) *I have two sisters, both of them are teachers.* (e) *This is the man to who I sent the email.* (f) *He forgot my birthday, what really upset me.*
::: solution
(a) *The book (which) I borrowed from the library is overdue.* (b) *Shenzhen, **which** is next to Hong Kong, …* (c) *She was born in Dali, **which** she has visited many times since.* (d) *I have two sisters, **both of whom** are teachers.* (or two sentences) (e) *This is the man **to whom** I sent the email.* / *This is the man (who) I sent the email **to**.* (f) *He forgot my birthday, **which** really upset me.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Join the sentences {level=2}
Join each pair with a relative clause. Decide whether it is defining or non-defining. (a) *I met a woman. Her son goes to school with mine.* (b) *Guilin is famous for its hills. Millions of tourists visit it each year.* (c) *The meeting was cancelled. I had prepared for it all week.* (d) *He lent me his car. This was very kind of him.* (e) *I have read forty pages of the report. Most of them are about costs.*
::: solution
(a) *I met a woman **whose son** goes to school with mine.* (defining)

(b) *Guilin, **which millions of tourists visit each year**, is famous for its hills.* (non-defining)

(c) *The meeting **(that) I had prepared for all week** was cancelled.* / formal: *The meeting **for which I had prepared** all week was cancelled.* (defining)

(d) *He lent me his car, **which was very kind of him**.* (sentential)

(e) *I have read forty pages of the report, **most of which are** about costs.* (non-defining)
:::
:::

::: exercise Formal and informal {level=2}
Rewrite each sentence in a more formal style, with the preposition before the relative word. (a) *That's the company I used to work for.* (b) *The issue we're most worried about is safety.* (c) *Is this the colleague you were talking to?* (d) *The friends I went on holiday with were all from my university.*
::: solution
(a) *That is the company **for which** I used to work.* (b) *The issue **about which** we are most worried is safety.* (c) *Is this the colleague **to whom** you were talking?* (d) *The friends **with whom** I went on holiday were all from my university.* — Note that (c) sounds very stiff in conversation: formality should fit the situation.
:::
:::

::: exercise Ambiguous attachment {level=3}
Each sentence can be read in two ways. Explain the two meanings, and rewrite the sentence to express each one clearly. (a) *I spoke to the assistant of the manager who was on holiday.* (b) *She sent me photos of the lake, which were beautiful.* (c) *He didn't pass the test, which surprised nobody.*
::: hint
Ask which noun — or which clause — the relative word refers to.
:::
::: solution
(a) Was the assistant or the manager on holiday? *I spoke to the manager's assistant, who was on holiday* (the assistant). *The manager was on holiday, so I spoke to his assistant* (the manager).

(b) *Which* could refer to *photos* or *the lake*; the plural verb *were* points to *photos*, but a reader may still stumble. *She sent me some beautiful photos of the lake.* / *She sent me photos of the lake, which is beautiful.*

(c) Usually: the fact that he didn't pass surprised nobody. But it could also mean that he didn't pass the test, and the test surprised nobody (unlikely here). To make the first meaning certain: *Nobody was surprised that he didn't pass the test.*
:::
:::

::: exercise A paragraph with relative clauses {level=3}
Rewrite this paragraph as three or four sentences, using at least four relative clauses of different kinds (defining, non-defining, *whose*, preposition + *which*, sentential *which*). *Kunming is in Yunnan Province. Its climate is mild all year. People call it the City of Eternal Spring. I lived there for two years. I studied at a university in Kunming. The university has a famous botanical garden. Students from many countries come to Kunming. This makes the city very international.*
::: solution
A model (many versions are possible):

*Kunming, **which** is in Yunnan Province, has a climate **that** is mild all year, **which** is why people call it the City of Eternal Spring. I lived there for two years, studying at a university **whose** botanical garden is famous. Students come to Kunming from many countries, **which** makes the city very international.*

Check: non-defining *which* (Kunming); defining *that* (climate); sentential *which* (the reason for the name); *whose* (the university's garden); sentential *which* (the effect). A preposition + *which* could replace the second sentence: *The university **at which** I studied for two years has a famous botanical garden.*
:::
