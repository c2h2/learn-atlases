Here are three Chinese sentences with the same structure, 如果 …… 就 ……:

- 如果明天下雨，我们就不去了。
- 如果我有钱，我就买一辆车。
- 如果你昨天来了，你就见到她了。

In Chinese the form of the verbs does not change: whether the condition is likely, unlikely or impossible is left to the context and to words such as 明天, 昨天 and 早就. In English the three need three different verb forms:

- *If it **rains** tomorrow, we **won't go**.* (a real possibility)
- *If I **had** money, I **would buy** a car.* (I don't have money: an unreal present)
- *If you **had come** yesterday, you **would have seen** her.* (you didn't come: an unreal past)

The tense of an English conditional does two jobs at once: it places the condition in time, and it tells the listener whether the speaker thinks the condition is real or not. A learner who sees only the first job will say ✗ *If I have money, I will buy a car* (which sounds as if money might arrive any day) or ✗ *If you came yesterday, you saw her* (which means something else entirely). This lesson explains the system as a whole: real and unreal conditions, the "step back" in tense that marks unreality, mixed conditionals, the alternatives to *if*, and the formal and implied conditionals of written English. It builds on the adverbial clauses of [[complex-sentences/adverbial-clauses]]; wishes and other unreal forms follow in [[complex-sentences/unreal-past]].

## Real and unreal conditions

::: definition Real and unreal conditionals {#def-conditional}
A **conditional sentence** has a **condition** (the *if*-clause, also called the protasis) and a **consequence** (the main clause, or apodosis).

- In a **real** (or **open**) conditional, the speaker leaves open whether the condition is or will be true: *If it rains tomorrow, we'll stay at home.* *If you heated the milk, it's probably still warm.*
- In an **unreal** (also **remote**, **hypothetical**, or for the past **counterfactual**) conditional, the speaker presents the condition as false, or unlikely, or merely imagined: *If I were taller, I'd play basketball.* *If we had left earlier, we'd have caught the train.*
:::

Classroom books often number the main patterns. The numbers are useful labels, but they cover only part of what English does:

| label | *if*-clause | main clause | meaning | example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| "zero" | present | present | general truth or habit | *If you **heat** ice, it **melts**.* |
| "first" | present | *will* / modal / imperative | real future possibility | *If it **rains**, we'**ll stay** in.* |
| "second" | past | *would* / *could* / *might* + verb | unreal or unlikely, present or future | *If I **won** the lottery, I'**d travel**.* |
| "third" | past perfect | *would* / *could* / *might* **have** + participle | unreal past (counterfactual) | *If she **had asked**, I'**d have helped**.* |

::: theorem Real conditionals {#rule-real}
In a real conditional, each clause takes the tense that its own time and meaning require, as in any other sentence:

1. **Future**: present in the *if*-clause, *will*, a modal or an imperative in the main clause: *If you **need** help, **call** me.* *If the price **goes** up, we **might** cancel.*
2. **General truths and habits**: present in both clauses (*if* is close to *when* or *whenever*): *If I **drink** coffee after six, I **can't** sleep.*
3. **Past and present**: any combination that makes sense: *If she **was** at the meeting, she **knows** about the plan.* *If you **didn't** get the email, it **went** to the wrong address.* *If he**'s** been working all night, he**'ll** be tired.*

*Will* is used in an *if*-clause in two cases: when it means **willingness** or a polite request (*If you**'ll** wait here, I'll fetch the manager.* *If you **will** insist on driving fast, …* = ‘if you are determined to’), and when the *if*-clause is a **consequence** of the main clause rather than its cause: *If it **will** help you, I'll lend you my notes* (my lending comes first; your being helped follows).
:::

::: proof
Quirk et al. (1985), Huddleston and Pullum (2002) and Swan (2016) agree that real conditionals have no special tense pattern: the rule "present in the *if*-clause" for future reference is the same rule as for time clauses ([[complex-sentences/adverbial-clauses#rule-time-tense]]), and the combinations in rule 3 are as common in real conversation as the textbook types. Real texts contain many *if*-sentences, like those in rule 3, that fit none of the numbered types, which is why the numbering should be treated as a starting point rather than a description of the system. The two uses of *will* in rule 1's last paragraph are described in all three grammars; outside them, ✗ *If it will rain tomorrow* is a mistake.
:::

::: theorem Unreal conditionals: the step back {#rule-unreal}
To make a condition unreal, English moves its verbs **one step back** in tense:

1. **Unreal present or future**: past tense in the *if*-clause; *would* (or *could*, *might*) + bare infinitive in the main clause. *If I **knew** her number, I **would call** her.* (I don't know it.) *If you **left** now, you **could** still **catch** the train.* (It's unlikely you will.)
2. **Unreal past**: past perfect in the *if*-clause; *would* (*could*, *might*) + ***have*** + past participle in the main clause. *If I **had known** her number, I **would have called** her.* (I didn't know it.)
3. In the unreal present, ***were*** can be used for all persons: *If I **were** you, …* *If she **were** here, …* *Were* is the standard choice in formal writing and in the fixed phrase *If I were you*; ***was*** is common and acceptable in informal English: *If she **was** here, she'd know what to do.*
4. ***Would*** does **not** normally appear in the *if*-clause: ✗ *If I would know her number…* ✗ *If I would have known…*
:::

::: proof
The past tense here does not refer to past time: *If I knew her number* is about now. Huddleston and Pullum (2002) call this the **modal preterite** and Swan (2016) the **past for unreal situations**: the past form expresses distance from reality, as it expresses distance from now in its ordinary use and distance from the listener in polite requests (*I wondered if you could help*). The past perfect is needed for the unreal past because the simple past is already taken. On *were*, Huddleston and Pullum call it **irrealis *were*** and note that *was* is increasingly used in its place except in formal style; Swan gives the same register difference. *Would* in the *if*-clause (*If I would have known*) is frequent in informal American speech, but it is not accepted in standard writing on either side of the Atlantic (Swan 2016); the exceptions are polite *would* (*If you **would** come this way…* = ‘if you are willing’) and *would like* (*If you **would like** a copy, …*).
:::

::: example Real or unreal? {#ex-real-unreal}
What does each sentence tell you about the speaker's view of the facts? (a) *If he's at home, he'll answer the phone.* (b) *If he were at home, he'd answer the phone.* (c) *If he was at home, he didn't answer the phone.* (d) *If he had been at home, he'd have answered the phone.*
::: solution
(a) Real: the speaker doesn't know whether he's at home. If he is, he will answer.

(b) Unreal present: the speaker assumes he is **not** at home (perhaps because nobody is answering).

(c) Real past: the speaker doesn't know whether he was at home; if he was, then he chose not to answer (or didn't hear). Note that the past tense here is a real past, not a step back.

(d) Unreal past: he wasn't at home at the relevant time; that is why he didn't answer.

(b) and (c) both contain past tenses in the *if*-clause, but only (b) has *would*: the main clause shows which reading is meant.
:::
:::

::: warning *Will* and *would* in the *if*-clause
✗ *If it will be sunny tomorrow, we'll go to the beach.* ✓ *If it **is** sunny tomorrow, we'll go to the beach.*

✗ *If I would have more time, I would learn Japanese.* ✓ *If I **had** more time, I would learn Japanese.*

✗ *If I would have known, I would have come.* ✓ *If I **had known**, I would have come.*

The *will*/*would* goes in the main clause. Learners often put it in both clauses because both clauses refer to the future or to an imagined situation; in English, the *if* itself already carries that meaning.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence means that the speaker did **not** study medicine?
- [ ] *If I studied medicine, I'll become a doctor.*
- [ ] *If I study medicine, I'll become a doctor.*
- [x] *If I had studied medicine, I'd be a doctor now.*
- [ ] *If I studied medicine, I would become a doctor.*
::: solution
*Had studied* is the unreal past: the speaker did not study medicine, and as a result is not a doctor now. The second sentence leaves the future open; the fourth imagines a future that is unlikely (the speaker probably will not study medicine, but hasn't necessarily failed to). The first mixes a real past with a future and does not make sense.
:::
:::

## Mixed conditionals

The *if*-clause and the main clause need not refer to the same time. When they do not, the tenses are **mixed**: each clause keeps the form that fits its own time.

::: theorem Mixed conditionals {#rule-mixed}
1. **Unreal past condition, unreal present result**: past perfect + *would* + bare infinitive. *If I **had taken** the job in Shenzhen, I **would be** rich now.* (I didn't take it; I'm not rich.)
2. **Unreal present condition, unreal past result**: past + *would have* + participle. *If I **spoke** French, I **would have understood** the film last night.* (I don't speak French — a permanent fact — so I didn't understand.)
3. Real conditions can also mix times freely, as in [[#rule-real]]: *If you **missed** the lecture, you **can** borrow my notes.*
:::

::: proof
Mixed conditionals are described in all the major grammars (Quirk et al. 1985; Swan 2016); they show that English conditionals are not fixed formulas but combinations of two rules — the step back for unreality, and the ordinary tense for each clause's time. Pattern 1 is much more common than pattern 2, because regrets about past decisions and their present results are a frequent topic. In pattern 2 the *if*-clause usually describes a permanent state (*If I were taller*, *If she weren't so shy*).
:::

::: example Building mixed conditionals {#ex-mixed}
Write one conditional sentence for each situation. (a) *I didn't buy a ticket. Now I can't go to the concert.* (b) *She isn't careful. She made a lot of mistakes in the test.* (c) *We didn't book a table. We're standing in a queue now.* (d) *He's very tall. He didn't fit in the small car.*
::: solution
1. Decide the time of each clause: is the condition past or present? the result?
2. Step back each unreal clause: present → past (with *would*), past → past perfect (with *would have*).

(a) Past condition, present result: *If I **had bought** a ticket, I **could go** to the concert.*

(b) Present condition (a permanent quality), past result: *If she **were** more careful, she **wouldn't have made** so many mistakes in the test.*

(c) Past condition, present result: *If we **had booked** a table, we **wouldn't be standing** in a queue now.*

(d) Present condition, past result: *If he **weren't** so tall, he **would have fitted** in the small car.* (*wasn't* is also acceptable in informal English.)
:::
:::

## Other ways to state a condition

::: theorem Conjunctions of condition {#rule-conjunctions}
1. ***Unless*** means roughly ‘if … not’ or ‘except if’: *We'll eat outside **unless** it rains.* (= if it doesn't rain). Use *if … not*, not *unless*, when the negative condition is the **cause** of the result, especially with feelings: *I'll be upset **if** she **doesn't** come* (✗ *unless she comes*); and usually in unreal past conditionals: *If you **hadn't** helped me, I would have failed* (*unless* is rare here).
2. ***Provided (that)***, ***providing (that)***, ***as long as*** and ***on condition that*** stress that the condition is necessary: *You can borrow the car **as long as** you're careful.* *On condition that* is formal and usual in agreements.
3. ***Only if*** makes the condition the only one; ***even if*** says the result holds whether or not the condition is met ([[complex-sentences/adverbial-clauses#rule-concession]]). With *only if* at the start, the main clause inverts: ***Only if** all members agree **can** the rule be changed* ([[complex-sentences/emphasis-inversion]]).
4. ***Suppose***, ***supposing*** and ***what if*** introduce an imagined situation, with real or unreal tenses: ***Suppose** you **lost** your passport — what would you do?* ***What if** she **says** no?*
5. ***Otherwise*** (an adverb) and ***or*** (a coordinator) give the result of the condition **not** being met: *Hurry up, **otherwise** / **or** we'll miss the bus.*
6. ***But for*** + noun, ***without*** + noun, ***if it weren't for*** and ***if it hadn't been for*** give an unreal condition with a noun phrase: ***If it hadn't been for** your help, I would have failed.* ***But for** your help, …* (formal) ***Without** your help, …*
:::

The relation between *unless* and *if not* is a nice case for logic. In the figure, let *p* be "you hurry" and *q* "you'll miss the bus".

The comparison shows that this is logically the same as *you hurry or you miss the bus* (p ∨ q): both are false only in the row where you don't hurry and still don't miss it. ::: example *Unless*, *if* or *in case*? {#ex-unless}
Choose the best word, and explain. (a) *I won't sign the contract (unless / if) my lawyer reads it first.* (b) *I'd be surprised (unless / if) he didn't win.* (c) *Write down my number (in case / if) you need it.* (d) *You can't come in (unless / in case) you have a ticket.* (e) *(Unless / If) you hadn't reminded me, I'd have forgotten.*
::: solution
(a) *unless* — ‘except if my lawyer reads it’. (*if* would make the reading the cause of not signing, which makes no sense.)

(b) *if* — *didn't win* is the cause of the surprise; *unless he won* would mean the opposite.

(c) *in case* — a precaution: write it now, because you might need it later.

(d) *unless* — ‘except if you have a ticket’.

(e) *If* — an unreal past with a negative cause; *unless* is very rarely used in unreal past conditions.
:::
:::

## Formal conditionals and inversion

Three forms are typical of formal English — official letters, instructions, academic and legal writing:

1. ***should*** in the *if*-clause makes a condition seem less likely, or just more polite: *If you **should** need any help, please contact me.*
2. ***were to*** + infinitive presents a future condition as hypothetical: *If the government **were to** raise taxes, prices would rise.*
3. **Inversion without *if***: the auxiliary comes before the subject.
   - *Should you need any help, please contact me.*
   - *Were the government to raise taxes, prices would rise.* *Were I in your position, I would resign.*
   - *Had I known about the problem, I would have acted.*

In negative inverted conditionals, *not* follows the subject and is not contracted: ***Had** I **not** seen it myself, …* (✗ *Hadn't I seen it myself*). Only *should*, *were* and *had* invert in this way in modern English; ✗ *Did I know her number, I would call* is not possible.

::: proof
Quirk et al. (1985), Huddleston and Pullum (2002) and Swan (2016) all describe conditional inversion as formal and limited to these three auxiliaries. Biber et al. (1999) found inverted conditionals to be rare in conversation and concentrated in written registers, especially fiction and academic prose. *Should you need* is frequent in business letters and emails, where it is polite rather than stiff.
:::

## Implied conditions and other conditional meanings

A condition is often present without *if*:

- **In the context**: *I **wouldn't** do that.* (= if I were you) *It **would** be nice to see you.* (= if you came)
- **In a phrase**: ***With** a bit more practice, you **could** win.* ***In** your place, I'd refuse.* ***Without** a visa, you **can't** enter.*
- **In an imperative + *and* / *or***: ***Press** this button **and** the door opens.* (= if you press …) ***Hurry up, or** we'll be late.* (= if you don't hurry …)

::: example Making the condition explicit {#ex-implied}
Rewrite each sentence as a full conditional with *if*. (a) *Without a passport, you can't board the plane.* (b) *Leave now and you'll catch the last bus.* (c) *I wouldn't trust him.* (d) *Be careful, or you'll cut yourself.* (e) *But for the rain, the harvest would have failed.*
::: solution
1. Find the hidden condition: a phrase (*without*, *but for*), an imperative, or the context.
2. Decide whether it is real or unreal, and choose the tenses.

(a) *If you don't have a passport, you can't board the plane.* — real.

(b) *If you leave now, you'll catch the last bus.* — real; imperative + *and* = *if*.

(c) *If I were you, I wouldn't trust him.* — the condition is in the context: an unreal present.

(d) *If you aren't careful, you'll cut yourself.* — imperative + *or* = *if … not*.

(e) *If it hadn't rained (If it hadn't been for the rain), the harvest would have failed.* — unreal past.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which inverted conditional is correct?
- [ ] *Hadn't we left early, we would have missed the train.*
- [ ] *Did I know the answer, I would tell you.*
- [x] *Were it not for the subsidies, the farm would close.*
- [ ] *Should you will need a taxi, ask at reception.*
::: solution
Only *should*, *were* and *had* invert in conditionals, and *not* follows the subject without contraction: *Had we not left early…*. *Were it not for* is the formal equivalent of *If it weren't for*. *Should* is followed by a bare infinitive: *Should you need a taxi…*
:::
:::

Some *if*-sentences are not about cause at all. In *If you're hungry, there's some soup in the fridge*, the soup is there whether or not you are hungry: the *if*-clause gives the condition under which the information is **relevant**. These are often called **speech-act** (or relevance) conditionals. Others are polite formulas: *If I could just say something…*, *If you don't mind me asking, …*, *If I remember rightly, …*.

::: note English and Chinese
- **Tense versus context.** Chinese conditionals with 如果, 要是, 假如 or 假设 (often with 的话 at the end of the condition and 就 in the result) do not change the verb to mark unreality. Li and Thompson (1981) note that the hypothetical or counterfactual reading comes from context. English grammar forces the speaker to choose: real or unreal, present or past. The learner's task is to ask "Do I believe this condition is true?" before writing the verb.
- **Counterfactual signals in Chinese.** Chinese does have words that point to an unreal past: 要不是 (‘if it hadn't been for’), 早知道 (‘if I had known’), 本来 and 早就 in the result. 要不是你提醒我，我早就忘了 is *If you hadn't reminded me, I'd have forgotten long ago*; 早知道这样，我就不来了 is *If I'd known it would be like this, I wouldn't have come.* These words are good triggers for the English past perfect + *would have*.
- **Unmarked conditions.** Chinese often states a condition with no conjunction at all (意合): 你不去我也不去 — *If you don't go, I won't go either*; 下雨就别来了 — *Don't come if it rains.* An English sentence needs *if*, *unless*, *as long as* or an imperative + *and*/*or*.
- **The counterfactual debate.** In 1981 the psychologist Alfred Bloom argued in *The Linguistic Shaping of Thought* that because Chinese lacks a grammatical counterfactual, Chinese speakers found counterfactual reasoning harder. Terry Au (1983) repeated his experiments with better-translated materials and found no such difference, and later studies agreed with her. The lack of a grammatical form in Chinese is a fact about grammar, not about how Chinese speakers think: the meaning is expressed by other means.
:::

::: history
Conditionals have interested logicians since antiquity and linguists more recently. Joseph Greenberg's list of language universals (1963) included the observation that in conditional statements the condition normally comes before the conclusion in all languages, as it does in most English *if*-sentences. In 1971 Michael Geis and Arnold Zwicky described the "invited inference" by which listeners take *If you mow the lawn, I'll give you five dollars* to imply *If you don't, I won't* — the extra reading in the truth-table figure above. A volume edited by Elizabeth Traugott and colleagues, *On Conditionals* (1986), brought together typological, logical and historical studies, including Bernard Comrie's survey of how languages mark hypothetical conditions. The teaching labels "first", "second" and "third conditional" became standard in English-language teaching in the second half of the twentieth century; the major reference grammars do not use them, and describe the system instead in terms of real and unreal conditions.
:::

## Where this leads

The step back in tense also appears after *wish*, *if only*, *it's time* and *would rather*: [[complex-sentences/unreal-past]]. Inversion after *only if*, *not until* and in formal conditionals is in [[complex-sentences/emphasis-inversion]]. Modal verbs in the result clause (*might have*, *could have*, *should have*) are studied in [[modality/past-modals]], and the logic of *if* in arguments in [[essay-writing/arguing-a-point]].

::: summary
- A **real** conditional leaves the truth of the condition open; an **unreal** one presents it as false, unlikely or imagined ([[#def-conditional]]).
- Real conditionals use the tenses their times require; for the future, present in the *if*-clause, *will* in the main clause ([[#rule-real]]).
- Unreal conditionals **step back** one tense: past + *would* for the present or future, past perfect + *would have* for the past; *were* for all persons in formal English ([[#rule-unreal]]).
- *Will* and *would* normally belong in the main clause, not the *if*-clause (except for willingness or politeness).
- **Mixed** conditionals combine an unreal past condition with a present result, or the reverse ([[#rule-mixed]]).
- *Unless* ≈ ‘except if’; *provided*, *as long as*, *on condition that* stress necessity; *in case* is a precaution, not a condition; *but for* / *without* / *if it hadn't been for* + noun ([[#rule-conjunctions]]).
- Formal English uses *should*, *were to* and inversion: *Should you need…*, *Had I known…*
- Chinese marks unreality by context and words like 要不是 and 早知道; English marks it by tense.
:::
