A short exchange in an office:

> **Sara:** *Have you sent the invoice?*
> **Li Wei:** *Yes, I have. And the contract?*
> **Sara:** *Not yet. I'll do it after lunch, if I can.*
> **Li Wei:** *Do you think the client will sign?*
> **Sara:** *I hope so. They didn't last time, but this offer is a better one.*

Almost every reply leaves something out or replaces it with a small word. *I have* = *I have sent the invoice*. *And the contract?* = *And have you sent the contract?* *Not yet* = *I have not sent it yet*. *If I can* = *if I can do it*. *I hope so* = *I hope that they will sign*. *They didn't* = *they didn't sign*. *A better one* = *a better offer*. Without these short forms, the conversation would be slow and heavy; with the wrong ones, it would be confusing.

Chinese also leaves a great deal out — often more than English: 发了吗？发了。 But it leaves out different things. Chinese drops subjects and objects freely and answers by repeating the verb; English keeps subjects and objects, and answers with the **auxiliary**. A Chinese speaker who transfers the Chinese habit says ✗ *Yes, I sent* or ✗ *I hope* (for *I hope so*), and an English speaker who hears *Yes, sent* may find it abrupt. This lesson explains **ellipsis** (leaving words out) and **substitution** (replacing them with *one*, *so*, *do so* and similar words): what may be left out, what must stay, and which substitute fits which kind of element.

## Ellipsis and substitution

::: definition Ellipsis and substitution {#def-ellipsis}
**Ellipsis** is the omission of words that the listener or reader can recover exactly from the context: *I can't swim, but my sister **can** __.* (= can swim)

**Substitution** is the replacement of words by a short form that stands for them: *This plan is better than the last **one**.* (= plan) *"Will it rain?" "I think **so**."* (= that it will rain)

Both avoid repetition. The omitted or replaced words must be **recoverable**: normally from the text before (or, less often, after), sometimes from the situation.
:::

Halliday and Hasan (1976) classified both by what they replace: a noun or noun phrase (**nominal**), a verb phrase (**verbal**) or a whole clause (**clausal**). The table gives the main English forms.

| what is left out or replaced | ellipsis | substitution |
|---|---|---|
| noun (head of a noun phrase) | *Which shirt? — The blue __.* | *the blue **one*** |
| verb phrase | *She can't come, but I **can** __.* | *I'll **do so**.* / *I'll **do it**.* |
| whole clause | *Someone called, but I don't know **who** __.* | *I think **so**.* / *I hope **not**.* |

## Ellipsis after an auxiliary

::: theorem Verb phrase ellipsis {#rule-vp-ellipsis}
1. To avoid repeating a verb phrase, keep the **first auxiliary** (or modal) and leave out the rest: *I haven't finished yet, but Tom **has**.* *"Can you help?" "Yes, I **can**."* *He said he would apologise, and he **will**.*
2. If there is no auxiliary, use ***do***, ***does*** or ***did***: *She speaks Japanese better than I **do**.* *"Who broke it?" "Ali **did**."*
3. *Be* is kept even as a main verb: *"Are you ready?" "Yes, I **am**."* *She's tired, and so **am** I.*
4. Further auxiliaries can be kept or left out: *"Have you been waiting long?" "Yes, I **have**."* or *"… I **have been**."*
5. The auxiliary cannot be contracted at the end: *"Are you coming?" "Yes, I **am**."* (✗ *Yes, I'm.*) Negative contractions are fine, because *n't* is part of the auxiliary: *"No, I'**m not**."* *"No, she **isn't**."*
:::

::: proof
Quirk et al. (1985) and Huddleston and Pullum (2002) describe verb phrase ellipsis as a property of the English auxiliary system: the auxiliaries are the words that can stand at the end of an elliptical clause, which is why short answers, question tags and responses such as *So do I* are all built on them. A contracted auxiliary cannot end a clause because a contraction leans on the word after it, and at the end there is none. *Do* in rule 2 is the same "dummy" *do* that forms questions and negatives when there is no other auxiliary ([[core-grammar/questions-and-negatives]]).
:::

### *To* for a whole infinitive

After verbs that take a *to*-infinitive, the infinitive can be reduced to ***to***:

- *"Would you like to come?" "I'd love **to**."* (✗ *I'd love.*)
- *I didn't want to go, but I **had to**.*
- *You can stay if you **want to**.* / *if you **like**.* (*if you want* and *if you like* without *to* are also common.)
- *"Did you call her?" "No, I forgot **to**."* / *"I'm **going to**."* / *"I'm **trying to**!"*

Leaving out the whole infinitive, including *to*, is usually wrong after *want*, *would like*, *need*, *hope*, *try*, *mean*, *have to* and *ought to*: ✗ *I'd like*, ✗ *I didn't mean*.

::: example Short answers and responses {#ex-short}
Give a natural short reply, keeping only what is needed. (a) *"Have you ever been to Xinjiang?"* (yes) (b) *"Does this bus go to the station?"* (no) (c) *"Will you be at the meeting?"* (yes) (d) *"Who wants more rice?"* (I) (e) *"Do you want to come with us?"* (yes, I'd love it) (f) *"You didn't lock the door!"* (you're wrong — I locked it)
::: solution
1. Find the first auxiliary of the question; if there is none, use *do/does/did*.
2. Keep the auxiliary, drop the rest; do not contract at the end.

(a) *Yes, I have.* (b) *No, it doesn't.* (c) *Yes, I will.* (d) *I do!* (*Me!* is also common in speech.) (e) *Yes, I'd love to.* (f) *I did!* — emphatic *did*, stressed, contradicting the statement.

Answers such as *Yes, I've been* (wrong order of ellipsis), *No, it doesn't go* (too long but correct) or *Yes, I'd love* (missing *to*) show the typical problems.
:::
:::

## Other kinds of ellipsis

**In coordination.** When two clauses are joined by *and*, *but* or *or*, repeated words can go from the second:

- shared subject and auxiliary: *She **was** born in Lanzhou and __ grew up in Xi'an.* *We **can** walk or __ take a taxi.*
- a repeated verb (called **gapping**, formal and written): *Li Ming ordered noodles, and Wang Fang __ dumplings.*
- a shared end: *He was born in __, and still lives in, Hangzhou.* (rare and formal)

**After *wh*-words** (sometimes called **sluicing**): *Someone has taken my bike, but I don't know **who** __.* *She's moving — I'm not sure **where** __.* *I'll call you, but I don't know **when** __.*

**In comparisons**: *She is taller than **I am** / than **me** / than **I**.* After *than* and *as*, a short clause with its auxiliary (*than I am*) is safe in all styles; a bare object pronoun (*than me*) is normal in speech and most writing; a bare subject pronoun (*than I*) is formal and can sound stiff (Swan 2016).

**In fixed short conditions**: *if so*, *if not*, *if necessary*, *if possible*, *when ready*, *where possible* ([[complex-sentences/adverbial-clauses]]).

**In speech, at the start of a sentence.** Informal speech often drops a subject, auxiliary or article that the situation makes obvious: *__ Sounds good.* *__ Told you so.* *__ Want a coffee?* *__ Anybody seen my keys?* *See you later.* This **situational ellipsis** is natural in conversation and in quick messages, but in formal writing it reads as careless.

::: example Recovering what is missing {#ex-recover}
Write out in full what has been left out of each sentence, and name the kind of ellipsis. (a) *I'd like to help, but I can't.* (b) *Somebody has eaten my sandwich, and I know who.* (c) *Zhang Hui speaks Russian, and her husband Korean.* (d) *"Coming to lunch?" "In a minute."* (e) *He earns more than I do.*
::: solution
(a) *… but I can't **help**.* — verb phrase ellipsis after a modal.

(b) *… and I know who **has eaten my sandwich**.* — ellipsis after a *wh*-word (sluicing).

(c) *… and her husband **speaks** Korean.* — gapping in coordination (formal and written).

(d) *"**Are you** coming to lunch?" "**I'll come** in a minute."* — situational ellipsis in informal speech.

(e) *He earns more than I **earn**.* — comparative ellipsis with *do* standing for the verb.

In each case the missing words can be recovered exactly from the context; that is what makes the ellipsis acceptable.
:::
:::

## Substitution: *one*, *so*, *do so*

::: theorem *One* and *ones* {#rule-one}
***One*** (plural ***ones***) replaces a **countable** noun (or a noun with its modifiers) when another member of the same class is meant:

- *I lost my umbrella, so I bought a new **one**.* (a new umbrella)
- *"Which shoes?" "The black **ones** on the left."*
- *This question is harder than the last **one**.*

*One* is **not** used:

1. for **uncountable** nouns: *I prefer white rice to brown __.* (✗ *brown one*) *Do you want fresh milk or long-life __?*
2. directly after a possessive or *a/an* without an adjective: *This is **mine**.* (✗ *my one* in standard writing, though *my one* occurs in informal British speech) *I need **one**.* (not ✗ *a one*)
3. for the **same** thing already mentioned: use ***it***. *I lost my umbrella, and then I found **it**.* (the same one) — *I lost my umbrella, so I bought a new **one**.* (a different one)
:::

::: proof
The rule is described by Quirk et al. (1985) and Swan (2016). *One* stands for a countable noun with or without its modifiers, so it can follow *the*, *this*, *which*, adjectives and numbers (*the one*, *this one*, *which one*, *two big ones*), but not an uncountable noun, which has no singular "one" to pick out. *It* is a personal pronoun and refers to the same entity; *one* refers to another entity of the same kind. The contrast exists in Chinese, too — 我找到了 versus 我买了一把新的 — but Chinese often uses no word at all for the object, while English must choose.
:::

::: theorem *So* and *not* for a clause {#rule-so-not}
After verbs of thinking and saying — *think*, *believe*, *suppose*, *expect*, *hope*, *imagine*, *guess* (mainly AmE), *be afraid*, *say*, *tell* — ***so*** replaces a positive *that*-clause and ***not*** a negative one:

| positive | negative (two forms) |
|---|---|
| *I think **so**.* | *I **don't** think **so**.* (usual) / *I think **not**.* (formal) |
| *I expect **so**.* | *I **don't** expect **so**.* / *I expect **not**.* |
| *I suppose **so**.* | *I **don't** suppose **so**.* / *I suppose **not**.* |
| *I hope **so**.* | *I hope **not**.* (only this form: ✗ *I don't hope so*) |
| *I'm afraid **so**.* | *I'm afraid **not**.* (only this form) |

*So* is **not** used after *know* or *be sure*: *"Is it true?" "I know."* / *"I'm sure (it is)."* (✗ *I know so*, ✗ *I'm sure so*).
:::

::: proof
Swan (2016) and Quirk et al. (1985) list the verbs and the two negative patterns. With *think*, *believe*, *expect*, *suppose* and *imagine*, English usually puts the negative on the main verb (*I don't think so*, *I don't think she's coming*) rather than in the clause — a phenomenon called **negative transfer** or **transferred negation**. *Hope* and *be afraid* do not allow this: *I don't hope she's coming* means something different (I do not have that hope), so the negative must stay in the substitute: *I hope not*. *So* also appears at the front in some fixed responses: *So I hear.* *So I believe.* (= I have heard / I believe that this is true.)
:::

::: quiz
*"Will the shop be open on Sunday?"* You think it won't be, and you'd be sorry if it isn't. Which answer is correct?
- [ ] *I don't hope so.*
- [x] *I'm afraid not.*
- [ ] *I'm afraid don't.*
- [ ] *I don't afraid so.*
::: solution
*Be afraid* takes *not* for a negative clause and expresses regret: *I'm afraid not* = I'm sorry, but I think it won't be open. *Hope* also takes only *not* (*I hope not*), but that would mean you want the shop to be closed.

### *Do so*, *do it*, *do that*

To replace a verb phrase with an action verb, English has several forms:

- ***do so*** — formal, common in academic and official writing: *Students who wish to change courses must **do so** before 1 October.*
- ***do it*** / ***do that*** — neutral, for a specific, deliberate action: *"Can you fix the printer?" "I'll **do it** after lunch."* *I can't believe you **did that**!*
- ***do the same*** — the same action by someone else: *She resigned, and her deputy **did the same**.*
- **ellipsis** (auxiliary only) — the most common and natural in conversation: *"Can you fix the printer?" "I **will** after lunch."*

*Do so*, *do it* and *do that* need a verb of **action**: ✗ *She knows the answer, and I do so too* (*know* is a state) ✓ *… and so do I* / *… and I do too*.

::: example *One*, *it*, *so* or *do so*? {#ex-substitute}
Fill each gap with *one*, *ones*, *it*, *so*, *not* or *do so*. (a) *These glasses are dirty. Can I have some clean ___?* (b) *"Is the museum free?" "I think ___, but check the website."* (c) *I bought a lamp last week, but ___ doesn't work.* (d) *Applicants who have not yet registered should ___ immediately.* (e) *"Will it be expensive?" "I hope ___!"* (f) *"Which cake would you like?" "The ___ with the strawberries."*
::: solution
(a) *ones* — another set of the same kind (plural countable).

(b) *so* — replaces *that it is free*.

(c) *it* — the same lamp.

(d) *do so* — formal; replaces *register*.

(e) *not* — a negative wish: *I hope that it won't be expensive*. (✗ *I don't hope so*.)

(f) *one* — another member of the class *cake*.
:::
:::

### *That of* and *those of*

In formal comparisons, ***that of*** (singular) and ***those of*** (plural) replace a noun that has been mentioned, so that like is compared with like:

- *The climate of Kunming is milder than **that of** Beijing.* (= the climate of Beijing)
- *Our prices are lower than **those of** our competitors.*

✗ *The climate of Kunming is milder than Beijing* compares a climate with a city. In informal English the possessive does the same job: *Kunming's climate is milder than **Beijing's***.

## Responses: *So do I*, *Neither can she*

To say that the same is true of another subject, English uses an inverted response with the auxiliary:

- positive: ***So** + auxiliary + subject*: *"I'm hungry." "**So am I**."* *"Tom likes jazz." "**So does** Mei."*
- negative: ***Neither*** / ***Nor*** + auxiliary + subject: *"I can't swim." "**Neither can I**."* *"We didn't see it." "**Nor did** we."*
- informal alternatives: *Me too.* / *Me neither.* (AmE also *Me either.*); *I am too.* / *I can't either.*

The auxiliary must match the first statement: *"I've been to Paris." "So **have** I."* (✗ *So do I*) *"I went to Paris." "So **did** I."*

A different pattern, ***So*** + subject + auxiliary (without inversion), agrees with a surprising statement about the same subject: *"You've got paint on your face." "**So I have**!"* (= Yes, you're right.)

::: warning Answering negative questions
In Chinese, 对 or 是 agrees with the **statement** in the question, so 你不去吗？—— 对，我不去 means ‘Right, I'm not going.’ In English, *yes* and *no* follow the **fact**: *yes* goes with a positive answer and *no* with a negative one, whatever the form of the question.

✗ *"Aren't you coming?" "Yes, I'm not."* ✓ *"No, I'm not."* (= 对，我不去)

✗ *"Didn't you lock the door?" "No, I did."* ✓ *"Yes, I did."* (= 不，我锁了)

✗ *"You don't like coffee?" "Yes."* (unclear) ✓ *"No, I don't."* or *"Actually, I do."*

Adding the short answer with its auxiliary (*No, I'm not*; *Yes, I did*) makes the meaning clear even if the *yes* or *no* is wrong.
:::

::: example Editing a repetitive paragraph {#ex-edit}
Reduce the repetition in this paragraph using ellipsis and substitution, without making it unclear. *My sister wanted a new phone, so she bought a new phone last week. Her old phone was three years old, and her old phone's battery didn't last a day. I told her that I didn't think that the new phone was worth the price. She said that she thought that the new phone was worth the price. I haven't bought a new phone myself, but I may buy a new phone soon.*
::: solution
1. Replace repeated countable nouns with *one*, or with *it* for the same phone.
2. Replace repeated *that*-clauses after *think* with *so*.
3. Reduce repeated verb phrases to the auxiliary.

*My sister wanted a new phone, so she bought **one** last week. Her old **one** was three years old, and **its** battery didn't last a day. I told her I didn't think **it** was worth the price, but she said she thought **so**. I haven't bought a new phone myself, but I **may** __ soon.*

Note *it* (not *one*) for the new phone in the fourth sentence, because it is the same phone; *she thought so* for *she thought that it was worth the price*; and *I may* for *I may buy a new phone*. The first *a new phone* in the last sentence is kept, because a new referent (my phone) is being introduced.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Zero objects.** Chinese often leaves out an object or subject that is clear from context — what Li and Thompson (1981) call zero anaphora: 你看了那部电影吗？看了。很好看。 English must normally keep both: *"Have you seen that film?" "Yes, I have. It's very good."* ✗ *Yes, I saw. Very good.* is understandable but sounds like a note, not a reply.
- **Answering with the verb or with the auxiliary.** Chinese answers yes/no questions by repeating the verb (去了, 会, 是): English answers with the auxiliary (*I did*, *I can*, *I am*), adding *yes* or *no* before it. Practising short answers until they are automatic is one of the most useful things a learner can do for spoken English.
- **我也是.** 我也是 can be *So am I*, *So do I*, *So have I*, *So can I* or *Me too*, depending on the auxiliary of the first sentence; ✗ *I also am* or ✗ *So am I* after *I like jazz* are common errors. In informal speech *Me too* avoids the choice.
- **我想是吧, 不会吧.** These are *I think so* and *I don't think so* (or *I hope not*); ✗ *I think yes*, ✗ *I think it* are word-for-word versions.
- **One.** Chinese 的 after an adjective (红的, 新的) does the job of English *one*: 我要红的 — *I'll have the red one.* But 的 is also used with uncountable nouns (我要热的, of water or tea), where English cannot use *one*: *I'd like it hot*, or simply *Hot, please*.
:::

::: history
Ellipsis and substitution were brought into the study of whole texts by M. A. K. Halliday and Ruqaiya Hasan's *Cohesion in English* (1976), which treated them, with reference and conjunction, as the main ways in which sentences are tied together into a text; their categories are still used in teaching cohesion ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]]). Grammarians had long described the elliptical sentence, but in 1969 John Robert Ross gave the name **sluicing** to the ellipsis after *wh*-words (*Someone called, but I don't know who*), and Jason Merchant's *The Syntax of Silence* (2001) argued that the missing clause is really present in the structure and simply not pronounced. For Chinese, Li and Thompson's grammar (1981) described how freely Mandarin omits subjects and objects that are clear from context — the habit that makes English ellipsis, which is limited to particular places, difficult for Chinese speakers.
:::

## Where this leads

Ellipsis and substitution are part of **cohesion** — the ties that make sentences into a text — which is taught in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]] and [[style-and-rhetoric/cohesion-emphasis]]. Short answers and tags in conversation are practised in [[listening-and-conversation/turn-taking]]; reading pronouns and substitutes in long texts in [[reading-skills/following-the-thread]]. Avoiding wordiness in writing is the subject of [[style-and-rhetoric/concision]].

::: summary
- **Ellipsis** leaves out words that can be recovered; **substitution** replaces them with *one*, *so*, *do so* and similar words ([[#def-ellipsis]]).
- To avoid repeating a verb phrase, keep the **first auxiliary** (or *do/does/did*); don't contract it at the end: *Yes, I am* ([[#rule-vp-ellipsis]]).
- Keep ***to*** for a repeated infinitive: *I'd love to*, *I didn't mean to*.
- ***One/ones*** replaces a countable noun of the same kind; use ***it*** for the same thing; nothing for uncountable nouns ([[#rule-one]]).
- ***So*** and ***not*** replace a clause after *think*, *hope*, *expect*, *be afraid*: *I think so*, *I don't think so*, *I hope not*, *I'm afraid not* ([[#rule-so-not]]).
- *Do so* is formal; *do it* and *do that* are neutral; all need an action verb.
- *So do I*, *Neither can she*: the auxiliary must match the first statement.
- *Yes* and *no* follow the fact, not the form of the question: *"Aren't you coming?" "No, I'm not."*
:::

::: exercise Make the dialogue natural {level=3}
Rewrite this dialogue so that it sounds natural, using ellipsis and substitution. **Kenji:** *Are you going to the conference in March?* **Hana:** *Yes, I am going to the conference in March. Are you going to the conference in March?* **Kenji:** *I want to go to the conference, but I don't know if I can go to the conference. My boss hasn't decided if I can go to the conference.* **Hana:** *Do you think your boss will let you go?* **Kenji:** *I hope that my boss will let me go. Last year my boss didn't let me go.* **Hana:** *I went last year. The conference was good, but the hotel was bad. This year I've booked a better hotel.*
::: solution
A model:

**Kenji:** *Are you going to the conference in March?*
**Hana:** *Yes, I am. Are you?*
**Kenji:** *I want to, but I don't know if I can. My boss hasn't decided yet.*
**Hana:** *Do you think she'll let you?*
**Kenji:** *I hope so. She didn't last year.*
**Hana:** *I went last year. The conference was good, but the hotel wasn't. This year I've booked a better one.*

Check: *I am* (auxiliary), *Are you?* (auxiliary), *I want to* (infinitive to *to*), *if I can* (modal), *hasn't decided* (clausal ellipsis: *if I can go*), *let you* (verb phrase ellipsis after *let*), *I hope so* (clause), *She didn't* (auxiliary *did*), *wasn't* (auxiliary), *a better one* (countable noun). The boss's gender is invented; *he* or *they* would do equally well.
