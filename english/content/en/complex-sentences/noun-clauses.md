Four sentences from emails written by learners, each easy to understand and each not quite English:

- ✗ *Could you tell me where is the registration office?*
- ✗ *Is very important that every student attends the first meeting.*
- ✗ *I am not sure if or not the room is free.*
- ✗ *We discussed about that the price was too high.*

All four contain a clause that does the job of a noun: it is the object of *tell*, the subject of *is important*, the complement of *sure*, the complement of a preposition. English has strict rules for how such clauses are introduced, what word order they have, and where they can go — and the rules are different from those of Chinese, where 我不知道他住在哪儿 and 他住在哪儿 have the same order, and 很重要 needs no subject.

This lesson covers the four kinds of finite noun clause — *that*-clauses, *wh*-clauses, *whether*/*if*-clauses and the *what*-clauses that mean ‘the thing that’ — and the use of *it* as a stand-in subject or object. Non-finite noun clauses (*I want **to leave***, *I enjoy **swimming***) were the subject of [[verb-patterns/ing-or-infinitive]] and [[verb-patterns/object-and-verb]], and they appear here only for comparison.

## What a noun clause is

::: definition Noun clause {#def-noun-clause}
A **noun clause** (also **nominal clause**; Huddleston and Pullum's **content clause** covers most of them) is a subordinate clause that does the job of a noun phrase. It can be:

- the **subject**: ***What he said** was true.*
- the **object**: *I know **that she's right**.*
- the **complement** of *be*: *The problem is **that we have no money**.*
- the complement of an **adjective**: *I'm sure **(that) she'll come**.*
- the complement of a **noun**: *the fact **that he lied***
- the complement of a **preposition**: *It depends on **whether it rains**.*

The replacement test from [[complex-sentences/clause-structure#rule-functions]] identifies it: a noun clause can usually be replaced by *it*, *that* or *something*.
:::

There are four main kinds, according to the word that introduces them:

| kind | introduced by | meaning | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *that*-clause | *that* (often left out) | a statement or idea | *I think **(that) it's going to rain**.* |
| *wh*-interrogative clause | *who*, *what*, *which*, *when*, *where*, *why*, *how* | an indirect question for information | *I don't know **where she lives**.* |
| *whether*/*if*-clause | *whether*, *if* | an indirect yes/no question | *I wonder **whether she'll come**.* |
| nominal relative clause | *what*, *whatever*, *whoever*, *where* … | ‘the thing (person, place) that …’ | *I'll do **what I can**.* |

## *That*-clauses

::: theorem Where *that*-clauses go {#rule-that}
A *that*-clause can be:

1. the object of verbs of saying, thinking, knowing and feeling: *say*, *tell (someone)*, *explain*, *think*, *believe*, *know*, *realise*, *hope*, *feel*, *agree*, *suggest*, *notice*. *She **realised** that she had left her keys at home.*
2. the complement of *be*: *The trouble **is** that nobody told us.*
3. the complement of adjectives of certainty and feeling: *sure*, *certain*, *aware*, *afraid*, *glad*, *sorry*, *surprised*, *disappointed*. *I'm **afraid** that we're fully booked.*
4. the complement of nouns such as *fact*, *idea*, *belief*, *news*, *hope*, *claim*, *possibility*, *rumour*, *suggestion*, *evidence*: *the **news** that the bridge was closed*.
5. the subject, but in practice it is usually moved to the end with *it* in front ([[#rule-it]]).

A *that*-clause **cannot** follow a preposition (see [[#rule-preposition]]).
:::

::: theorem Leaving out *that* {#rule-that-omission}
*That* can be left out:

- after common verbs of saying and thinking, especially *think*, *say*, *know*, *suppose*, *hope*, *guess*: *I think __ you're right.* *She said __ she'd be late.*
- after adjectives of feeling and certainty: *I'm sure __ it'll be fine.* *I'm glad __ you came.*

*That* is usually kept:

- when the clause is the **subject**: ***That** he was guilty was clear to everyone.* (✗ *He was guilty was clear*)
- after **nouns**: *the fact **that** he was guilty*, *the idea **that** we could win*
- after less common or formal verbs: *The report **states that** …*, *She **argued that** …*
- when words come between the verb and the clause: *He told me **on Friday that** he was leaving.*
- in formal writing generally.
:::

::: proof
Biber et al. (1999) found that *that* is omitted in the great majority of *that*-clauses in conversation, and kept in most of them in academic prose; omission is most frequent after the commonest verbs (*think* and *say*) and when the subject of the clause is a short pronoun (*I think __ **it**'s…*). Swan (2016) and Quirk et al. (1985) give the same conditions. The reason for keeping *that* in the other positions is processing: at the start of a sentence, after a noun, or after a gap, the reader needs a signal that a new clause has begun. Without *that*, *He told me on Friday he was leaving* could be read as *He told me (something) on Friday* + a new sentence.
:::

::: warning *That* and *what*
✗ *I think what he is right.* ✓ *I think (that) he is right.*

✗ *What I know is what he is right.* ✓ *What I know is **that** he is right.*

*That* introduces a statement and has no meaning of its own. *What* means ‘the thing that’ (or asks a question), and it fills a gap inside its clause: in *I know **what** he wants __*, *what* is the object of *wants*. In *he is right* nothing is missing, so *what* cannot be used.
:::

### Noun + *that*: content or relative?

After a noun, *that* can introduce two different kinds of clause.

- *The news **that the bridge was closed** surprised us.* — a noun clause: it gives the **content** of the news. The news = *the bridge was closed*. The clause is complete; *that* has no job inside it and cannot be replaced by *which*.
- *The news **that we heard __ on the radio** surprised us.* — a relative clause: *that* is the object of *heard*, there is a gap, and *which* can replace it.

Only some nouns can take a content clause: nouns of saying, thinking and fact, as listed in [[#rule-that]]. ✗ *the book that the bridge was closed* is impossible, because a book does not have content in this sense.

## *It* as a stand-in

::: theorem Anticipatory *it* {#rule-it}
1. **Subject.** A *that*-clause, *to*-infinitive clause or *wh*-clause as subject is usually moved to the end of the sentence, and its place is taken by ***it***: *It's clear **that she's upset**.* (rather than *That she's upset is clear.*) *It doesn't matter **what you wear**.* *It's important **to arrive early**.* This is called **extraposition**.
2. **Object.** After *find*, *make*, *think*, *consider* and *take* + an adjective or noun, the clause must be moved to the end, with *it* as object: *I find **it** strange **that nobody called**.* *She made **it** clear **that she disagreed**.* (✗ *She made clear that…* is common, but grammars and editors prefer *made it clear*; ✗ *I find strange that…*)
3. **Seem, appear, happen.** These verbs take *it* + *that*: *It seems **that** the train is late.* (or *The train seems to be late*.)

English needs this *it* because every finite clause needs a subject ([[core-grammar/sentence-patterns#rule-subject]]). ✗ *Is important that…* ✓ ***It** is important that…*
:::

::: proof
Quirk et al. (1985) and Huddleston and Pullum (2002) both describe extraposition as the normal order: a clause in subject position is heavy, and English places heavy material at the end (the principle of end-weight in [[complex-sentences/clause-structure#rule-main-visible]]). Biber et al. (1999) found extraposed *that*-clauses far more frequent than subject *that*-clauses in every register; a *that*-clause in subject position is limited to formal writing, where it can link to the previous sentence (*That the policy failed is now widely accepted.*). The dummy *it* has no meaning: it only fills the subject slot. It should not be confused with *there*, which introduces the existence of something (*There is a problem*), nor with the *it* of cleft sentences (*It was Mei who called*, [[complex-sentences/emphasis-inversion]]).
:::

::: example Moving the clause to the end {#ex-extraposition}
Rewrite each sentence with anticipatory *it*. (a) *That the meeting was cancelled is a pity.* (b) *To finish the report by Friday will be difficult.* (c) *Whether we win or lose doesn't matter.* (d) *Nobody had told us. We thought this was strange.* (e) *Learning a language takes time. Everyone knows this.*
::: solution
(a) *It's a pity (that) the meeting was cancelled.* — once the clause is at the end, *that* can even be left out in informal style.

(b) *It will be difficult to finish the report by Friday.*

(c) *It doesn't matter whether we win or lose.*

(d) *We thought **it** strange that nobody had told us.* (formal) / *We thought **it was** strange that nobody had told us.* (neutral). With *find*: *We found it strange that nobody had told us.*

(e) *It is well known that learning a language takes time.* / *Everyone knows (that) learning a language takes time.* — (e) shows that *it* + passive (*It is known that*) is another use of anticipatory *it*, common in academic writing ([[verb-patterns/the-passive]]).
:::
:::

## Indirect questions: *wh*-clauses and *whether*/*if*

::: theorem Word order in indirect questions {#rule-word-order}
A question inside another sentence — an **indirect** or **embedded** question — has the word order of a **statement**: subject before verb, and no *do*, *does* or *did*.

| direct question | indirect question |
|---|---|
| *Where **is the station**?* | *Can you tell me where **the station is**?* |
| *What **does she want**?* | *I don't know what **she wants**.* |
| *Why **did they leave**?* | *Nobody knows why **they left**.* |
| *How much **does it cost**?* | *Do you know how much **it costs**?* |
| *Who **called**?* (the *wh*-word is the subject) | *I asked who **called**.* (no change) |

The question mark at the end depends on the **main** clause: *Do you know where she lives?* (a question) — *I don't know where she lives.* (a statement)
:::

::: proof
In a direct question, English puts an auxiliary before the subject (inversion), and uses *do* if there is no other auxiliary. Inversion is a mark of the main clause in English; subordinate clauses keep the basic subject–verb order (Quirk et al. 1985; Swan 2016). The exception in the table is regular: when the *wh*-word itself is the subject, a direct question has no inversion either (*Who called?*), so nothing changes. In some varieties of English, notably Irish English and Indian English, and in informal speech generally, inverted order in embedded questions does occur (*I asked him where was he going*), and it is systematic in those varieties; but in standard written English and in exams the statement order is expected.
:::

::: warning Polite questions with *Could you tell me…*
✗ *Could you tell me what time does the museum open?* ✓ *Could you tell me what time **the museum opens**?*

✗ *Do you know where is the toilet?* ✓ *Do you know where **the toilet is**?*

✗ *I wonder why did she say that.* ✓ *I wonder why **she said** that.*

These indirect questions are among the most common polite forms in English ([[pragmatics/requests-refusals]]), so the error is very noticeable. The question is already in *Could you tell me* or *Do you know*; the rest is a statement.
:::

::: theorem *Whether* or *if*? {#rule-whether}
Indirect yes/no questions use *whether* or *if*. After verbs such as *ask*, *know*, *wonder*, *see*, *find out* and *check*, both are possible; *if* is more common in speech, *whether* in formal writing: *I asked **if / whether** the shop was open.*

Use **only *whether***:

1. when the clause is the **subject** or comes before the main clause: ***Whether** the plan will work is unclear.*
2. after a **preposition**: *It depends **on whether** it rains.* *There was a debate **about whether** to continue.*
3. before a ***to*-infinitive**: *I can't decide **whether to go**.*
4. directly before ***or not***: *I don't know **whether or not** she'll come.* (*I don't know if she'll come or not* is fine, with *or not* at the end.)
5. as the complement of *be* or of a noun, in careful writing: *The question is **whether** we can afford it.* *the question (of) **whether** …*

*If* is also avoided where it could be read as a condition: *Let me know **if** you can come* can mean ‘tell me whether you can come’ or ‘tell me, in case you can come’; *whether* has only the first meaning.
:::

::: proof
Swan (2016) and Quirk et al. (1985) list these restrictions in nearly the same terms. They follow from the fact that *if* is primarily a conditional conjunction: in positions where a conditional clause cannot stand (as subject, after a preposition, before an infinitive), interrogative *if* cannot stand either. Huddleston and Pullum (2002) note that *if* is acceptable in some of these positions in informal speech (*The question is if…*), but *whether* is always safe.
:::

::: example *Whether* only, or both? {#ex-whether}
Fill in *whether* or *if*. Where both are possible, say so. (a) *I'm not sure ___ I locked the door.* (b) *___ we go or not depends on the weather.* (c) *She asked me ___ to bring anything.* (d) *They argued about ___ the results were reliable.* (e) *Ask the driver ___ this bus goes to the airport.* (f) *I don't know ___ or not he got my message.*
::: solution
(a) both — *if* is more informal.

(b) *Whether* — the clause is the subject.

(c) *whether* — before a *to*-infinitive.

(d) *whether* — after a preposition.

(e) both.

(f) *whether* — directly before *or not*. (With the order *I don't know if he got my message or not*, *if* is possible.)
:::
:::

## *What*-clauses and *-ever* clauses

::: definition Nominal relative clause {#def-nominal-relative}
A **nominal relative clause** (Huddleston and Pullum call it a **fused relative**) is a clause introduced by *what*, *whatever*, *whoever*, *whichever*, *where*, *when* or *how*, in which the antecedent and the relative word are fused into one: *what* = ‘the thing(s) that’; *whoever* = ‘any person who’; *where* = ‘the place where’.

- ***What you need** is a holiday.* (= The thing that you need …)
- *I'll eat **whatever you cook**.* (= anything that you cook)
- ***Whoever finishes first** gets a prize.*
- *This is **where I work**.*
:::

A nominal relative clause looks like an indirect question but means something different:

- *I asked **what she wanted**.* — an indirect question: ‘I asked: "What do you want?"’
- *I gave her **what she wanted**.* — a nominal relative: ‘I gave her the thing that she wanted.’

The difference matters for translation: the first is 我问她想要什么, the second 我给了她她想要的东西.

::: example Question or ‘the thing that’? {#ex-what}
Decide whether each clause in bold is an indirect question or a nominal relative, and translate the sentence into Chinese. (a) *I don't understand **what he means**.* (b) ***What he means** is that we should wait.* (c) *She asked **who was responsible**.* (d) ***Whoever is responsible** will have to pay.* (e) *Tell me **where you bought it**.* (f) *I'll put it back **where I found it**.*
::: solution
Ask whether the sentence is about an unknown answer (a question) or about a thing, person or place (a nominal relative). Paraphrase with ‘the thing that …’ to test.

(a) Either reading is possible, and they come to the same thing: ‘I don't understand the answer to "What does he mean?"’ — 我不明白他是什么意思.

(b) Nominal relative: ‘the thing that he means’ — 他的意思是我们应该等一等.

(c) Indirect question: she asked "Who is responsible?" — 她问谁负责.

(d) Nominal relative with *-ever*: ‘any person who is responsible’ — 不管是谁负责，都得赔.

(e) Indirect question — 告诉我你在哪儿买的.

(f) Nominal relative: ‘the place where I found it’ — 我会把它放回原处.

Verbs of asking and not knowing (*ask*, *wonder*, *not know*, *tell me*) take indirect questions; verbs of giving, doing and placing take nominal relatives.
:::
:::

::: quiz
In which sentence is the clause in bold a **nominal relative** (‘the thing that’), not an indirect question?
- [ ] *She wondered **what the letter said**.*
- [x] *She read out **what the letter said**.*
- [ ] *Nobody could tell her **what the letter said**.*
- [ ] *She asked **what the letter said**.*

One reads out a thing — ‘the words that the letter said’ — so the clause is a nominal relative. *Wonder*, *tell her* and *ask* concern the answer to a question: ‘What did the letter say?’

The *-ever* words also begin adverbial clauses of concession (***Whatever** you do, don't panic* — ‘it doesn't matter what you do’), which are covered in [[complex-sentences/adverbial-clauses]].

## Noun clauses after prepositions

::: theorem No preposition before *that* {#rule-preposition}
A preposition can be followed by a *wh*-clause, a *whether*-clause or an *-ing* clause, but **not** by a *that*-clause.

| before a noun or *wh*/*-ing* | before a *that*-clause: drop the preposition |
|---|---|
| *I'm aware **of** the risk.* | *I'm aware **that** it's risky.* |
| *She insisted **on** paying.* | *She insisted **that** she pay.* |
| *We were surprised **at** his reaction.* | *We were surprised **that** he reacted so strongly.* |
| *It depends **on** whether we have time.* | — |
| *We talked **about** what to do.* | *We agreed **that** we would…* |

When a preposition must be kept, put *the fact* in front of the *that*-clause: *Despite **the fact that** it rained, …* (✗ *Despite that it rained*). A few fixed conjunctions end in a preposition + *that*: *in that* (formal, ‘because’), *except that*, *now that*, *given that*, *in order that*.
:::

::: proof
Quirk et al. (1985) and Huddleston and Pullum (2002) both note that prepositions in English do not take *that*-clauses as complements (with the few fixed exceptions listed), and that the preposition of verbs and adjectives such as *insist on*, *aware of* and *afraid of* simply disappears before a *that*-clause. The learner error ✗ *We discussed about that…* combines two problems: *discuss* takes no preposition ([[verb-patterns/prepositional-verbs]]), and *about* cannot be followed by *that*.
:::

::: example Correcting noun clauses in an email {#ex-email}
Correct the mistakes. *Dear Mr Haddad, I am writing to ask if or not there are still places on the summer course. I would also like to know how long does the course last and what is the fee. Is important for me that the course includes speaking practice, because I am worried about that my speaking is weak. Could you also tell me that whether accommodation is provided?*
::: solution
1. *if or not there are* → ***whether** there are* (or *if there are … or not*). *Whether* is needed directly before *or not*.
2. *how long does the course last* → *how long **the course lasts*** — statement order.
3. *what is the fee* → *what **the fee is*** — statement order.
4. *Is important* → ***It is** important* — anticipatory *it*.
5. *worried about that my speaking is weak* → *worried **that** my speaking is weak* (drop the preposition), or *worried **about** my speaking*.
6. *tell me that whether* → *tell me **whether*** — one opener only.

Corrected: *Dear Mr Haddad, I am writing to ask whether there are still places on the summer course. I would also like to know how long the course lasts and what the fee is. It is important for me that the course includes speaking practice, because I am worried that my speaking is weak. Could you also tell me whether accommodation is provided?*
:::

## Verbs in *that*-clauses: three special cases

1. **After verbs and adjectives of proposal and necessity** — *suggest*, *recommend*, *insist*, *demand*, *propose*, *essential*, *important*, *vital* — the verb in the *that*-clause can be a base form (the subjunctive), *should* + base form, or an ordinary tense: *It's essential that he **be** / **should be** / **is** told.* This is introduced in [[verb-patterns/object-and-verb#rule-that-clause]] and treated fully in [[complex-sentences/unreal-past]].
2. **After reporting verbs in the past**, the tense in the clause is often shifted back: *She said that she **was** tired.* This is the subject of [[complex-sentences/reported-speech]].
3. **After verbs of emotion and evaluation** — *It's strange that…*, *I'm surprised that…* — British English often uses ***should*** for an idea rather than a fact: *It's strange that he **should say** that.* The ordinary tense (*that he said that*) is also correct and is usual in American English (Swan 2016).

### Facts and beliefs

Some verbs take the truth of their *that*-clause for granted: *I **regret** that I told her*, *She **realised** that the door was open*, *He **knows** that she's right*. The speaker who uses them accepts that the clause is true. Others make no such commitment: *She **believes** that the door was open*, *He **claims** that she's right*. Linguists call the first kind **factive** verbs. The difference is important in reporting what others say ([[complex-sentences/reported-speech]]) and in academic writing, where *X **showed** that…* commits the writer and *X **claimed** that…* keeps a distance ([[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]]).

::: note English and Chinese
- **No word for *that*.** Chinese has no word that introduces a statement clause: 我知道他来了, 我觉得你说得对. That is one reason why Chinese speakers rarely have trouble leaving *that* out after *think* — but also why they leave it out where English needs it, after nouns and at the start of a sentence (✗ *The fact he lied…*, ✗ *He lied was clear*).
- **Questions keep their order in Chinese.** In Chinese, a question word stays in the position of the answer (你住在哪儿? — 我住在北京), and an embedded question has exactly the same form as a direct one: 我不知道他住在哪儿. English direct questions move the *wh*-word to the front and invert the auxiliary; embedded questions move the *wh*-word but do **not** invert. Learners who have practised *Where does he live?* carry the inversion into embedded questions, which Chinese gives no reason to expect. The cure is to treat the embedded question as a statement: *where he lives*.
- **Yes/no questions.** Chinese embedded yes/no questions use the A-not-A form or 是否: 我不知道他来不来, 不清楚他是否同意. Both correspond to *whether*: *I don't know **whether** he's coming*, *It's not clear **whether** he agrees.* ✗ *I don't know he comes or not comes.*
- **Subjectless sentences.** Chinese 很重要 or 看来他不会来了 need no subject. English needs a dummy *it*: *It's important…*, *It seems (that) he isn't coming.* Chinese 重要的是…… corresponds closely to *What is important is…* (a *wh*-cleft, [[complex-sentences/emphasis-inversion]]).
- **Topic first.** Chinese often puts a clause first as a topic: 他为什么走，我不知道. English can do this for emphasis (*Why he left, I don't know*), but the ordinary order puts the clause after the verb: *I don't know why he left.*
:::

::: history
The study of noun clauses — often called **complement clauses** in linguistics — grew rapidly in the 1960s. Peter Rosenbaum's *The Grammar of English Predicate Complement Constructions* (1967) was one of the first book-length studies of which English verbs take *that*-clauses, infinitives and *-ing* forms. In an influential paper titled "Fact" (1970), Paul and Carol Kiparsky showed that verbs which presuppose the truth of their complement (*regret*, *resent*, *be aware*) behave differently in grammar from verbs which do not (*believe*, *claim*, *suppose*), and introduced the term **factive**. The corpus counts of the *Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English* (1999) later showed how strongly the omission of *that* is tied to conversation and to a handful of very common verbs.
:::

The subjunctive after *suggest* and *essential* is in [[complex-sentences/unreal-past]]. *What*-clauses used for emphasis (*What I need is a rest*) are in [[complex-sentences/emphasis-inversion]]. ::: summary
- A noun clause does the job of a noun phrase: subject, object, complement of *be*, an adjective, a noun or a preposition ([[#def-noun-clause]]).
- *That* can be left out after common verbs and adjectives, especially in speech; keep it after nouns, at the start of a sentence and in formal writing ([[#rule-that-omission]]).
- Clauses as subject are usually moved to the end with **anticipatory *it***: *It's clear that…*; *I find it strange that…* ([[#rule-it]]).
- Indirect questions use **statement word order** and no *do*: *Can you tell me where the station is?* ([[#rule-word-order]])
- *Whether* and *if* both introduce indirect yes/no questions; only *whether* is used as subject, after a preposition, before a *to*-infinitive and before *or not* ([[#rule-whether]]).
- *What* = ‘the thing that’ (a nominal relative); *that* introduces a statement and has no meaning of its own ([[#def-nominal-relative]]).
- A preposition is never followed by a *that*-clause: drop the preposition, or use *the fact that* ([[#rule-preposition]]).
:::

::: exercise *That*, *what* or nothing? {level=1}
Fill each gap with *that* or *what*, or leave it empty if nothing is needed. Give all possibilities. (a) *I knew ___ she wouldn't come.* (b) *___ she said made everyone laugh.* (c) *The idea ___ we could finish in a day was unrealistic.* (d) *Show me ___ you've written.* (e) *___ he was innocent soon became clear.*
::: solution
(a) *that* or nothing. (b) *What*. (c) *that* (content clause after a noun; normally kept). (d) *what*. (e) *That* — a subject *that*-clause, which needs *that* (more natural: *It soon became clear that he was innocent*).
:::
:::

::: exercise Add *it* {level=1}
Add *it* where it is needed. (a) *Is a pity you can't come.* (b) *I find difficult to concentrate in the library.* (c) *Seems that the flight has been delayed.* (d) *She made clear that she was unhappy.* (e) *Doesn't matter who pays.*
::: solution
(a) ***It** is a pity …* (b) *I find **it** difficult …* (c) ***It** seems that …* (d) *She made **it** clear that …* (e) ***It** doesn't matter who pays.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Correct the sentences {level=2}
Correct the mistakes. One sentence is correct. (a) *We need to discuss about whether we should hire more staff.* (b) *She was proud of that her daughter had won.* (c) *I'm not sure what is the answer.* (d) *Whether the plan will succeed depends on the budget.* (e) *Everybody is talking about if the company will close.* (f) *I'm worried about that I might fail.*
::: solution
(a) *We need to discuss whether we should hire more staff.* (b) *She was proud that her daughter had won.* (or *proud of her daughter's win*) (c) *I'm not sure what the answer is.* (d) Correct. (e) *… talking about **whether** the company will close.* (f) *I'm worried **that** I might fail.* (or *worried about failing*)
:::

::: exercise Noun clause or relative clause? {level=2}
Is the *that*-clause a noun (content) clause or a relative clause? Test by trying *which*. (a) *The rumour that the factory would close spread quickly.* (b) *The rumour that she spread was untrue.* (c) *There is evidence that the drug works.* (d) *The evidence that the police found was destroyed.* (e) *He made the claim that he had been robbed.*
::: solution
(a) noun clause (the rumour = the factory would close; *which* impossible). (b) relative clause (*that* = object of *spread*; *which* possible). (c) noun clause. (d) relative clause. (e) noun clause.
:::
:::

::: exercise From Chinese {level=2}
Translate into English, using a noun clause. (a) 我不知道他明天来不来。 (b) 他说的话没人相信。 (c) 很明显，这个计划行不通。 (d) 问题是我们没有足够的时间。 (e) 请告诉我会议几点开始。
::: solution
(a) *I don't know whether / if he's coming tomorrow.* (b) *Nobody believed what he said.* (c) *It's clear (that) this plan won't work.* (d) *The problem is (that) we don't have enough time.* (e) *Please tell me what time the meeting starts.* / *Could you tell me what time the meeting starts?*
:::

::: exercise Improve the paragraph {level=3}
Rewrite this paragraph from a student report so that it is correct and reads well. Use at least one anticipatory *it*, one *whether*-clause and one *what*-clause. *Is well known people who sleep less get ill more often. But is not clear the reason is. Some researchers think is because of stress, others believe that sleep itself protects the body. My question is if or not students know this. I asked twenty students how many hours did they sleep. That they said surprised me.*
::: hint
Look for missing subjects, inverted embedded questions, *if* where only *whether* works, and *that* where *what* is needed.
:::
::: solution
A model:

*It is well known that people who sleep less get ill more often. However, it is not clear what the reason is. Some researchers think (that) it is because of stress; others believe that sleep itself protects the body. My question was whether students know this. I asked twenty students how many hours they slept, and what they told me surprised me.*

Changes: anticipatory *it* (twice); *what the reason is* (statement order, *what* not *the*); *think it is* (subject added); *whether* for *if or not* (complement of *be*, and before *or not*); *how many hours they slept* (statement order); *what they told me* for *That they said* (*what* = the thing that).
:::
:::

::: exercise Write a polite enquiry {level=3}
You want to join a weekend photography course. Write a short email (60–90 words) to the organiser asking five questions — about dates, price, equipment, level and certificate — all as indirect questions or noun clauses, with no direct questions.
::: solution
A model:

*Dear Ms Petrova,*

*I saw your advertisement for the weekend photography course and I would like to know **when the next course starts**. Could you also tell me **how much it costs** and **whether I need to bring my own camera**? I am a beginner, so I am not sure **if the course is suitable for my level**. Finally, I wonder **whether participants receive a certificate**.*

*I look forward to hearing from you.*

*Best regards,*
*Chen Hao*

Check every embedded question for statement order (*when the next course starts*, not *when does the next course start*) and for *whether* where *if* is not possible.
:::
