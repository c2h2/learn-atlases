Here is a sentence from a school newsletter, written for this lesson:

> *Parents who have not yet returned the form that was sent home last week should do so by Friday, because the trip cannot go ahead unless we know how many children are coming.*

It has thirty-two words and seven verbs: *have returned*, *was sent*, *should do*, *cannot go*, *know*, *are coming* and the *return* hidden in *have not yet returned*. Only one of them, *should do*, belongs to the main statement: *Parents … should do so by Friday.* Every other verb sits in a clause that hangs on something else: on *Parents*, on *the form*, on the whole main clause, on *know*. A reader who sees this can take the sentence in at one reading. A reader who does not has to guess, and a writer who does not will produce sentences that look like this one but fall apart: ✗ *Parents have not returned the form should do so by Friday.*

At level 3 most of the difficulty of English is no longer in single words or single verb forms but in the way clauses are put inside other clauses. This first lesson gives the vocabulary and the method for the rest of the course: what a clause is, the difference between main and subordinate clauses and between finite and non-finite ones, the three jobs a subordinate clause can do, and how clauses are layered inside each other. The later lessons then take each kind of clause in turn. The seven basic clause patterns (SV, SVO, SVC and so on) are taken as known from [[core-grammar/sentence-patterns]], and the punctuation of simple and joined clauses from [[sentences-and-paragraphs/complete-sentences]] and [[sentences-and-paragraphs/punctuation]].

## Clauses

::: definition Clause {#def-clause}
A **clause** is a unit of grammar built around one verb phrase. A typical clause has a **subject** and a **predicate** (the verb with its objects, complements and adverbials): *the trip | cannot go ahead*. Each verb phrase is the centre of its own clause, so a sentence with four verb phrases contains four clauses, one inside or beside another.

A **sentence**, in writing, is what stands between a capital letter and a full stop (or question or exclamation mark). It contains at least one clause.
:::

The verb phrase can be several words long — *should have been told* is one verb phrase — so the rule is "one verb phrase, one clause", not "one verb, one clause". *Have not yet returned* is one verb phrase with *not yet* inside it.

::: definition Main clause and subordinate clause {#def-main-sub}
A **main clause** (also called an **independent clause**) is a clause that is not part of another clause. It can stand alone as a sentence.

A **subordinate clause** (also called a **dependent clause**) is a clause that does a job inside another clause — as its subject or object, as a modifier of one of its nouns, or as an adverbial. The clause it belongs to is its **superordinate clause** (or, more loosely, the **matrix** clause).
:::

In the newsletter sentence, *because the trip cannot go ahead …* is subordinate: it gives the reason for the main statement and cannot stand alone (✗ *Because the trip cannot go ahead.* is a fragment, [[sentences-and-paragraphs/complete-sentences]]). Inside it, *unless we know …* is subordinate to *the trip cannot go ahead*, and inside that, *how many children are coming* is the object of *know*. One clause can be main from the point of view of a clause below it and subordinate from the point of view of a clause above it. This layering is called **embedding**, and it is what makes a sentence complex.

Grammars differ on one point of terms. Quirk et al. (1985) treat a subordinate clause as part of the clause it belongs to: in *I know that she left*, the main clause is the whole sentence, and *that she left* is its object, as *the answer* is in *I know the answer*. School books usually call only *I know* the main clause. The first view is more exact, since *know* needs its object; this course uses it when it matters.

## Finite and non-finite clauses

::: definition Finite, non-finite and verbless clauses {#def-finite}
A **finite clause** has a verb phrase whose first verb shows **tense** (present or past: *returns*, *returned*, *is coming*, *was sent*) or is a **modal** (*should*, *can*, *will*). Imperatives (*Return the form.*) and the subjunctive (*that he return the form*) are also counted as finite. A finite clause normally has a subject.

A **non-finite clause** has a verb phrase in one of four forms that show no tense:

| form | example of the clause |
|---|---|
| *to*-infinitive | *We asked them **to return the form**.* |
| bare infinitive | *We made them **return the form**.* |
| *-ing* form | ***Returning the form late**, she apologised.* |
| *-ed* participle | *The form, **returned by Friday**, will be checked.* |

A **verbless clause** has no verb at all but works like a reduced clause: *Although **tired**, she kept going.* *If **in doubt**, ask.* *She left the room, **her face pale**.*
:::

::: theorem Where finite and non-finite clauses occur {#rule-finite}
1. A main clause in standard written English contains a finite verb. ✗ *My brother a doctor.* ✓ *My brother is a doctor.* ✗ *The students to arrive at nine.* ✓ *The students will arrive at nine.*
2. Non-finite and verbless clauses are always subordinate.
3. A non-finite clause usually has **no subject of its own**. Its subject is understood, normally from the superordinate clause: in *She stopped **to rest***, the one who rests is *she*. When it has one, it is marked: *for* + noun before a *to*-infinitive (*It's important **for the children** to rest*), or a noun before an *-ing* or *-ed* form (***The rain having stopped**, we went out*).
4. Non-finite clauses carry no tense of their own. Their time is read from the superordinate clause: *the man **sitting** by the door* can be now or in the past, depending on the main verb.
:::

::: proof
Quirk et al. (1985) and Huddleston and Pullum (2002) both draw the line between finite and non-finite clauses in this way, by the form of the first verb, and both note that non-finite clauses are compact because they leave out what can be recovered from the context: the subject and the tense. That is their value in writing — *Arriving late, she…* is shorter than *When she arrived late, she…* — and also their danger, because the reader must find the missing subject in the right place. If the wrong noun is closest, the result is the "dangling" participle of [[complex-sentences/participle-infinitive-clauses]]. Rule 1 is a rule of standard writing; informal speech often has verbless sentences (*Nice day.* *Coffee?*), which are discussed under ellipsis in [[complex-sentences/ellipsis-substitution]]. Headlines (*Minister to visit Kenya*) and notes also leave out finite verbs, by the conventions of those genres.
:::

The finite verb is the anchor of a clause, and in English it is easy to find because it agrees with the subject and carries tense. That is the first thing to look for in any long sentence ([[reading-skills/long-sentences]] gives the reading procedure step by step).

::: quiz
How many **finite** verb phrases are there in this sentence? *Hoping to finish early, the team, which had worked all weekend, asked whether they could leave at four.*
- [ ] one
- [ ] two
- [x] three
- [ ] five
::: solution
The finite verb phrases are *had worked* (past perfect), *asked* (past) and *could leave* (modal). *Hoping* is an *-ing* form and *to finish* a *to*-infinitive: both head non-finite clauses. So the sentence has five clauses — three finite and two non-finite — but only one of them, *the team … asked whether they could leave at four*, is the main clause.
:::
:::

::: warning Two finite verbs, no link
A Chinese sentence can put two verb phrases side by side with no joining word. An English clause cannot have two finite verbs unless something links them: a conjunction, a relative pronoun, or a non-finite form for one of them.

✗ *There are many students want to study abroad.* ✓ *There are many students **who** want to study abroad.* ✓ *Many students want to study abroad.*

✗ *The man stood at the door was my uncle.* ✓ *The man **who** stood at the door was my uncle.* ✓ *The man **standing** at the door was my uncle.*

✗ *I have a friend lives in Chengdu.* ✓ *I have a friend **who** lives in Chengdu.*

The first error is the most common: it comes from 有很多学生想出国, where 有 introduces a noun that is also the subject of 想. In English, *there are* + noun needs a relative clause (or a reduced one: *many students **wanting** to study abroad*) or a plain sentence without *there are*.
:::

## Three jobs for a subordinate clause

A subordinate clause does one of the same jobs as a noun phrase, an adjective or an adverbial. This gives the three main families, each of which has its own lesson.

::: theorem The functions of subordinate clauses {#rule-functions}
1. A **noun clause** (also called a **nominal clause**, or in Huddleston and Pullum's terms a **content clause**) does the job of a noun phrase: subject, object, complement, or the complement of a preposition, noun or adjective. *I know **that she left**.* ***What he said** surprised me.* *It depends on **whether it rains**.* → [[complex-sentences/noun-clauses]]
2. A **relative clause** modifies a noun, like an adjective, and comes **after** the noun: *the form **that was sent home***. → [[complex-sentences/relative-clauses]]
3. An **adverbial clause** adds information about time, place, reason, condition, contrast, purpose or result to the whole clause, like an adverb: ***When the rain stopped**, we went out.* → [[complex-sentences/adverbial-clauses]]

Each family has finite and non-finite members:

| family | finite | non-finite |
|---|---|---|
| noun clause | *I hope **that I'll win**.* | *I hope **to win**.* *I enjoy **winning**.* |
| relative clause | *the people **who live here*** | *the people **living here*** · *a place **to live*** |
| adverbial clause | ***When I opened the door**, I saw her.* | ***Opening the door**, I saw her.* · *I came **to see you**.* |

A fourth, smaller family, the **comparative clause**, completes a comparison: *It was easier **than we expected**.* *She works as hard **as her sister does**.*
:::

::: proof
The three-way division goes back to traditional grammar and is kept in Quirk et al. (1985) and Biber et al. (1999), which add comparative clauses as a separate type. Huddleston and Pullum (2002) organise the material differently: they call noun clauses "content clauses" and analyse words such as *before*, *after* and *since* as prepositions that can take a clause as their complement, so that *before she left* is a preposition phrase, like *before lunch*. Their reason is that most of these words take either a noun phrase or a clause, with the same meaning. For a learner the two analyses lead to the same sentences. What matters in practice is a test of function, which works in either framework:

- a **noun clause** can be replaced by *it*, *that* or *something*: *I know **that she left*** → *I know **it***;
- a **relative clause** can be replaced by an adjective or deleted, leaving the noun: *the form **that was sent home*** → *the **blue** form*;
- an **adverbial clause** can be replaced by an adverb such as *then*, *there*, *so* or *for that reason*, and can usually move: ***When the rain stopped**, we went out* → ***Then** we went out*.

The same word can introduce different kinds of clause, so the word alone is not enough: *that* begins a noun clause in *I know **that** she left* and a relative clause in *the form **that** was sent home*; *when* begins an adverbial clause in *Call me **when** you arrive* and a noun clause in *I don't know **when** she'll arrive*.
:::

::: example What job does the clause do? {#ex-functions}
Say whether the clause in bold is a noun clause, a relative clause or an adverbial clause, and whether it is finite or non-finite. (a) *I wonder **where they went**.* (b) *The town **where they went** was tiny.* (c) ***Where they went**, crowds followed.* (d) *The best thing **to do** is wait.* (e) *The best thing is **to wait**.* (f) *She left early **to catch the train**.*
::: solution
Apply the replacement test to each.

(a) *I wonder **it**?* does not work, but *I wonder **something*** does in meaning: it is the object of *wonder*. **Noun clause**, finite (*went*).

(b) It modifies *the town* and can be replaced by an adjective (*The **little** town was tiny*). **Relative clause**, finite.

(c) It can be replaced by *there* (***There**, crowds followed*) and tells us the place of the main event. **Adverbial clause**, finite. (This use of *where* is formal and rather literary.)

(d) *to do* modifies *thing* (*the best **possible** thing*). **Relative clause**, non-finite (*to*-infinitive).

(e) *to wait* is the complement of *is*: *The best thing is **that**.* **Noun clause**, non-finite.

(f) *to catch the train* gives the purpose of *left* and can be replaced by *for that reason*. **Adverbial clause**, non-finite.

The same word, *where*, introduced three different kinds of clause in (a)–(c); the *to*-infinitive did three different jobs in (d)–(f).
:::
:::

::: quiz
In *The news **that the factory would close** shocked the town*, what is the clause in bold?
- [ ] a relative clause modifying *news*
- [x] a noun clause giving the content of *news*
- [ ] an adverbial clause of reason
- [ ] a main clause
::: solution
The clause tells us *what* the news was: the news = the factory would close. This is a noun clause that is the complement of the noun *news* (sometimes called an **appositive** clause). A relative clause would have a gap inside it that *that* fills — *the news **that** we heard __ on the radio*, where *that* is the object of *heard*. In *the factory would close* nothing is missing, so *that* is not a relative pronoun. [[complex-sentences/noun-clauses]] explains the difference in detail.
:::
:::

## Coordination, subordination and embedding

Clauses can be joined in two ways. **Coordination** links clauses of equal rank with *and*, *but*, *or* (and *nor*, *so*, *yet*): *The bus was late, **and** we missed the start.* **Subordination** puts one clause inside another, as a part of it: *We missed the start **because** the bus was late.* The basics of both are in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coordination]] and [[sentences-and-paragraphs/subordination]]; here we look at what happens when they are combined.

::: definition Simple, compound and complex sentences {#def-sentence-types}
School grammars name sentences by the clauses they contain:

- a **simple sentence** has one clause: *The bus was late.*
- a **compound sentence** has two or more main clauses joined by coordination: *The bus was late, and we missed the start.*
- a **complex sentence** has at least one subordinate clause: *We missed the start because the bus was late.*
- a **compound-complex sentence** has both: *The bus was late because of the snow, and by the time we arrived the speeches had started.*

The terms describe the shape of a sentence, not its difficulty: a short complex sentence (*I think she's right*) is easier to read than a long compound one.
:::

Real sentences are rarely just one level deep. A subordinate clause can contain its own subordinate clauses, which can contain others. Take this sentence:

*When the rain stopped, the children who had been waiting inside ran out to see what had happened to the garden.*

| level | clause | job |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | *the children … ran out …* | main clause |
| 1 | *When the rain stopped* | adverbial (time) in the main clause |
| 1 | *who had been waiting inside* | relative, modifying *the children* |
| 1 | *to see …* | adverbial (purpose) in the main clause, non-finite |
| 2 | *what had happened to the garden* | noun clause, object of *see* |

::: theorem Keep the main clause visible {#rule-main-visible}
In clear writing, especially at this level:

1. **Keep the subject and its finite verb close together.** A long relative clause between them (*The report **that the committee which the minister appointed last spring published in May** argues …*) makes the reader hold the subject in memory too long.
2. **Put long, complex material at the end** of the sentence where possible, after the main verb. English has a strong tendency, called **end-weight**, to place heavier elements later.
3. **Avoid more than two levels of the same kind of clause** stacked on each other in one sentence, especially relative clauses inside relative clauses.
:::

::: proof
Quirk et al. (1985) describe the principle of end-weight, and Biber et al. (1999) show from corpus counts that long subjects are much rarer than long objects and complements in all registers: speakers and writers move heavy material to the right. Psycholinguistic research explains why. A sentence in which a clause is buried in the middle of another clause of the same kind (centre-embedding) is hard to process even when it is grammatical: *The rat the cat the dog chased killed ate the malt* is correct English but almost unreadable, while the same content with the clauses at the end — *This is the dog that chased the cat that killed the rat that ate the malt* — is easy. These are principles of style, not rules of grammar: a sentence that breaks them is not wrong, but it costs the reader more. They return in [[complex-sentences/emphasis-inversion]] and in [[style-and-rhetoric/clarity]].
:::

::: example Rewriting a centre-embedded sentence {#ex-end-weight}
Rewrite the sentence so that the main clause is easy to find. *The proposal that the students who had complained about the canteen, which had raised its prices twice that year, made to the head teacher was accepted.*
::: solution
1. Find the finite verbs: *had complained*, *had raised*, *made*, *was accepted*.
2. Find the main clause: the last verb, *was accepted*, has the subject *The proposal*. The core is *The proposal … was accepted*, with twenty words between subject and verb.
3. Find what hangs on *proposal*: *that the students … made to the head teacher* is a relative clause (*that* is the object of *made*). Inside it, *who had complained about the canteen* modifies *the students*, and inside that, *which had raised its prices twice that year* modifies *the canteen*: three levels of relative clause.
4. Move the main clause forward and spread the information. One possible version:

*The head teacher accepted the proposal from the students who had complained about the canteen, which had raised its prices twice that year.*

Here the main clause comes first (*The head teacher accepted the proposal*), and the relative clauses follow at the end, where English prefers heavy material. The passive was turned into the active to put *the head teacher* first. Another version splits the sentence: *The canteen had raised its prices twice that year, and some students complained. The head teacher accepted their proposal.*
:::
:::

## Finding the structure of a long sentence

A complex sentence can be taken apart in a fixed order. The steps below are the grammatical version of the reading procedure in [[reading-skills/long-sentences]].

::: theorem Analysing a complex sentence {#rule-analyse}
1. **Find the finite verb phrases.** Each one is the centre of a finite clause.
2. **Find the clause openers**: subordinators (*because*, *although*, *if*, *when* …), relative words (*who*, *which*, *that*, *whose*, *where*), *wh*-words and *whether*/*if* in noun clauses, and *that*.
3. **Look for hidden openers.** *That* is often left out before noun clauses (*I think __ she's right*) and before object relative clauses (*the book __ I bought*). A noun followed directly by another subject and verb usually signals a missing *that*.
4. **Find the non-finite clauses**: *to*-infinitives, *-ing* forms and *-ed* participles that are not part of a finite verb phrase.
5. **Bracket each subordinate clause** from its opener to the end of its verb's objects and adverbials. What remains outside all brackets is the main clause: it must contain a subject and a finite verb.
6. **Name the job of each clause** with the replacement test of [[#rule-functions]].
:::

Step 3 is where most problems lie. Compare *The book I bought* (a noun with a relative clause, *that* left out) with *The book I bought was boring* (the same, followed by the main verb *was*). In speech, intonation shows the structure; in writing, the reader must notice that *I bought* cannot be the main clause, because the sentence started with *The book*.

::: example A sentence with a hidden *that* {#ex-hidden}
Analyse: *The professor said the results the team had published were wrong.*
::: solution
1. Finite verb phrases: *said*, *had published*, *were*. Three finite clauses.
2. Openers: none are visible.
3. Hidden openers: after *said*, a noun clause follows with *that* left out (*said __ the results … were wrong*). After *the results*, another subject and verb, *the team had published*, follows directly: a relative clause with *that* left out (*the results __ the team had published*).
4. Non-finite clauses: none.
5. Brackets: *The professor said [the results [the team had published __] were wrong].* The gap __ after *published* is where the object of *published* would be: the results.
6. Jobs: *The professor said …* is the main clause; *the results … were wrong* is a noun clause, object of *said*; *the team had published* is a relative clause modifying *the results*.

With the openers written in: *The professor said **that** the results **that** the team had published were wrong.* Both versions are correct; in writing, keeping the first *that* helps the reader here, because *said the results* could at first be misread as *said* + object.
:::
:::

Sometimes a sentence leads the reader to the wrong structure first, and only the last words show the mistake. Psycholinguists call these **garden-path sentences**, after the phrase *lead someone up the garden path* ‘mislead someone’:

- *The old man the boats.* (*The old* is the subject, ‘old people’; *man* is the verb, ‘work on’.)
- *The horse raced past the barn fell.* (= *The horse **that was** raced past the barn fell* — *raced past the barn* is a reduced relative clause.)
- *While the man hunted the deer ran into the woods.* (= *While the man hunted, the deer ran …*)

The last one shows why a comma after an introductory adverbial clause helps ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/subordination#rule-sub-comma]]). Careful writers avoid garden paths by keeping optional words such as *that* and by punctuating.

::: example Building a complex sentence {#ex-build}
Combine these five short sentences into one sentence with one main clause, using at least one relative clause, one adverbial clause and one non-finite clause. *The museum reopened last month. It had been closed for two years. The repairs cost twice as much as planned. Many visitors came on the first day. They wanted to see the new dinosaur hall.*
::: solution
1. Choose the main statement. The news is that many visitors came: *Many visitors came on the first day.* (Choosing *The museum reopened* is also possible; the choice decides what the sentence is about.)
2. Turn the background into subordinate clauses. *When the museum reopened last month* (adverbial, time). *which had been closed for two years* (relative, about the museum). *after repairs that cost twice as much as planned* (*after* + noun phrase containing a relative clause).
3. Turn the purpose into a non-finite clause: *wanting to see the new dinosaur hall*, or *to see the new dinosaur hall*.
4. Put heavy material after the main verb where possible.

*When the museum reopened last month after two years of repairs, which cost twice as much as planned, many visitors came on the first day to see the new dinosaur hall.*

Here *which* refers to *repairs*. Putting the relative clause straight after *the museum* (*When the museum, which had been closed …, reopened*) would separate the subject from its verb by fifteen words. Not every combination is better than the separate sentences; in a news report, two sentences might be best.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **形合 and 意合.** 连淑能 (1993) describes English as a language that relies on **形合** (hypotaxis): the relations between clauses are shown by form — conjunctions, relative pronouns, non-finite verbs — and a clause must be explicitly attached to another. Chinese relies more on **意合** (parataxis): clauses are placed side by side and the reader works out the relation from meaning and order. 你不来，我不去 is a complete conditional sentence in Chinese with no word for *if*; English needs one: *If you don't come, I won't go.* The difference is a strong tendency, not an absolute rule — Chinese has 如果, 因为, 虽然 and uses them, especially in writing — but it explains why a translation from Chinese often needs words that the Chinese did not have.
- **Running sentences.** A Chinese written sentence can run on through many comma-separated clauses, a style Chinese grammarians call 流水句, in which the topic is stated once and then several comments follow. A word-for-word English version produces comma splices ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/complete-sentences]]). The English writer has to decide which clause is the main one and make the others subordinate, coordinate or separate.
- **Verbs in series.** Chinese can put verbs one after another without any joining form: 他去图书馆借书 ('he go library borrow book'). Li and Thompson (1981) call this a serial verb construction. English allows only one finite verb per clause, so the second verb usually becomes non-finite: *He went to the library **to borrow** some books.* Translating from Chinese, first decide which verb is the main one; the others become infinitives, *-ing* clauses or separate clauses joined by *and*.
- **No finite form in Chinese.** Chinese verbs have no tense endings, so there is no visible difference between a "finite" and a "non-finite" verb, and whether Chinese has a finiteness distinction at all is debated among linguists. English marks it on every verb. A learner should therefore check every English clause for its anchor: one finite verb, with a subject.
:::

::: history
Teaching the analysis of sentences into clauses is old. In the nineteenth century, schools in Britain and America taught "parsing" (naming the part of speech of each word) and "analysis" (dividing a sentence into clauses and naming their functions). In 1877 Alonzo Reed and Brainerd Kellogg's *Higher Lessons in English* popularised a way of drawing sentences as diagrams, with subordinate clauses hanging below the words they modify; Reed–Kellogg diagrams were taught in American schools for about a century. Modern linguistics replaced them with tree diagrams. Noam Chomsky's *Syntactic Structures* (1957) gave formal grammars in which a sentence can contain another sentence, which explained how a finite set of rules can produce sentences of any length; and in 1970 the psychologist Thomas Bever used *The horse raced past the barn fell* to show that readers build structure as they go and can be led down a garden path.
:::

::: summary
- A **clause** is built around one verb phrase; a **sentence** contains one or more clauses ([[#def-clause]]).
- A **main clause** can stand alone; a **subordinate clause** does a job inside another clause, and can contain its own subordinate clauses ([[#def-main-sub]]).
- A **finite** clause has a verb showing tense or a modal; **non-finite** clauses use *to*-infinitives, bare infinitives, *-ing* or *-ed* forms, have no tense and usually no subject of their own ([[#def-finite]], [[#rule-finite]]).
- Subordinate clauses are **noun clauses**, **relative clauses** or **adverbial clauses** (plus comparative clauses); find the job with the replacement test ([[#rule-functions]]).
- One English clause has one finite verb: two finite verbs need a link (✗ *There are many students want to…*).
- To analyse a long sentence, find the finite verbs, the openers (including the hidden *that*), the non-finite clauses, and bracket what is subordinate ([[#rule-analyse]]).
- Keep subject and verb close, and put heavy clauses at the end ([[#rule-main-visible]]).
- English shows clause relations by form (形合); Chinese often leaves them to meaning (意合), so translations need explicit links.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Count the clauses {level=1 check="4"}
How many clauses (finite and non-finite) are there in this sentence? *After she had finished her degree, Zhang Min decided to look for a job that would let her travel.*
::: solution
**Four**: *After she had finished her degree* (finite, adverbial); *Zhang Min decided …* (finite, main); *to look for a job …* (non-finite, object of *decided*); *that would let her travel* (finite, relative, modifying *a job*). *Travel* after *let her* is a bare infinitive inside the pattern *let* + object + verb ([[verb-patterns/causatives]]); if you count it as a fifth, non-finite clause, as Quirk et al. would, that analysis is also defensible.
:::
:::

::: exercise Finite or non-finite? {level=1}
Say whether each clause in bold is finite, non-finite or verbless. (a) *I'd like **to come with you**.* (b) *She said **she'd come with us**.* (c) ***Feeling tired**, he went to bed.* (d) *The bridge, **built in 1890**, is still in use.* (e) ***If possible**, reply by Monday.* (f) *It's essential **that she be informed**.*
::: solution
(a) non-finite (*to*-infinitive). (b) finite (*would* is a modal). (c) non-finite (*-ing*). (d) non-finite (*-ed* participle). (e) verbless. (f) finite (a subjunctive, [[complex-sentences/unreal-past]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Noun, relative or adverbial? {level=1}
Name the job of each clause in bold. (a) *I asked **when the shop opened**.* (b) *Call me **when the shop opens**.* (c) *Monday is the day **when the shop opens late**.* (d) *She told me **that she was leaving**.* (e) *The man **that she was leaving** was heartbroken.* (f) *I've come **to say goodbye**.*
::: solution
(a) noun clause (object of *asked*). (b) adverbial clause (time). (c) relative clause (modifies *the day*). (d) noun clause (object of *told*). (e) relative clause (modifies *the man*; *that* is the object of *leaving*). (f) adverbial clause, non-finite (purpose).
:::
:::

::: exercise Correct the sentences {level=2}
Each sentence has two finite verbs without a link. Correct it in two different ways. (a) *There is a girl in my class speaks four languages.* (b) *The teacher taught us English last year has retired.* (c) *I met a man knew my father.* (d) *There are many tourists visit the old town every summer.*
::: solution
(a) *There is a girl in my class **who** speaks four languages.* / *A girl in my class speaks four languages.*

(b) *The teacher **who** taught us English last year has retired.* / *Our English teacher from last year has retired.* (✗ *The teacher teaching us English last year* does not work: see [[complex-sentences/participle-infinitive-clauses]].)

(c) *I met a man **who** knew my father.* / *I met a friend of my father's.*

(d) *There are many tourists **who** visit the old town every summer.* / *Many tourists visit the old town every summer.* (The second is shorter and more natural.)
:::
:::

::: exercise Bracket the subordinate clauses {level=2}
Put square brackets round every subordinate clause, and underline (or write out) the main clause. (a) *Although nobody expected it, the team that had lost every match won the final.* (b) *I'm not sure whether the man you saw was the manager.* (c) *Having read the instructions twice, Ahmed still didn't know what to do.*
::: hint
Clauses can be inside other clauses: brackets can be nested.
:::
::: solution
(a) *[Although nobody expected it], the team [that had lost every match] won the final.* Main clause: *the team … won the final.*

(b) *I'm not sure [whether the man [you saw] was the manager].* Main clause: *I'm not sure …*; the *whether*-clause is a noun clause (complement of *sure*), and *you saw* is a relative clause with *that* left out, inside it.

(c) *[Having read the instructions twice], Ahmed still didn't know [what to do].* Main clause: *Ahmed still didn't know …*; *Having read …* is a non-finite adverbial clause; *what to do* is a non-finite noun clause, object of *know*.
:::
:::

::: exercise Combine the sentences {level=2}
Combine each group into one sentence with one main clause. Use the words in brackets. (a) *The flight was cancelled. We had to stay at the airport overnight. (because)* (b) *I bought a guidebook. It was full of mistakes. (which)* (c) *Li Na finished her report. Then she went home. (after / -ing)* (d) *We wanted to know something. Would the museum be open on Monday? (whether)*
::: solution
(a) *Because the flight was cancelled, we had to stay at the airport overnight.* / *We had to stay … because the flight was cancelled.*

(b) *I bought a guidebook which was full of mistakes.* or, with commas if you have only one guidebook in mind, *The guidebook I bought, which was full of mistakes, …* (see [[complex-sentences/relative-clauses]]).

(c) *After finishing her report, Li Na went home.* / *After she had finished her report, Li Na went home.*

(d) *We wanted to know whether the museum would be open on Monday.* (Note the word order: *the museum would be*, not *would the museum be*.)
:::
:::

::: exercise Untangle and rewrite {level=3}
Analyse the sentence (main clause, subordinate clauses, their jobs), then rewrite it so that it is easier to read, without losing information. *The fact that the bus company which the council pays to run the school service has not said when the new timetable that parents were promised in March will start is causing anger.*
::: hint
The main verb is near the end. How long is the subject?
:::
::: solution
**Analysis.** Main clause: *The fact … is causing anger.* The subject is everything from *The fact* to *start*: twenty-six words. Inside it: *that the bus company … has not said …* is a noun clause giving the content of *fact*; *which the council pays to run the school service* is a relative clause modifying *the bus company*, with a non-finite clause *to run the school service* inside it; *when the new timetable … will start* is a noun clause, object of *said*; *that parents were promised in March* is a relative clause modifying *the new timetable*.

**Rewrite** (one of many): *Parents are angry because the bus company has still not said when the new timetable will start. They were promised it in March, and the council pays the company to run the school service.* Or in one sentence, with the main clause first: *It is causing anger that the bus company, which the council pays to run the school service, has not said when the new timetable will start, although parents were promised it in March.* The two-sentence version is clearer.
:::
:::

::: exercise From Chinese to English {level=3}
Translate into natural English. Decide which clause is the main one and how the others should be attached. (a) 我昨天在超市碰到一个老同学，十年没见了，聊了半天。 (b) 他去北京开会，顺便看了看几个朋友。 (c) 你不说，我也知道。
::: solution
Models (other good versions are possible):

(a) *Yesterday in the supermarket I ran into an old classmate **I hadn't seen for ten years**, and we chatted for ages.* — The Chinese has one topic and three comments. English makes *ran into an old classmate* the main clause, attaches 十年没见了 as a relative clause to *classmate*, and coordinates the chat with *and*. (Or two sentences: *… an old classmate. We hadn't seen each other for ten years, so we talked for ages.*)

(b) *He went to Beijing **for a meeting** and **while he was there** he visited some friends.* / *He went to Beijing for a meeting and took the chance to visit some friends.* — The serial verbs 去……开会 become a main verb and a purpose phrase; 顺便 becomes *while he was there* or *took the chance*.

(c) *I know **even if you don't tell me**.* / *You don't need to tell me — I know.* — The Chinese has no word for *if* or *even if* (意合); English must choose one.
:::
