A student hands in a short paragraph:

*on monday i went to the british museum with my english teacher, professor hall. We saw a famous chinese vase. The guide said it was made in the fifteenth century. It was beatiful and I realy enjoyed it.*

The grammar is fine, but a reader notices two things at once. Several words that should begin with a capital letter do not (*on*, *monday*, *i*, *british*, *museum*, *english*, *professor hall*, *chinese*), and two words are misspelt (*beatiful*, *realy*). Chinese has no capital letters, and Chinese characters are not spelt letter by letter, so both of these are new skills for a Chinese speaker — but they are also among the easiest to learn, because most of them follow clear rules.

This lesson sets out where English uses capital letters, the main spelling rules for adding endings, the words most often confused, and the differences between British and American spelling.

## Capital letters

::: definition Proper noun and common noun {#def-proper}
A **proper noun** is the name of one particular person, place, organisation, day, month, language and so on: *Li Na*, *Shanghai*, *Monday*, *Spanish*. A **common noun** names any member of a class: *a woman*, *a city*, *a day*, *a language*. In English, proper nouns — and adjectives made from them — begin with a capital letter.
:::

::: theorem Where English uses capital letters {#rule-capitals}
Use a capital letter for:

1. the **first word of a sentence**, and the first word of a quoted sentence: *She said, "It's late."*
2. the pronoun ***I***, wherever it appears;
3. **names of people and pets**: *Wang Fang*, *Ahmed Khan*, *Mr Brown*;
4. **names of places, buildings and organisations**: *China*, *Hunan Province*, *the Yellow River*, *Fifth Avenue*, *the British Museum*, *Tsinghua University*, *the United Nations*;
5. **nationalities, languages and peoples, and adjectives made from them**: *Chinese*, *English*, *a Japanese restaurant*, *French cheese*;
6. **days, months and festivals**: *Monday*, *July*, *the Spring Festival*, *New Year's Day* — but **not** the seasons: *spring*, *summer*, *autumn* (AmE also *fall*), *winter*;
7. **titles used before a name**: *Professor Hall*, *Dr Chen*, *Aunt Mei*, *President Lopez* — but not the same words used as common nouns: *She is a professor.* *My aunt lives in Dalian.*;
8. **the main words in titles** of books, films, songs and so on (see "Titles" below).
:::

::: proof
Capitals mark names. A name refers to one particular thing, and the capital tells the reader that the word is being used as a name, not as an ordinary word: *the white house* is any white house, *the White House* is one building in Washington. This is why *Professor Hall* has a capital (it is part of a name) but *a professor* does not, and why the same word can go both ways: *I'm studying **history**.* but *the **History** Department* (the name of a department). Languages and nationalities have capitals in English because they are derived from names of countries and peoples; English differs here from French and Spanish, which write *français* and *español* with small letters. The seasons are not names in English, so they take no capital, though older texts and poetry sometimes give them one. These rules are the same in British and American English (Swan 2016; *The Chicago Manual of Style* 2017).
:::

Some pairs to compare:

- *We go to the **park** every Sunday.* / *We go to **Hyde Park** every Sunday.*
- *My **mother** is a doctor.* / *Can I go out, **Mum**?* (*Mum* — AmE *Mom* — is used like a name when you speak to her, so it has a capital.)
- *Turn **north** at the station.* / *She grew up in the **North** of England.* / *The weather in **northern China** is dry.* (Compass points take a capital only when they are part of a name or the name of a region.)
- *The **river** is wide here.* / *the **Yangtze River**, the **Yangtze***

::: warning Small letters for languages and nationalities
Because Chinese has no capitals, learners often write languages and nationalities with small letters:

✗ *I am chinese and I speak english.* ✓ *I am **Chinese** and I speak **English**.*

✗ *She likes korean food.* ✓ *She likes **Korean** food.*

The opposite error is to give capitals to ordinary nouns, often for emphasis: ✗ *My hobbies are Music and Sport.* ✓ *My hobbies are **music** and **sport**.* School subjects are not names: *I study maths and physics.* (A subject that is a language keeps its capital: *I study English and maths.*)
:::

### Titles

In the titles of books, films, songs, articles and so on, two styles are used.

- **Title case** — capitals for the first word and all the main words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, pronouns), but not for short articles, prepositions and conjunctions in the middle: *The Old Man and the Sea*, *A Guide to Writing for Beginners*. This is usual in American English and in the titles of books in both varieties.
- **Sentence case** — a capital only for the first word and for names: *A guide to writing for beginners*. Many British newspapers and many academic reference styles (for article titles) use it.

Style guides differ on the details of title case (for example, whether a preposition of five letters such as *about* takes a capital), so follow the guide you are asked to use, and be consistent.

### Chinese names in English

Chinese names written in Pinyin follow the rules of the Chinese standard for Pinyin spelling (汉语拼音正词法基本规则, GB/T 16159): the family name and the given name each start with a capital, and a given name of two syllables is written as **one word**: *Wang Xiaoming*, *Zhuge Liang*, not ✗ *Wang Xiao Ming* or ✗ *wang xiaoming*. Place names are also written as one word with one capital: *Beijing*, *Chongqing*, *Heilongjiang*. An apostrophe separates syllables when the boundary is unclear: *Xi'an* (two syllables, not *xian*).

In English-speaking contexts, readers may not know which part of a Chinese name is the family name. Some people reverse the order (*Xiaoming Wang*); international documents often write the family name in capitals (*WANG Xiaoming*). Both are common. Names spelt in other romanisations — *Chan*, *Wong*, *Lee*, the Cantonese and other spellings used in Hong Kong, Taiwan and many overseas communities — are written the way their owners write them.

::: example Writing Chinese names and places {#ex-names}
Write these correctly in an English sentence: 欧阳晓梅 (a person), 西安, 黄山, 华南理工大学, 春节.
::: solution
1. 欧阳晓梅: the family name 欧阳 has two syllables, written as one word with one capital; the given name 晓梅 is also one word: *Ouyang Xiaomei*.
2. 西安: one place name, one word, with an apostrophe because *xian* could be read as one syllable: *Xi'an*.
3. 黄山: a name made of a specific part and a word for ‘mountain’. In English texts the usual form is *Huangshan* or *Mount Huang*, with capitals in both.
4. 华南理工大学: the official English name of an institution, with capitals for all the main words: *South China University of Technology*. (Check the official English name of an institution on its own website rather than translating word for word.)
5. 春节: a festival, so a capital: *the Spring Festival* (or *Chinese New Year*).

✓ *Ouyang Xiaomei, who studies at South China University of Technology, spent the Spring Festival in Xi'an and then climbed Huangshan.*
:::
:::

::: example Correcting capitals {#ex-capitals}
Correct the capital letters in the paragraph at the start of the lesson: *on monday i went to the british museum with my english teacher, professor hall. We saw a famous chinese vase. The guide said it was made in the fifteenth century.*
::: solution
1. First words of sentences: *on* → *On*. (*We* and *The* are already correct.)
2. The pronoun *I*: *i* → *I*.
3. Days: *monday* → *Monday*.
4. Names of buildings and institutions: *the british museum* → *the British Museum* (the name of one museum; *the* stays small).
5. Languages and nationalities: *english* → *English*; *chinese* → *Chinese*.
6. Titles before names, and names: *professor hall* → *Professor Hall*.
7. Check what should stay small: *teacher*, *vase*, *guide* and *century* are common nouns; *fifteenth century* is not a name.

✓ *On Monday I went to the British Museum with my English teacher, Professor Hall. We saw a famous Chinese vase. The guide said it was made in the fifteenth century.*
:::

## Spelling rules for endings

English spelling is famously irregular, but the changes that happen when we add an ending (*-ed*, *-ing*, *-er*, *-est*, *-s*, *-ly*, *-ness* …) are mostly regular. Three rules cover most of them.

::: theorem Doubling the final consonant {#rule-doubling}
Before an ending that begins with a vowel (*-ed*, *-ing*, *-er*, *-est*), double the final consonant of a word that ends in **one vowel letter + one consonant letter**, if the final syllable is **stressed**:

- one-syllable words: *stop* → *stopped*, *big* → *bigger*, *sit* → *sitting*, *plan* → *planned*;
- longer words stressed on the last syllable: *beGIN* → *beginning*, *preFER* → *preferred*, *forGET* → *forgetting*.

Do **not** double:

- after two vowel letters or before two consonants: *rain* → *rained*, *help* → *helped*;
- *w*, *x* and *y*: *fix* → *fixed*, *play* → *playing*, *show* → *showed*;
- when the last syllable is unstressed: *VISit* → *visited*, *OPen* → *opening*, *HAPpen* → *happened*.

**British and American difference:** British English doubles a final *l* even after an unstressed syllable (*travel* → *travelled*, *cancel* → *cancelled*, *model* → *modelling*); American English usually does not (*traveled*, *canceled*, *modeling*).
:::

::: proof
The doubled consonant keeps the vowel short. English spelling often shows a "long" vowel with a silent *e* (*hope*, *write*), so *hoping* — with one *p* — keeps the long vowel of *hope*, while *hopping* — with two — keeps the short vowel of *hop*. The same contrast separates *writing* (from *write*) and *written*, *later* and *latter*, *diner* and *dinner*. Stress matters because only a stressed syllable needs its vowel marked so clearly. The British *-ll-* in *travelled* is an exception to this logic, which Noah Webster removed from American spelling in the early nineteenth century; both spellings have been standard in their own country since then.
:::

::: theorem Silent *e* and final *y* {#rule-e-y}
1. **Silent *e***: drop a final silent *e* before an ending that begins with a vowel (*make* → *making*, *hope* → *hoped*, *write* → *writer*, *nice* → *nicest*), but keep it before an ending that begins with a consonant (*hope* → *hopeful*, *love* → *lovely*, *safe* → *safety*). Words in *-ee*, *-ye* and *-oe* keep the *e* before *-ing*: *agreeing*, *seeing*, *dyeing*, *canoeing*. Words in *-ie* change to *-ying*: *lie* → *lying*, *die* → *dying*.
2. **Final *y***: after a consonant, change *y* to *i* before most endings (*carry* → *carried*, *happy* → *happiness*, *city* → *cities*, *easy* → *easily*), but keep it before *-ing* (*carrying*, *studying*). After a vowel, keep the *y*: *play* → *played*, *day* → *days*, *enjoy* → *enjoyment*. (Exceptions: *say* → *said*, *pay* → *paid*, *lay* → *laid*, *day* → *daily*.)
:::

A few common exceptions to the silent *e* rule are worth learning: *argue* → *argument*, *true* → *truly*, *whole* → *wholly*, *nine* → *ninth*; and *e* is kept in *noticeable* and *courageous* to keep the "soft" sound of *c* and *g*.

::: warning "I before e except after c"
Many learners know the rhyme "*i* before *e*, except after *c*". It works for some words with the vowel /iː/ — *believe*, *piece*, *field*, but *receive*, *ceiling* — but it has so many exceptions that it is not a reliable rule: *science*, *their*, *weird*, *seize*, *height*, *foreign*, *neighbour*, *eight*, *ancient*. Learn these words individually, with a dictionary, rather than trusting the rhyme.
:::

::: example Adding endings {#ex-endings}
Add the ending and explain the spelling. (a) *swim* + *-ing* (b) *open* + *-ed* (c) *happy* + *-ly* (d) *write* + *-ing* (e) *study* + *-ed* (f) *refer* + *-ed* (g) *travel* + *-er* (British and American)
::: solution
(a) *swimming* — one syllable, one vowel + one consonant: double the *m*.

(b) *opened* — the stress is on the first syllable (*OPen*): do not double.

(c) *happily* — consonant + *y*: change *y* to *i*.

(d) *writing* — silent *e* dropped before a vowel ending; no doubling, because *write* ends in *e*, not in one vowel + one consonant.

(e) *studied* — consonant + *y*: change to *i* before *-ed* (but *studying*, keeping *y* before *-ing*).

(f) *referred* — stressed on the last syllable (*reFER*), one vowel + one consonant: double the *r*. (Compare *REFerence*, where the stress has moved and *r* is not doubled.)

(g) British *traveller*, American *traveler* — the stress is on the first syllable, so only British English doubles the *l*.
:::
:::

## Words that are often confused

Many spelling errors are real words in the wrong place, so a spell checker does not find them. These pairs and groups cause the most trouble:

| words | use | example |
|---|---|---|
| *its* / *it's* | possessive / *it is*, *it has* | *The dog wagged **its** tail. **It's** late.* |
| *their* / *there* / *they're* | possessive / place, *there is* / *they are* | ***They're** leaving **their** bags **there**.* |
| *your* / *you're* | possessive / *you are* | ***You're** right about **your** brother.* |
| *whose* / *who's* | possessive / *who is*, *who has* | ***Who's** the man **whose** car is outside?* |
| *then* / *than* | time / comparison | *She's taller **than** me. **Then** we left.* |
| *to* / *too* / *two* | preposition, infinitive / also, excessively / 2 | *I want **to** go **too**. It's **too** expensive for **two**.* |
| *affect* / *effect* | verb / usually a noun | *The rain **affected** the match. It had a big **effect**.* |
| *lose* / *loose* | verb, /luːz/ / adjective ‘not tight’, /luːs/ | *Don't **lose** your keys. These trousers are **loose**.* |
| *quite* / *quiet* | adverb / adjective ‘not noisy’ | *It's **quite** **quiet** here.* |
| *accept* / *except* | verb ‘take, agree’ / ‘apart from’ | *I **accept** all the points **except** the last.* |
| *weather* / *whether* | rain, sun … / ‘if’ | *I don't know **whether** the **weather** will improve.* |
| *advice* / *advise* | noun / verb | *She **advised** me to get some **advice**.* |
| *practice* / *practise* | BrE noun / BrE verb (AmE *practice* for both) | BrE *I **practise** every day; **practice** helps.* |

The British pair *practice* / *practise* works like *advice* / *advise*, and so does *licence* (noun) / *license* (verb). American English uses *practice* and *license* for both noun and verb.

::: quiz
Which sentence has no spelling errors?
- [ ] *Their going to lose there tickets if their not careful.*
- [ ] *I'd rather walk then take the bus, weather or not it rains.*
- [x] *Whose idea was it to leave the bags there?*
- [ ] *The new rules will effect everyone accept the managers.*
::: solution
*Whose* (possessive) and *there* (place) are correct. The first should be *They're … their … they're*; the second *than* and *whether*; the fourth *affect* and *except*.
:::

## British and American spelling

::: theorem British and American spelling {#rule-variety}
British and American English differ in the spelling of some groups of words. The main patterns are:

| British | American | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *-our* | *-or* | *colour*/*color*, *favour*/*favor*, *neighbour*/*neighbor* |
| *-re* | *-er* | *centre*/*center*, *metre*/*meter*, *theatre*/*theater* |
| *-ise* or *-ize* | *-ize* | *realise* or *realize*/*realize*, *organise* or *organize*/*organize* |
| *-yse* | *-yze* | *analyse*/*analyze*, *paralyse*/*paralyze* |
| *-ll-* | *-l-* | *travelled*/*traveled*, *cancelling*/*canceling* |
| *-ence* | *-ense* | *defence*/*defense*, *licence* (noun)/*license* |
| *-ogue* | *-og* or *-ogue* | *catalogue*/*catalog*; *dialogue* is usual in both |

and single words such as *programme*/*program* (but *program* for computers in both), *tyre*/*tire*, *grey*/*gray*, *cheque*/*check*, *jewellery*/*jewelry*, *aluminium*/*aluminum*, *mum*/*mom*. Both spellings are correct in their own variety. In one text, use **one** system consistently.
:::

::: proof
Most of the differences go back to Noah Webster, whose dictionaries of 1806 and 1828 used simpler spellings (*color*, *center*, *traveled*) that became standard in the United States, while British spelling kept the forms recorded in Samuel Johnson's dictionary of 1755. The *-ize* ending is not American only: it is older than *-ise* in many words, the *Oxford English Dictionary* and Oxford University Press use it ("Oxford spelling"), and many British writers and publishers use it, while others prefer *-ise*. Some verbs are spelt *-ise* in both varieties, because the ending is not the suffix *-ize* at all: *advertise*, *advise*, *exercise*, *revise*, *supervise*, *surprise*, *televise*. Teachers and examiners accept either system; what they notice is a mixture, such as *colour* and *center* in the same essay.
:::

::: example Making a text consistent {#ex-consistent}
This text mixes British and American spellings. Rewrite it once in British and once in American spelling: *My favorite place in our neighbourhood is the theater. Last week I traveled there to see a programme about the history of color photography, and I realised that I had never analyzed a photo before.*
::: solution
1. Find every word that has two spellings: *favorite*, *neighbourhood*, *theater*, *traveled*, *programme*, *color*, *realised*, *analyzed*.
2. Choose the British form of each: *favourite*, *neighbourhood*, *theatre*, *travelled*, *programme*, *colour*, *realised* (or *realized*), *analysed*.
3. Choose the American form of each: *favorite*, *neighborhood*, *theater*, *traveled*, *program*, *color*, *realized*, *analyzed*.

British: *My favourite place in our neighbourhood is the theatre. Last week I travelled there to see a programme about the history of colour photography, and I realised that I had never analysed a photo before.*

American: *My favorite place in our neighborhood is the theater. Last week I traveled there to see a program about the history of color photography, and I realized that I had never analyzed a photo before.*

In British English *realized* would also be correct, as long as the writer uses *-ize* in every such word in the text.

## Becoming a better speller

- **Use the rules for endings**, which cover thousands of words.
- **Learn words in families**: *nation*, *national*, *nationality*; *sign*, *signal*, *signature* (the *g* in *sign* is silent, but you hear it in *signal*). See [[word-formation/spelling-changes]].
- **Link spelling and sound**: many errors come from pronunciation (*realy* for *really*, *beatiful* for *beautiful*, *libary* for *library*). Say the word slowly, syllable by syllable: *beau-ti-ful*, *li-bra-ry*. The patterns of English spelling and sound are explained in [[sounds-of-english/spelling-and-sound]].
- **Keep a list** of the words you misspell, and test yourself on it.
- **Set your spell checker** to the variety you use (English (UK) or English (US)), but remember that it cannot see a real word in the wrong place: *form* for *from*, *there* for *their*, *quiet* for *quite*.

::: note English and Chinese
- **No capitals in Chinese.** Chinese characters have no capital and small forms, so the idea that a word changes its first letter because it is a name is new. Pinyin, however, does use capitals: the Pinyin spelling rules (GB/T 16159) give capitals to the first letter of a sentence and of personal and place names. So *Beijing* and *Wang Fang* are already familiar; what needs practice is capitals for languages, nationalities, days and months, which Chinese marks in other ways (星期一, 英语).
- **Spelling is not Pinyin.** Pinyin is a regular system, in which each letter or letter group stands for the same sound. English spelling is less regular, because it keeps old pronunciations and words borrowed from other languages. Do not expect to spell an English word from its sound alone, and do not read English letters with Pinyin values.
- **Errors from pronunciation.** Some spelling errors of Chinese speakers come from pronunciation: final consonants that are not pronounced may be left out in writing (*wan* for *want*, *ol* for *old*); some speakers of southern varieties confuse *l* and *n* in speech, and the confusion can appear in spelling. Saying a word clearly, and listening for its final sounds, helps spelling too.
- **Two models.** Learners in China meet both British and American spelling in textbooks, dictionaries, films and websites. Both are correct; choose one for each piece of writing.
:::

::: history
English spelling was unsettled for centuries; Shakespeare's contemporaries spelt even common words in several ways. Printers gradually fixed many spellings, and Samuel Johnson's *A Dictionary of the English Language* (1755) became the reference for British spelling. In the United States, Noah Webster promoted American forms, first in *The American Spelling Book* (1783), a schoolbook used by generations of children, and then in his dictionaries, above all *An American Dictionary of the English Language* (1828). Chinese romanisation has its own history: the *Hanyu Pinyin* scheme was adopted in 1958, and the rules for writing Pinyin words — including capitals and writing a two-syllable given name as one word — were set out in the national standard GB/T 16159, first issued in 1996 and revised in 2012.
:::

::: summary
- Capital letters mark names (proper nouns) and the start of sentences ([[#def-proper]]).
- Use capitals for *I*, names of people and places, organisations, nationalities and languages (*Chinese*, *English*), days, months and festivals, and titles before names — but not for seasons, school subjects or titles used as common nouns ([[#rule-capitals]]).
- Write Pinyin names with two capitals and the given name as one word: *Wang Xiaoming*, *Xi'an*.

(a) *admitted* — stressed on the last syllable (*adMIT*). (b) *visited* — stressed on the first (*VISit*). (c) *occurred* — stressed on the last (*ocCUR*). (d) *offered* — stressed on the first (*OFfer*). (e) *controlled* — stressed on the last (*conTROL*), in both varieties. (f) British *labelled*, American *labeled* — stressed on the first syllable; only British English doubles the *l*.
:::

::: exercise British or American? {level=2}
Say whether each spelling is British (BrE), American (AmE) or used in both. (a) *colour* (b) *center* (c) *organize* (d) *analyse* (e) *traveler* (f) *catalogue* (g) *exercise* (h) *program* (for a computer)
::: solution
(a) BrE. (b) AmE. (c) both (standard in AmE; one of two British spellings). (d) BrE. (e) AmE. (f) BrE (AmE uses both *catalog* and *catalogue*). (g) both (*exercise* is never spelt with *-ize*). (h) both.

::: exercise Correct a paragraph {level=3}
Correct the capitals and spelling. There are seven capital-letter errors and six spelling errors. Use British spelling.

*last saturday my freind Kenji and i went to a Concert in the city center. The band was from ireland and the music was realy beatiful. after the concert we stoped at a japanese cafe and talked untill midnight.*
::: hint
Spelling: look at *freind*, *center*, *realy*, *beatiful*, *stoped*, *untill*. Capitals: look at the first words of sentences, days, *i*, nationalities, countries and ordinary nouns.
:::
::: solution
*Last Saturday my friend Kenji and I went to a concert in the city centre. The band was from Ireland and the music was really beautiful. After the concert we stopped at a Japanese café and talked until midnight.*

Capitals: *Last*, *Saturday*, *I*, *concert* (small: a common noun), *Ireland*, *After*, *Japanese* — seven changes. Spelling: *friend*, *centre* (British), *really*, *beautiful*, *stopped*, *until*. (*Cafe* without the accent is also correct; *café* keeps the French accent.)
:::

::: exercise Write a short profile {level=3}
Write five sentences about a person you know: their name, where they are from, the languages they speak, when their birthday is, and what they like doing in a particular season. Then check every capital letter against [[#rule-capitals]].
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

*My best friend is Zhang Yiran. She comes from Kunming, in Yunnan Province, but now she lives in Melbourne. She speaks Chinese, English and a little Japanese. Her birthday is on 3 March. In summer she loves hiking in the mountains near her home town.*

Check: names (*Zhang Yiran*, two capitals, given name as one word), places (*Kunming*, *Yunnan Province*, *Melbourne*), languages (*Chinese*, *English*, *Japanese*), months (*March*) have capitals; the season (*summer*) and common nouns (*mountains*, *home town*) do not. (American English would write *March 3*.)
