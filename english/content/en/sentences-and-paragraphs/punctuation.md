Here is the start of an email typed by a student who forgot to switch her keyboard from Chinese to English:

> Hello，my name is Li Hua。I like reading、music and sport。My favourite book is《The Little Prince》。

The words are correct, but an English reader sees at once that the punctuation is not English. The commas and full stops are too wide and sit in the wrong place, the mark 、 does not exist in English, there are no spaces after the commas, and English does not use 《》 for titles. Corrected, it looks like this:

> Hello, my name is Li Hua. I like reading, music and sport. My favourite book is *The Little Prince*.

Punctuation is the part of writing that the reader does not hear but sees all the time. This lesson goes through the English marks one by one — end marks, the comma, the apostrophe, the colon and semicolon, dashes and hyphens, quotation marks and brackets — with the uses that a B1 writer needs, the differences between British and American practice, and the differences from Chinese punctuation.

## Typing English punctuation

::: theorem Half-width marks and spacing {#rule-typing}
In English text:

1. Use the **English (half-width) marks**: `, . ; : ? ! ' " ( )`, not the full-width Chinese marks `，。；：？！“”（）`.
2. Put **no space before** a comma, full stop, semicolon, colon, question mark or exclamation mark, and **one space after** it (unless the line or paragraph ends there).
3. Brackets and quotation marks have a space **outside** them and none **inside**: *(see below)*, *"Hello," she said.*
4. Use one space after a full stop, not two. (Two spaces were a typewriter habit; most current style guides, including *The Chicago Manual of Style*, recommend one.)
:::

Each Chinese mark has a place where English uses something else:

| Chinese | English | example |
|---|---|---|
| 。 | . | *I live in Hefei.* |
| ， | , | *Yes, I do.* |
| 、 (enumeration comma) | , | *red, white and blue* |
| ； | ; | *It was late; we left.* |
| ： | : | *one rule: be on time* |
| “” | "" or '' (see below) | *"Hello," he said.* |
| 《》 (title marks) | *italics* for books, films, newspapers; quotation marks for articles, songs, chapters | I'm reading *Jane Eyre*. |
| …… (six dots) | … (three dots) | *Well … maybe.* |
| —— (two-character dash) | — or – (see "Dashes") | *She was — at last — ready.* |
| · (between parts of a foreign name) | a space | 卡尔·马克思 → *Karl Marx* |

## End marks

- The **full stop** (BrE *full stop*, AmE *period*) ends a statement or a polite request: *Please send me the file.*
- The **question mark** ends a direct question: *Where do you live?* Not an indirect question, which is a statement: ✗ *I wonder where he lives?* ✓ *I wonder where he lives.* ✓ *Can you tell me where he lives?* (The last one is a direct question that contains an indirect one.)
- The **exclamation mark** (AmE *exclamation point*) shows strong feeling: *What a beautiful view!* In formal writing it is rare; in emails to friends and in messages it is common, but one is enough: ? *See you tomorrow!!!*

Full stops also appear in some abbreviations. Here British and American English differ a little: British English usually writes *Mr*, *Mrs*, *Ms*, *Dr* and *St* without a full stop (because the last letter of the word is kept), while American English writes *Mr.*, *Mrs.*, *Ms.*, *Dr.* Both write *e.g.* and *i.e.* with stops, though some British publishers leave them out.

## The comma

The comma is the most frequent punctuation mark and the one with the most uses. You have already met three of them in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coordination]] and [[sentences-and-paragraphs/subordination]].

::: theorem Main uses of the comma {#rule-comma}
Use a comma:

1. **between items in a list**: *tea, coffee and juice*;
2. **before *and*, *but*, *or*, *so*, *yet*** joining two independent clauses: *I knocked, but nobody answered.*;
3. **after an introductory part** — an adverbial clause, a longer phrase or a linking word: *When we arrived, …*; *After a long and tiring journey, …*; *However, …*;
4. **around extra information** that could be removed without changing the main meaning — a non-defining relative clause, an apposition, a comment: *My brother, who lives in Shenzhen, is a doctor.* *Mr Lin, our new neighbour, is very friendly.* *The test, in my opinion, was too long.*;
5. **before or after the name of a person you address**: *Thank you, Anna.* *Anna, could you help me?*;
6. **with direct speech**, between the reporting words and the quotation: *She said, "I'm ready."* *"I'm ready," she said.*

Do **not** use a comma between a subject and its verb, between a verb and its object, or alone between two independent clauses.
:::

::: proof
All six uses mark a boundary inside a sentence that the reader needs to see: between the parts of a list, between two clauses, between the background and the main statement, around information that is outside the main structure, around a name that is not part of the sentence's grammar, and between a quotation and the words that report it. Where there is no such boundary — between a subject and its verb — a comma breaks the sentence in the wrong place: ✗ *The man who lives next door, is a doctor.* This error is common when the subject is long, because the writer pauses there when reading aloud; but English commas follow grammar more than breath (Trask 1997). Rule 4 has an important consequence: commas can change the meaning of a relative clause, as the next section shows.
:::

### Commas that change the meaning

Compare:

- *My brother who lives in Shenzhen is a doctor.* — no commas: the clause tells us **which** brother. The writer has more than one brother.
- *My brother, who lives in Shenzhen, is a doctor.* — commas: the clause is extra information about the only brother.

A relative clause without commas is called **defining** (it defines which person or thing); with commas it is **non-defining**. The difference is explained fully in [[complex-sentences/relative-clauses]]. A famous joke shows how a comma for a person you address changes the meaning:

- *Let's eat, Grandma!* — an invitation to Grandma.
- *Let's eat Grandma!* — a very different plan.

::: warning A comma between the subject and the verb
When the subject is long, learners often put a comma after it, where they would pause in speech.

✗ *The students who arrived late, missed the first part of the test.* ✓ *The students who arrived late missed the first part of the test.*

✗ *What surprised me most, was the price.* ✓ *What surprised me most was the price.*

Find the main verb; if a single comma separates it from its subject, remove the comma.
:::

::: example Placing commas {#ex-commas}
Add commas where they are needed, and say which use of [[#rule-comma]] each one shows: *When I visited Chengdu last spring my cousin Zhao Lei who is a chef took me to a restaurant that sells hotpot noodles and dumplings.*
::: solution
1. Find the main clause: *my cousin … took me to a restaurant*. Subject *my cousin*, verb *took*.
2. *When I visited Chengdu last spring* is an introductory adverbial clause: comma after it (use 3).
3. *Zhao Lei* names the cousin. Is it extra or defining? If the writer has only one cousin, or the name is just added information, put it between commas — but the writer may have several cousins, and then *my cousin Zhao Lei* defines which one, with no commas. Here we choose no commas: *my cousin Zhao Lei*.
4. *who is a chef* gives extra information about Zhao Lei, who is already identified by name: non-defining, between commas (use 4).
5. *that sells hotpot, noodles and dumplings* defines the restaurant: no comma before *that*. Inside it there is a list: commas between the items (use 1).

✓ *When I visited Chengdu last spring, my cousin Zhao Lei, who is a chef, took me to a restaurant that sells hotpot, noodles and dumplings.*

Note that there is no comma between *my cousin Zhao Lei, who is a chef,* and *took*: the second comma closes the extra information; it does not separate the subject from the verb.
:::
:::

## The apostrophe

::: theorem The apostrophe {#rule-apostrophe}
The apostrophe (') has two main uses.

1. **Possession**: *'s* after a singular noun or a plural noun that does not end in *-s* (*the teacher's desk*, *the children's toys*, *women's football*); only the apostrophe after a plural ending in *-s* (*the teachers' room*, *my parents' house*).
2. **Contraction**: it shows that letters are missing: *it's* (*it is* / *it has*), *don't*, *I'm*, *she'd*, *they're*.

The apostrophe is **not** used to make ordinary plurals (✗ *apple's for sale*), and **not** in the possessive pronouns *its*, *yours*, *hers*, *ours*, *theirs*.
:::

::: proof
The possessive *'s* comes from an Old English case ending (*-es*), and the apostrophe was added much later by printers. The rule for plurals in *-s* avoids writing *-s's* (*the teachers's room*), which would also be hard to say. For names that end in *-s*, practice varies: *James's car* and *James' car* are both found; many guides (Swan 2016; *The Chicago Manual of Style* 2017) prefer *James's*, which also matches the pronunciation /ˈdʒeɪmzɪz/. *Its* without an apostrophe follows the other possessive pronouns (*his*, *hers*), which never have one, while *it's* is a contraction like *he's*. Confusing *its* and *it's* is one of the commonest spelling errors of native speakers too.
:::

- *The cat hurt **its** paw.* (possessive: no apostrophe)
- ***It's** raining.* (= *it is*) ***It's** been a long day.* (= *it has*)
- *the 1990s* (a decade: no apostrophe is now usual; *the 1990's* is also seen, especially in older American texts)
- *two PhDs*, *three CDs* (no apostrophe in plurals of abbreviations)

Contractions belong to informal and neutral writing. Many teachers and academic style guides ask students not to use them in essays, and formal letters usually avoid them. This is a style convention, not a grammar rule: contractions are fine in emails to friends, in dialogue and in most journalism.

::: example Checking apostrophes {#ex-apostrophe}
Correct the apostrophes in this note: *The Wangs house is next to the Lees'. Its a lovely place and it's garden is full of tomatoe's. Theyre selling some of there vegetable's at the farmers market on Saturday.*
::: solution
1. Look at every word ending in *-s* and ask: is it a plural, a possessive or a contraction?
2. *The Wangs house* — the house of the Wang family (plural *the Wangs*): possessive plural → *the Wangs' house*. *the Lees'* — the house of the Lee family: correct.
3. *Its a lovely place* — *it is*: contraction → *It's*. *it's garden* — possessive pronoun → *its garden*.
4. *tomatoe's* — an ordinary plural: no apostrophe, and the plural of *tomato* is *tomatoes*.
5. *Theyre* — *they are* → *They're*. *there vegetable's* — *there* should be the possessive *their*, and *vegetables* is a plural with no apostrophe.
6. *the farmers market* — a market for or of farmers (plural): *the farmers' market*. (Some names of this kind are written without an apostrophe, as a fixed compound: *a farmers market*; both are found.)

✓ *The Wangs' house is next to the Lees'. It's a lovely place, and its garden is full of tomatoes. They're selling some of their vegetables at the farmers' market on Saturday.*
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence uses apostrophes correctly?
- [ ] *The company changed it's name in 2010.*
- [ ] *We visited my grandparent's farm. They have twenty cow's.*
- [x] *My parents' flat is near the children's hospital.*
- [ ] *Your's sincerely, Wang Fang*
::: solution
*My parents' flat* — plural *parents*, so the apostrophe follows the *-s*; *the children's hospital* — irregular plural *children*, so *'s*. The first should be *its name*; in the second, *grandparents' farm* (two grandparents) and *twenty cows* (an ordinary plural); the fourth should be *Yours sincerely*.

## The colon and the semicolon

::: theorem The colon {#rule-colon}
A colon (:) comes after a **complete clause** and introduces something that explains, completes or lists what the clause promises:

- a list: *You need three things: a passport, a visa and a return ticket.*
- an explanation or result: *The reason was simple: nobody had told him.*
- a quotation, especially a long or formal one.

In running prose, do not put a colon directly after a verb or a preposition: ✗ *You need: a passport, a visa and a ticket.* ✓ *You need a passport, a visa and a ticket.*
:::

After a colon, British English normally continues with a small letter. American practice varies: some guides (APA, for example) use a capital when a complete sentence follows; *The Chicago Manual of Style* uses a small letter unless the colon introduces more than one sentence, a quotation or a speech. Colons are also used in times (*10:30*; British English also writes *10.30*), in titles and subtitles (*Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*), and after the greeting in formal American letters (*Dear Dr. Lee:*), where British English uses a comma or nothing.

::: theorem The semicolon {#rule-semicolon}
A semicolon (;) has three uses:

1. between two **closely related independent clauses**, without a conjunction: *Some people love the city; others can't wait to leave.*
2. before a **linking adverb** such as *however*, *therefore*, *otherwise* that begins a second independent clause: *The flight was cancelled; however, the airline found us seats on the next one.*
3. between the items of a **list when the items contain commas**: *We visited Xi'an, in Shaanxi; Luoyang, in Henan; and Kaifeng, also in Henan.*

A semicolon is never followed by a dependent clause or a phrase: ✗ *We stayed at home; because it was raining.*
:::

::: proof
A semicolon is stronger than a comma and weaker than a full stop. That is why it can join two independent clauses (which a comma cannot) while still showing that they belong together (which a full stop does not). What comes on each side of it should normally be able to stand as a sentence, except in use 3, where it acts as a "super-comma" to keep a complicated list clear. Semicolons are much more common in formal writing than in everyday writing; a learner can write well without them, but should not use them in place of commas. The Chinese semicolon (；) works in a similar way between parallel clauses, though Chinese writers use it more often in lists of clauses than English writers do.
:::

## Dashes, hyphens and brackets

A **hyphen** (-) is short and joins words or parts of words, with no spaces: *a well-known writer*, *twenty-one*, *a ten-minute walk*, *re-enter*.

A **dash** is longer and separates parts of a sentence. Two kinds are used:

- the **em dash** (—), usually written without spaces in American English: *The answer—as everyone knew—was no.*
- the **en dash** (–), usually written with spaces in British English: *The answer – as everyone knew – was no.*

A dash marks an interruption or a strong break, and a pair of dashes can enclose extra information more emphatically than commas. Dashes are common in informal writing; in formal writing, use them sparingly. The en dash without spaces is also used for ranges: *1990–2000*, *pages 10–25*, *the London–Paris train*. In careful writing, write *from 1990 to 2000* or *between 1990 and 2000*, not *from 1990–2000*.

**Brackets** (BrE *brackets*, AmE *parentheses*) enclose extra information that is less important than the main sentence: *The museum (which is free on Sundays) is near the river.* If a whole sentence is in brackets, its full stop goes inside: *(This is explained below.)* Otherwise it goes outside: *… near the river (see the map).*

Commas, dashes and brackets can all enclose extra information; they differ in strength. Commas are neutral, dashes are emphatic, and brackets are quiet, like a whisper aside.

## Quotation marks

::: theorem Quotation marks in British and American English {#rule-quotes}
1. **American English** uses double quotation marks, and single ones for a quotation inside a quotation: *"She said 'thank you' and left."*
2. **British English** often uses single quotation marks, with double ones inside: *'She said "thank you" and left.'* Many British newspapers and some publishers use double marks, as in American English.
3. **Commas and full stops**: in American English they always go **inside** the closing quotation mark: *"I'm tired," she said.* *He called it "a mistake."* In British English they go inside when they belong to the quoted words, and outside when they do not: *He called it 'a mistake'.*
4. **Question and exclamation marks** go inside if they belong to the quotation and outside if they belong to the whole sentence, in both varieties: *She asked, "Are you ready?"* *Did she really say "no"?*
:::

::: proof
These are conventions of printing, not of grammar, and they grew up separately in the two countries. The American rule of putting commas and full stops inside is often explained by the look of the printed line; the British rule follows the logic of what was quoted, which is why it is sometimes called "logical punctuation". Both are described in the standard guides of each country (*The Chicago Manual of Style* 2017; *New Hart's Rules* and Trask 1997 for British practice), and British fiction often places commas inside for dialogue, as American English does. Choose one system and keep to it within a text.
:::

When you write **dialogue**:

- put the spoken words in quotation marks;
- use a comma (not a full stop) between the speech and *she said*, *he asked*, unless the speech ends with *?* or *!*;
- start a new paragraph for each new speaker.

> "Are you coming tonight?" asked Mei.
> "I'm not sure," said Tom. "I have an essay to finish."
> "Come after you've finished, then."

::: example Punctuating dialogue in two styles {#ex-dialogue}
Punctuate this exchange first in American style and then in British style: *where's the station asked the tourist it's just round the corner said Ahmed you can't miss it*
::: solution
1. Find the spoken words: *where's the station*; *it's just round the corner*; *you can't miss it*.
2. Find the reporting words: *asked the tourist*; *said Ahmed*.
3. Add capitals and end marks to the speech: a question mark after the question (inside the quotation marks, because it belongs to it); a comma after *corner*, because *said Ahmed* follows.
4. Ahmed's second sentence is a new sentence in the same speech, so it gets new quotation marks and a capital.
5. Start a new line for the new speaker.

American style:

> "Where's the station?" asked the tourist.
> "It's just round the corner," said Ahmed. "You can't miss it."

British style (single quotation marks):

> 'Where's the station?' asked the tourist.
> 'It's just round the corner,' said Ahmed. 'You can't miss it.'

Here the comma after *corner* goes inside in both styles, because in dialogue it is treated as part of the speech.
:::
:::

::: example From Chinese keyboard to English text {#ex-ime}
Correct the punctuation and spacing: *我的爱好：Reading，swimming、and cooking。I'm reading《Pride and Prejudice》now……It's great！*
::: solution
1. Translate the Chinese heading: 我的爱好 → *My hobbies*. Keep the colon, but make it half-width with a space after it.
2. Replace every full-width mark with its English equivalent: ， → , ； 、 → , ； 。 → . ； ！ → !
3. Add a space after each comma and full stop.
4. Decide the list style. 、 before *and* becomes a comma (with the serial comma) or disappears (without it).
5. Replace 《》 with italics: *Pride and Prejudice*.
6. Replace the six-dot …… with an English ellipsis of three dots, or simply a full stop.
7. Decide the case after the colon: a list of nouns, so a small letter.

✓ My hobbies: reading, swimming and cooking. I'm reading *Pride and Prejudice* now. It's great!

(American style with the serial comma: *reading, swimming, and cooking*.)
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Width and spacing.** Chinese punctuation marks are full-width: each occupies the space of one character and needs no space after it. English marks are half-width and are followed by a space. Chinese input methods produce full-width marks, so always switch to English input before typing English. Mixed text in Chinese documents follows Chinese rules for Chinese sentences and English rules for English ones.
- **The enumeration comma 、.** English has no separate mark for list items; the ordinary comma does both jobs that Chinese gives to 、 and ，.
- **The comma between clauses.** The Chinese comma separates clauses of one sentence (see [[sentences-and-paragraphs/complete-sentences]]); the English comma cannot do this alone.
- **Titles.** Chinese title marks 《》 (and 〈〉 for parts of works) have no English equivalent; English uses italics for whole works and quotation marks for parts (articles, chapters, songs). In handwriting, titles can be underlined.
- **Quotations.** Chinese often introduces a quotation with a colon: 他说：“我累了。” English normally uses a comma: *He said, "I'm tired."* Chinese quotation marks are curly and full-width (“” and ‘’; 「」 in traditional-character texts in Taiwan and Hong Kong); English uses half-width marks, curly or straight.
- **Ellipsis and dash.** The Chinese ellipsis …… has six dots and the Chinese dash —— takes two character spaces; English uses three dots (…) and a single dash (— or –).
- **The full stop.** The Chinese full stop is a small circle (。); the English one is a dot (.), which is also the decimal point (*3.5*). Both languages use a comma to group thousands in numbers (*10,000*).
- **Names.** The middle dot (·) between parts of a transliterated foreign name in Chinese becomes a space in English: 托马斯·哈代 → *Thomas Hardy*.
:::

::: history
English punctuation was settled by printers, editors and grammarians over several centuries, and many of its details — single or double quotation marks, the serial comma — were fixed as the "house styles" of particular publishers. Chinese took a different path. Classical texts were usually written without punctuation; in 1919, Hu Shi, Qian Xuantong, Liu Fu and others proposed a set of new-style marks, drawing partly on Western ones such as the question mark, exclamation mark and quotation marks, and China's Ministry of Education approved them in 1920. In 1951 the government's publishing administration issued 《标点符号用法》 for the new People's Republic, and the rules are now set by the national standard GB/T 15834, first issued in 1995 and revised in 2011.
:::

::: summary
- In English text, use half-width marks with no space before and one space after; never the Chinese full-width marks, 、 or 《》 ([[#rule-typing]]).
- Commas separate list items, come before *and*/*but*/*so* between clauses, follow introductory parts, enclose extra information, set off names of people addressed and separate speech from reporting words; never put one between a subject and its verb ([[#rule-comma]]).
- The apostrophe shows possession (*the teacher's*, *the teachers'*, *the children's*) and contraction (*it's* = *it is*); it never makes plurals, and *its* has none ([[#rule-apostrophe]]).
- A colon follows a complete clause and introduces a list or explanation; a semicolon joins two independent clauses, comes before *however* between clauses, and separates complex list items ([[#rule-colon]], [[#rule-semicolon]]).
- Hyphens join words; dashes separate parts of a sentence (AmE — without spaces, BrE – with spaces); brackets hold quieter extra information.
- American English uses double quotation marks with commas and full stops inside; British English often uses single marks and puts commas and full stops inside only when they belong to the quotation ([[#rule-quotes]]). Choose one system and be consistent.
:::

::: exercise Commas and meaning {level=2}
Explain the difference in meaning between each pair. (a) *The students who had finished left the room.* / *The students, who had finished, left the room.* (b) *I'd like to thank my sister, Lan.* / *I'd like to thank my sister Lan.*
::: solution
(a) Without commas, the clause defines which students left: only those who had finished (others stayed). With commas, the clause adds information about all the students: they had all finished, and they all left.

(b) With the comma, *Lan* can be read in two ways: as the name of the person being addressed (the speaker is talking to Lan and thanking the sister), or as extra information (the writer's only sister, who is called Lan). Without the comma, *Lan* identifies which sister (the writer has more than one). The context decides; if the writer is speaking to Lan, *Thank you, Lan, for …* is clearer.
:::

::: exercise Punctuate the dialogue {level=3}
Punctuate this dialogue in British or American style (say which), with a new line for each speaker: *is this seat free asked Kenji yes go ahead said the woman I'm getting off at the next stop anyway thanks said Kenji*
::: hint
Three speeches; the woman's speech has two sentences. Put a question mark inside the quotation marks after the question.
:::
::: solution
American style:

> "Is this seat free?" asked Kenji.
> "Yes, go ahead," said the woman. "I'm getting off at the next stop anyway."
> "Thanks," said Kenji.

British style uses single quotation marks in the same places: *'Is this seat free?' asked Kenji.* and so on. A comma after *Yes* is usual. Writing *Thanks!* with an exclamation mark is also possible.
:::
:::

::: exercise Correct a notice {level=3}
This notice for an international students' club contains eight punctuation errors. Rewrite it correctly in British style.

*Welcome to the clubs first meeting！Its on Friday at 7.00 pm in Room 12; next to the library. Please bring: a photo, your student card and a pen. Members who's names are on the list, can pick up their cards at the door. For more information, contact Ms. Zhou Yan.*
::: solution
*Welcome to the club's first meeting! It's on Friday at 7.00 pm in Room 12, next to the library. Please bring a photo, your student card and a pen. Members whose names are on the list can pick up their cards at the door. For more information, contact Ms Zhou Yan.*

The errors: (1) *clubs* → *club's* (possession). (2) *！* → *!* (half-width). (3) *Its* → *It's* (= *it is*). (4) *; next to* → *, next to* (a phrase, not a clause, follows). (5) *bring:* → *bring* (no colon after a verb). (6) *who's* → *whose* (possessive; *who's* = *who is*). (7) the comma after *list* separates the subject from its verb: remove it. (8) *Ms.* → *Ms* (British style; *Ms.* is American). *7.00 pm* is the usual British form; American English writes *7:00 p.m.*
