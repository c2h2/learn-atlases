Two students were asked to write about a market. Here is the first paragraph:

> The market is very big and very busy. There are many people and many things. It is very interesting and very lively. I like it very much.

And here is the second:

> Every Sunday morning, the street outside my grandmother's flat turns into a market. By seven o'clock, farmers have laid out piles of green bok choy, red chillies and muddy lotus roots on plastic sheets along the pavement. An old man sells live fish from a blue tank, and the water splashes onto the shoes of anyone who stands too close. The air smells of frying dough sticks and wet earth, and everyone seems to be shouting a price.

Both paragraphs are grammatically correct, and both are about a busy market. The first *tells* the reader that the market is interesting; the second *shows* it, with specific things the reader can see, hear and smell. This is the main skill of description.

Narration — telling what happened — needs a different set of tools: a clear sequence of events, the right past tenses, and linking words of time. This lesson covers both, at paragraph length. It builds on the paragraph structure of [[sentences-and-paragraphs/topic-sentences]] and the ordering and linking of [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]].

## Description

::: definition Description and narration {#def-description}
A **description** says what a place, a person or an object is like at one time: its appearance, sounds, atmosphere and character. A **narrative** tells a sequence of events that happened, usually in the past, in the order that makes them clear. Many real texts mix the two: a story stops to describe a place, and a description of a person includes a short story that shows what they are like.
:::

::: theorem Make descriptions specific {#rule-specific}
In a descriptive paragraph:

1. start with a sentence that gives the **overall impression** or the main point (*The street turns into a market every Sunday*);
2. support it with **specific, concrete details** — exact things, colours, numbers, names, sounds and smells — rather than general adjectives such as *nice*, *interesting*, *beautiful*, *very big*;
3. arrange the details in a clear **spatial order** (near to far, left to right, outside to inside) or by **sense** (sight, then sound, then smell);
4. choose a few details that create one impression, rather than listing everything.
:::

::: proof
General adjectives give the writer's judgement but no evidence for it, so the reader cannot share the experience: *interesting* and *lively* could describe almost anything. Concrete nouns and verbs (*muddy lotus roots*, *the water splashes onto the shoes*) let the reader build a picture and reach the judgement themselves. This advice — often summarised as "show, don't tell" — is a guideline from creative-writing teaching, not a grammar rule: a short *telling* sentence is useful as a topic sentence or a summary, and in a report or an email, plain statements are usually better than vivid detail. Spatial order is one of the patterns introduced in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]].
:::

### Useful language for places

- ***There is / There are*** to introduce things: *There is a small temple at the end of the lane.* Avoid starting every sentence with *There is*.
- **Prepositions of place**: *at the end of*, *next to*, *opposite*, *behind*, *in front of*, *on the left*, *in the distance*, *above*, *below* (see [[core-grammar/prepositions-time-place]]).
- **Verbs of position and movement** make a description livelier than *be*: *The road **winds** up the hill. Tall buildings **surround** the square. A river **runs** through the town.*
- **The senses**: *It smells of …*, *You can hear …*, *It feels …*, *The air is full of …*.
- **Tense**: the **present simple** for places as they usually are (*Every Sunday, the street turns into a market*), the **past simple** for a place as you saw it on one occasion (*When I arrived, the square was empty*).

::: example Making a description specific {#ex-specific}
Rewrite this description so that it shows rather than tells: *My bedroom is very nice. It has many things. It is quite small but comfortable. I like it.*
::: solution
1. **Find the main impression.** *Small but comfortable* is the real point; *very nice* and *I like it* add nothing.
2. **Replace general words with concrete details.** What makes it comfortable? Choose three or four things: a bed, a window, a desk, shelves.
3. **Choose an order.** Spatial: from the door, moving round the room.
4. **Use verbs other than *be* and *have*.**

*My bedroom is small, but it is the most comfortable place I know. A narrow bed fills the wall opposite the door, covered with a thick blue quilt that my grandmother made. Under the window, my desk holds a lamp, a pile of library books and a cactus that refuses to die. On the shelf above, my old football trophies stand next to my sister's postcards from Chengdu. In the evening, when the lamp is on and the door is shut, the noise of the city seems very far away.*
:::
:::

## Describing people

A description of a person usually covers **appearance**, **character** and often **habits** — and a good description gives an example that shows the character, rather than a list of adjectives.

::: theorem Patterns for describing people {#rule-people}
1. **Appearance with *have***: *She has long black hair and brown eyes.* *He has a beard.* — not ✗ *Her hair is long black* (when describing what she looks like, use *have* + adjectives + noun, or *Her hair is long and black*).
2. ***Be* + adjective** for height, build and age: *He **is** tall and thin.* *She **is** in her sixties.*
3. **Wearing**: *He **was wearing** a grey suit.* (past continuous for one occasion); *She usually **wears** glasses.* (present simple for a habit).
4. ***Look* + adjective** describes appearance or how someone seems: *She **looks** tired.* ***Look like* + noun** compares: *He **looks like** his father.* — not ✗ *He looks like tired.*
5. ***What … like?*** asks for a description of character: *"What's your teacher **like**?" "She's strict but fair."* ***What does … look like?*** asks about appearance.
6. For **character**, support each adjective with an example of behaviour: *My uncle is very generous: when I started university, he gave me his old laptop and refused to take any money for it.*
:::

::: example Describing a person {#ex-person}
Improve this description of a teacher: *Mr Zhou is tall. He is kind, funny and strict. He is a good teacher. Everyone likes him.*
::: solution
1. **Main impression first**: what makes him a good teacher? Say it in the first sentence.
2. **Appearance**: one or two details with *have* and *be*, and something he usually wears or does.
3. **Character**: keep two adjectives and give each an example of behaviour, instead of listing three.
4. **End** with the effect on the students, which replaces *Everyone likes him*.

*Mr Zhou, my maths teacher at secondary school, was the strictest teacher I ever had, and also the kindest. He was tall and thin, with grey hair, and he always wore the same brown jacket. He never let anyone hand in homework late, and he gave a short test every Monday. But if you stayed after class with a question, he would explain it again and again, drawing diagrams on scraps of paper until you understood. Many of his former students still send him cards at New Year.*
:::
:::

### The order of adjectives

When two or more adjectives come before a noun, English tends to put them in a fixed order:

| opinion | size | age | shape | colour | origin | material | purpose | noun |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *beautiful* | *small* | *old* | *round* | *red* | *Chinese* | *wooden* | *tea* | *table* |

So: *a lovely old red bicycle*, *a small Japanese restaurant*, *a large plastic shopping bag* — not ✗ *a red old lovely bicycle*.

::: warning How strict is the adjective order?
The order in the table is a strong tendency that native speakers follow without thinking, and learners' grammars describe it in similar terms (Swan 2016). But it is not an absolute rule:

- Real descriptions rarely put more than **two or three** adjectives before a noun. A string such as *a beautiful small old round red Chinese wooden table* is grammatical in principle but never written in practice. Put the rest after the noun or in another sentence: *a small round table, made of red Chinese wood*.
- Adjectives of the **same type** are separated by commas or *and*: *a warm, friendly smile*; *a black and white cat*.
- The order can change for emphasis: *a big beautiful house* and *a beautiful big house* are both common.

Learn the order as a guide for two or three adjectives, not as a list to fill.
:::

::: quiz
Which is the most natural?
- [ ] *a wooden old small box*
- [x] *a small old wooden box*
- [ ] *an old wooden small box*
- [ ] *a small wooden old box*
::: solution
Size (*small*) comes before age (*old*), which comes before material (*wooden*). The others are understandable but sound wrong to most speakers.
:::
:::

## Narration

::: theorem Tenses in narrative {#rule-narrative-tenses}
In a story about the past:

1. use the **past simple** for the main events, in the order they happened: *I **got** off the train, **found** a taxi and **gave** the driver the address.*
2. use the **past continuous** for background actions in progress when the main events happened: *It **was raining**, and people **were hurrying** home.*
3. use the **past perfect** for an event that happened **before** the time of the story, when you need to make the earlier time clear: *When I reached the station, the last train **had** already **left**.*
4. keep to the past. Do not move into the present unless you are deliberately using the present to make a story vivid, which is common in spoken stories and jokes (*So I'm standing at the bus stop, and this man comes up to me …*). Do not mix the two without reason.
:::

::: proof
The past simple moves a story forward: each verb is a new event. The past continuous does not move the story; it sets the scene in which events happen. The past perfect lets the writer go back in time without changing the order of the main sequence. If every verb is past simple, the reader cannot tell what was background and what was an event; if the writer slips between past and present, the reader loses track of the time. These forms are taught in detail in [[core-grammar/past-simple]] and [[tense-and-aspect/narrative-tenses]]; this lesson uses them at paragraph length. The past perfect is needed only when the order would otherwise be unclear: *After I had finished dinner, I went out* and *After I finished dinner, I went out* are both correct, because *after* already shows the order.
:::

### Linking words of time

- **Sequence**: *first*, *then*, *next*, *after that*, *later*, *finally*.
- **Time clauses**: *when*, *while*, *as*, *as soon as*, *before*, *after*, *until*: *As soon as the doors opened, everyone rushed in.* (see [[sentences-and-paragraphs/subordination]]).
- **Time phrases**: *at first*, *a few minutes later*, *the next morning*, *by the time we arrived*, *in the end*.
- **Sudden events**: *suddenly*, *all of a sudden*, *just then*.

Vary these, and remember that the most natural link is often a time clause, not a word at the start of every sentence.

::: warning *Then … then … then* and *suddenly*
A common weakness in learners' stories is a chain of short sentences all beginning with *Then*, or *And then*:

✗ *I woke up late. Then I ran to the station. Then I missed the train. Then I called my boss. Then he was angry.*

Combine events into longer sentences with time clauses and participles, and keep *then* for real changes of stage:

✓ *I woke up late and ran to the station, but the train was just leaving as I reached the platform. When I called my boss to explain, he was not pleased.*

*Suddenly* has the opposite problem: when every event is sudden, none of them is. Use it once, for an event that really is unexpected.

Note also *in the end* (‘after a long time or many changes’), *at the end* (‘at the final point of something’: *at the end of the film*) and *at last* (‘after a long wait’): *We waited for hours. **At last** the bus came.* / *We argued for a long time, but **in the end** we agreed.*
:::

### The shape of a narrative

A short narrative paragraph usually follows a recognisable shape: it **sets the scene** (who, where, when), describes the **events** that build up to a problem or a high point, says how it was **resolved**, and often ends with a **comment** on what it meant.

::: widget graph
title: The shape of a short narrative
nodes: orientation@0.4,1; complicating action@2.2,2.6; high point@4,4; resolution@5.8,2.6; coda@7.6,1
edges: orientation>complicating action; complicating action>high point; high point>resolution; resolution>coda
caption: A simplified version of the structure that Labov and Waletzky (1967) found in spoken personal stories. The orientation says who, where and when; the complicating action is the series of events; the high point is the moment of greatest tension; the resolution says what finally happened; the coda brings the listener back to the present (*I've never forgotten it*). Throughout the story, the teller also shows why it matters — what Labov called evaluation. Click a stage to highlight it.
:::

::: example Writing a narrative paragraph {#ex-narrative}
Use these notes to write a narrative paragraph of about 100 words: *last winter — Harbin — visit friend — train arrives late at night — phone battery dead — no address — taxi driver — kind — lets me use his phone — friend comes to station — never forget.*
::: solution
1. **Orientation**: who, where, when — past simple and past continuous for the scene.
2. **Complicating action**: the late arrival and the dead battery — the problem. Use the past perfect for the address you had not written down.
3. **High point and resolution**: the taxi driver's help.
4. **Coda**: a final comment.
5. **Link** with time clauses rather than *then*.

*Last winter I went to Harbin to visit a friend from university. My train was delayed by snow, and it was nearly midnight when it finally arrived. As I walked out of the station, I realised that my phone battery was dead, and I had not written my friend's address anywhere else. It was minus twenty degrees, and I was starting to panic when a taxi driver asked if I needed help. He lent me his phone and waited with me in his warm car until my friend arrived. He would not take any money. I have never forgotten his kindness.*
:::
:::

::: example Choosing the tenses {#ex-tenses}
Put the verbs in brackets into the past simple, past continuous or past perfect: *When I (arrive) at the party, most of the guests already (leave). A few people (dance) in the kitchen, and someone (play) the guitar. I (look) for my friend Ella, but she (go) home an hour before.*
::: solution
1. **Main events** (past simple): *arrived*, *looked*.
2. **Before the time of the story** (past perfect): *had already left*, *had gone* — these happened before I arrived.
3. **Background in progress** (past continuous): *were dancing*, *was playing*.

*When I **arrived** at the party, most of the guests **had already left**. A few people **were dancing** in the kitchen, and someone **was playing** the guitar. I **looked** for my friend Ella, but she **had gone** home an hour before.*
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence shows that the film started *before* we arrived?
- [ ] *When we arrived at the cinema, the film started.*
- [x] *When we arrived at the cinema, the film had started.*
- [ ] *When we arrived at the cinema, the film was starting.*
- [ ] *When we were arriving at the cinema, the film started.*
::: solution
The past perfect *had started* puts the start of the film before our arrival. In the first sentence, the film started at the moment we arrived (or just after); in the third, it was just about to start or in the process of starting.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Tense.** Chinese marks time with words (昨天, 已经, 以前) and aspect particles (了, 过, 着), not with tense endings on the verb, so learners sometimes leave English verbs in the present in a story about the past, or switch between present and past. Check every verb in a narrative. Note also that 了 does not always correspond to the English past simple, and 已经 does not always need the past perfect: *I have already eaten* (present perfect) and *I had already eaten when she called* (past perfect) both translate 我已经吃了.
- **然后.** In spoken Chinese, 然后 is often used as a general linker between almost every clause. Translated as *then* every time, it produces the *Then … then …* chain above. Use time clauses (*when*, *after*, *as soon as*) and *and* instead.
- **Describing appearance.** Chinese 她头发很长 (topic + comment) becomes *She has long hair* (with *have*) or *Her hair is long*, not ✗ *She hair is long*. 他长得像他爸爸 is *He looks like his father*; 他看起来很累 is *He looks tired*, without *like*.
- **Four-character expressions.** Descriptive Chinese often uses four-character idioms (人山人海, 车水马龙). English rarely has fixed equivalents; translate the picture with concrete details (*the square was packed with people*) rather than with a translated idiom (✗ *people mountain people sea*).
- **Punctuation.** Long Chinese descriptions joined with commas (一逗到底) need full stops or linking words in English; see [[sentences-and-paragraphs/complete-sentences]].
:::

::: history
The idea that a story has a beginning, a middle and an end goes back to Aristotle's *Poetics* (4th century BC), which said that a complete plot is one that "has a beginning, a middle and an end". In 1863 the German writer Gustav Freytag described the structure of five-act plays as a rise to a climax and a fall, a shape later drawn as "Freytag's pyramid". In 1967 the American linguists William Labov and Joshua Waletzky studied ordinary people's spoken stories of personal experience and found a regular structure (abstract, orientation, complicating action, evaluation, resolution and coda), which has been widely used in the study and teaching of narrative ever since.
:::

## Where this leads

Description and narration appear again in later courses: describing data in [[essay-writing/describing-data]], narrative technique in literature in [[reading-literature/narrative]], and the full system of past tenses in [[tense-and-aspect/narrative-tenses]]. Before you hand in a story or a description, go through the checklist in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/editing]].

::: summary
- Description says what something is like; narration tells what happened ([[#def-description]]).
- Make descriptions specific: one main impression, concrete details instead of general adjectives, a spatial or sensory order ([[#rule-specific]]).
- Describe people with *have* (appearance), *be* (height, age), *look* + adjective and *look like* + noun, and support character adjectives with examples ([[#rule-people]]).
- Two or three adjectives before a noun tend to follow the order opinion–size–age–shape–colour–origin–material–purpose; this is a strong tendency, not a list to fill.
- In narratives, use the past simple for events, the past continuous for background and the past perfect for earlier events; do not drift into the present ([[#rule-narrative-tenses]]).
- Link events with time clauses and phrases; avoid *Then … then …* chains and overusing *suddenly*.
- A short narrative usually moves from orientation through events to a high point, a resolution and a comment.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Show, don't tell {level=1}
Replace each general sentence with one or two sentences of specific detail. (a) *The weather was bad.* (b) *The restaurant was crowded.* (c) *My grandfather is very active.*
::: solution
Possible answers: (a) *A cold wind blew rain sideways across the street, and my umbrella turned inside out twice.* (b) *Every table was full, and a line of people waited by the door, holding numbered tickets.* (c) *My grandfather is eighty-two, but he swims every morning and still cycles to the market twice a week.*
:::

::: exercise Have, be or look? {level=1}
Complete with the correct form of *have*, *be*, *look* or *look like*. (a) *My sister ___ short curly hair.* (b) *He ___ very tall for his age.* (c) *You ___ tired. Are you OK?* (d) *She ___ her mother — they have the same eyes.* (e) *"What ___ your new boss ___?" "Friendly, but very busy."*
::: solution
(a) *has*. (b) *is*. (c) *look*. (d) *looks like*. (e) *What **is** your new boss **like**?*
:::
:::

::: exercise Adjective order {level=1}
Put the adjectives in a natural order. (a) *a (leather / black / new) jacket* (b) *a (Italian / little / lovely) café* (c) *an (wooden / old / enormous) door* (d) *a (cotton / white / long) dress*
::: solution
(a) *a new black leather jacket* (b) *a lovely little Italian café* (c) *an enormous old wooden door* (d) *a long white cotton dress*
:::
:::

::: exercise Narrative tenses {level=2}
Correct the tense errors. *Last summer I go to Qingdao with my parents. On the first day, we walk along the beach when it start to rain. We run into a small café. The owner just open it that morning, so we are her first customers.*
::: solution
*Last summer I **went** to Qingdao with my parents. On the first day, we **were walking** along the beach when it **started** to rain. We **ran** into a small café. The owner **had** just **opened** it that morning, so we **were** her first customers.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Break the *then* chain {level=2}
Rewrite as two or three sentences, using time clauses instead of *then*: *I got home. Then I opened the door. Then I saw water on the floor. Then I found that a pipe was broken. Then I called a plumber.*
::: solution
A possible answer: *When I got home and opened the door, I saw water on the floor. As soon as I found that a pipe had broken, I called a plumber.*
:::
:::

::: exercise In the end, at the end or at last? {level=2}
Complete each sentence. (a) *We looked everywhere for the keys, and ___ we found them in the fridge.* (b) *There's a café ___ of the street.* (c) *The plane was six hours late. When it ___ landed, everyone clapped.* (d) *I wasn't sure which phone to buy, but ___ I chose the cheaper one.*
::: solution
(a) *in the end* (or *at last*, if the emphasis is on the long wait). (b) *at the end*. (c) *at last* (here *finally* is also natural: *When it finally landed*). (d) *in the end*.
:::

::: exercise Describe a place {level=3}
Write a descriptive paragraph (100–130 words) about a place you know well — a street, a park, a room, a station. Begin with the main impression, use at least four concrete details, follow a spatial or sensory order, and use at least two verbs other than *be* and *have*.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

> The reading room of our university library is the quietest place on campus. Tall windows run along one side, and in the afternoon the sun lays long yellow rectangles across the rows of desks. Each desk has a green lamp and a power socket, and most of them are covered with laptops, water bottles and piles of books. The only sounds are the soft tapping of keyboards, the turning of pages and, now and then, a cough that seems far too loud. At the far end, behind a glass wall, the librarians work at their computers. Even in exam week, when every seat is taken, the room feels calm.

Check: main impression first (*the quietest place on campus*); spatial order (windows → desks → far end); senses (sight, sound); verbs *run*, *lays*, *work*.
:::
:::

::: exercise Tell a story {level=3}
Write a narrative paragraph (100–150 words) about a time when something went wrong but ended well. Include an orientation, a problem, a high point, a resolution and a short comment at the end. Use the past simple, the past continuous and the past perfect at least once each.
::: hint
Plan in five lines first: who/where/when; what went wrong; the worst moment; how it was solved; what you learned or felt.
:::
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

> On the morning of my driving test, everything seemed to be going well. I had practised for months, and the examiner was smiling as we left the test centre. We were driving along a quiet road when a dog ran out in front of the car. I braked hard, and the car stopped just in time, but my hands were shaking so much that I stalled the engine twice at the next junction. I was sure I had failed. When we returned to the centre, however, the examiner told me that I had passed: my quick reaction to the dog had impressed her more than my stalling had worried her. Since then I have always believed that one mistake does not decide everything.

Check: orientation (morning, test); background (*were driving*); earlier event (*had practised*); high point (the dog); resolution (passing); coda (the last sentence).
:::
