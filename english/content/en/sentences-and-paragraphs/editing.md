Here is a paragraph from a student's first draft:

> In my opinion, online class has many advantage. Firstly student can study at home, they don't need to spend time on the bus. Secondly the teacher can record the lesson, so student can watch it again when they don't understand. But online class also have some problem, for example some students is not concentrate.

The ideas are clear, and the paragraph has a topic sentence and an order. But in five lines there are more than ten errors: missing plurals (*advantage*, *problem*), missing articles (*student*), a comma splice (*at home, they don't*), agreement errors (*class … have*, *students is*), a wrong verb form (*is not concentrate*) and missing commas after *Firstly* and *Secondly*. Most of these are errors the writer knows how to correct; they happened because the writer was thinking about ideas, not form.

That is normal. Nobody writes a perfect first draft, in any language. Good writers get their texts right by **editing**: reading the draft again, deliberately, looking for specific problems. This last lesson of the course gives you a method and a checklist for editing your own writing, and shows how to use the feedback you get from teachers and readers.

## Revising and proofreading

::: definition Revising, editing and proofreading {#def-revising}
**Revising** means improving the content and organisation of a draft: the main point, the order of paragraphs, the support, what to add or cut. **Editing** (in the narrow sense) means improving sentences: clarity, grammar, word choice, sentence boundaries. **Proofreading** is the final check for small surface errors — spelling, punctuation, capitals, typing mistakes and formatting. In everyday use, *editing* also covers all three.
:::

::: theorem Edit from large to small {#rule-large-small}
Work through a draft in this order:

1. **Content and purpose**: Does the text answer the question or do the job? Is the main point clear?
2. **Paragraphs**: Does each paragraph have one main idea and a topic sentence? Are they in a sensible order? ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/topic-sentences]], [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]])
3. **Sentences**: Are the sentence boundaries correct? Are the sentences clear?
4. **Grammar and words**: verb forms, agreement, articles, plurals, prepositions, word choice.
5. **Proofreading**: spelling, punctuation, capitals, numbers, layout.

Do separate readings for separate problems, rather than trying to see everything at once.
:::

::: proof
There is no point in polishing the commas in a paragraph that you will later delete, so large changes come first. Reading for one kind of problem at a time works better than reading for everything, because attention is limited: when you read for meaning, your mind corrects small errors automatically and you do not see them. Writing teachers and style guides for both British and American writers recommend this "top-down" order; for longer essays the same process is described in [[essay-writing/revising-editing]], and editing for style in [[style-and-rhetoric/editing-for-style]].
:::

## An editing checklist

::: theorem A checklist for sentences and grammar {#rule-checklist}
When you edit, check these points one at a time:

1. **Sentence boundaries**: every sentence has a subject and a finite verb; no comma splices or run-on sentences; no accidental fragments ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/complete-sentences]]).
2. **Verb forms and tense**: the right tense, kept consistent; *-s* on third-person singular present verbs; correct past forms; no *be* + base form (✗ *is not concentrate*).
3. **Agreement**: the verb agrees with its subject (*students are*, *the class has*), especially when words come between them (*The quality of the rooms **is** …*).
4. **Nouns and articles**: plurals on countable nouns (*many advantages*); a singular countable noun has a determiner (*a*, *the*, *my*, *this*: *a student*, not ✗ *student can study*).
5. **Punctuation**: commas after introductory words and clauses; semicolons and colons used correctly; apostrophes ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/punctuation]]).
6. **Capitals and spelling**: proper nouns, *I*, the start of sentences; commonly confused words (*their/there/they're*, *its/it's*); one variety of spelling, British or American, throughout ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/capitals-spelling]]).
7. **Your own list**: the errors that you personally make most often.
:::

::: proof
These points cover the errors that are both frequent in learners' writing and noticeable to readers. They are also errors that a learner at this level can usually correct alone, once they look for them — unlike, say, collocation or idiom, which need more knowledge. Most English teachers' correction schemes and learner-corpus studies show the same small set of categories again and again: articles, agreement, verb forms, sentence boundaries, prepositions and spelling. Your own list matters most, because each writer's errors are different: one learner always forgets articles; another always writes *it's* for *its*.
:::

::: example Editing the opening paragraph {#ex-edit}
Edit the paragraph at the start of the lesson, using the checklist.
::: solution
1. **Sentence boundaries**: *Firstly student can study at home, they don't need …* is a comma splice. Use *and* or a full stop, or make the second clause a reason: *so they do not need …*.
2. **Agreement**: *online class also have* → *online classes also have*; *some students is* → *some students are* (and in fact the whole phrase needs changing; see 3).
3. **Verb forms**: *is not concentrate* → *cannot concentrate* or *do not concentrate*.
4. **Nouns and articles**: *online class* in general → *online classes*; *many advantage* → *many advantages*; *student can* → *students can*; *some problem* → *some problems*.
5. **Punctuation**: comma after *Firstly* and *Secondly*; a full stop or colon before *for example* rather than a comma.
6. **Style**: *In my opinion* is not needed in a paragraph that clearly gives an opinion, but it is not wrong.

*Online classes have many advantages. Firstly, students can study at home, so they do not need to spend time on the bus. Secondly, the teacher can record the lesson, so students can watch it again if they do not understand something. However, online classes also have some problems. For example, some students find it hard to concentrate.*

The paragraph now has the same ideas, but the reader no longer stops at every line.
:::
:::

## Proofreading techniques

Your eyes see what you meant to write, not what you wrote. These techniques make the text look new:

- **Leave time.** Put the draft away for a few hours or a day. Errors are much easier to see when the text is no longer fresh in your mind.
- **Read aloud**, slowly. You hear missing words, repeated words and sentences that are too long.
- **Print it** or change the font or size. A different layout makes old text look unfamiliar.
- **Read backwards**, sentence by sentence, from the end. This stops you following the meaning and makes you look at each sentence as a sentence.
- **Search** for your typical errors: use the search function to find every *its*, every *which*, every comma before *however*.
- **Check names, numbers and dates** separately against the original source.

::: warning Spelling checkers, grammar checkers and AI tools
Spelling checkers, grammar checkers and AI writing tools catch many errors and are worth using. But:

- A spelling checker does not catch a real word in the wrong place: *their/there*, *form/from*, *quite/quiet*, *lose/loose*.
- Check that the tool is set to the variety you want (UK or US English); otherwise it will "correct" *colour* to *color*, or the reverse.
- Grammar checkers miss some errors and flag some correct sentences. Accept a suggestion only if you understand why it is better.
- If an AI tool rewrites whole sentences, the result may no longer say what you meant, may sound unlike you, and — in a course or an exam — may break the rules on your own work (see [[academic-writing/academic-integrity]]).

Use the tools as a second reader, not as the writer.
:::

::: example Proofreading what the checker missed {#ex-proofread}
This text has passed a spelling checker without any warnings. Find the errors. *Thank you for you're message. I am afraid I will be late for are meeting, because my train leaves quite late. Its no problem if you start without me; I will join you as soon as I can. Please let me no if the room changes.*
::: solution
1. **Read slowly, word by word**, or sentence by sentence from the end, so that the meaning does not carry you past the words.
2. **Stop at every word that has a sound-alike partner**, because these are the errors a spelling checker cannot see. For each one, ask which spelling the meaning needs:
   - *you're* (= *you are*) → *your* (possessive: *your message*);
   - *are* (verb) → *our* (possessive: *our meeting*);
   - *Its* (possessive) → *It's* (= *It is*);
   - *no* → *know* (*let me know*).
3. **Do not change correct words.** *Quite* (‘fairly’) is right here; it is not *quiet*. The semicolon joins two closely related independent clauses correctly.

There are four errors:

*Thank you for your message. I am afraid I will be late for our meeting, because my train leaves quite late. It's no problem if you start without me; I will join you as soon as I can. Please let me know if the room changes.*
:::
:::

::: quiz

- [ ] *I recieved your email yesterday.*
- [ ] *The goverment announced new rules.*
- [x] *I will meat you at the station.*
- [ ] *We had a wonderfull time.*
::: solution
*Meat* is a real word, so a spelling checker does not flag it, even though the writer meant *meet*. The other three are misspellings of *received*, *government* and *wonderful*, which any spelling checker will mark.

## Rules you do not need to follow

When you edit, you may remember "rules" from school that are not rules of English at all. Changing correct sentences to obey them wastes time and can make the writing worse.

::: theorem Zombie rules {#rule-zombie}
The following are **not** errors in standard English, in either British or American usage, and you do not need to "correct" them:

1. **Starting a sentence with *And*, *But* or *So***: *But the plan failed.* This is common in good writing; very formal academic texts use it less, and *However* is an alternative, not a correction.
2. **Ending a sentence with a preposition**: *Who did you speak to?* *This is the book I was looking for.* Changing these to *To whom did you speak?* makes them very formal, not more correct.
3. **Splitting an infinitive**: *to really understand*, *to quickly check*. Put the adverb where it sounds natural and is clear.
4. **Using *I* in writing**: personal emails, descriptions, narratives and many essays use *I*. Some academic genres prefer to avoid it; that is a matter of style for the genre ([[academic-writing/academic-style]]).
5. **Never repeating a word**: repeating a key term is often clearer than changing it ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]]).

Modern usage guides, such as Swan (2016) and Butterfield (2015), accept the first three as standard English; the last two are questions of style, not of grammar.
:::

::: warning Do not over-correct
Some learners, after being corrected, start to apply the correction everywhere. Common examples:

- After learning about comma splices, they put a full stop before every *and* or *but*. Two clauses joined by a comma **and** a conjunction are correct: *It was late, but we kept working.*
- After learning that *the* is often missing, they add it to general plural and uncountable nouns: ✗ *The students need the sleep.* (meaning students in general) ✓ *Students need sleep.*
- After learning to avoid informal words, they replace every *but* with *nevertheless* and every *so* with *consequently*.

Each correction applies to a specific situation. Before you change something, ask what exactly the problem was.
:::

## Using feedback

Many teachers mark errors with codes rather than correcting them, so that the writer does the correction and remembers it.

| code | meaning | example | correction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sp** | spelling | *recieve* | *receive* |
| **WW** | wrong word | *I learned him English.* | *I taught him English.* |
| **WF** | wrong form | *She is very confidence.* | *She is very confident.* |
| **T** | tense | *Yesterday I go home.* | *Yesterday I went home.* |
| **Agr** | agreement | *The price are high.* | *The price is high.* |
| **Art** | article | *I bought car.* | *I bought a car.* |
| **P** | punctuation | *It rained, we stayed in.* | *It rained, so we stayed in.* |
| **^** | something missing | *She ^ a doctor.* | *She is a doctor.* |
| **WO** | word order | *I like very much tea.* | *I like tea very much.* |
| **?** | meaning unclear | | rewrite the sentence |

Codes differ between teachers and schools; check the list your teacher uses.

::: theorem Learning from feedback {#rule-feedback}
1. **Correct every marked error yourself** before you look at any model answer.
2. **Ask** about any mark you do not understand.
3. **Keep an error log**: for each piece of work, note your errors by type, with the wrong and the corrected form.
4. **Look for patterns**: after three or four pieces of work, the most frequent types become your personal checklist (point 7 of [[#rule-checklist]]).
5. **Use the log when you edit** your next text: search for those errors first.
:::

::: proof
Corrections that the writer makes are processed more actively than corrections that the writer only reads, and research on written corrective feedback suggests that it can improve accuracy, especially when it focuses on a limited number of error types (see Bitchener and Ferris 2012 for a review of the research). The value of feedback also depends on what the writer does with it: a corrected essay that goes into a drawer unread changes little. An error log turns individual corrections into a small number of patterns that the writer can look for.
:::

::: example Correcting from codes {#ex-codes}
Correct the errors marked with codes. *Last year I (T) visit Xi'an with my family. We saw (Art) Terracotta Warriors and (WW) made a lot of photos. The food was very (WF) delight, and the people (Agr) was friendly.*
::: solution
1. **T** — the tense: the trip was last year → *visited*.
2. **Art** — an article: the Terracotta Warriors are a unique, named group → *the Terracotta Warriors*.
3. **WW** — wrong word: English *takes* photos (*make* is a common error from other languages) → *took a lot of photos*.
4. **WF** — wrong form: an adjective is needed after *very*, not a noun → *delightful* (or *delicious*, for food).
5. **Agr** — agreement: *people* is plural → *were*.

*Last year I visited Xi'an with my family. We saw the Terracotta Warriors and took a lot of photos. The food was delicious, and the people were friendly.*
:::
:::

::: example Building an error log {#ex-log}
A student has corrected three essays. Her errors were: *people is* (×2), *the life is* (×1), *he go* (×3), *I very like* (×2), *informations* (×2), *she don't* (×2), comma splices (×4), *advices* (×1). What should go on her personal checklist?
::: solution
1. **Group the errors by type**, not by word.
   - Agreement (*people is*, *he go*, *she don't*): 2 + 3 + 2 = **7**.
   - Comma splices: **4**.
   - Uncountable nouns with *-s* (*informations*, *advices*): 2 + 1 = **3**.
   - Word order (*I very like*): **2**.
   - Article with a general noun (*the life*): **1**.
2. **Rank them.** Agreement comes first, then sentence boundaries, then uncountable nouns.
3. **Write each type as a check** she can do on her next text:
   - find each subject and check that its verb agrees, especially third-person *-s* and *doesn't*;
   - check each comma between two clauses;
   - search for *-s* on *information*, *advice*, *equipment*, *homework*;
   - check the position of *very much* (*I like it very much*).
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Errors that come from Chinese.** Chinese has no articles, no plural endings on most nouns, no subject–verb agreement and no tense endings, so these are the points where Chinese-speaking learners most often need to check their English, even at advanced levels. This does not mean the learner does not know the rule; it means the form does not come automatically and needs a separate reading.
- **Full-width punctuation.** If you type in a Chinese input mode, English text may contain full-width Chinese punctuation: ，。：；“”（）！？. These look different (they take a full character's width) and can cause problems in documents and online forms. Use half-width punctuation in English (, . : ; " " ( ) ! ?), followed by one space. In particular, English has no 、 (the enumeration comma) and no 《》 (title marks): use a comma between items and italics or quotation marks for titles. There is no space before English punctuation marks (✗ *Hello !*).
- **Numbers and units.** English writes a space between a number and most units (*5 km*, *20 kg*), no space before *%* (*40%*), and uses commas, not spaces, to group thousands in ordinary writing (*12,000*). Chinese 万 must be converted: 3万 = *30,000*, 1.5亿 = *150 million*.
- **一逗到底.** A Chinese paragraph may legitimately run several clauses together with commas; in English, each comma between two independent clauses needs checking for a comma splice.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct and does **not** need editing?
- [ ] *She don't like coffee, she prefers tea.*
- [x] *Which room is the meeting in?*
- [ ] *The informations was very useful.*
- [ ] *He is very interesting in history.*
::: solution
*Which room is the meeting in?* is natural, standard English; ending with a preposition is not an error. The others need editing: *She doesn't like coffee; she prefers tea.* (agreement and a comma splice); *The information was very useful.* (uncountable noun); *He is very interested in history.* (wrong form).
:::
:::

::: history
Several of the "rules" that editors still apply come from a small number of writers. In 1672 the poet John Dryden criticised Ben Jonson for ending a sentence with a preposition, and in 1762 Robert Lowth's *A Short Introduction to English Grammar* said that the construction suited conversation but that putting the preposition before the relative was "more graceful" in a formal style; later teachers turned this preference into a ban. The objection to the split infinitive appeared in the nineteenth century. Henry Fowler's *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage* (1926) called the preposition rule a "superstition", and in 1981 the American writing scholar Joseph Williams, in an essay called "The Phenomenology of Error", showed that readers often fail to notice "errors" of this kind even in the texts of the writers who condemned them. Linguists who write about usage now call such rules *zombie rules*: they have been declared dead many times, but they keep coming back.
:::

- Revising improves content and organisation; editing improves sentences; proofreading catches small surface errors ([[#def-revising]]).
