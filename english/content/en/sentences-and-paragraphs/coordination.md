Read this short invitation:

*We've invited the twins, Li Na and Zhou Bin.*

How many people are coming? Perhaps two — the twins are called Li Na and Zhou Bin. Perhaps four — the twins, and also Li Na, and also Zhou Bin. One small comma would decide: *We've invited the twins, Li Na, and Zhou Bin* can only mean four people. Many British writers would not use that comma; most American publishers would. Both habits are correct, and this lesson shows when the comma matters.

Small words such as *and*, *but*, *or* and *so* are among the most frequent words in English, and they do a big job: they join ideas of equal importance. This lesson explains these **coordinating conjunctions**, the punctuation around them, and the rule of **parallel structure** that keeps the joined parts in balance. It also looks at an old school rule — "never begin a sentence with *And* or *But*" — and at what careful writers actually do.

## Coordinating conjunctions

::: definition Coordination {#def-coordination}
**Coordination** joins two or more parts of equal grammatical rank: two words, two phrases or two independent clauses. The words that join them are **coordinating conjunctions**. The main ones are *and*, *but*, *or*, *so*, *yet*, *nor* and *for*.
:::

Coordination can join words, phrases and clauses:

- Words: *bread **and** butter*, *slowly **but** carefully*, *tea **or** coffee*
- Phrases: *in the kitchen **or** in the garden*, *She washed the dishes **and** cleaned the floor.*
- Clauses: *I called her, **but** she didn't answer.*

American teachers often teach the seven conjunctions with the memory word **FANBOYS**: *for*, *and*, *nor*, *but*, *or*, *yet*, *so*. Grammarians argue about whether *so* and *for* really belong in the group (Quirk et al. 1985 treat them as borderline cases), but for punctuation they behave like the others, and that is what matters for a writer.

| conjunction | meaning | example |
|---|---|---|
| *and* | addition; also sequence | *She plays the piano **and** sings.* *He opened the door **and** went in.* |
| *but* | contrast | *The flat is small, **but** it's very bright.* |
| *or* | alternative | *We can walk, **or** we can take the bus.* |
| *so* | result | *It was raining, **so** we stayed in.* |
| *yet* | surprising contrast (more formal than *but*) | *The plan was simple, **yet** it worked perfectly.* |
| *nor* | negative addition ‘and not’ | *She didn't phone, **nor** did she write.* |
| *for* | reason (formal, literary) | *He said nothing, **for** he was afraid.* |

In everyday writing, *and*, *but*, *or* and *so* do almost all the work. *Yet* is common in formal writing; *for* in this sense is rare outside literature and formal prose, and *because* is the normal word.

::: warning *Nor* needs question order
After *nor* at the start of a clause, the subject and the auxiliary change places, as in a question:

✗ *I don't eat meat, nor I eat fish.* ✓ *I don't eat meat, nor **do I** eat fish.*

A simpler choice in everyday English is *and … not* or *or*: ✓ *I don't eat meat or fish.*
:::

::: quiz
Which conjunction fits best? *The hotel was expensive, ___ the rooms were dirty and noisy.*
- [ ] *so*
- [x] *yet*
- [ ] *or*
- [ ] *for*
::: solution
*Yet* (or *but*) shows a surprising contrast: you expect an expensive hotel to be clean and quiet. *So* would mean the rooms were dirty *because* the hotel was expensive, *or* gives an alternative and *for* gives a reason, which make no sense here.
:::
:::

## The comma before a conjunction

::: theorem Commas with coordinating conjunctions {#rule-comma-coord}
1. When a conjunction joins **two independent clauses**, careful writers usually put a comma before it: *The film was long, **but** nobody left early.* The comma is often left out when both clauses are short and the meaning is clear: *I knocked and she opened the door.*
2. When a conjunction joins **two words or phrases** — for example two verbs with the same subject — there is normally **no comma**: *She stood up and left the room.*
3. A comma alone never joins two independent clauses (see [[sentences-and-paragraphs/complete-sentences]]).
:::

::: proof
The comma before *and* or *but* tells the reader that a new clause, with its own subject, is starting. Compare *I met the manager and her assistant* with *I met the manager, and her assistant showed me the office.* Without the comma the reader may first take *her assistant* as a second object of *met* and then have to go back. When the second part has no subject of its own (*She stood up and left*), there is no new clause, so there is nothing to signal. Usage guides for both varieties describe these patterns (Swan 2016; *The Chicago Manual of Style* 2017); they also note that the comma between short clauses is optional, so writers differ, and a missing comma there is not an error.
:::

Compare:

- *I finished my homework, and my sister made dinner.* — two subjects, two clauses: comma.
- *I finished my homework and made dinner.* — one subject (*I*), two verbs: no comma.
- *We waited for an hour, but the bus never came.* — two clauses: comma.
- *We waited for an hour but didn't see a bus.* — one subject: no comma.

? *She finished her work, and went home.* — this comma is unusual, because *went home* has no subject. Some writers use it to make a pause for effect, but in ordinary writing leave it out: ✓ *She finished her work and went home.*

::: example Comma or no comma? {#ex-comma-or-not}
Decide whether each sentence needs a comma before the conjunction. (a) *Li Wei speaks English and Spanish.* (b) *Li Wei speaks English and his brother speaks Spanish.* (c) *The shop was open but there was nobody inside.* (d) *She checked the address but still got lost.*
::: solution
1. For each sentence, look at what comes after the conjunction. Does it have its own subject and finite verb?
2. (a) *Spanish* is a noun, a second object of *speaks*. No new clause: **no comma**.
3. (b) *his brother speaks Spanish* has a subject and a verb: a second independent clause. **Comma**: *Li Wei speaks English, and his brother speaks Spanish.*
4. (c) *there was nobody inside* has its own subject (*there*) and verb (*was*). **Comma** is usual: *The shop was open, but there was nobody inside.* (It is short, so some writers would leave it out.)
5. (d) *still got lost* has no subject; it shares *she*. **No comma**.
:::
:::

## Lists and the serial comma

When three or more items are joined, English writes commas between them and a conjunction (usually *and* or *or*) before the last one.

::: theorem Lists of three or more {#rule-list}
In a list of three or more items, separate the items with commas and put *and* or *or* before the last item: *apples, pears **and** plums*. A comma before that final *and* or *or* — *apples, pears, **and** plums* — is called the **serial comma** (or **Oxford comma**).

- In **American English** most style guides (*The Chicago Manual of Style*, APA, MLA) use the serial comma; newspapers following the Associated Press stylebook usually do not.
- In **British English** it is usually left out, though some publishers, including Oxford University Press, use it.
- In both varieties, writers add it when a list would otherwise be **ambiguous**, and leave it out when it would cause confusion.

Either habit is correct; use one consistently.
:::

::: proof
The serial comma is a matter of house style, not grammar, which is why guides disagree. Its supporters point to cases like the invitation at the start of the lesson, where the last two items could be read as an explanation of the first (*the twins, Li Na and Zhou Bin* — "the twins, namely Li Na and Zhou Bin"). Its opponents point out that the extra comma can create a different ambiguity: in *I met my teacher, a doctor, and a lawyer*, the comma after *teacher* could suggest that the teacher *is* a doctor. In each case the careful writer reads the sentence as a stranger would and, if needed, rewrites it: *I met a doctor, a lawyer and my teacher.*
:::

Some more points about lists:

- **Don't put a comma before the first item or after the last one.** ✗ *I bought, rice, eggs and milk.*
- **Don't repeat *and* between every item** in ordinary prose: ? *I bought rice and eggs and milk.* This is possible in speech or for a special effect (to suggest a long, tiring list), but the normal written form is *rice, eggs and milk*.
- **Don't leave out the final *and*** in ordinary prose: ✗ *I bought rice, eggs, milk.* A list without *and* is a deliberate stylistic effect in literature and speeches, not normal writing.
- **Items that are long or contain commas** can be separated with semicolons: *The prizes went to Wang Fang, from Chengdu; Ana Silva, from Lisbon; and Tom Baker, from Leeds.* ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/punctuation]] explains this use.)

::: example Translating a Chinese list {#ex-list}
Write this sentence in English, once in British style and once in American style: 这家店卖书、文具、咖啡和蛋糕。
::: solution
1. Find the items: 书 *books*, 文具 *stationery*, 咖啡 *coffee*, 蛋糕 *cakes*. Chinese separates them with the enumeration comma 、 and puts 和 before the last one.
2. Replace each 、 with an English comma followed by a space, and keep *and* before the last item.
3. British style, usually without the serial comma: *This shop sells books, stationery, coffee and cakes.*
4. American style, usually with the serial comma: *This shop sells books, stationery, coffee, and cakes.*
5. Check for ambiguity. Could *coffee and cakes* be read as one item? Here it makes no difference to the meaning, so either style is fine.
:::
:::

## Meanings and pitfalls: *and*, *but*, *or*, *so*

### *And*

*And* adds. Between clauses it often also suggests that the second event comes **after** the first, or happens **because** of it:

- *She took out her key **and** opened the door.* (first … then)
- *He missed the last bus **and** had to walk home.* (because of that)

So the order of clauses joined by *and* is not always free: *She opened the door and took out her key* describes a different, stranger event.

### *But*

*But* shows contrast: the second part is in some way unexpected after the first.

- *The test was hard, **but** most students passed.*
- *It's an old car **but** a very reliable one.*

Don't use *but* when there is no contrast: ? *Shanghai is a big city, but it has a lot of people.* (There is no contrast: a big city is expected to have many people. Use *and*, or a full stop.)

### *Or*

In a statement, *or* gives alternatives. In a negative sentence, English uses *or* where many learners expect *and*:

- *I don't drink tea **or** coffee.* — I drink neither of them.
- ? *I don't drink tea and coffee.* — this means I don't drink them **together**, as a mixture; it is not the normal way to say that you drink neither.

*Or* can include both alternatives (**inclusive** *or*) or exclude one of them (**exclusive** *or*). The context usually decides:

- *Applicants should speak English **or** French.* — an applicant who speaks both is welcome (inclusive).
- *Would you like tea **or** coffee?* — you are expected to choose one (exclusive).

To make the exclusive meaning clear, English uses *either … or*: *You can **either** pay now **or** pay on delivery.* To make the inclusive meaning clear, writers add *or both*.

:::

### *So*

*So* gives a result: *It was late, **so** we took a taxi.* The cause comes first, the result after *so*. Don't confuse it with *so that*, which gives a purpose: *We left early **so that** we could get good seats.* (see [[sentences-and-paragraphs/subordination]]).

::: warning *Because* … *so* in one sentence
Chinese often uses 因为 and 所以 together. English uses **one** of them:

✗ *Because it was raining, so we stayed at home.*

✓ *Because it was raining, we stayed at home.* ✓ *It was raining, so we stayed at home.*

The same is true of *although* … *but*; both are explained in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/subordination]].
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence uses the conjunction correctly?
- [ ] *I don't like horror films and thrillers.*
- [ ] *Because I was hungry, so I made a sandwich.*
- [x] *I was hungry, so I made a sandwich.*
- [ ] *She is a teacher, but she works in a school.*
::: solution
*I was hungry, so I made a sandwich* gives a cause and a result with one conjunction. The first should be *I don't like horror films or thrillers*; the second uses *because* and *so* together; the fourth uses *but* where there is no contrast (teachers are expected to work in schools).
:::
:::

## Starting a sentence with *And* or *But*

Many learners were told at school never to begin a sentence with *And* or *But*. This is not a rule of English grammar. Writers have begun sentences with these words for more than a thousand years, from Old English prose to today's newspapers, novels and academic journals. R. W. Burchfield, in the third edition of Fowler's usage dictionary (1996), noted that standard authors had done so since Anglo-Saxon times, and current guides (Swan 2016; Butterfield 2015; *The Chicago Manual of Style* 2017) agree that it is acceptable.

::: remark What careful writers do
- They use a sentence-initial *But* to give a contrast more weight than a comma would: *The plan looked perfect on paper. **But** nobody had asked the people who would use it.*
- They use a sentence-initial *And* to add a point with emphasis, or to keep a story moving: *The rain stopped. **And** then, at last, the sun came out.*
- They do not put a comma after *And* or *But* at the start of a sentence, unless another phrase follows that needs commas: ✓ *But the shop was closed.* ✓ *But, to our surprise, the shop was closed.*
- They do not overdo it. Ten sentences in a row starting with *And* sound like a child's story.

The old advice probably arose because young writers produced long chains of *and … and … and*, and teachers wanted them to vary their sentences. Some teachers and examiners still follow it, so in an exam you may decide to use *However* instead of *But* — but that is a choice about your reader, not a rule of the language.
:::

## Parallel structure

::: definition Parallel structure {#def-parallel}
**Parallel structure** (or **parallelism**) means that the parts joined by a coordinating conjunction have the same grammatical form: two nouns, two *-ing* forms, two *to*-infinitives, two clauses, and so on.
:::

::: theorem Keep coordinated parts parallel {#rule-parallel}
Parts joined by *and*, *but*, *or* and by the pairs *both … and*, *either … or*, *neither … nor* and *not only … but also* should have the same grammatical form. With the pairs, each word of the pair goes directly before one of the two parallel parts.
:::

::: proof
Coordination tells the reader that the joined parts are equal. When they have different forms, the reader has to stop and re-read, and the sentence sounds careless: ✗ *She likes swimming, to read and cooking.* The repair is to choose one form for all the parts: *She likes swimming, reading and cooking.* With the pairs, the position of the first word tells the reader what kind of part to expect: after *not only French* the reader expects another noun, after *not only speaks* another verb. Reference grammars describe the strict pattern (Quirk et al. 1985), and usage guides (Swan 2016) note that a mismatched position, as in *She not only speaks French but also German*, is common in speech and informal writing; in careful writing, it is better to match.
:::

Some typical repairs:

- ✗ *The job involves answering the phone, to write emails and meetings with clients.* ✓ *The job involves answering the phone, writing emails and meeting clients.*
- ✗ *He is tall, handsome and has a lot of money.* ✓ *He is tall, handsome and rich.* ✓ *He is tall and handsome, and he has a lot of money.*
- ✗ *I prefer walking to take the bus.* ✓ *I prefer walking to taking the bus.*
- ? *She not only speaks French but also German.* ✓ *She speaks not only French but also German.*
- ? *You can either come with us or stay here alone.* — this one is fine: *come* and *stay* are both verbs after *either* and *or*.

With *either … or* and *neither … nor* as the subject, the verb usually agrees with the nearer subject in formal writing: *Neither my brother nor my parents **are** coming.* *Either the teacher or the students **have** made a mistake.* Agreement is explained in [[nouns-and-articles/agreement]].

::: example Making a sentence parallel {#ex-parallel}
Find the problem and correct it: *In my free time I enjoy to go to the cinema, cooking for friends and I also read novels.*
::: solution
1. Find the coordinated parts. *Enjoy* is followed by a list of three activities joined by commas and *and*: (i) *to go to the cinema*, (ii) *cooking for friends*, (iii) *I also read novels*.
2. Name the form of each part: (i) a *to*-infinitive, (ii) an *-ing* form, (iii) a whole clause.
3. Choose the form that fits the verb. *Enjoy* takes an *-ing* form (*enjoy cooking*; see [[verb-patterns/ing-or-infinitive]]). So all three parts become *-ing* forms.
4. Remove *I also*, which belongs to a clause, not to a list item.

✓ *In my free time I enjoy going to the cinema, cooking for friends and reading novels.* (With the serial comma: *… cooking for friends, and reading novels.*)
:::
:::

::: example From a chain of *and* to a better sentence {#ex-and-chain}
Improve this sentence from a student's story: *We arrived at the beach and we put up the umbrella and we swam and then we were hungry and so we had lunch.*
::: solution
1. The sentence is grammatically correct: every clause is joined by *and*. But five clauses in one chain are tiring, and *and then* and *and so* hide the relations between the events.
2. Group the events: arriving and setting up (sequence); swimming and getting hungry (cause); lunch (result).
3. Use a mix of joints: a verb pair with one subject, a full stop, a *so* clause and a time clause.

✓ *We arrived at the beach and put up the umbrella. After a swim we were hungry, so we had lunch.*

The new version has two sentences instead of one, fewer words, and each link (*and*, *after*, *so*) shows a real relation. Time clauses with *after* and *when* are the topic of [[sentences-and-paragraphs/subordination]].
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence has parallel structure?
- [ ] *The course teaches you how to plan, writing and editing an essay.*
- [ ] *She is clever, kind and has a good sense of humour.*
- [x] *The course teaches you how to plan, write and edit an essay.*
- [ ] *I like neither the colour nor how big it is.*
::: solution
*Plan, write and edit* are three verbs of the same form after *how to*. In the first, *writing and editing* do not match *plan*. In the second, *has a good sense of humour* is a verb phrase in a list of adjectives (*clever, kind and funny* would match). In the fourth, a noun (*the colour*) is paired with a clause (*how big it is*); *I like neither the colour nor the size* is parallel.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **和 is not *and*.** Chinese 和, 与, 跟 and 同 normally join nouns and noun phrases (我和你, 苹果和梨), not clauses: one does not say ✗ 我昨天去了超市和买了牛奶. Between clauses, Chinese usually puts the clauses side by side with a comma, or uses words such as 而且, 并且, 也, 又 and 就. English *and* joins clauses freely. Because the Chinese habit is to put clauses side by side, learners often leave out *and* and write a comma splice: ✗ *I went to the supermarket, I bought some milk.* ✓ *I went to the supermarket and bought some milk.*
- **或者 and 还是.** Chinese has two words for *or*: 还是 in questions that ask for a choice (你喝茶还是咖啡？) and 或者 in statements (星期六或者星期天都可以). English uses *or* for both: *Would you like tea or coffee?* *Saturday or Sunday is fine.*
- **The enumeration comma 、.** Chinese separates the items of a list with the enumeration comma (顿号): 苹果、香蕉和橙子. English has no such mark; it uses the ordinary comma (,), followed by a space: *apples, bananas and oranges*.
- **Pairs of connectives.** Chinese often uses connectives in pairs (因为……所以, 虽然……但是, 不但……而且). English has some pairs too (*both … and*, *either … or*, *not only … but also*), but *because … so* and *although … but* are not among them.
:::

::: history
The **Oxford comma** takes its name from the house style of Oxford University Press, whose rules for printers, first drawn up by Horace Hart in the 1890s and later published as *Hart's Rules*, called for a comma before the final *and* of a list. *The Chicago Manual of Style*, first published in 1906, also came to recommend it, which is one reason why the comma is usual in American books. Newspapers, short of space, tended to leave it out. The school rule against beginning sentences with *And* and *But* has no such source in any major grammar; the large reference grammars of the twentieth century (Quirk et al. 1985; Huddleston & Pullum 2002) describe sentence-initial conjunctions as normal English.
:::

## Where this leads

Coordination joins equal ideas. When one idea depends on another — a reason, a condition, a time — English uses subordination, the topic of [[sentences-and-paragraphs/subordination]]. Commas and semicolons in lists are covered fully in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/punctuation]], and linking adverbs such as *however*, *therefore* and *in addition*, which link sentences rather than clauses, are the topic of [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]]. The course *Complex Sentences* returns to coordination and ellipsis in [[complex-sentences/ellipsis-substitution]].

::: summary
- Coordinating conjunctions (*and*, *but*, *or*, *so*, *yet*, *nor*, *for*) join parts of equal rank: words, phrases or independent clauses.
- Before a conjunction that joins two independent clauses, careful writers usually put a comma ([[#rule-comma-coord]]); with two verbs that share a subject, there is normally no comma.
- In a list, use commas between items and *and* or *or* before the last. The serial (Oxford) comma before that final *and* is usual in American English and optional in British English; use it to avoid ambiguity and be consistent ([[#rule-list]]).
- In negative sentences, use *or*: *I don't drink tea or coffee.* Don't use *but* without a contrast, and don't combine *because* with *so*.
- Starting a sentence with *And* or *But* is normal English; use it for emphasis and not too often, with no comma after the conjunction.
- Keep coordinated parts parallel in form, and put each word of *both … and*, *either … or*, *neither … nor*, *not only … but also* directly before its part ([[#rule-parallel]]).
- Chinese 和 joins nouns, not clauses, and Chinese lists use 、; English joins clauses with *and* and separates list items with commas.
:::

::: exercise Ambiguous lists {level=3}
Each sentence can be read in two ways. Explain the two readings, then rewrite the sentence so that it has only the meaning given. (a) *I'd like to thank my parents, Professor Huang and Dr Lee.* (Meaning: four people.) (b) *For breakfast we had juice, toast, bacon and eggs.* (Meaning: bacon and eggs is one dish, and there are three items.)
::: hint
In (a), a phrase after a comma can be read as an explanation of the word before it. In (b), think about which items go together.
:::
::: solution
(a) Reading 1: the parents *are* Professor Huang and Dr Lee (two people). Reading 2: the parents, plus Professor Huang, plus Dr Lee (four people). For four people, add the serial comma: *I'd like to thank my parents, Professor Huang, and Dr Lee.* — or reorder: *I'd like to thank Professor Huang, Dr Lee and my parents.*

(b) Reading 1: four separate items (juice; toast; bacon; eggs). Reading 2: three items, the last being the dish *bacon and eggs*. To make the second clear, use the serial comma before the final *and* of the list, so that *bacon and eggs* stands together: *For breakfast we had juice, toast, and bacon and eggs.*

::: exercise Rewrite a stringy paragraph {level=3}
This paragraph is grammatically correct, but it uses *and* too often and hides the relations between ideas. Rewrite it in 3–5 sentences, using *but*, *so*, a full stop and at least one sentence that begins with *But* or *And* for emphasis.

*I wanted to buy a new phone and I went to the shop on Saturday and the shop assistant showed me three models and they were all very expensive and I didn't have enough money and I decided to wait for the sales and I went home and I was a bit disappointed.*
::: solution
A model answer:

*On Saturday I went to the shop to buy a new phone. The assistant showed me three models, but they were all very expensive, and I didn't have enough money. So I decided to wait for the sales. I went home a bit disappointed. But at least I hadn't spent money I didn't have.*

The relations are now shown: *to buy* (purpose), *but* (contrast with expectations), *So* (result, given weight by a new sentence) and *But* (a final contrast for emphasis). Other good answers are possible; check that every clause is correctly joined and that *and* is used only where the meaning is really "also".
