Zhou Min has just arrived in Manchester to study. She writes a short email to her new English teacher:

> I arrived in Manchester on Sunday, the weather was cold and wet, I stayed in my room and slept for twelve hours. Because I was very tired. Now I feel much better, I am looking forward to your class.

Every word is clear, and the teacher understands her. But an English reader feels that something is wrong with the **sentences**. Some clauses run into each other with only a comma between them, and one "sentence" — *Because I was very tired.* — is only part of a sentence. In Chinese, the first part would be one perfectly good sentence: 我星期天到了曼彻斯特，天气又冷又湿，我待在房间里睡了十二个小时。English draws the line between sentences in a different place.

This lesson explains what makes a written English sentence complete, and how to find and fix the three most common sentence-boundary errors: **fragments**, **run-on sentences** and **comma splices**. These are the errors that teachers mark most often in learners' writing, and they are also the easiest to fix once you can see them.

## What is a sentence?

To talk about sentences, we first need the idea of a **clause**. You met clauses in [[core-grammar/sentence-patterns]]: a clause is a group of words with a subject and a verb.

::: definition Clause, independent clause, dependent clause {#def-clause}
A **clause** is a group of words that contains a subject and a finite verb (a verb that shows tense: *works*, *worked*, *is*, *can go*).

- An **independent clause** (also called a main clause) can stand alone as a sentence: *the weather was cold*.
- A **dependent clause** (also called a subordinate clause) begins with a word such as *because*, *although*, *when*, *if* or *which*, and cannot stand alone: *because I was very tired*.
:::

Look at the difference:

- *I was very tired.* — independent: it is a complete statement.
- *Because I was very tired.* — dependent: the reader waits for the rest (*Because I was very tired, I went to bed early.*).
- *When the train arrived.* — dependent: *when* the train arrived, what happened?
- *The train arrived.* — independent.

A word like *because* or *when* changes an independent clause into a dependent one. It makes the clause point to another clause. The lesson [[sentences-and-paragraphs/subordination]] looks at these words in detail.

::: definition Sentence (in writing) {#def-sentence}
In writing, a **sentence** begins with a capital letter and ends with a full stop (AmE *period*), a question mark or an exclamation mark. A complete written sentence contains **at least one independent clause**. It may also contain more independent clauses (joined correctly) and any number of dependent clauses.
:::

So these are all complete sentences:

- *It rained.* (one independent clause)
- *It rained, so we stayed at home.* (two independent clauses joined by *so*)
- *Because it rained, we stayed at home.* (a dependent clause + an independent clause)
- *We stayed at home because it rained and the roads were flooded.* (an independent clause + a dependent clause that contains two parts)

The length of a sentence has nothing to do with whether it is complete. *Go!* is a complete sentence (an imperative, with the subject *you* understood). A sentence of sixty words can be complete too, if its clauses are joined correctly.

::: theorem The sentence rule {#rule-sentence}
In standard written English:

1. Every sentence contains at least one independent clause.
2. Two independent clauses in one sentence must be **joined**: by a coordinating conjunction (*and*, *but*, *or*, *so*, *yet*) usually with a comma before it, or by a semicolon. A comma alone does not join them.
3. A dependent clause must be attached to an independent clause in the same sentence.
:::

::: proof
English marks the end of a sentence with a full stop, and the reader uses the full stop to decide where one statement ends and the next begins. Inside a sentence, the reader needs a signal to know how two statements relate: *and* adds, *but* contrasts, *so* gives a result, *because* gives a reason. A comma is a weak mark with many jobs — it separates items in a list, sets off introductory words and marks small pauses — so it cannot tell the reader that a new statement has begun. This is why every major usage guide (Swan 2016; Trask 1997; *The Chicago Manual of Style* 2017) treats a comma alone between two independent clauses as an error in formal writing. The rule is about **writing**: in speech, the voice and pauses show the boundaries, and speakers chain clauses together freely. In informal writing and in literature, writers sometimes break the rule on purpose; see "Breaking the rule on purpose" below.
:::

::: quiz
Which of these is a complete sentence?
- [ ] *Although the bus was late.*
- [ ] *The students in the library on the third floor.*
- [x] *Stop.*
- [ ] *Waiting for my friend outside the cinema.*
::: solution
*Stop.* is an imperative: the subject *you* is understood, and *stop* is a finite verb. It is a complete sentence. *Although the bus was late* is a dependent clause. *The students in the library on the third floor* has a subject but no verb. *Waiting for my friend outside the cinema* has no subject and no finite verb (*waiting* is an *-ing* form, not a finite verb).
:::
:::

## Fragments

::: definition Fragment {#def-fragment}
A **sentence fragment** is a group of words written as a sentence (with a capital letter and a full stop) that is not a complete sentence, because it has no independent clause.
:::

Fragments in learners' writing are usually of four kinds.

1. **A dependent clause alone.** The writer puts a full stop before *because*, *when*, *although* or *which*.
   - ✗ *I stayed at home. Because I was ill.*
   - ✓ *I stayed at home because I was ill.*
2. **A phrase with an *-ing* form or a *to*-infinitive.** These are not finite verbs, so they cannot make a sentence alone.
   - ✗ *We spent the whole day in the park. Playing football and having a picnic.*
   - ✓ *We spent the whole day in the park, playing football and having a picnic.*
   - ✗ *She went to the library. To borrow some books.*
   - ✓ *She went to the library to borrow some books.*
3. **An added example or detail.** The writer adds information after a full stop, often with *for example*, *such as*, *especially* or *including*.
   - ✗ *I like outdoor sports. For example, hiking and cycling.*
   - ✓ *I like outdoor sports, for example hiking and cycling.* ✓ *I like outdoor sports. For example, I enjoy hiking and cycling.*
4. **A missing subject or verb.** This is the kind you met in [[core-grammar/sentence-patterns]].
   - ✗ *My hometown very beautiful.* ✓ *My hometown is very beautiful.*
   - ✗ *Is a good idea.* ✓ *It is a good idea.*

::: theorem Fixing a fragment {#rule-fragment}
To fix a fragment, either

1. **attach it** to the sentence before or after it (removing the full stop and, if needed, adding a comma), or
2. **complete it** by adding the missing subject, finite verb or independent clause.

Choose the fix that keeps the meaning clear. Attaching is usually best for dependent clauses and *-ing* phrases; completing is usually best when the fragment carries a new idea.
:::

::: example Finding and fixing fragments {#ex-fragments}
Find the fragments in this text and fix them.

*Last summer I worked in a hotel in Qingdao. Which was a very good experience. I learned a lot. Especially how to deal with difficult guests. The manager was very patient. Always helping new staff.*
::: solution
1. Check each "sentence" for an independent clause.
   - *Last summer I worked in a hotel in Qingdao.* — subject *I*, verb *worked*: complete.
   - *Which was a very good experience.* — begins with *which*: a dependent clause alone. Fragment.
   - *I learned a lot.* — complete.
   - *Especially how to deal with difficult guests.* — no subject, no finite verb: a fragment of the "added detail" kind.
   - *The manager was very patient.* — complete.
   - *Always helping new staff.* — *helping* is an *-ing* form; no subject: fragment.
2. Fix each one. *Which* refers to the whole job, so attach it to the first sentence with a comma. *Especially …* adds a detail to *I learned a lot*, so attach it. *Always helping …* describes the manager; attach it or complete it.

Corrected: *Last summer I worked in a hotel in Qingdao, which was a very good experience. I learned a lot, especially how to deal with difficult guests. The manager was very patient and always helped new staff.*
:::
:::

::: remark Fragments on purpose
Not every fragment is a mistake. Careful writers use fragments in some situations:

- **Answers and replies** in dialogue and messages: *"When are you leaving?" "Tomorrow morning."*
- **Notes, lists, headings, forms and adverts**: *Two bedrooms. Close to the station. Available from June.*
- **Emphasis in fiction, journalism and informal essays**, usually short and after a full sentence: *She opened the envelope. Nothing. Not even a note.*

What makes these acceptable is that the writer chooses them, and the reader sees the choice. In formal writing — essays, reports, applications — keep to complete sentences. A fragment there looks like an accident, not a choice.
:::

::: quiz
Which of these is a fragment?
- [ ] *Although it was raining, we went for a walk.*
- [x] *We went for a walk. Although it was raining.*
- [ ] *We went for a walk, although it was raining.*
- [ ] *It was raining, but we went for a walk.*
::: solution
In the second answer, *Although it was raining* stands alone after a full stop: it is a dependent clause with no independent clause, so it is a fragment. The other three join the dependent clause to an independent clause, or join two independent clauses with *but*.
:::
:::

## Run-on sentences and comma splices

The opposite problem is to put two or more independent clauses into one sentence without joining them properly.

::: definition Run-on sentence and comma splice {#def-run-on}
- A **run-on sentence** (also called a **fused sentence**) joins two independent clauses with **no** punctuation and no conjunction: ✗ *It was late I went home.*
- A **comma splice** joins two independent clauses with **only a comma**: ✗ *It was late, I went home.*

Some teachers and books use *run-on sentence* for both kinds.
:::

::: warning A long sentence is not a run-on
Many learners think a "run-on sentence" is a sentence that is too long. It is not. Length does not matter: ✗ *I'm tired I'm going home.* is a run-on with seven words, while the following sentence of more than thirty words is correct, because its clauses are properly joined: ✓ *When we arrived at the station, the last train had already left, so we walked back to the hotel, which took nearly an hour because it was raining heavily.* A very long sentence may be hard to read, and you may decide to split it — but that is a question of style, not of correctness.
:::

The comma splice is far more common than the fused sentence in learners' writing, especially in the writing of Chinese speakers (see the note on English and Chinese below). Some typical examples:

- ✗ *My sister is a nurse, she works in a hospital in Wuhan.*
- ✗ *The museum was closed, we went to a café instead.*
- ✗ *I don't like this phone, the battery doesn't last long.*
- ✗ *Thank you for your email, I will reply next week.*

Each of these contains two independent clauses: *My sister is a nurse* and *she works in a hospital in Wuhan* could each be a sentence.

### Four ways to fix a comma splice

::: theorem Joining two independent clauses {#rule-fix}
Two independent clauses can be correctly written in four main ways:

1. **Two sentences**, with a full stop: *The museum was closed. We went to a café instead.*
2. **A comma and a coordinating conjunction** (*and*, *but*, *or*, *so*, *yet*): *The museum was closed, so we went to a café instead.*
3. **A semicolon**, when the two ideas are closely linked: *The museum was closed; we went to a café instead.*
4. **A subordinating conjunction**, which makes one clause dependent: *Because the museum was closed, we went to a café instead.*
:::

::: proof
The four fixes are not equal in meaning. A full stop keeps the two ideas separate and lets the reader work out the link. A conjunction or a subordinator **names** the link: *so* and *because* say that one event caused the other, *but* and *although* say that the second is surprising after the first. A semicolon sits between: it says that the clauses belong together but leaves the link unnamed. Good writers choose the fix that shows the relation they mean, and they vary their choice: a paragraph in which every sentence is fixed with *so* or with a full stop sounds mechanical. Semicolons are more common in formal writing than in emails and messages; the full stop and the conjunction are safe everywhere. The use of the comma before *and*, *but* and *so* is explained in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coordination]].
:::

::: warning *Then*, *however* and *therefore* are not conjunctions
Words such as *then*, *however*, *therefore*, *also*, *besides* and *otherwise* link ideas, but grammatically they are adverbs, not conjunctions. They cannot join two independent clauses with a comma.

✗ *We had lunch, then we visited the old town.* ✓ *We had lunch, and then we visited the old town.* ✓ *We had lunch. Then we visited the old town.*

✗ *The flight was delayed, however we arrived on time.* ✓ *The flight was delayed; however, we arrived on time.* ✓ *The flight was delayed. However, we arrived on time.*

✗ *He didn't study, therefore he failed.* ✓ *He didn't study, **so** he failed.* ✓ *He didn't study; therefore, he failed.*

The comma before *then* is common in informal writing, and some readers accept it, but in formal writing use *and then* or a full stop. Linking adverbs like *however* are the subject of [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]].
:::

::: example Four corrections of one comma splice {#ex-four-ways}
Correct the comma splice in four ways and say which version fits a formal email best: ✗ *The meeting room is too small, we will use the hall.*
::: solution
1. Find the two independent clauses: *The meeting room is too small* and *we will use the hall*. The comma alone joins them: a comma splice.
2. Decide how the ideas are related: the second is a **result** of the first.
3. Write the four versions:
   - Full stop: *The meeting room is too small. We will use the hall.*
   - Comma + conjunction: *The meeting room is too small, so we will use the hall.*
   - Semicolon: *The meeting room is too small; we will use the hall.*
   - Subordinator: *Because the meeting room is too small, we will use the hall.* (or *We will use the hall because the meeting room is too small.*)
4. Choose. All four are correct. In an email, the version with *so* or *because* is clearest, because it names the reason; *As the meeting room is too small, we will use the hall* is a slightly more formal variant. The two short sentences are also fine, especially in a quick message.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correctly joined?
- [ ] *I called you twice, you didn't answer.*
- [ ] *I called you twice you didn't answer.*
- [ ] *I called you twice, however you didn't answer.*
- [x] *I called you twice, but you didn't answer.*
::: solution
*But* is a coordinating conjunction, so a comma + *but* correctly joins the two independent clauses. The first is a comma splice, the second is a fused (run-on) sentence, and in the third *however* is an adverb, which cannot join clauses after a comma; write *I called you twice; however, you didn't answer.*
:::
:::

## Breaking the rule on purpose

Careful writers do sometimes join independent clauses with commas. This happens in a few special cases:

- **Very short, parallel clauses**, often in threes: *I came, I saw, I conquered.* (the usual English translation of Caesar's *Veni, vidi, vici*); *She laughed, she cried, she laughed again.*
- **Contrasts of the type** *not X, Y*: *It's not a holiday, it's a business trip.*
- **Fiction, personal writing and transcribed speech**, where the commas show fast, breathless thought.

Usage guides (Trask 1997; Swan 2016) describe these as accepted in literary and informal writing. They are not accepted in essays, reports or exam answers, where the reader expects the standard rule. A learner should first master the rule; breaking it well depends on knowing it.

Question tags and reporting clauses are not comma splices at all: *You're Wang Fang's brother, aren't you?* and *"I'm hungry," she said.* are correct, because *aren't you* and *she said* are not independent statements of their own.

## Finding boundary errors in your own writing

Most writers cannot see sentence-boundary errors when they read quickly for meaning, because the meaning is clear. A slower method helps.

1. **Find the verbs.** Underline every finite verb.
2. **Find the subjects.** For each verb, ask "who or what?"
3. **Count the clauses** in each sentence (one subject–verb pair = one clause).
4. **Check each joint.** Between every two independent clauses, there must be a full stop, a semicolon, or a comma + *and/but/or/so/yet*. Every dependent clause must be attached to an independent one.
5. **Read aloud.** Where your voice falls and stops, you may need a full stop.

::: example Checking a paragraph {#ex-paragraph}
Use the method to correct the email at the start of the lesson.

*I arrived in Manchester on Sunday, the weather was cold and wet, I stayed in my room and slept for twelve hours. Because I was very tired. Now I feel much better, I am looking forward to your class.*
::: solution
1. Finite verbs: *arrived*, *was*, *stayed*, *slept*, *was*, *feel*, *am looking*.
2. Subjects: *I arrived*; *the weather was*; *I stayed … and slept* (one subject, two verbs joined by *and* — one clause); *I was*; *I feel*; *I am looking*.
3. Joints: *I arrived … , the weather was …* — comma splice. *the weather was cold and wet, I stayed …* — comma splice. *Because I was very tired.* — fragment. *Now I feel much better, I am looking forward …* — comma splice.
4. Corrections, choosing the link that fits the meaning:

✓ *I arrived in Manchester on Sunday. The weather was cold and wet, so I stayed in my room and slept for twelve hours because I was very tired. Now I feel much better, and I am looking forward to your class.*

Other good versions are possible: *I was very tired, so I stayed in my room …*; *Now that I feel much better, I am looking forward to your class.* Each joint now has a full stop, a comma + conjunction, or a subordinator.
:::
:::

::: example From a Chinese sentence to English sentences {#ex-chinese}
Write this Chinese sentence in English, with correct sentence boundaries: 我们班有三十个学生，大部分来自南方，只有五个人是北方人，大家相处得很好。
::: solution
1. Count the ideas (clauses) in the Chinese: (a) 我们班有三十个学生; (b) 大部分来自南方; (c) 只有五个人是北方人; (d) 大家相处得很好. Chinese joins all four with commas; English cannot.
2. Decide how the ideas are related. (b) and (c) give details about the students in (a); (c) contrasts with (b); (d) is a new point.
3. Choose the joints:

✓ *There are thirty students in my class. Most of them come from the south, and only five are from the north. We all get on very well.*

Another good version: *There are thirty students in my class, most of whom come from the south; only five are from the north. We all get on very well.* (*most of whom* makes (b) a dependent clause.) What is not possible is ✗ *There are thirty students in my class, most of them come from the south, only five are from the north, we all get on very well.*
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese and English draw sentence boundaries by different principles, and this is the main reason why comma splices are so frequent in Chinese speakers' English.

- **The Chinese comma joins clauses.** The Chinese national standard for punctuation (GB/T 15834-2011 《标点符号用法》) says that the comma (，) marks pauses between the clauses of a complex sentence (复句内各分句之间的停顿). A Chinese sentence often contains several clauses joined only by commas, and the full stop (。) comes when a whole unit of meaning is finished. People sometimes call this habit 一逗到底, "commas all the way to the end", and Chinese grammarians describe loosely joined sentences of this kind as 流水句 ("running-water sentences").
- **The English comma does not.** In English, the end of an independent clause is a grammatical boundary, and it needs a full stop, a semicolon or a conjunction. Translating Chinese commas one for one into English therefore produces comma splices.
- **Parataxis and hypotaxis.** Chinese often leaves the relation between clauses unstated and lets the reader infer it (意合, parataxis); English usually states it with a conjunction such as *because*, *although* or *so* (形合, hypotaxis) — a contrast discussed at length by 连淑能 (2010). When you write in English, ask what the relation between two clauses is, and either name it with a conjunction or use a full stop.
- **Subjects.** Chinese clauses often share an unstated subject (……，到了曼彻斯特，睡了十二个小时). In English, a new clause needs its own subject (see [[core-grammar/sentence-patterns]]); if you do not want to repeat the subject, join the verbs with *and* inside one clause: *I arrived and went straight to bed.*
:::

::: history
Punctuation has not always worked the way it does now. In medieval manuscripts and early printed books, marks mostly showed where a reader should pause when reading aloud. Historians of punctuation such as M. B. Parkes (*Pause and Effect*, 1992) describe how, from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, English printers and grammarians increasingly tied punctuation to grammar, so that the full stop came to mark the end of a grammatical sentence. Chinese has a parallel history: classical texts were written without punctuation, and learning to divide a text into sentences and pauses — 句读 — was part of a basic education; Han Yu's essay 《师说》, from the early ninth century, mentions it. Modern Chinese punctuation, with its comma and full stop, was officially adopted only in 1920.
:::

::: summary
- A clause has a subject and a finite verb. An independent clause can stand alone; a dependent clause, introduced by *because*, *when*, *although*, *which* and similar words, cannot.
- A written sentence starts with a capital letter, ends with a full stop, question mark or exclamation mark, and contains at least one independent clause ([[#rule-sentence]]).
- A fragment has no independent clause. Fix it by attaching it to a neighbouring sentence or by completing it ([[#rule-fragment]]). Writers use fragments on purpose in replies, notes, adverts and fiction, but not in formal writing.
- A run-on sentence joins two independent clauses with nothing; a comma splice joins them with only a comma. Length has nothing to do with it.
- Join two independent clauses with a full stop, a comma + *and/but/or/so/yet*, a semicolon, or a subordinator such as *because* ([[#rule-fix]]). *Then*, *however* and *therefore* are adverbs and cannot join clauses after a comma.
- Chinese joins clauses with commas (一逗到底); English needs a full stop or a conjunction at the end of every independent clause.
:::

A model answer:

*My grandmother lives in a small village in Yunnan, and I visit her every summer. Her house is old, but it has a beautiful garden full of flowers and vegetables. She gets up at five every morning and works in the garden until breakfast. Although she is over eighty, she is stronger than me. I can never keep up with her.*

The errors: (1) *…in Yunnan, I visit…* — comma splice (fixed with *, and*). (2) *Her house is old it has…* — run-on (fixed with *, but*: the garden is a pleasant contrast with the old house). (3) *Full of flowers and vegetables.* — fragment (attached to *garden*). (4) *…every morning, she works…* — comma splice (fixed by joining the two verbs with *and*). (5) *Although she is over eighty.* — fragment (attached to the next clause, with a comma). (6) *…than me, I can never…* — comma splice (fixed with a full stop; *, so I can never keep up with her* is also good). Other correct joints are acceptable.

::: exercise Write and check {level=3}
Write a paragraph of 80–100 words about your daily routine. Use at least one sentence joined with *and*, *but* or *so*, at least one sentence with *because*, *when* or *although*, and at least one sentence with a semicolon. Then check every joint with the five-step method in this lesson.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

*On weekdays I get up at half past six because my first class starts at eight. I have a quick breakfast, and then I cycle to the university. When the weather is bad, I take the bus; it is slower, but I stay dry. I usually have lunch with my classmates in the canteen. In the afternoon I study in the library until five. After dinner I call my parents or watch a film. I try to go to bed before eleven, but I don't always manage it.*

Check: every independent clause ends with a full stop or is joined by *, and*, *, but*, or a semicolon; *because my first class starts at eight* and *When the weather is bad* are attached to independent clauses; there are no commas alone between clauses.
