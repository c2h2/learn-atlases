In Chinese, a sentence such as 虽然天气很冷，但是我们还是去了海边 uses two connectives, 虽然 and 但是, and sounds natural with both. A word-for-word English version is:

✗ *Although the weather was very cold, but we still went to the beach.*

Native readers find this sentence strange: it seems to say "but" twice. English uses **one** word to show the contrast:

- ✓ *Although the weather was very cold, we still went to the beach.*
- ✓ *The weather was very cold, but we still went to the beach.*

The difference comes from the way English joins clauses. *But* joins two equal clauses (coordination, the topic of [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coordination]]). *Although* makes one clause depend on the other (subordination). A clause cannot be both at once. This lesson explains subordination: the words that introduce dependent clauses of reason, contrast, time, condition and purpose, where those clauses go, how to punctuate them, and the pairs of connectives that work in Chinese but not in English.

## Dependent clauses and subordinators

::: definition Subordinator and adverbial clause {#def-subordinator}
A **subordinating conjunction** (or **subordinator**) is a word such as *because*, *although*, *when*, *if* or *so that* that begins a dependent clause and links it to an independent clause. A dependent clause that tells us why, when, on what condition or with what purpose something happens is an **adverbial clause**: it does the job of an adverbial in the main clause.
:::

In *We stayed at home **because it was raining***, the clause *because it was raining* answers the question "why?", just as the adverb *there* answers "where?" in *We stayed there*. The adverbial clause cannot stand alone (✗ *Because it was raining.* is a fragment, as explained in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/complete-sentences]]); it hangs on the main clause.

| meaning | subordinators | example |
|---|---|---|
| reason | *because*, *since*, *as* | *I took a taxi **because** I was late.* |
| contrast | *although*, *though*, *even though*, *while*, *whereas* | ***Although** he is seventy, he runs every day.* |
| time | *when*, *while*, *before*, *after*, *until*, *as soon as*, *since* | *Call me **when** you arrive.* |
| condition | *if*, *unless*, *as long as* | *We'll eat outside **if** it's sunny.* |
| purpose | *so that*, *in order that* | *Speak slowly **so that** everyone can follow.* |

Coordination joins ideas as equals. Subordination shows that one idea is the main point and the other is background: a cause, a condition, a time. This is why the choice matters. *The shop was closed, so we went home* and *Because the shop was closed, we went home* say nearly the same thing, but the second puts the reason in the background and the decision in the foreground.

::: quiz
Which word begins a dependent clause?
- [ ] *but*
- [ ] *so*
- [x] *unless*
- [ ] *however*
::: solution
*Unless* is a subordinator: *unless you hurry* cannot stand alone. *But* and *so* are coordinating conjunctions, and *however* is a linking adverb (see [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]]).
:::
:::

## Position and punctuation

An adverbial clause can usually come **before** or **after** the main clause.

- ***When the film ended,** everybody clapped.*
- *Everybody clapped **when the film ended**.*

::: theorem Commas with adverbial clauses {#rule-sub-comma}
1. When the adverbial clause comes **first**, put a comma after it: *If you need help, call me.*
2. When the main clause comes first, there is usually **no comma** before the subordinator: *Call me if you need help.*
3. Clauses of **contrast** with *although*, *though*, *even though*, *whereas* and *while* (meaning ‘whereas’) usually take a comma even when they come second: *He runs every day, although he is seventy.*
:::

::: proof
A clause at the start of a sentence delays the subject of the main clause, so the comma shows the reader where the background ends and the main statement begins. Without it the reader can stumble: *When the teacher finished the students left* — at first the reader may take *the students* as the object of *finished*. When the main clause comes first, the subordinator itself (*if*, *because*, *when*) clearly marks the start of the new clause, so no comma is needed. Contrast clauses are different because they add a separate, surprising fact after a complete statement, which most writers mark with a pause. These patterns are described in both British and American guides (Swan 2016; *The Chicago Manual of Style* 2017). Rule 1 is often relaxed after a very short clause (*If so we'll leave.*), and rule 2 has an exception for *because* when a comma prevents a misreading, but the patterns above are always safe.
:::

::: example Moving the clause {#ex-position}
Rewrite each sentence with the adverbial clause in the other position, and punctuate it correctly. (a) *I'll call you as soon as I get home.* (b) *Because the road was icy, the bus drove very slowly.* (c) *Although she was nervous, Wang Fang gave an excellent talk.*
::: solution
1. Find the subordinator and the end of its clause in each sentence.
2. Move the clause and apply [[#rule-sub-comma]].
3. (a) The clause *as soon as I get home* moves to the front, so a comma follows it: *As soon as I get home, I'll call you.*
4. (b) The clause *Because the road was icy* moves to the end, so the comma goes: *The bus drove very slowly because the road was icy.*
5. (c) The clause moves to the end. It is a contrast clause, so a comma before *although* is usual. The name must now come first, and the pronoun goes into the clause: *Wang Fang gave an excellent talk, although she was nervous.*
:::
:::

Notice the pronoun in (c). When the dependent clause comes first, English can use a pronoun before the name (*Although **she** was nervous, **Wang Fang** gave …*). When the main clause comes first, the name normally comes first (*Wang Fang gave …, although **she** was nervous*).

## Reason: *because*, *since*, *as*

- ***Because*** is the most direct way to give a reason, and the only one that can answer a *why* question: *"Why are you late?" "Because I missed the bus."* (In conversation this one-clause answer is normal, although in formal writing it would be a fragment.)
- ***Since*** and ***as*** give a reason that the reader probably knows already, or that is not the main point. They usually come at the start of the sentence: ***Since** you're here, you can help me.* ***As** it was getting late, we decided to leave.* *As* in this sense is more common in British English, and both are a little more formal than *because*.

*Since* can also mean time (*I've lived here **since** I was ten*), so check that the meaning is clear.

::: warning *Because* and *because of*
*Because* is followed by a clause (subject + verb). *Because of* is followed by a noun phrase.

✗ *We stayed at home because of it was raining.* ✓ *We stayed at home **because it was raining**.* ✓ *We stayed at home **because of the rain**.*

The same pattern holds for *although* (+ clause) and *despite* / *in spite of* (+ noun or *-ing* form), and for *while* (+ clause) and *during* (+ noun):

✗ *Despite it was cold, we went out.* ✓ *Although it was cold, we went out.* ✓ *Despite the cold, we went out.*

✗ *During I was in London, I visited the British Museum.* ✓ *While I was in London, I visited the British Museum.* ✓ *During my stay in London, I visited the British Museum.*
:::

## Contrast: *although*, *though*, *even though*, *whereas*

- ***Although*** and ***though*** introduce a fact that makes the main clause surprising: ***Although** the hotel was cheap, it was very comfortable.* *Though* is a little less formal.
- ***Even though*** is stronger: the main clause is very surprising. ***Even though** he had a map, he got lost.*
- ***Whereas*** and ***while*** compare two facts that are different, without surprise: *My sister loves maths, **whereas** I prefer languages.*

::: theorem One connective for one link {#rule-one-link}
English marks the relation between two clauses with **one** connective. In standard English, a clause introduced by *although*, *though* or *even though* is not followed by *but* in the main clause, and a clause introduced by *because*, *since* or *as* is not followed by *so*:

✗ *Although the test was hard, but I passed.* ✓ *Although the test was hard, I passed.* ✓ *The test was hard, but I passed.*

✗ *Because I was ill, so I didn't go to work.* ✓ *Because I was ill, I didn't go to work.* ✓ *I was ill, so I didn't go to work.*

The main clause **may** contain an adverb that adds emphasis, such as *still* or *nevertheless*: ✓ *Although the test was hard, I **still** passed.*
:::

::: proof
A subordinator makes its clause dependent; a coordinating conjunction joins two independent clauses. In ✗ *Although the test was hard, but I passed*, the first clause is dependent (because of *although*), and *but* would need an independent clause before it — so the sentence has no proper structure. Grammatically, it is as if the writer had built half of one kind of sentence and half of another. Adverbs such as *still*, *nevertheless* and *anyway* are not conjunctions, so they can appear in the main clause without breaking the structure. The pattern *although … yet* is found in older English literature, but it is now rare and sounds old-fashioned. Chinese grammar works differently: 虽然 and 但是 are a normal pair, and so are 因为 and 所以; see the note on English and Chinese below.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct in standard written English?
- [ ] *Although I had an umbrella, but I still got wet.*
- [x] *Although I had an umbrella, I still got wet.*
- [ ] *Despite I had an umbrella, I got wet.*
- [ ] *I had an umbrella, although I got wet.*
::: solution
The second uses one connective (*although*) and the adverb *still*. The first uses *although* and *but* together. The third uses *despite* before a clause (*despite having an umbrella* or *despite my umbrella* would be correct). The fourth puts the contrast on the wrong clause: the surprising fact is getting wet, so the main clause should be *I got wet*.
:::
:::

## Time: *when*, *while*, *before*, *after*, *until*, *as soon as*

- ***When*** — at the time that: *When I was a child, I lived with my grandparents.*
- ***While*** — during the time that, often with a progressive verb: *Someone called **while** you were out.*
- ***Before*** and ***after*** — earlier or later: *Wash your hands **before** you eat.* ***After** we had dinner, we went for a walk.*
- ***Until*** — up to the time that: *Wait here **until** I come back.*
- ***As soon as*** — immediately after: *I'll tell you **as soon as** I know.*

::: theorem Present tense for the future in time and condition clauses {#rule-present-future}
In clauses of time (*when*, *before*, *after*, *until*, *as soon as*) and condition (*if*, *unless*, *as long as*), English uses a **present** tense to refer to the future. *Will* goes in the main clause, not in the dependent clause.

✗ *When I will arrive, I will call you.* ✓ ***When I arrive**, I will call you.*

✗ *If it will rain tomorrow, we will stay at home.* ✓ ***If it rains tomorrow**, we will stay at home.*
:::

::: proof
The main clause already shows that the whole situation is in the future, so the dependent clause, which only sets the time or the condition, does not need to mark the future again. This is a firm rule of standard English in both British and American usage (Swan 2016; Quirk et al. 1985). There are a few exceptions where *will* in an *if*-clause has its own meaning, such as willingness — *If you'll wait a moment, I'll get the manager* (‘if you are willing to wait’) — but these are a separate, polite use, not a way of marking future time. Future forms are explained in [[tense-and-aspect/future-forms]].
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *I'll send you the photos when I will get home.*
- [x] *I'll send you the photos when I get home.*
- [ ] *I send you the photos when I will get home.*
- [ ] *When I will get home, I send you the photos.*
::: solution
The future is marked once, in the main clause (*I'll send*); the time clause uses the present (*when I get home*). The other three put *will* in the time clause, or leave it out of the main clause, or both.
:::
:::

## Condition: *if*, *unless*, *as long as*

- ***If*** — a condition: *If you're hungry, there's some soup in the fridge.*
- ***Unless*** — ‘if … not’: *You'll miss the train **unless** you leave now.* (= *if you don't leave now*)
- ***As long as*** — ‘only if’: *You can borrow my bike **as long as** you bring it back tonight.*

✗ *Unless you don't hurry, you'll be late.* — *unless* already contains the negative. ✓ *Unless you hurry, you'll be late.* ✓ *If you don't hurry, you'll be late.*

In English, *then* after an *if*-clause is possible but usually not needed: *If you heat ice, (then) it melts.* The full system of conditional sentences is the topic of [[complex-sentences/conditionals]].

## Purpose: *so that*

*So that* gives a purpose, often with *can*, *could* or *will*: *I'm saving money **so that** I can travel next year.* When the subject of both clauses is the same, a *to*-infinitive is shorter and more common: *I'm saving money **to travel** next year.* (*In order to* is a more formal version.)

Don't confuse *so that* (purpose) with *so* (result): *He left early **so that** he could catch the train* (that was his aim); *He left early, **so** he caught the train* (that was the result).

::: example Choosing the subordinator {#ex-choose}
Complete the text with *although*, *because*, *if*, *until* and *when*, using each once.

*___ I started university, I didn't know anyone. ___ I was shy, I joined the photography club ___ I wanted to make friends. I didn't say much ___ the third meeting, but now I go every week. ___ you are new, I recommend joining a club.*
::: solution
1. Read each gap and ask what relation it shows.
2. Gap 1: a time in the past — *When I started university, …*
3. Gap 2: being shy makes joining a club surprising — contrast: *Although I was shy, …*
4. Gap 3: the reason for joining — *… because I wanted to make friends.*
5. Gap 4: the point in time up to which she said little — *… until the third meeting …* (Here *until* is followed by a noun phrase; it can be a preposition as well as a subordinator.)
6. Gap 5: a condition — *If you are new, …*

Note the commas: after each clause at the start of a sentence (*When…*, *Although…*, *If…*), but none before *because*, which follows its main clause.
:::
:::

## Combining short sentences

Learners at this level often write in very short sentences, which can sound like a list. Subordination lets you combine them and show which idea is the main one.

::: example Combining sentences {#ex-combine}
Combine these four sentences into one or two, using subordination: *I was walking to the station. It started to rain. I didn't have an umbrella. I went into a café.*
::: solution
1. Decide the main event. The story leads to *I went into a café*; that should be a main clause.
2. Decide the relation of each other sentence to it: *I was walking to the station* gives the time; *It started to rain* also gives the time (the moment of change); *I didn't have an umbrella* gives the reason.
3. Choose subordinators: *while* or *as* for the background action, *when* for the moment, *because* or *since* for the reason.

✓ *While I was walking to the station, it started to rain. Because I didn't have an umbrella, I went into a café.*

✓ *I was walking to the station when it started to rain, and since I didn't have an umbrella, I went into a café.*

Both versions keep all the information and make the relations clear. Avoid putting everything into one very long sentence; two sentences are often clearer.
:::
:::

::: example Rewriting *although … but* {#ex-although}
Rewrite this sentence correctly in three different ways: ✗ *Although my English is not very good, but I can understand most films.*
::: solution
1. The sentence uses two connectives for one contrast. Keep only one.
2. Version with *although*: *Although my English is not very good, I can understand most films.*
3. Version with *but*: *My English is not very good, but I can understand most films.*
4. Version with *despite* + noun phrase, or with *even though* for a stronger contrast: *Despite my limited English, I can understand most films.* / *Even though my English is not very good, I can understand most films.*

An adverb can be added to the main clause in the first version: *Although my English is not very good, I can **still** understand most films.*
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Pairs of connectives.** Chinese complex sentences often mark the relation twice, with one connective in each clause: 虽然……但是, 因为……所以, 即使……也, 如果……就, 只要……就. In English, the relation is marked once (*although*, *because*, *even if*, *if*, *as long as*). Of these, 如果……就 is the closest to English: *then* after an *if*-clause is possible, though usually unnecessary. With 即使……也 and 虽然……但是, the second word has to go; an adverb like *still* can keep its meaning.
- **Order.** In Chinese, the dependent clause (偏句) normally comes before the main clause (正句): 因为下雨，我们没去. English allows both orders, and the main clause very often comes first: *We didn't go because it was raining.* Do not feel that the reason must come first.
- ***When* and 的时候.** Chinese marks time with 的时候 at the end of the clause (我小的时候); English puts *when* at the start (*when I was young*). ✗ *I was young time …* comes from translating 的时候 word for word.
- **No future marking.** Chinese verbs do not mark future time, so the English rule against *will* in *when*- and *if*-clauses is not a problem of Chinese transfer; learners make the error because they apply the English *will* everywhere the meaning is future.
- **Hypotaxis.** English, as 连淑能 (2010) describes, tends to show relations between clauses explicitly with conjunctions (形合), while Chinese often leaves them implicit (意合). So when you translate a Chinese sentence with no connectives at all, you often need to **add** one in English: 下雨了，我们回家吧 → *It's raining, so let's go home.* or *Since it's raining, let's go home.*
:::

::: history
Teachers of writing have long tried to measure how learners use subordination. In *Grammatical Structures Written at Three Grade Levels* (1965), Kellogg Hunt introduced the **T-unit** — one main clause with all the dependent clauses attached to it — and found that older school students wrote longer T-units, packing more ideas into each one through dependent clauses and shorter phrases. His work led to **sentence combining**, an exercise in which students join short sentences into longer ones, studied by Frank O'Hare in *Sentence Combining* (1973) and still used in writing classes. The example and the exercise on combining sentences in this lesson are of this kind.
:::

## Where this leads

Subordinate clauses that act as adverbials are only one kind of dependent clause. Relative clauses (*the book **that I bought***) and noun clauses (*I think **that he's right***) are the topics of [[complex-sentences/relative-clauses]] and [[complex-sentences/noun-clauses]], and [[complex-sentences/adverbial-clauses]] and [[complex-sentences/conditionals]] go further into the clauses of this lesson. The next lesson, [[sentences-and-paragraphs/punctuation]], brings together the punctuation of all the clause types you have met so far. In longer texts, linking adverbs such as *however* and *therefore* connect sentences rather than clauses; they are the topic of [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]].

::: summary
- A subordinator (*because*, *although*, *when*, *if*, *so that* …) begins a dependent clause and attaches it to a main clause; the dependent clause cannot stand alone.
- Adverbial clauses express reason, contrast, time, condition and purpose. They can usually come before or after the main clause.
- Put a comma after an adverbial clause at the start of a sentence; when the main clause comes first, there is usually no comma, except before contrast clauses with *although*, *though*, *whereas* ([[#rule-sub-comma]]).
- Use one connective for one link: no *although … but*, no *because … so*. An adverb such as *still* may stand in the main clause ([[#rule-one-link]]).
- *Because*, *although* and *while* take a clause; *because of*, *despite* and *during* take a noun phrase.
- In time and condition clauses, use a present tense for the future: *when I arrive*, *if it rains* ([[#rule-present-future]]).
- *Unless* means ‘if … not’; *so that* gives a purpose, *so* a result.
:::
