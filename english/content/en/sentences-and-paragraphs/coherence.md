Read these two versions of the same paragraph. They contain the same information.

> **A.** Moreover, the train is more comfortable. I prefer the train to the plane for trips between Beijing and Shanghai. Besides, you can use your phone during the whole trip. The high-speed train takes about four and a half hours. Furthermore, the station is in the city. On the other hand, the flight is two hours. What's more, the airport is far from the centre. In a word, the train is better.

> **B.** For trips between Beijing and Shanghai, I prefer the train to the plane. The flight takes only about two hours, while the high-speed train takes about four and a half. However, the airport is far from the city centre, and passengers have to arrive early for security checks, so the total time is often similar. The train is also more comfortable: there is more room, and you can use your phone for the whole journey. For these reasons, I almost always take the train.

Paragraph A is hard to follow, although it is about one topic and every sentence is correct. The point comes second; the facts jump back and forth between train and plane; and almost every sentence begins with a linking word — *Moreover*, *Besides*, *Furthermore*, *What's more* — that does not show a real relation. Paragraph B puts the point first, compares the two in a clear order, uses only a few linking words, each with a clear job, and refers back to what has been said (*the total time*, *for these reasons*). Paragraph B is **coherent**.

The previous lesson, [[sentences-and-paragraphs/topic-sentences]], showed how to keep a paragraph on one point. This lesson shows how to make it easy to follow: putting ideas in a clear order, using linking words correctly and not too often, and connecting sentences through pronouns, *this* + noun, and the repetition of key words.

## Coherence and cohesion

::: definition Coherence and cohesion {#def-coherence}
A text is **coherent** when its ideas are connected in a way the reader can follow: each sentence fits with the ones before and after it, and the reader understands why it is there. **Cohesion** is the set of language devices that show these connections on the surface of the text: linking words (*however*, *for example*), words that refer back (*she*, *this*, *the problem*), repeated words and synonyms, and similar ties between sentences.
:::

The two ideas go together. Cohesive devices help a reader to see the connections, but they cannot create connections that are not there: in Paragraph A, the word *Moreover* does not make the first sentence follow from anything. A coherent paragraph starts with a clear plan; cohesion makes the plan visible. The terms come from Halliday and Hasan's *Cohesion in English* (1976).

## Putting ideas in order

::: theorem Choose an order that fits the purpose {#rule-order}
Organise the supporting sentences of a paragraph in an order the reader can recognise. The most common orders are:

1. **time order** — for stories, histories and processes (*first … then … after that … finally*);
2. **space order** — for descriptions of places and objects (from left to right, from outside to inside, from near to far);
3. **order of importance** — for reasons and arguments (often the most important last, so that it stays in the reader's mind; in reports and emails, often the most important first);
4. **general to specific** — a statement followed by explanation and examples;
5. **comparison and contrast** — one thing, then the other; or point by point;
6. **problem and solution**, or **cause and effect**.

Keep to one order within a paragraph.
:::

::: proof
A reader who recognises the order can predict what kind of sentence is coming next, and so reads faster and remembers more. When the order changes without warning, as in Paragraph A, which moves from train to train to plane to train to plane, the reader has to rebuild the plan alone. Composition handbooks for both British and American students list these orders as the standard patterns of paragraph development, and the choice depends on the genre: narratives use time order ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/describing-narrating]]), essays often use order of importance or comparison ([[essay-writing/arguing-a-point]]).
:::

::: example Putting sentences in order {#ex-order}
Put these sentences in a coherent order and say which order you used.

*(a) Then add the eggs and stir quickly for about a minute. (b) Fried rice is one of the easiest dishes to cook at home. (c) Finally, add the rice, some soy sauce and chopped spring onions, and fry everything for three more minutes. (d) First, heat a little oil in a wok until it is very hot. (e) Use rice that was cooked the day before, because fresh rice is too soft.*
::: solution
1. Find the topic sentence: (b) makes a general point about the whole paragraph, so it comes first.
2. Look for the type of text: the other sentences describe a process, so **time order** is natural.
3. Use the sequence words to order the steps: *First* (d), *Then* (a), *Finally* (c).
4. Decide where (e) goes. It is advice about the rice, which is needed before cooking starts, so it comes after the topic sentence and before the steps.

Order: **(b), (e), (d), (a), (c)**.

*Fried rice is one of the easiest dishes to cook at home. Use rice that was cooked the day before, because fresh rice is too soft. First, heat a little oil in a wok until it is very hot. Then add the eggs and stir quickly for about a minute. Finally, add the rice, some soy sauce and chopped spring onions, and fry everything for three more minutes.*
:::
:::

## Linking words and phrases

::: definition Linking adverbial {#def-linking}
A **linking adverbial** (also called a **connector**, **transition** or **linking word**) is a word or phrase such as *however*, *for example*, *in addition* or *as a result* that shows how a sentence relates to the one before it. Unlike a conjunction (*and*, *but*, *because*), it does not join two clauses into one sentence.
:::

| relation | everyday and neutral | more formal |
|---|---|---|
| addition | *also*, *too*, *as well* | *in addition*, *moreover*, *furthermore* |
| contrast | *but*, *however*, *on the other hand* | *in contrast*, *nevertheless*, *nonetheless* |
| result | *so* | *therefore*, *as a result*, *consequently*, *thus* |
| example | *for example*, *for instance* | *e.g.* (in notes and lists) |
| sequence | *first*, *then*, *next*, *after that*, *finally* | *firstly*, *secondly* (BrE), *subsequently* |
| summary | *in short*, *overall* | *in conclusion* (at the end of a whole text), *to sum up* |

::: theorem Punctuating linking adverbials {#rule-linker-punct}
A linking adverbial belongs to one sentence and links it to another. It can:

1. **start a new sentence**, usually followed by a comma: *The hotel was expensive. **However,** the rooms were excellent.*
2. come after a **semicolon** that joins two independent clauses: *The hotel was expensive; **however,** the rooms were excellent.*
3. stand **inside** the sentence, between commas: *The rooms, **however,** were excellent.*
4. come at the **end** of a clause (mainly *though*, *too*, *as well*, *however* in informal style): *The rooms were excellent, **though**.*

It cannot join two independent clauses after a comma alone (✗ *The hotel was expensive, however the rooms were excellent.*). Short sequence words (*then*, *so*, *also*) often take no comma.
:::

::: proof
Linking adverbials are adverbs or prepositional phrases, not conjunctions, so they do not create a grammatical link between clauses — that is why the comma splice in ✗ *…expensive, however the rooms…* is an error (see [[sentences-and-paragraphs/complete-sentences]]). The comma after a sentence-initial linker shows that it applies to the whole sentence; the pair of commas around a linker in the middle shows that it is an interruption. Placing *however* in the middle is common in careful writing because it puts the contrast exactly where it belongs: *The rooms, however, …* contrasts the rooms with the price. Usage guides note that *so*, *then* and *also* behave more like conjunctions in informal English and often take no comma (Swan 2016).
:::

::: example Choosing and punctuating linkers {#ex-linkers}
Complete the paragraph with *for example*, *however*, *as a result* and *in addition*, and punctuate them correctly.

*Our town has made cycling much safer in the last five years. ___ it has built twenty kilometres of new cycle lanes. ___ the speed limit near schools has been reduced. ___ the number of accidents involving cyclists has fallen. ___ some roads in the centre are still dangerous at rush hour.*
::: solution
1. Read the topic sentence: the town has made cycling **safer**. The next sentences give evidence, a result and a limitation.
2. Sentence 2 gives a concrete case of the topic sentence → *For example,*
3. Sentence 3 adds a second measure → *In addition,*
4. Sentence 4 says what happened because of the measures → *As a result,*
5. Sentence 5 says something that goes against the general picture → *However,*
6. Each linker starts its sentence and takes a comma.

*Our town has made cycling much safer in the last five years. For example, it has built twenty kilometres of new cycle lanes. In addition, the speed limit near schools has been reduced. As a result, the number of accidents involving cyclists has fallen. However, some roads in the centre are still dangerous at rush hour.*

This paragraph uses a linker in four sentences out of five, which is acceptable here because each one marks a different relation. In a longer paragraph, use fewer.
:::
:::

::: warning Too many linking words
Many learners believe that good writing has a linking word at the start of every sentence. Studies of learner writing suggest otherwise. Granger and Tyson (1996), comparing essays by French-speaking learners with essays by native speakers, found that learners overused some connectors (such as *indeed*) and underused others (such as *however*), and Field and Yip (1992) found that Cantonese-speaking students in Hong Kong used more connectors than native-speaker students did, especially at the start of sentences. Readers find a paragraph like A at the start of this lesson mechanical, and a linker that does not match the real relation is worse than none.

- Use a linker where the relation between two sentences is **not obvious without it**.
- Prefer **one clear linker** to several; vary them, but don't hunt for unusual ones.
- Remember that **pronouns, *this* + noun and repeated key words** also link sentences, often more naturally.
:::

::: warning Linkers that are often misused
- ***On the contrary*** means that the opposite of what was just said is true: *"Was the film boring?" "On the contrary, it was fascinating."* It does not mean ‘in contrast’ (相比之下). ✗ *I like tea. On the contrary, my wife likes coffee.* ✓ *I like tea. My wife, in contrast, likes coffee.* ✓ *I like tea, but my wife likes coffee.*
- ***On the other hand*** introduces a contrasting point about the **same** question, often after *on the one hand* (or without it): *The job is well paid. On the other hand, the hours are long.* It does not add a similar point: ✗ *The job is well paid. On the other hand, it is close to my home.*
- ***At last*** means ‘after a long wait’ (*At last the bus came*). To introduce the final point of a list, use *finally* or *lastly*: ✗ *At last, exercise improves your mood.* ✓ *Finally, exercise improves your mood.*
- ***Besides*** as a linker is informal and often adds an extra, slightly different reason: *I don't want to go out. Besides, it's raining.* In formal writing, use *in addition* or *moreover*.
- ***In a word*** introduces a one-word or very short summary (*In a word, no.*). For the summary of a paragraph or text, use *in short*, *overall* or, at the end of a whole text, *in conclusion*.
:::

::: quiz
Which linker fits best? *Many people think that learning a language as an adult is impossible. ___, millions of adults learn new languages successfully every year.*
- [ ] *On the contrary*
- [x] *In fact*
- [ ] *In addition*
- [ ] *At last*
::: solution
*In fact* introduces a statement that corrects the belief reported in the first sentence. *On the contrary* normally answers a statement directly, as a denial (*"It's impossible." "On the contrary, it's quite easy."*); after a sentence that reports other people's opinion, *In fact* is the natural choice. *In addition* adds a similar point, and *At last* means ‘after a long wait’.
:::
:::

## Reference and repetition

Linking words are not the only way to connect sentences. Most of the connections in a well-written paragraph come from words that **refer back** to something already mentioned.

- **Pronouns**: *Wang Fang moved to Shenzhen last year. **She** had found a job there.*
- ***This*, *that*, *these*, *those* + noun**: *The rent went up by forty per cent. **This increase** forced many families to move.*
- **Repetition** of a key word: *The museum is free. **The museum** also has a good café.* (Repeating the noun is clearer than *it* when another noun has come in between.)
- **Synonyms** and general words: *We bought a used **car**. **The vehicle** was ten years old.* (Use synonyms for variety, but never at the cost of clarity.)

::: theorem Make reference clear {#rule-reference}
1. A pronoun (*it*, *they*, *this*) must refer clearly to **one** earlier noun or idea. If two nouns could be meant, repeat the noun.
2. When *this* refers to a whole idea, add a noun that names the idea: *This decision …*, *This problem …*, *These changes …*.
3. Repeating a key word is not a mistake. Repeat the words that carry the topic of the paragraph; vary only where repetition would sound heavy.
:::

::: proof
A pronoun is a promise that the reader already knows what is meant. When there are two possible meanings, as in *My sister told my mother that **she** had passed the exam*, the reader cannot be sure who passed. A bare *this* at the start of a sentence (*This made everyone angry.*) can refer to the last noun, the last sentence or the whole paragraph; adding a noun (*This delay …*) removes the doubt and also summarises what has been said. School advice to "avoid repetition" can lead learners to replace a key word with a series of synonyms (*car*, *vehicle*, *automobile*, *means of transport*) until the reader wonders whether these are different things; good writers repeat key terms, especially in explanations and arguments.
:::

### Old information first, new information last

English sentences usually begin with information the reader already knows — often something from the previous sentence — and end with new information. When each sentence picks up where the last one ended, the paragraph flows.

- ✓ *Our city has built a new **metro line**. **The line** connects the airport with the main **railway station**. **The station** is now only twenty minutes from the airport.*
- ? *Our city has built a new metro line. The airport and the main railway station are connected by it. Twenty minutes is now all it takes to get from the airport to the station.*

In the first version, each sentence starts with something the reader has just met (*the line*, *the station*) and adds something new at the end.

::: example Improving the flow {#ex-flow}
Rewrite the second and third sentences so that each begins with known information: *Many students in our class want to study medicine. A lot of hard work and long years of training are needed for this career. Five years is the length of the course at most universities in China, and there are further years of practice after it.*
::: solution
1. Find the new information at the end of sentence 1: *medicine*.
2. Sentence 2 should start from it. *This career* is already at the end of sentence 2; move it to the start: *Medicine is a career that needs …* or *This career needs …*
3. Sentence 3 is about the course; the course is new, but it is part of the training just mentioned. Start with *The training* or *The course*.

✓ *Many students in our class want to study medicine. This career needs a lot of hard work and many years of training. At most universities in China, the course itself takes five years, and further years of practice follow.*

(Some Chinese medical programmes are longer; the point here is the order of information, not the exact figure.)
:::
:::

::: example Making reference clear {#ex-reference}
Correct the unclear references: *Li Ming and his brother opened a café near the university. He does the cooking and he serves the customers. It became very popular, and this surprised them.*
::: solution
1. *He does the cooking and he serves the customers*: there are two men. Which *he* is which? Repeat the names: *Li Ming does the cooking, and his brother serves the customers.*
2. *It became very popular*: *it* could be the café or (less likely) the university. Since the café is the topic, *The café* is clearer.
3. *this surprised them*: *this* refers to the whole idea of the café's popularity. Add a noun: *its success*.

✓ *Li Ming and his brother opened a café near the university. Li Ming does the cooking, and his brother serves the customers. The café soon became very popular, and its success surprised them both.*
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which second sentence connects best with the first? *The government has raised the price of cigarettes by twenty per cent.*
- [ ] *Fewer young people are expected to start smoking.*
- [x] *This increase is expected to stop many young people from starting to smoke.*
- [ ] *It is expected to stop many young people from starting to smoke, which is good for them.*
- [ ] *Moreover, fewer young people are expected to start smoking.*
::: solution
*This increase* refers back to the price rise and names it, so the reader sees at once how the sentences are linked (old information first). The first does not show the link; in the third, *it* could mean the government, the price or the increase; in the fourth, *Moreover* wrongly suggests an additional point rather than a result.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Lists of linkers from school.** Many Chinese learners were taught to structure essays with 首先、其次、再次、最后、总之, and translate them as *Firstly, Secondly, Thirdly, Lastly, In a word*. Used once in a whole essay, sequence words are clear; used in every paragraph, they make the writing mechanical, and *In a word* is not the normal English way to sum up a text (use *In short*, *Overall* or *In conclusion*).
- **相反 and *on the contrary*.** Chinese 相反 can introduce simple contrast (我喜欢茶，相反，我太太喜欢咖啡 is heard in speech), but English *on the contrary* contradicts what has just been said. For contrast, use *in contrast*, *however*, *whereas* or *but*.
- **Zero pronouns and repetition.** Chinese often leaves out a pronoun when the reference is clear (他买了一辆车，开了三年); English needs one (*He bought a car and drove **it** for three years*). Chinese also tolerates repeating a full noun phrase more than English does in some contexts, and English uses *this* + noun and *the* + noun where Chinese might use 这 alone. Check that every English clause has the pronouns and determiners it needs.
- **Implicit and explicit links.** Chinese can leave the relation between sentences implicit (意合) more often than English, which tends to make it explicit (形合; 连淑能 2010). But "explicit" does not mean "a linker before every sentence": in English, too, much of the linking is done by reference, repetition and order.
:::

::: history
The study of how sentences hang together began long before the word *cohesion*. In the 1920s and 1930s, linguists of the Prague School, among them Vilém Mathesius, described how a sentence moves from what is already known (the *theme*) to what is new, an idea later developed by M. A. K. Halliday. In *Cohesion in English* (1976), Halliday and Ruqaiya Hasan classified the ties between sentences into reference, substitution, ellipsis, conjunction and lexical cohesion, and their categories became the basis of much teaching. From the 1990s, corpora of learners' writing, such as the International Corpus of Learner English begun by Sylviane Granger at the University of Louvain, allowed researchers to compare how learners and expert writers actually use connectors.
:::

Coherence matters in every kind of writing in the rest of this course: emails ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/emails-messages]]), descriptions and stories ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/describing-narrating]]), and the editing checklist ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/editing]]). In longer texts, signposting between paragraphs is the topic of [[essay-writing/signposting]], and the finer points of cohesion and emphasis are covered in [[style-and-rhetoric/cohesion-emphasis]].

::: summary
- A coherent paragraph has ideas connected in a way the reader can follow; cohesion is the set of language devices that show these connections ([[#def-coherence]]).
- Put supporting sentences in a recognisable order — time, space, importance, general to specific, comparison, problem and solution — and keep to it ([[#rule-order]]).
- Linking adverbials (*however*, *for example*, *as a result*) link sentences, not clauses: start a new sentence or follow a semicolon, with a comma after them ([[#def-linking]], [[#rule-linker-punct]]).
- Use linkers only where the relation is not obvious, and choose the right one: *on the contrary* contradicts, *on the other hand* contrasts, *at last* means ‘after a long wait’.
- Pronouns, *this* + noun, repeated key words and synonyms connect sentences; make every reference clear and repeat key words when needed ([[#rule-reference]]).
- Begin sentences with known information and end with new information, so that each sentence picks up from the last.
:::

Answers will vary. A model:

> The biggest change in my home town in recent years has been the new riverside park. Ten years ago, the riverbank was full of old warehouses, and nobody went there after dark. Check: time order (ten years ago → 2019 → now); *This project* refers to the whole rebuilding; *However* introduces a contrasting point; *park* is repeated as the key word.
