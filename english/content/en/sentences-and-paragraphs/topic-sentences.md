A student was asked to write a paragraph on the question *What do you like about your home town?* Here is her first draft:

> My home town is Yangzhou, in Jiangsu Province. It has a long history. My grandparents have lived there all their lives. Yangzhou is famous for its food, especially its fried rice and its breakfast dumplings. Last year my cousin got married, and there were more than two hundred guests at the wedding. The weather in summer is very hot and humid. I want to study abroad next year. In conclusion, Yangzhou is a nice place.

Every sentence is correct. But after reading it, we still do not know what she likes about Yangzhou, and why. The paragraph moves from history to grandparents to food to a wedding to the weather to her own plans, and the last sentence — *Yangzhou is a nice place* — does not follow from what came before. The problem is not grammar but **organisation**: there is no main point, and nothing holds the sentences together.

This lesson shows how a good English paragraph is built: a **topic sentence** that states the main point, **supporting sentences** that develop it, sometimes a **concluding sentence**, and the quality that makes them belong together, **unity**. The next lesson, [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]], shows how to make the sentences flow from one to the next.

## What is a paragraph?

::: definition Paragraph {#def-paragraph}
A **paragraph** is a group of sentences that develops **one main idea**. On the page, a new paragraph is shown either by **indenting** its first line or by leaving a **blank line** before it (block style).
:::

How long is a paragraph? It depends on the kind of text. In essays and reports, a body paragraph often has five to ten sentences (very roughly 100–200 words). In newspapers, websites and emails, paragraphs are much shorter, often one to three sentences, because readers scan them on narrow screens. A one-sentence paragraph is normal in a news report or an email but unusual in an essay, where it usually means that an idea has not been developed.

There are two layouts, and you should use one of them, not both:

- **Indented**: the first line of each paragraph starts a little to the right (in print, about the width of a few letters), with no blank line between paragraphs. This is usual in books.
- **Block**: no indent, and a blank line between paragraphs. This is usual in emails, on websites and in many business documents.

## The topic sentence

::: definition Topic sentence and controlling idea {#def-topic-sentence}
A **topic sentence** states the main idea of a paragraph. It has two parts: the **topic** (what the paragraph is about) and the **controlling idea** (what the writer says about the topic). The controlling idea limits what the rest of the paragraph can contain.
:::

- *[Topic] **Living in a small town** [controlling idea] **has three big advantages**.* — The paragraph will name and explain three advantages.
- *[Topic] **My first week at university** [controlling idea] **was more difficult than I expected**.* — The paragraph will show what was difficult.
- *[Topic] **Yangzhou** [controlling idea] **is a wonderful place for anyone who loves food**.* — The paragraph will be about food, and nothing else.

The same topic can have many controlling ideas, and each one leads to a different paragraph: *Yangzhou has a long and interesting history.* / *Yangzhou is a good city for students.* / *Yangzhou has changed a great deal in the last twenty years.*

::: theorem A good topic sentence {#rule-topic}
A good topic sentence:

1. **makes a point** — it says something about the topic that the paragraph will explain or support, rather than only naming the topic (*This paragraph is about my home town* names a topic but makes no point);
2. is **neither too broad nor too narrow** — not so general that a book would be needed (*Food is important in China*), and not so specific that there is nothing to add (*Yangzhou fried rice contains egg*);
3. usually comes **at the start** of the paragraph in essays, reports and other explanatory writing.
:::

::: proof
The topic sentence tells the reader what to expect, so that every following sentence can be understood as part of a plan. Readers of explanatory texts, especially in study and at work, often read the first sentence of each paragraph to find their way through a text (the strategy described in [[reading-skills/main-ideas]]), so a point placed there is easy to find. Rule 3, however, describes a convention of teaching and of certain genres, not a law of English. When Richard Braddock (1974) examined essays published in American magazines, he found that many paragraphs had no single explicit topic sentence, and that fewer began with one than writing textbooks suggested. Experienced writers sometimes build up to the point at the end of a paragraph, or let it be understood without stating it. For a learner writing essays, emails and reports, starting with the point is the safest choice, because it is what readers of those texts expect.
:::

::: quiz
Which is the best topic sentence for a paragraph in an essay?
- [ ] *I am going to write about my favourite season.*
- [ ] *Autumn is a season.*
- [x] *Autumn is the best season for travelling in northern China.*
- [ ] *The leaves on the trees outside my window turned yellow on 10 October.*
::: solution
The third has a topic (*autumn*) and a clear controlling idea (*the best season for travelling in northern China*) that the paragraph can support with reasons. The first only announces the topic; the second makes no point; the fourth is a single fact, too narrow to develop into a paragraph.
:::
:::

::: example Finding the topic and the controlling idea {#ex-controlling}
Underline the topic and the controlling idea in each topic sentence, and say what the paragraph will probably contain. (a) *Learning to cook has saved me a lot of money.* (b) *There are several ways to make a long train journey more comfortable.* (c) *My grandmother taught me patience.*
::: solution
1. Find what the sentence is about (the topic) — usually the subject.
2. Find what the writer says about it (the controlling idea) — usually the rest of the predicate.
3. Ask what a reader would expect to follow.

(a) Topic: *learning to cook*. Controlling idea: *has saved me a lot of money*. The paragraph will compare cooking at home with eating out, perhaps with numbers. It should not discuss the pleasure of cooking or healthy food, unless these are linked to money.

(b) Topic: *a long train journey*. Controlling idea: *there are several ways to make it more comfortable*. The paragraph will list and explain some ways (food, a pillow, a seat by the window …), probably in order of importance.

(c) Topic: *my grandmother*. Controlling idea: *taught me patience*. The paragraph will tell one or two stories or habits that show how she taught it. It should not describe her appearance or her life story in general.
:::
:::

::: example Narrowing a broad topic sentence {#ex-narrow}
A student wants to write one paragraph for a college magazine and begins: *The internet has changed everything.* Improve the topic sentence.
::: solution
1. Test it: could one paragraph support it? No — "everything" would need a book. The sentence is too broad, and it gives the reader no idea what the paragraph will say.
2. Narrow the **topic**: which part of the internet, for whom? For example, *online maps*, *video calls*, *online shopping for students*.
3. Make the **controlling idea** definite: what exactly has changed, and how?
4. Check that it is not too narrow: is there enough to say in five to eight sentences?

Possible topic sentences:

- *Video calls have changed the way students living abroad stay in touch with their families.*
- *Online maps have made travelling in a foreign city much less stressful.*

Each one tells the reader what to expect, and each can be supported with two or three points and details.
:::
:::

## Supporting sentences

After the topic sentence come the **supporting sentences**, which explain, prove or illustrate the controlling idea. Support can take several forms:

- **reasons** — *because …*, why the point is true;
- **examples** — particular cases that show it;
- **facts and numbers** — information that can be checked;
- **explanation** — what something means or how it works;
- **description or a short story** — details that make the point real.

Strong paragraphs usually have two levels of support: a few **main supporting points**, each followed by one or two **details** that explain or illustrate it.

Here is the Yangzhou paragraph rewritten around one controlling idea. The topic sentence is first; then come three supporting points, each with a detail; the last sentence concludes.

> Yangzhou is a wonderful place for anyone who loves food. First, it is the home of Yangzhou fried rice, which is now eaten all over the world, but which tastes best in the city where it began. Second, the city has a famous breakfast culture. On a weekend morning, families sit for hours in teahouses, eating steamed dumplings and drinking green tea. Finally, the food is not expensive. A large breakfast for two costs less than a cinema ticket. For me, a visit home always means a visit to my favourite teahouse.

Every sentence now helps the reader understand why Yangzhou is good for food lovers. The history, the wedding, the weather and the plan to study abroad have gone — not because they are uninteresting, but because they belong to other paragraphs.

## The concluding sentence

A **concluding sentence** ends a paragraph by returning to the point. It can restate the topic sentence in other words, summarise the support, or give a final comment or consequence.

- *For these reasons, I believe a small town is the best place to bring up children.* (restates)
- *Patience, in short, was the most valuable thing she gave me.* (restates with emphasis)
- *For me, a visit home always means a visit to my favourite teahouse.* (personal comment)

A concluding sentence is useful in a paragraph that stands alone — an exam answer, a short report, a paragraph on a form. In a long essay, many body paragraphs end without one, and lead instead into the next paragraph. Avoid a concluding sentence that only repeats the topic sentence word for word, and avoid *In conclusion* at the end of a single body paragraph: it signals the end of a whole text (see [[essay-writing/conclusions]]).

## Unity

::: definition Unity {#def-unity}
A paragraph has **unity** when every sentence in it supports or develops the controlling idea of its topic sentence.
:::

::: theorem Keep every sentence on the point {#rule-unity}
Check each sentence of a paragraph against the controlling idea. A sentence that does not support it should be removed, or moved to a paragraph where it fits. If many sentences do not fit, the topic sentence may be the problem: rewrite it so that it states the point the paragraph really makes, or split the paragraph into two.
:::

::: proof
A reader uses the controlling idea to understand each new sentence: *How does this help to show that Yangzhou is good for food lovers?* When a sentence does not answer that question, the reader has to guess why it is there, and the main point becomes harder to see. This is the problem in the first draft of the Yangzhou paragraph. Unity is related to, but different from, coherence: a paragraph can stay on one point and still be hard to follow if its sentences are in a confusing order or poorly linked. That second quality is the subject of [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]].
:::

::: example Finding sentences that break unity {#ex-unity}
Which two sentences do not belong in this paragraph? Explain why.

*(1) Cycling to work has made my life healthier. (2) I ride about eight kilometres each day, which is the best exercise I get. (3) My bike is red and I bought it second-hand. (4) Because I arrive at work awake and energetic, I no longer need a coffee in the morning. (5) Some of my colleagues take the metro, which is very crowded. (6) I have also lost three kilograms since I started in March.*
::: solution
1. Find the controlling idea in sentence (1): cycling to work has made the writer's life **healthier**.
2. Test each sentence: does it show that cycling has made life healthier?
   - (2) exercise — yes.
   - (3) the colour and history of the bike — no. It is about the bike, not about health.
   - (4) energy, no need for coffee — yes.
   - (5) colleagues and the crowded metro — no. It could support a paragraph about the advantages of cycling over public transport, but not this one.
   - (6) weight loss — yes.
3. Remove (3) and (5). The paragraph now has three supporting points: exercise, energy and weight.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A paragraph begins: *Online classes have several disadvantages for students.* Which sentence breaks the unity of the paragraph?
- [ ] *Many students find it hard to concentrate at home.*
- [ ] *Asking a quick question is more difficult than in a classroom.*
- [x] *Online classes allow teachers to record their lessons.*
- [ ] *A poor internet connection can make students miss part of a lesson.*
::: solution
Recording lessons is an **advantage** (for students, too, since they can watch again), so it does not support a paragraph about disadvantages. It could appear in a paragraph about advantages, or in a sentence that admits a point on the other side, which is the subject of [[essay-writing/counter-arguments]].
:::
:::

## Writing a paragraph step by step

1. **Decide your point.** Ask yourself: what do I want the reader to understand or believe after this paragraph? Write it as one sentence. That is your topic sentence.
2. **List your support.** Write down reasons, examples and details. Keep only those that support the point.
3. **Choose an order** — most important last, in time order, from general to specific (see [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]]).
4. **Write**, one supporting point at a time, with a detail for each.
5. **Decide whether you need a concluding sentence.**
6. **Check unity**: read each sentence and ask, "Does this support my topic sentence?"

::: example Building a paragraph from notes {#ex-build}
Write a paragraph from these notes. Choose a topic sentence, remove any note that does not fit, and add a concluding sentence. *Topic: my university library. Notes: open until 11 p.m.; quiet study rooms on the 4th floor; built in 1998; staff help you find books; free coffee machine broke last month; you can borrow laptops for a day.*
::: solution
1. Find a controlling idea that most of the notes support. Opening hours, quiet rooms, helpful staff and laptops all show that the library is **useful for students**. The building date and the broken coffee machine do not.
2. Write the topic sentence: *Our university library offers students everything they need for serious study.*
3. Order the support. Here a natural order is from the most basic (time) to the most special (laptops).
4. Write, adding small details.
5. Add a concluding sentence.

> Our university library offers students everything they need for serious study. It is open until eleven every night, so students who work or have classes during the day can still use it. The study rooms on the fourth floor are completely quiet, which makes them perfect before exams. If you cannot find a book, the staff are always happy to help. Students can even borrow a laptop for a day if their own computer is broken. It is no surprise that the library is full almost every evening.

The notes *built in 1998* and *the coffee machine broke* have been left out, because they do not show that the library helps students study.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **中心句 and 总分总.** Chinese school writing also teaches a sentence that states the centre of a paragraph (中心句) and the "general – specific – general" pattern (总分总). The English paragraph with a topic sentence, supporting sentences and a concluding sentence is very similar, so many Chinese learners already know the idea. The main difference is how strongly English academic and business writing expects the point to be stated **early and explicitly**, in the paragraph's first sentence.
- **Building up to the point.** Some Chinese writing — especially narrative and descriptive essays (记叙文, 散文) — opens with a scene, a quotation or background before the main idea. English writing does this too in stories and journalism, but in essays, reports and emails readers expect the point first. In those genres, put the background after the topic sentence.
- **Claims about "Chinese thinking".** In 1966 Robert Kaplan suggested that writers from different cultures organise paragraphs in different ways, and drew the "Oriental" paragraph as a spiral that circles around its topic. Later researchers criticised this picture as too simple: it was based on student essays, and differences between genres and between individual writers are much larger than any national pattern. It is better to think of the topic sentence as a convention of certain **genres**, which any writer can learn, than as a feature of a culture.
- **Indentation.** Chinese paragraphs are traditionally indented by two characters (首行缩进两字). English uses either a shorter indent or no indent with a blank line between paragraphs.
:::

::: history
Rules for the English paragraph were first set out in nineteenth-century textbooks of rhetoric. Alexander Bain's *English Composition and Rhetoric* (1866) gave a set of laws for the paragraph, including that it should have unity and that the opening sentence should usually indicate its subject; these ideas spread through American and British school teaching as the "topic sentence". In 1974 Richard Braddock tested the rule against published essays and found that professional writers used explicit topic sentences much less regularly than the textbooks claimed. The topic sentence has remained a central part of writing teaching all the same, because it gives learners and readers a clear and useful plan.
:::

## Where this leads

A paragraph also needs its sentences in a sensible order, linked by words such as *however* and *for example*: that is [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]]. Topic sentences return in the paragraphs of emails ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/emails-messages]]) and of description and narration ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/describing-narrating]]). In a whole essay, the thesis statement plays the role of a topic sentence for the text, and each body paragraph argues one point: see [[essay-writing/thesis-introduction]] and [[essay-writing/arguing-a-point]]. Finding topic sentences when you read is practised in [[reading-skills/main-ideas]].

::: summary
- A paragraph develops one main idea; it is shown by an indent or by a blank line (block style) — use one layout, not both ([[#def-paragraph]]).
- A topic sentence has a topic and a controlling idea; the controlling idea limits what the paragraph can contain ([[#def-topic-sentence]]).
- A good topic sentence makes a point, is neither too broad nor too narrow, and in essays, reports and emails usually comes first ([[#rule-topic]]). This is a convention of these genres; experienced writers sometimes place the point later.
- Supporting sentences give reasons, examples, facts, explanation or details; good paragraphs have main supporting points, each with a detail.
- A concluding sentence is useful in a paragraph that stands alone; it returns to the point without repeating it word for word.
- Unity means that every sentence supports the controlling idea; remove or move sentences that do not ([[#rule-unity]]).
:::

Name the topic and the controlling idea. (a) *Public libraries are more important today than ever.* (b) *My first job taught me three useful lessons.* (c) *Hot pot is the perfect meal for a cold evening with friends.*
::: solution
(a) Topic: *public libraries*; controlling idea: *are more important today than ever*. (b) Topic: *my first job*; controlling idea: *taught me three useful lessons*. (c) Topic: *hot pot*; controlling idea: *is the perfect meal for a cold evening with friends*.

::: exercise Too broad, too narrow or good? {level=1}
Label each topic sentence B (too broad), N (too narrow) or G (good). (a) *Technology has changed the world.* (b) *My phone has 128 gigabytes of storage.* (c) *Smartphones have made it easier for me to keep in touch with my family.* (d) *There are many countries in Asia.* (e) *Learning a musical instrument as a child has several long-term benefits.*
::: solution
(a) B. (b) N. (c) G. (d) B (and it is a fact with nothing to argue). (e) G.

::: exercise Which sentence does not belong? {level=1}
Find the sentence that breaks the unity. *Our new flat is much better than the old one. It is close to the metro, so I get to work in twenty minutes. The kitchen is large and bright. My sister is studying to be a lawyer. The rent is lower, even though the flat is bigger.*
::: solution
*My sister is studying to be a lawyer.* — it has nothing to do with why the new flat is better.

::: exercise Write topic sentences {level=2}
Write a topic sentence for a paragraph on each topic. Make the controlling idea clear. (a) public transport in your city (b) learning English through films (c) working while studying
::: solution
Answers will vary. Models:

(a) *Public transport in Chengdu is cheap, fast and easy to use.*

(b) *Watching films in English has improved my listening more than any textbook.*

(c) *Having a part-time job while studying has both advantages and disadvantages.*

Check that each one names the topic and says something definite about it that a paragraph can develop.

::: exercise Improve the topic sentence {level=2}
Each paragraph has a weak topic sentence. Rewrite it so that it states the point of the paragraph.

(a) *This paragraph is about my neighbour. He is eighty-two but still swims every morning in the river. He grows all his own vegetables. Last winter he walked to the top of the mountain behind our village.*

(b) *Exams are a thing. They make students study only what will be tested. They cause a lot of stress. They measure what a student can do on one day, not over a whole year.*
::: solution
Models:

(a) *My neighbour is the most active eighty-two-year-old I know.* (All the support — swimming, gardening, climbing — shows that he is active.)

(b) *Exams are not a good way to measure what students have learned.* (or *Exams have several serious weaknesses.* — the three supporting sentences each give a weakness.)
:::
:::

::: exercise Add support {level=2}
Write three supporting sentences for this topic sentence: *Living with a roommate can teach you useful skills.*
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

*First, you learn to share: the kitchen, the bathroom and sometimes the cost of food. You also have to learn to solve small conflicts calmly, for example about noise late at night. Finally, many people find that living with someone from a different background makes them more open-minded.*

Each sentence names a skill (sharing, solving conflicts, being open-minded) and gives a short detail or example.
:::
:::

::: exercise Rewrite the Yangzhou paragraph {level=3}
Look again at the first draft of the Yangzhou paragraph at the start of the lesson. Write a **different** paragraph from its material, with a different controlling idea: for example, the history of Yangzhou, or why the writer wants to study abroad. Use only the sentences that support your controlling idea, and add details of your own.
::: hint
Choose a controlling idea that at least two of the original sentences support, then add your own supporting details.
:::
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model with a different controlling idea:

> Although I love my home town, I want to spend next year studying abroad. Yangzhou is a beautiful city with a long history, but it is small, and I have lived there all my life. My grandparents have never left Jiangsu Province, and I would like to see more of the world than they have. Studying in another country would also improve my English and help me find a job with an international company. Most of all, I want to find out whether I can live independently, far from my family.

Check: every sentence explains why she wants to study abroad. The food, the wedding and the weather have been left out.
:::
:::

::: exercise Write a paragraph {level=3}
Write a paragraph of 100–130 words in answer to the question *What is the most useful thing you have learned outside school?* Include a clear topic sentence, two or three supporting points with details, and a concluding sentence. Then underline your controlling idea and check every sentence for unity.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

> The most useful thing I have learned outside school is how to cook simple, healthy meals. My mother taught me when I was fifteen, mainly because she was tired of cooking every evening after work. At first I could only make fried eggs and noodles, but soon I learned to make soups and a few vegetable dishes. This skill became very important when I moved into a flat with friends at university: I ate better than most of my classmates and spent much less money. Cooking also helps me relax after a long day of study. School taught me a great deal, but it never taught me to look after myself in this way.

Check: the controlling idea is *how to cook simple, healthy meals* as the most useful thing; each sentence explains how it was learned or why it is useful.
:::
