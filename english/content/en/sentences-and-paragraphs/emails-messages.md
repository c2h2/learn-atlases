Here is an email that a student sent to her lecturer at a British university:

> **Subject:** (no subject)
>
> Dear teacher,
>
> Hello! I am Li Hua, a student in your class. Sorry to disturb you. I am very busy recently because I have three exams and my grandmother is ill, and I also have a part-time job. So I want to ask can I submit my essay later. Please kindly reply me as soon as possible.
>
> Thank you very much!!
>
> Li Hua

The lecturer understands the request, but the email makes it harder than it needs to be. There is no subject line, so it is easy to miss in a full inbox. *Dear teacher* is not how English-speaking students address a lecturer. The request comes after a long explanation, and it does not say which essay or how much more time is needed. *Please kindly reply me* is not standard English, and the double exclamation marks feel too informal. None of these points is a grammar error in the usual sense; they are matters of **convention** — what readers of English emails expect.

This lesson shows how to write formal and informal emails in English: the subject line, the greeting, a clear purpose, polite requests and an appropriate ending, with model emails written for the atlas. It also looks at short notes and messages, where the conventions are very different.

## Formal, neutral and informal

::: definition Register in emails {#def-register}
The **register** of an email is its level of formality, chosen to suit the reader and the purpose. Emails can be **formal** (to an organisation, a university office, a professor you have not met, an employer), **neutral** (to a colleague, a teacher you know, a landlord, a service you use) or **informal** (to friends and family).
:::

| feature | formal | neutral | informal |
|---|---|---|---|
| greeting | *Dear Dr Evans,* | *Hello Anna,* / *Dear Anna,* | *Hi Tom!* / *Hi,* |
| opening | *I am writing to …* | *I'm writing about …* / *Just a quick question about …* | *How are you?* / *Guess what!* |
| contractions | rare | common | normal |
| requests | *I would be grateful if you could …* | *Could you …?* | *Can you …?* |
| ending | *I look forward to hearing from you.* | *Thanks in advance.* | *See you soon!* |
| sign-off | *Yours sincerely,* / *Kind regards,* | *Best wishes,* / *Best,* | *Love,* / *Cheers,* (BrE) |

The safest choice when you are not sure is **neutral**: polite and clear, but not stiff. If the other person writes back informally (*Hi Li Hua*), you can usually follow their lead.

## The parts of an email

::: theorem The structure of a clear email {#rule-email-structure}
A clear email usually has these parts, in this order:

1. a **subject line** that says what the email is about, specifically;
2. a **greeting**;
3. an **opening** that states the purpose, in the first or second sentence;
4. the **details** the reader needs, in short paragraphs;
5. the **request** or action, stated clearly (who should do what, by when);
6. a **closing line**;
7. a **sign-off** and your name (and in formal emails, your role or student number).

Keep one main topic per email.
:::

::: proof
Most readers deal with many emails a day. They decide what to open from the subject line, and what to do from the first few lines. If the purpose is clear at the start, the reader can read the details with that purpose in mind — the same principle as the topic sentence of a paragraph ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/topic-sentences]]). A clear request at the end tells the reader exactly what action is needed. These conventions are taught in business-English and academic-skills courses in both Britain and the United States, and they apply in emails between people of all language backgrounds.
:::

### Subject lines

A subject line is a short label, not a sentence; it does not need a full stop.

- ✗ *(no subject)* ✗ *Hello* ✗ *Question*
- ✓ *Extension request: Essay 2 (Li Hua, Group B)*
- ✓ *Booking for 12 May: change of date*
- ✓ *Photos from Saturday*

### Greetings

::: theorem Greetings {#rule-greetings}
1. **Formal, name known**: *Dear* + title + **family name**: *Dear Ms Chen,* *Dear Mr Patel,* *Dear Dr Evans,* *Dear Professor Okafor,*. Do not use a title with a given name alone (✗ *Dear Ms Fang*, if Fang is her given name; ✗ *Dear Professor John*).
2. **Formal, name unknown**: *Dear Sir or Madam,* (traditional), or the name of a role or team: *Dear Admissions Team,* *Dear Hiring Manager,*. *To whom it may concern* is used mainly at the top of references and certificates.
3. **Neutral**: *Dear* + first name, or *Hello* + name: *Dear Anna,* *Hello Anna,*.
4. **Informal**: *Hi Tom,* *Hi everyone,* *Hey* (very informal).

Use *Ms* for a woman unless you know she prefers *Mrs* or *Miss*. If you do not know whether the person is a man or a woman, write the full name: *Dear Alex Morgan,*.

After the greeting, British English uses a comma or nothing; American English uses a comma, or a colon in formal business letters (*Dear Dr. Evans:*). British English writes *Mr*, *Ms*, *Dr* without a full stop; American English writes *Mr.*, *Ms.*, *Dr.*
:::

::: warning *Dear teacher* and *Respected Professor*
Chinese emails to teachers often begin 尊敬的王老师 or 老师您好. Translated word for word, these become ✗ *Respected teacher Wang* or ✗ *Dear teacher*. In English, *teacher* is not used as a title before a name, and *Dear teacher* without a name sounds like a message to nobody in particular.

✓ *Dear Ms Wang,* (a schoolteacher) ✓ *Dear Dr Wang,* / *Dear Professor Wang,* (a university lecturer — check their title on the university website)

You also do not need *Hello!* on a separate line after *Dear …*: the greeting is already *Dear*.
:::

::: quiz
You are writing to the international office of a university and do not know the name of the person who will read your email. Which greeting is best?
- [ ] *Dear teacher,*
- [ ] *Hi guys,*
- [x] *Dear International Office Team,*
- [ ] *Respected Sir,*
::: solution
Addressing the team or office is polite and accurate. *Dear teacher* is not used this way; *Hi guys* is far too informal; *Respected Sir* is not a standard English greeting and assumes the reader is a man (*Dear Sir or Madam* would be the traditional choice).
:::
:::

::: example Matching greetings and sign-offs {#ex-greetings}
Choose a greeting and a sign-off for each email, and say what register it is. (a) To Dr Amina Yusuf, a lecturer you have not met, asking about a course. (b) To the customer service department of an online shop, about a missing parcel. (c) To your cousin Lily, about a family dinner.
::: solution
1. For each email, decide how well you know the reader and whether you know their name.
2. (a) Formal, name known: *Dear Dr Yusuf,* … *Yours sincerely,* (BrE) / *Sincerely,* (AmE) / *Kind regards,* (both). Not *Dear Amina*: you have not met, and she has not invited you to use her first name.
3. (b) Formal or neutral, name unknown: *Dear Customer Service Team,* or *Dear Sir or Madam,* … *Kind regards,* (or, in traditional British style after *Dear Sir or Madam*, *Yours faithfully,*).
4. (c) Informal: *Hi Lily,* … *Love,* / *See you soon,* / *Speak soon,*.
5. Check that the two ends match: a formal greeting with *Love* or an informal *Hi* with *Yours faithfully* would sound strange.
:::
:::

## Stating your purpose

Start with the reason for writing. Useful openings:

- *I am writing to ask about the deadline for the application.*
- *I am writing to apply for the position of part-time tutor.*
- *I'm writing to let you know that I can't attend Friday's seminar.*
- *Thank you for your email of 3 May.* / *Thanks for getting back to me so quickly.*
- *Following our conversation yesterday, I'm sending you the report.*

If you need to introduce yourself, do it in one short sentence, together with the purpose: *My name is Li Hua, and I am a first-year student in your Tuesday seminar. I am writing to ask whether …*

Avoid opening with an apology for writing (*Sorry to disturb you*). In English, it is not needed for an ordinary request, and it delays the purpose. Save apologies for real problems: *I'm sorry I missed the meeting.*

## Making requests

::: theorem Polite requests {#rule-requests}
In emails, requests are usually made as **questions** or with **conditional** forms, which leave the reader free to say no:

- neutral: *Could you send me the form?* *Would you be able to meet on Thursday?*
- more formal: *Would it be possible to extend the deadline by a week?* *I would be grateful if you could confirm my booking.*
- tentative: *I was wondering if you could help me with something.*
- informal: *Can you send me the photos?*

*Please* + imperative (*Please send me the form.*) is fine for simple, expected actions (for example, from a service you are paying for), but sounds too direct for a favour.
:::

::: proof
A request asks the reader to do something, so it limits their freedom. English, especially in Britain and the United States, softens this with indirect forms that present the request as a question about ability or possibility, or as a wish (*I would be grateful if …*). The past forms *could*, *would* and *was wondering* add distance and therefore politeness, as described in pragmatics as "negative politeness" (see [[pragmatics/requests-refusals]]). The degree of softening depends on how big the request is and how well you know the reader: asking a friend to send photos needs little; asking a professor to extend a deadline needs more. Norms vary between individuals and settings within every country, so these are tendencies, not laws.
:::

::: warning *Please kindly*, *reply me* and *revert*
- *Please kindly …* is common in some varieties of English, including Indian English and English in China, and is understood everywhere. In British and American usage, readers often find it stiff or old-fashioned, because *please* and *kindly* do the same job. Use one of them: ✓ *Could you please reply by Friday?* ✓ *Please reply by Friday.*
- *Reply* takes *to* before the person or message: ✗ *Please reply me.* ✓ *Please reply to me.* ✓ *Please reply to this email.* More natural still: ✓ *I look forward to your reply.*
- *Revert* meaning ‘reply’ (*I will revert to you*) is used in Indian and some Asian business English but is unfamiliar to many British and American readers. *I'll get back to you* is understood everywhere.
:::

::: example Making a request more polite {#ex-requests}
Rewrite each request for an email to the person given. (a) To your landlord: *Fix the heating.* (b) To a professor you have not met: *I want to join your research project. Tell me how.* (c) To a friend: *I would be grateful if you could possibly lend me your bike on Saturday.*
::: solution
1. Decide how well you know the reader and how big the request is.
2. (a) A landlord has a duty to fix the heating, so the request is expected, but a bare imperative sounds rude. A neutral question with a reason works: *The heating in my flat stopped working yesterday. Could you arrange for someone to repair it this week?*
3. (b) A large favour from a stranger: use a formal, tentative form and explain why: *I am very interested in your research on urban air quality, and I was wondering whether there might be an opportunity for me to join the project. Would it be possible to meet briefly to discuss this?*
4. (c) The original is far too formal for a friend; it sounds cold or even sarcastic. Use an informal form: *Can I borrow your bike on Saturday?* or *Could I borrow your bike on Saturday? I'll bring it back by six.*
:::
:::

## Closing and signing off

End with a **closing line** that looks forward or offers help, then a **sign-off** with a comma, and your name on the next line.

- Closing lines: *I look forward to hearing from you.* (formal) *Please let me know if you need any more information.* *Thanks again for your help.* *See you on Friday!* (informal)
- Sign-offs:
  - formal, British: *Yours sincerely,* (when you began with a name: *Dear Ms Chen*) and *Yours faithfully,* (when you began *Dear Sir or Madam*);
  - formal, American: *Sincerely,* or *Sincerely yours,*;
  - neutral, both varieties: *Kind regards,* *Best regards,* *Best wishes,* *Regards,* *Best,*;
  - informal: *Cheers,* (mainly British), *See you,* *Love,* (family and close friends).

In formal emails, add your full name, and if useful your role or student number: *Li Hua (student no. 2304517)*. If your reader may not know how to address you, you can help them: *Li Hua (Ms)*, or write your family name in capitals: *LI Hua*.

::: example Rewriting a learner's email {#ex-rewrite}
Rewrite the email at the start of the lesson so that it follows the conventions of a neutral-to-formal email to a lecturer, Dr Sarah Evans.
::: solution
1. **Subject line**: name the topic and the essay: *Extension request: Essay 2*.
2. **Greeting**: title + family name: *Dear Dr Evans,*.
3. **Purpose first**: say who you are and what you want in the first two sentences.
4. **Details**: give the reason briefly; the lecturer does not need every problem, only enough to understand.
5. **Request**: specific — which essay, how long, and a polite form.
6. **Closing and sign-off**: thanks, a neutral sign-off, full name and group.
7. **Remove** *Sorry to disturb you*, *Please kindly reply me* and the double exclamation marks.

> **Subject:** Extension request: Essay 2
>
> Dear Dr Evans,
>
> I am a student in your Tuesday seminar (Group B), and I am writing to ask whether I could have a short extension for Essay 2, which is due on Friday.
>
> My grandmother has been seriously ill this month, and I have spent several days helping my family. I have finished my research and about half of the essay, but I need a little more time to complete it properly.
>
> Would it be possible to submit the essay by next Wednesday, 21 May? I can send you a draft of what I have written so far if that would be helpful.
>
> Thank you very much for considering my request.
>
> Kind regards,
>
> Li Hua (student no. 2304517)

The new email is about the same length, but the purpose comes in the first sentence and the request is specific.
:::
:::

## Informal emails and messages

Emails to friends follow few rules: they begin with *Hi* and a name, use contractions, short sentences and exclamation marks, and end with *See you soon*, *Love* or simply the name.

> **Subject:** Saturday!
>
> Hi Mei,
>
> Thanks so much for having us on Saturday — the dumplings were amazing! Kenji's still talking about them.
>
> I've put the photos in a shared folder: I'll send you the link tonight. And don't forget you promised to teach me your grandmother's recipe!
>
> See you next week,
>
> Ana

**Text messages and chat** are even more informal. Writers often leave out subjects and articles, use abbreviations and emoji, and send several short messages instead of one long one:

- *Running late — be there in 10 mins.*
- *Thx! See u tmrw.*
- *Got your msg. Call you after class.*

**Notes** left for someone at home or at work are similar: *Gone to the post office. Back by 4. — Ahmed* These "fragments" (see [[sentences-and-paragraphs/complete-sentences]]) are normal in messages and notes, where speed matters and the context is shared. They are not appropriate in formal emails.

::: example Changing register {#ex-register}
Rewrite this informal message as a neutral email to a teacher you know: *Hi! can't come to class tmrw, got a doctor's appointment. can u send me the slides? thx!!*
::: solution
1. **Greeting**: a name, without *Hi!*: *Dear Mr Lin,* or *Hello Mr Lin,*.
2. **Full sentences**: add the subjects (*I*) and articles, write words in full (*tomorrow*, *you*, *thanks*), and use capitals.
3. **Purpose and reason**: one sentence each.
4. **Request**: a question with *could*.
5. **Closing and sign-off**.

> Dear Mr Lin,
>
> I'm afraid I won't be able to come to class tomorrow because I have a doctor's appointment. Could you send me the slides from the lesson, please? I'll make sure I catch up before Thursday.
>
> Thank you,
>
> Wang Jun

Contractions (*I'm*, *won't*, *I'll*) are fine in a neutral email.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which ending is most suitable for a formal email that began *Dear Sir or Madam* in British English?
- [ ] *Love, Chen Yu*
- [ ] *Yours sincerely, Chen Yu*
- [x] *Yours faithfully, Chen Yu*
- [ ] *Cheers, Chen Yu*
::: solution
In traditional British usage, *Yours faithfully* follows *Dear Sir or Madam*, and *Yours sincerely* follows a greeting with a name. Many people today use *Kind regards* in both cases, which is also acceptable. *Love* and *Cheers* are informal.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is the best opening for an email to a company asking about a job advertisement?
- [ ] *Sorry to bother you, I am a student and I saw your advertisement and I want to ask some questions.*
- [x] *I am writing to ask about the summer internship advertised on your website.*
- [ ] *Hello! How are you? I hope you are fine.*
- [ ] *Please kindly reply me about the internship.*
::: solution
The second states the purpose clearly and formally in the first sentence. The first apologises unnecessarily and runs several clauses together; the third is too informal for a company you do not know and delays the purpose; the fourth uses *kindly* with *please* and the non-standard *reply me*.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Greetings and titles.** Chinese 尊敬的…… and 老师 have no direct English equivalents. In English, use *Dear* + title + family name (*Dear Professor Zhang*). *Teacher* is a job, not a title.
- **您好 and 此致敬礼.** A Chinese letter may have 您好 after the greeting and 此致 / 敬礼 at the end. English has no fixed equivalents: after *Dear …* you begin at once with the purpose, and at the end you use a closing line and a sign-off such as *Kind regards*. Do not translate 此致敬礼 as ✗ *Salute!* or ✗ *With high respect*.
- **Names.** Many English speakers cannot tell which part of a Chinese name is the family name, or whether a name is a man's or a woman's. Help your reader: sign with your full name, and if you like add *(Ms)* or *(Mr)*, write your family name in capitals, or add the name you prefer to be called (*Wang Jun (Jun)*).
- **Directness.** English emails at work and in study usually state the purpose in the first sentence. A long introduction or several apologies before the request — common in polite Chinese messages — can make an English reader wait too long for the point.
- **WeChat habits.** Messages on chat apps in any language are short and informal. Emails to teachers, universities and employers in English are expected to be complete, with a subject line, a greeting, full sentences and a sign-off.
:::

::: history
The first email sent between two computers on a network is usually dated to 1971, when the engineer Ray Tomlinson, working on the ARPANET in the United States, chose the *@* sign to separate a user's name from the name of their computer. Many conventions of email, however, come from the much older business letter: the greeting with *Dear*, the closing *Yours sincerely* and *Yours faithfully*, and the habit of stating the purpose at the start. The title *Ms*, which does not show whether a woman is married, was suggested in print as early as 1901 but came into wide use only in the 1970s; it is now the usual default in both British and American English.
:::

## Where this leads

The paragraphs inside an email follow the principles of [[sentences-and-paragraphs/topic-sentences]] and [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]]. The politeness of requests, refusals and apologies is treated in depth in [[pragmatics/requests-refusals]] and [[pragmatics/writing-to-people]], and spoken messages in [[everyday-english/phone-messages]]. Checking an email before you send it is one use of the checklist in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/editing]].

::: summary
- Choose a register — formal, neutral or informal — to suit the reader and the purpose; neutral is the safest default ([[#def-register]]).
- A clear email has a specific subject line, a greeting, the purpose in the first or second sentence, short paragraphs of detail, a clear request, a closing line and a sign-off ([[#rule-email-structure]]).
- Formal greetings use a title and the family name (*Dear Dr Evans*); *Dear Sir or Madam* or a team name when the name is unknown; never *Dear teacher* ([[#rule-greetings]]).
- Make requests with *Could you …?*, *Would it be possible …?*, *I would be grateful if …*; avoid *please kindly* and *reply me* ([[#rule-requests]]).
- British *Yours sincerely* / *Yours faithfully*; American *Sincerely*; neutral *Kind regards*, *Best wishes*; informal *Cheers*, *Love*.
- Messages and notes may drop subjects and use abbreviations; formal emails need full sentences.
:::
