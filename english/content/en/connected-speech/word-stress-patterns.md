Look up *Japanese* in a dictionary and you will find /ˌdʒæpəˈniːz/: the main stress is on the last syllable, *-nese*. Now listen to someone say *a Japanese car* or *Japanese food*. Very often the strong beat moves to the front: **JA**panese **FOOD**. The same happens to *thirteen*: *She's thir**TEEN***, but ***THIR**teen **PEO**ple*. Did the speaker make a mistake? No. Stress in English is not fixed to a word like a label: it belongs to a word, but it also adjusts to the words around it.

[[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics]] showed what stress is and gave the main patterns for two-syllable words, the suffixes that fix the stress (*-ion*, *-ic*, *-ity*) and the suffixes that leave it alone (*-ness*, *-ment*, *-ful*). This chapter goes further. It looks at longer words, at endings counted from the end of the word, at prefixes, at compounds that break the "first part stressed" pattern, at phrasal verbs and the nouns made from them, and at **stress shift** — the movement in *Japanese food*. These patterns are the bridge between the word and the sentence: from the next chapter on, stress belongs to whole phrases.

## Stress levels and stress patterns

A long word does not simply have "one stressed syllable and the rest unstressed". It is useful to separate three levels.

::: definition Stress levels {#def-levels}
In a word of several syllables we distinguish:

1. **primary stress** — the strongest syllable, marked ˈ in a transcription: the *-ca-* of *education* /ˌedʒuˈkeɪʃn/;
2. **secondary stress** — a syllable with a full vowel and some prominence, earlier in the word than the primary stress, marked ˌ: the *ed-* of *education*;
3. **unstressed** syllables — with no mark. Most have a weak vowel (/ə/, /i/, /u/, /ɪ/) or a syllabic consonant, like the *-tion* of *education*; a few keep a full vowel, like the last syllable of *photograph* /ˈfəʊtəɡrɑːf/ (GB).

In running text we show the pattern with circles: O for the primary stress, o for every other syllable. *Education* is ooOo; *photograph* is Ooo; *thirteen* is oO.
:::

A secondary stress almost always comes **before** the primary stress. After the primary stress, English syllables are normally unstressed, even when their vowel is full. This matters for stress shift, below: a word like *Japanese* has a secondary stress *ready* to take over (*ˌJapaˈnese*), while a word like *Chinese* has a full vowel before its primary stress, /ˌtʃaɪˈniːz/, which can do the same.

::: theorem Endings that count from the end {#rule-ending}
Several common endings place the primary stress at a fixed distance from the end of the word.

1. **-ate**, in words of three or more syllables: stress on the third syllable from the end — **CON**centrate /ˈkɒnsəntreɪt/ (GB), **ED**ucate, com**MU**nicate /kəˈmjuːnɪkeɪt/, in**VES**tigate, ap**PRE**ciate /əˈpriːʃieɪt/.
2. **-ise / -ize** (GB uses both spellings, GA uses *-ize*): usually the same — **OR**ganise, **RE**cognise, **CRI**ticise, a**PO**logise /əˈpɒlədʒaɪz/ (GB), /əˈpɑːlədʒaɪz/ (GA).
3. **-ify**: stress on the syllable just before *-ify* — i**DEN**tify /aɪˈdentɪfaɪ/, **CLAS**sify, **JUS**tify.
4. **-ography, -ology, -onomy, -osophy, -ometer**: stress on the *o* just before the ending — ge**OG**raphy /dʒiˈɒɡrəfi/ (GB), bi**OL**ogy, as**TRON**omy, phi**LOS**ophy, ther**MOM**eter. Adding *-ical* moves it again: bio**LO**gical /ˌbaɪəˈlɒdʒɪkl/ (GB).
:::

::: proof
These endings come from Latin and Greek, and they behave like the stress-fixing suffixes of [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics#rule-suffix]]: the stress is decided by counting syllables from the end, not from the beginning. This is why the stress moves when a further suffix is added (*biology* → *biological*). The rules are tendencies with exceptions. Two-syllable *-ate* verbs, for example, are not covered by rule 1, and here GB and GA often differ: *donate* is /dəʊˈneɪt/ in GB but usually /ˈdoʊneɪt/ in GA; *rotate* and *frustrate* behave the same way (GB stress on *-ate*, GA on the first syllable), while *create* /kriˈeɪt/ is stressed on *-ate* in both. When the dictionary and the rule disagree, the dictionary wins.
:::

::: warning Do not stress the ending
✗ communi**CATE**, ✗ organ**ISE**, ✗ identi**FY**, ✗ geo**GRA**phy.
✓ com**MU**nicate, **OR**ganise, i**DEN**tify, ge**OG**raphy.

The endings *-ate*, *-ise* and *-ify* are long and contain a diphthong (/eɪt/, /aɪz/, /aɪ/), so they sound "strong", and learners often put the main stress on them. They do keep a full vowel, but they are not the stressed syllable: the beat comes earlier in the word.
:::

### GB and GA: *-ary*, *-ory*, *-ery*

One family of endings sounds very different in the two reference accents. After a stressed syllable, GB reduces *-ary*, *-ory* and *-ery* to weak vowels, and often loses one of them altogether; GA keeps a full vowel there, with a secondary stress.

| word | GB | GA |
|---|---|---|
| *dictionary* | /ˈdɪkʃənri/ (also /ˈdɪkʃənəri/) | /ˈdɪkʃəˌneri/ |
| *secretary* | /ˈsekrətri/ | /ˈsekrəˌteri/ |
| *territory* | /ˈterətri/ | /ˈterəˌtɔːri/ |
| *necessary* | /ˈnesəsəri/ | /ˈnesəˌseri/ |

The primary stress is in the same place in both accents; what differs is the rhythm after it. A GB *dictionary* has three syllables, quickly said after the stress (Ooo); a GA *dictionary* has four, with a second beat on *-nar-* (Ooo with a small peak, written ˈ…ˌ). Neither is a mistake. Choose one model and be consistent, and expect to hear both.

::: quiz
Where is the primary stress in *investigate*?
- [ ] *IN*-ves-ti-gate
- [x] in-*VES*-ti-gate
- [ ] in-ves-*TI*-gate
- [ ] in-ves-ti-*GATE*
::: solution
*-ate* in a word of three or more syllables puts the stress on the third syllable from the end ([[#rule-ending]]): count back *gate* (1), *ti* (2), *ves* (3). The word is in**VES**tigate /ɪnˈvestɪɡeɪt/. The ending *-gate* keeps a full vowel /eɪ/, but it is not stressed.
:::
:::

## Prefixes

Prefixes such as *un-*, *in-*, *dis-*, *mis-* and *re-* are usually **not** the primary stress of a word: the base keeps its own stress.

- **HAP**py → un**HAP**py /ʌnˈhæpi/; **POS**sible → im**POS**sible; a**GREE** → disa**GREE** /ˌdɪsəˈɡriː/; under**STAND** → misunder**STAND**.

Two details are worth knowing.

1. A prefix often keeps a **full vowel** and a secondary stress: *unhappy* has /ʌ/, not /ə/; *disagree* has /ɪ/ with secondary stress.
2. *re-* has two pronunciations. In old words where the meaning of 'again' has faded, it is weak: *recover* /rɪˈkʌvə/ (GB) 'get well', *remark*, *repeat*. When it clearly means 'again', it is a full /riː/ with secondary stress: *re-cover* /ˌriːˈkʌvə/ (GB) 'cover again', *rewrite*, *reopen*. The two *recover*s are told apart by this vowel.

When two words with prefixes are **contrasted**, the prefixes take the stress: *Did you say **EN**courage or **DIS**courage?* — *I said **UN**happy, not **HAP**py.* This is contrastive stress, and it belongs to the sentence, not the word: see [[connected-speech/sentence-stress]].

## Compounds: when the second part is stressed

The usual pattern for compound nouns is stress on the first part: *a **BUS** stop*, *a **BOOK**shop*, *a **GREEN**house* ([[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics]]). But a large group of compounds, and some kinds of compound adjective and verb, stress the second part. Dictionaries such as LPD and the major learner's dictionaries show the stress of compounds, and it is worth checking them; the rule below covers the main groups.

::: theorem Compounds stressed on the second part {#rule-compound}
The second part usually takes the primary stress in these groups (the first part keeps a secondary stress):

1. **Names of roads and places**, except those ending in *Street*: *Oxford **ROAD***, *Fifth **AV**enue*, *Trafalgar **SQUARE***, *Kew **GAR**dens* — but ***OX**ford Street*, ***BAKER** Street*.
2. **What it is made of**, when the first part is a material or an ingredient: *apple **PIE***, *paper **BAG***, *stone **WALL***.
3. **When or where**, when the first part names a time or a place: *Christmas **DAY***, *Christmas **EVE***, *town **HALL***, *summer **HOL**iday*.
4. **Compound adjectives**, especially those ending in *-ed* or *-ing*, or beginning with a number: *bad-**TEM**pered*, *open-**MIN**ded*, *good-**LOOK**ing*, *second-**CLASS***, *three-**WHEEL**er*.
5. **Compounds used as adverbs or verbs**: *north-**EAST***, *head-**FIRST***, *down**STAIRS***; *ill-**TREAT***, *down**GRADE***.

In group 3 compare ***CHRIST**mas card*, which follows the usual first-part pattern: a card is not made "at Christmas" in the same way, and the language does not explain every case.
:::

::: proof
The usual first-part stress marks a compound as one name for one kind of thing: a *greenhouse* is not just a house that happens to be green. When the first part only describes the second — what it is made of, when or where it is — the compound behaves more like a phrase of adjective + noun, and phrases are stressed at the end (*a green **HOUSE***). That is the reasoning behind groups 2 and 3, and it explains many cases. But the system has grown word by word, so it is not fully predictable: *apple **PIE*** is stressed at the end, while ***OR**ange juice*, also named after a fruit, is usually stressed at the start; *Street* names are stressed on the first part, *Road* names on the second, for no reason that speakers know (Roach 2009 and Cruttenden 2014 both list such cases and call them unpredictable). Learn the common ones as you meet them.
:::

::: example Street, road or square? {#ex-compound}
Mark the primary stress of each name, then say which group of [[#rule-compound]] it belongs to.

1. *Nanjing Road* 2. *Wall Street* 3. *Tiananmen Square* 4. *a paper cup* 5. *a Christmas tree* 6. *New Year's Day*
::: solution
1. *Nanjing **ROAD*** — group 1: a *Road* name, stressed on the second part.
2. ***WALL** Street* — the exception in group 1: *Street* names are stressed on the first part.
3. *Tiananmen **SQUARE*** — group 1 again (*Square*, like *Road* and *Avenue*).
4. *a paper **CUP*** — group 2: the first part names the material.
5. *a **CHRIST**mas tree* — this one is **not** in group 3: like *Christmas card*, it follows the usual first-part stress. A good dictionary marks it ˈ… .
6. *New Year's **DAY*** — group 3: a time word + *Day*, like *Christmas Day*.

Items 5 and 6 show why the rule is a guide, not a law: both begin with a festival, but only one is stressed at the end.
:::
:::

*Bus stop* is an ordinary compound noun: ***BUS** stop*. The others are in the groups of [[#rule-compound]] that stress the second part: *town **HALL*** (place), *apple **PIE*** (ingredient), *Oxford **ROAD*** (a road name not ending in *Street*).

## Phrasal verbs and the nouns made from them

Many everyday verbs are made of a verb and a particle: *set up*, *take off*, *break down*, *log in*, *check in*, *work out*. Many of them have a noun with the same parts. Stress tells them apart.

::: theorem Phrasal verbs and their nouns {#rule-phrasal}
1. In a **phrasal verb**, the **particle** is stressed: *The plane took **OFF***; *We set **UP** the room*; *The car broke **DOWN***; *You need to log **IN***. When an object noun follows, the noun usually carries the main stress of the phrase, but the particle is still a strong syllable with a full vowel: *set **UP** a **COM**pany*.
2. In the **noun** (or adjective) made from it, the **verb** part is stressed: *a smooth **TAKE**off*, *a new **SET**up*, *a **BREAK**down*, *your **LOG**in*, *the **CHECK**-in desk*, *a **WORK**out*.
3. A preposition after a verb (*look at*, *wait for*, *depend on*) is different: it is normally unstressed and weak. *I'm **LOOK**ing at it.* Only adverb particles take the stress.
:::

::: proof
Rule 2 is the compound rule again: the noun *setup* is one name for one thing, stressed on its first part like *bus stop*. Rule 1 follows from the stress of the sentence: a phrasal verb is a verb + adverb, and adverbs are content words, which are stressed ([[connected-speech/sentence-stress]]); at the end of a phrase, the last content word is the most prominent. Prepositions are function words, so in rule 3 they are weak ([[connected-speech/weak-forms]]). The difference can be heard: *What are you **LOOK**ing at?* (preposition) against *Could you look **UP** this word?* (particle).
:::

::: example Verb or noun? {#ex-phrasal}
Mark the stress of the words in bold and say whether each is a phrasal verb or a noun.

1. *There's been a **break-in** at the school.*
2. *Someone tried to **break in** last night.*
3. *What time is **check-in**?*
4. *We can **check in** online.*
5. *Let's **work out** the cost.*
6. *I do a short **workout** every morning.*
::: solution
1. ***BREAK**-in* — a noun (after *a*): stress on the verb part.
2. *break **IN*** — a phrasal verb: stress on the particle.
3. ***CHECK**-in* — a noun.
4. *check **IN*** — a phrasal verb.
5. *work **OUT** the **COST*** — a phrasal verb; the main stress of the phrase goes to the object *cost*, but *out* is still strong, with its full vowel /aʊ/.
6. ***WORK**out* — a noun.

The spelling helps a little (nouns are often written with a hyphen or as one word), but in speech the stress is the only clue.
:::
:::

## Stress shift

Now back to *Japanese food*. Words such as *Japanese*, *thirteen*, *afternoon*, *Chinese*, *unknown* and *bad-tempered* have two strong syllables: a secondary stress followed by the primary stress (ooO, oO). When such a word comes before another strong syllable, the primary stress often moves to the earlier one.

::: theorem Stress shift {#rule-shift}
A word with a secondary stress before its primary stress (pattern ˌ…ˈ…) often moves its main stress to the earlier syllable when it is followed closely by a stressed syllable in the same phrase:

- ˌthirˈteen → ***THIR**teen **PEO**ple*; ˌJapaˈnese → ***JA**panese **FOOD***; ˌafterˈnoon → ***AF**ternoon **TEA***; ˌbad-ˈtempered → *a **BAD**-tempered **TEA**cher*; ˌunˈknown → *an **UN**known **AR**tist*.

The shift does **not** happen when the word is at the end of the phrase (*She's thir**TEEN***; *It's Japa**NESE***) or when the next syllable is unstressed (*thir**TEEN** a**GAIN***). It is a tendency, frequent in fluent speech, not an obligation.
:::

::: proof
English tends to avoid two strong beats side by side (a **stress clash**). Stress shift spaces them out: *thirteen people* has the strong syllables *thir-* and *peo-* separated by an unstressed one, which gives an alternation of strong and weak (O o O o). Liberman and Prince (1977) called this the *rhythm rule*. It only works with words that already have an earlier strong syllable to move to: *Chinese* /ˌtʃaɪˈniːz/ can shift (***CHI**nese **FOOD***), but *Japan* /dʒəˈpæn/ cannot, because its first vowel is weak: we say *Ja**PAN**'s **EX**ports*, with no shift. The rhythm of English is discussed in [[connected-speech/rhythm]].
:::

::: example Thirteen or thirty? {#ex-shift}
A shop assistant says: *That's thirteen pounds.* A customer hears *thirty*. Using [[#rule-shift]], explain why the mistake is easy to make, and what the customer can listen for instead.
::: solution
1. Alone, *thirteen* is oO (/ˌθɜːˈtiːn/ GB) and *thirty* is Oo (/ˈθɜːti/ GB, /ˈθɜːrti/ GA), so the stress keeps them apart.
2. Before *pounds*, a stressed syllable, *thirteen* shifts: ***THIR**teen **POUNDS*** — now it has the same pattern as ***THIR**ty **POUNDS***. Stress no longer helps.
3. What still differs is the second syllable: *-teen* has the long vowel /iː/ and ends in /n/; *-ty* has the short weak /i/ and no /n/. In GA the *t* of *thirty* is often a tap, [ˈθɜːrɾi], which never happens in *thirteen*.

So the customer should listen for the long /iːn/, not for the stress — or simply check: *Sorry, one-three or three-oh?* [[everyday-english/numbers-in-conversation]] has more on saying and checking numbers.
:::
:::

## Same spelling, different vowel

[[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics]] showed noun–verb pairs that differ in stress (*a **RE**cord*, *to re**CORD***). A second group has the **same** stress in both uses, but a different final vowel. These are words in *-ate*: the verb has a full /eɪt/, the noun or adjective a weak /ət/.

| word | noun or adjective | verb |
|---|---|---|
| *estimate* | /ˈestɪmət/ *a rough estimate* | /ˈestɪmeɪt/ *estimate the cost* |
| *separate* | /ˈseprət/ *separate rooms* | /ˈsepəreɪt/ *separate the eggs* |
| *graduate* | /ˈɡrædʒuət/ *a university graduate* | /ˈɡrædʒueɪt/ *graduate next year* |
| *approximate* | /əˈprɒksɪmət/ (GB) *an approximate figure* | /əˈprɒksɪmeɪt/ (GB) *approximate the curve* |

The stressed syllable is the same, so a learner who gets the stress right will be understood either way; but using the full /eɪt/ in the noun or adjective makes it sound like the verb.

::: example A word family on the move {#ex-family}
Mark the primary (and any secondary) stress in *democrat*, *democracy*, *democratic*, and explain each one with the rules of this chapter and of [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics]]. What happens to the vowel of the first syllable?
::: solution
1. ***DEM**ocrat* /ˈdeməkræt/ — learned with the word; Ooo, with a full vowel /æ/ in the last syllable.
2. *de**MOC**racy* /dɪˈmɒkrəsi/ (GB), /dɪˈmɑːkrəsi/ (GA) — *-acy*, like *-ography*, puts the stress on the syllable before it; oOoo.
3. *ˌdemo**CRAT**ic* /ˌdeməˈkrætɪk/ — *-ic* stresses the syllable just before it ([[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics#rule-suffix]]); the first syllable has a secondary stress; ooOo.

The first vowel is full /e/ when the syllable is stressed (*democrat*, *democratic*) and weak /ɪ/ in *democracy*, where the stress has moved away. Because *democratic* has a secondary stress on *dem-*, it can shift in a phrase: ***DEM**ocratic **COUN**tries* ([[#rule-shift]]).
:::
:::

In which phrase does stress shift usually happen?
- [ ] *She's from Japan.*
- [x] *an afternoon nap*
- [ ] *It's at three in the afternoon.*
- [ ] *Is he thirteen?*
::: solution

In the other sentences the word is at the end of the phrase (*afternoon*, *thirteen*) or has no earlier strong syllable (*Japan* /dʒəˈpæn/).

::: note English and Chinese
- **Mandarin also uses weakness to tell words apart.** In Mandarin the difference between *dōngxī* (东西 'east and west') and *dōngxi* (东西 'thing'), or *dìdào* (地道 'tunnel') and *dìdao* (地道 'authentic'), is made by the second syllable: a full tone in one, a light, short neutral tone in the other. English *a **GREEN**house* and *a green **HOUSE*** work in a comparable way — the difference is which part is strong. So the idea that "weakness changes meaning" is not foreign to a Mandarin speaker; what is new is that in English it applies to almost every compound and phrase.
- **Particles of phrasal verbs.** Chinese has verb + complement compounds (*zhàn qǐlái* 站起来 'stand up'), whose complements are often light; learners may carry this over and say *stand up*, *give up*, *log in* with a weak particle. In English the particle of a phrasal verb is strong: *give **UP***, *log **IN***.
- **Long endings.** Learners who give every syllable a full tone tend to make long endings like *-ate*, *-ise* and *-ify* the strongest part of the word. Practise these words with a clear beat earlier: com**MU**nicate, **OR**ganise.
- **Numbers.** The *-teen*/*-ty* problem of [[#ex-shift]] affects learners and native speakers alike: it is a property of English, not a learner error.
:::

::: history
The first systematic study of English stress for learners was Roger Kingdon's *The Groundwork of English Stress* (1958), which listed the stress of thousands of words, suffixes and compounds. A decade later Noam Chomsky and Morris Halle's *The Sound Pattern of English* (1968) tried to derive English word stress from a small set of rules, counting syllables from the end of the word as the Latin stress rule does — the basis of [[#rule-ending]]. In 1977 Mark Liberman and Alan Prince proposed that stress is a matter of relative strength between neighbours, drawn as a tree or a grid; their account of the "rhythm rule" explained stress shift ([[#rule-shift]]) as the avoidance of clashing beats. Modern pronouncing dictionaries, above all LPD (1990; 3rd edition 2008), mark the stress of compounds and phrases and note where stress shift is likely.
:::

## Where this leads

Stress now moves from the word to the sentence: [[connected-speech/sentence-stress]] explains which words in a sentence are stressed and where the main stress (the nucleus) falls. The weakening of unstressed words is in [[connected-speech/weak-forms]], and the alternation of strong and weak beats behind stress shift is in [[connected-speech/rhythm]]. Prefixes and suffixes as a way of building words are the subject of [[word-formation/suffixes]], and word families as vocabulary in [[core-vocabulary/word-families]].

::: summary
- Long words have **primary** stress, **secondary** stress (before the primary) and unstressed syllables, some with full vowels ([[#def-levels]]).
- *-ate* and *-ise* in longer words usually stress the third syllable from the end; *-ify* and *-ology*, *-ography* stress the syllable before them ([[#rule-ending]]).
- *-ary*, *-ory*, *-ery* are weak in GB (*dictionary* /ˈdɪkʃənri/) and keep a full vowel in GA (/ˈdɪkʃəˌneri/).
- Prefixes rarely take the primary stress; *re-* meaning 'again' is a full /riː/ (*re-cover*).
- Road and square names, materials, times and places, many compound adjectives, adverbs and verbs stress the **second** part; *Street* names do not ([[#rule-compound]]).
- Phrasal verbs stress the particle (*set **UP***); the nouns made from them stress the verb (*a **SET**up*) ([[#rule-phrasal]]).
- Words like *thirteen* and *Japanese* shift their stress forward before a stressed syllable (*THIRteen PEOple*) ([[#rule-shift]]).
:::

Which syllable (1, 2, 3, …) carries the primary stress in *photosynthesis*? (Use the rule for words ending in a Greek element: the stress falls on the syllable before *-thesis*, as in *hypothesis*.)
::: solution
*Photosynthesis* /ˌfəʊtəʊˈsɪnθəsɪs/ (GB): pho-to-**SYN**-the-sis. Like *hy**POTH**esis* and *pa**REN**thesis*, the stress falls on the syllable just before *-thesis*: *syn*, the third syllable. The answer is 3. There is a secondary stress on *pho-*.
:::

::: exercise Stress shift {level=2}
Say whether stress shift is likely in each phrase, and mark the stresses.

1. *a Chinese restaurant* 2. *I'm Chinese.* 3. *fifteen minutes* 4. *a well-known writer* 5. *The writer is well-known.* 6. *Japan's economy*
::: solution
1. Likely: ***CHI**nese **RES**taurant*.
2. No: at the end of the phrase, *Chi**NESE***.
3. Likely: ***FIF**teen **MIN**utes*.
4. Likely: *a **WELL**-known **WRI**ter*.
5. No: *well-**KNOWN*** at the end.
6. No: *Japan* has no earlier strong syllable (/dʒəˈpæn/): *Ja**PAN**'s e**CON**omy*.
:::

::: exercise Noun, adjective or verb? {level=2}
Give the pronunciation of the last syllable of the word in bold (/eɪt/ or /ət/).

1. *Please **separate** the white clothes from the coloured ones.* 2. *We have **separate** bedrooms.* 3. *She's a **graduate** of Fudan University.* 4. *He'll **graduate** in June.* 5. *Give me an **estimate**.*
::: solution
1. /eɪt/ (verb) 2. /ət/ (adjective) 3. /ət/ (noun) 4. /eɪt/ (verb) 5. /ət/ (noun). The primary stress is on the first syllable in all of them.

::: exercise GB and GA {level=2}
Give the GB and the GA pronunciation of *secretary* and *territory*, and describe the difference in rhythm in one sentence.
::: solution
*secretary*: GB /ˈsekrətri/, GA /ˈsekrəˌteri/. *territory*: GB /ˈterətri/, GA /ˈterəˌtɔːri/. GB reduces the ending to weak vowels and usually has one syllable fewer; GA keeps a full vowel with a secondary stress, so the word has a second, weaker beat after the main one.
:::

::: exercise Correct the stresses {level=3}
A student reads aloud: *My SISter is a seCRETary at the uniVERsity. She has to orGANise meetings and comMUnicate with stuDENTS from interNAtional programmes, and she works on OXford ROAD.* Find the stresses that are wrong, correct them, and give the rule for each.
::: hint
Check *secretary*, *organise*, *students* and *Oxford Road*. Four of the stresses shown are correct.
:::
::: solution
- ✗ *se**CRE**tary* → ✓ ***SEC**retary* — learned with the word; the ending *-ary* is weak (GB) or has only a secondary stress (GA).
- ✗ *or**GAN**ise* → ✓ ***OR**ganise* — *-ise* in a longer word: third syllable from the end ([[#rule-ending]]).
- ✗ *stu**DENTS*** → ✓ ***STU**dents* — a two-syllable noun, stressed on the first syllable.
- *OXford ROAD* — both capitals are not equally right: the main stress is on ***ROAD*** (*Oxford **ROAD***, [[#rule-compound]]), with only a secondary stress on *Ox-*.
- Correct: ***SIS**ter*, *uni**VER**sity* (the stress before *-ity*), *com**MU**nicate* (*-ate*, third from the end), *inter**NA**tional* — though in *international programmes*, stress shift is likely: ***IN**ternational **PRO**grammes*.
:::
:::
