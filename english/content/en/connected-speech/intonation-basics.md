Say *Monday* as an answer to *When are you leaving?* Your voice falls on *Mon-*: *↘**Mon**day.* Now say it as a surprised question — *Monday? I thought you said Friday!* — and your voice rises: *↗**Mon**day?* The word is the same, its sounds and its stress are the same, and yet the two utterances do different jobs. The first gives information and is finished; the second asks for it.

The difference is **intonation**: the use of pitch — how high or low the voice is — to add meaning to whole stretches of speech. [[connected-speech/sentence-stress]] showed where the main pitch movement goes (the **nucleus**). This chapter is about *which* movement it is: the falls and rises of English, and their main uses in statements, questions, lists, question tags and longer sentences. The following chapter, [[connected-speech/intonation-meaning]], looks at how pitch shows attitude; and [[connected-speech/tones-and-intonation]] compares English intonation with the tones of Mandarin.

## Tones in English

::: definition Intonation and tone {#def-tone}
**Intonation** is the pattern of pitch over a tone unit ([[connected-speech/sentence-stress#def-nucleus]]). Its most important part is the **nuclear tone** (or simply **tone**): the pitch movement that begins on the nucleus and continues through the tail.

In English, a tone belongs to the **tone unit**, not to the word. Saying *Monday* with a fall or a rise does not make it a different word; it changes what the speaker is doing with it — telling, asking, continuing. In a **tone language** such as Mandarin, by contrast, the pitch pattern of each syllable is part of the word: *mā* 妈 'mother' and *mǎ* 马 'horse' are different words. The word *tone* is used in both cases, but it means two different things.
:::

English has a small number of nuclear tones. The four most important are shown here on the nucleus *Mon-* of *Monday* (the tail *-day* continues the movement):

| tone | mark | pitch movement | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **fall** | ↘ | from high (or mid) to low | *↘**Mon**day.* (an answer) |
| **rise** | ↗ | from low (or mid) upwards | *↗**Mon**day?* (a question) |
| **fall-rise** | ↘↗ | down, then up again | *↘↗**Mon**day …* (but not Tuesday) |
| **rise-fall** | ↗↘ | up, then down | *↗↘**Mon**day!* (impressed, or "obviously") |

Wells (2006) treats the fall, the rise and the fall-rise as the three basic tones of English, and the rise-fall as a stronger variety of the fall. Cruttenden (2014) and O'Connor and Arnold (1961) also distinguish a **high rise** from a **low rise**, and some writers add a **level** tone (a flat pitch, heard in lists and in chanted speech). For a learner, the fall, the rise and the fall-rise are enough to begin with.

::: proof
Pitch is relative. A child, a woman and a man saying *↘**Mon**day* start their falls at very different frequencies, but each falls from high to low **within their own voice range**. What a listener hears is the direction and size of the movement, not the absolute pitch. That is why intonation is described with arrows and contours rather than with numbers.

When the nucleus has a long tail, the movement is spread over it. In *I'll ˈsee you on ↘**Mon**day*, the fall happens on *Mon-* and *-day* is low. In *↗**Mon**day morning?* the rise begins on *Mon-* and continues up through *-day mor-ning*. If the nucleus is the very last syllable, the whole movement must happen on it: *I'm ↘↗**sure*** — a fall-rise squeezed into one syllable — is perfectly normal in English, but learners need to practise it.
:::

::: example One sentence, four tones {#ex-tones}
*She's a doctor* has its nucleus on *doc-*. What does each tone suggest?

1. *She's a ↘**doc**tor.* 2. *She's a ↗**doc**tor?* 3. *She's a ↘↗**doc**tor …* 4. *She's a ↗↘**doc**tor!*
::: solution
1. A **fall**: a plain statement of fact — the answer to *What does your sister do?*
2. A **rise**: a question in statement form, perhaps surprised — *Really? I thought she was a nurse.*
3. A **fall-rise**: a statement with something left unsaid — *She's a doctor* (but she's not a very good one / but that doesn't mean she's always right).
4. A **rise-fall**: impressed, or "obviously" — *She's a doctor! Of course she knows.*

The words, the sounds and the place of the nucleus are the same in all four; only the tone changes, and with it the speaker's message. The meanings of the fall-rise and the rise-fall are discussed further in [[connected-speech/intonation-meaning]].
:::

## Statements and questions

::: theorem Tones in statements and questions {#rule-questions}
1. **Statements** normally have a **fall**: *I ˈlive in ↘**Bris**tol.* *It's ˈstarting to ↘**rain**.*
2. **Wh-questions** (*what*, *where*, *who*, *why*, *how* …) normally have a **fall**: *ˈWhere do you ↘**live**?* *ˈWhat's the ↘**time**?* A rise is also possible; it sounds warmer, more interested or gentler: *ˈWhat's your ↗**name**?* (to a child).
3. **Yes/no questions** often have a **rise**: *Are you ↗**rea**dy?* *Do you ˈlike ↗**cof**fee?* A fall is also common, especially when the speaker is insistent, businesslike or already expects the answer: *Are you ↘**rea**dy?* (we have to go).
4. **Questions in statement form** — a statement used as a question, without inversion — need a **rise**, because nothing else shows that they are questions: *You're ↗**leav**ing?* *She ˈpassed the ↗**test**?*
5. **Alternative questions** (a choice between named options) **rise on the first** option and **fall on the last**: *Would you ˈlike ↗**tea** | or ↘**cof**fee?* With a single rise at the end, the same words ask a yes/no question: *Would you like ˈtea or ↗**cof**fee?* means "Would you like a hot drink?" (answer: *Yes, please* or *No, thanks*).
:::

::: proof
The fall is the "finished" tone of English: it tells the listener that the speaker has said what they wanted and that the message is complete. Statements and wh-questions are complete in this sense — a wh-question is a complete request for a particular piece of information. The rise signals that something is open: the speaker is waiting for the listener, or has more to say. A yes/no question is open in this way, which is why it so often rises.

But the link between questions and rises is weaker than many textbooks suggest. Grammar already shows that *Are you ready?* and *Where do you live?* are questions, so intonation is free to do other work. Fries (1964), who analysed yes/no questions in recordings of an American television panel game, found more falls than rises; and Wells (2006) and Cruttenden (2014) both note that falling yes/no questions are common in GB and GA. It is when there is **no** grammatical sign of a question (rule 4) that the rise becomes necessary.
:::

::: example Choosing the tone {#ex-questions}
Mark the nucleus and the most likely tone in each, and give a reason.

1. *What time does the film start?*
2. *Is this seat free?*
3. *You've never been to Beijing?*
4. *Do you want the red one or the blue one?* (There are only two.)
5. *I've lost my keys.*
::: solution
1. *ˈWhat ˈtime does the ˈfilm ↘**start**?* — a wh-question: fall.
2. *Is ˈthis seat ↗**free**?* — a yes/no question, polite and open: rise. (*Is this seat ↘**free**?* is also possible, but sounds more businesslike.)
3. *You've ˈnever been to Bei↗**jing**?* — a question in statement form, with surprise: rise. Without the rise it would be a statement.
4. *Do you want the ↗**red** one | or the ↘**blue** one?* — an alternative question: rise on the first option, fall on the last. (*One* is an empty word, so the nucleus is on the colour, as in [[connected-speech/sentence-stress#rule-deaccent]].)
5. *I've ˈlost my ↘**keys**.* — a statement: fall.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A shop assistant asks *Would you like a bag or a box?* with a rise on *bag* and a fall on *box*. What is the best answer?
- [ ] *Yes, please.*
- [x] *A bag, please.*
- [ ] *No, thank you.*
- [ ] *Either is fine, yes.*
::: solution
A rise on the first option and a fall on the last make this an **alternative question**: the assistant offers a choice between two things, and expects you to choose one. *Yes, please* would answer the other reading (one rise at the end), which asks whether you want any container at all.
:::
:::

::: warning Not every question rises
✗ *ˈWhere do you ↗**live**?* — said with a high rise to a stranger, as a routine question, this can sound like a request to repeat (*Where did you say you live?*) or like talking to a child.
✓ *ˈWhere do you ↘**live**?*

✗ *Would you like ↗**tea** | or ↗**cof**fee?* — two rises leave the list of choices open, so the listener may wait for a third option.
✓ *Would you like ↗**tea** | or ↘**cof**fee?*

The rule "questions go up" is a useful rule only for questions in statement form. For other questions, learn the fall as the normal tone of wh-questions and choose the rise or fall for yes/no questions by meaning.
:::

## Lists, tags and unfinished speech

::: theorem Lists and question tags {#rule-tags}
1. **Lists.** In a complete (closed) list, each item except the last has a **rise** (or a level tone), and the last item has a **fall**: *We ˈneed ↗**eggs**, | ↗**milk**, | ↗**bread** | and ↘**but**ter.* If every item rises, the list sounds open — there could be more: *We need ↗**eggs**, | ↗**milk**, | ↗**bread** …*
2. **Question tags with a fall** ask the listener to **agree**: the speaker is fairly sure. *It's ˈcold to↘**day**, | ↘**is**n't it?* (I'm making conversation.)
3. **Question tags with a rise** ask a **real question**: the speaker is not sure. *You've ˈgot the ↘**tick**ets, | ↗**have**n't you?* (Have you? I'm not sure.)

The tag is a tone unit of its own, and its nucleus is the auxiliary (*isn't*, *haven't*, *don't*). The main clause before it usually has a fall.
:::

::: proof
Both rules follow from the idea that a fall is "finished" and a rise is "open". In a closed list, the rises on the early items say "there is more to come", and the final fall says "that's all". With a falling tag, the speaker treats the matter as settled and asks for nothing more than agreement; the expected answer is *Yes, it is*. With a rising tag, the matter is still open and the listener is really asked to decide. The difference in grammar between these two tags is nil; only the tone tells them apart. GB and GA use tags in the same way, though tags are generally more frequent in British English.
:::

::: example Lists and tags in a conversation {#ex-tags}
Mark the tones, then explain what the tags mean.

**Zhou Min:** *We need plates, cups, forks and napkins.*
**Tom:** *You've bought the drinks, haven't you?*
**Zhou Min:** *Yes, I got them this morning. It's going to be a great party, isn't it?*
::: solution
**Zhou Min:** *We ˈneed ↗**plates**, | ↗**cups**, | ↗**forks** | and ↘**nap**kins.* — a closed list: rises, then a final fall.
**Tom:** *You've ˈbought the ↘**drinks**, | ↗**have**n't you?* — a rising tag: Tom does not know, and asks a real question. (With a falling tag, *↘**have**n't you*, he would be reminding her of something he is sure about.)
**Zhou Min:** *↘**Yes**, | I ˈgot them this ↘**mor**ning. | It's ˈgoing to be a ˈgreat ↘**par**ty, | ↘**is**n't it?* — a falling tag: she is confident and invites Tom to agree.
:::
:::

::: theorem Finished and unfinished {#rule-unfinished}
When a sentence is divided into several tone units ([[connected-speech/sentence-stress]]), the **non-final** tone units usually have a **rise** or a **fall-rise**, and only the **final** one has a fall:

- *If you're ↘↗**free** | we could ˈgo to the ↘**cin**ema.*
- *When I ˈgot ↗**home**, | I ˈmade some ↘**tea**.*
- *In the ↘↗**mor**ning | I ˈwork in the ↘**lib**rary.*

A fall at the end of a tone unit tells the listener that the speaker has finished — and gives them the chance to speak. A rise or fall-rise says "I haven't finished yet".

In **GB**, the fall-rise is the usual tone for these non-final units. Many **GA** speakers use a simple rise where GB speakers use a fall-rise, and the fall-rise is generally less frequent in GA (Wells 2006; Cruttenden 2014). Both are understood everywhere.
:::

::: proof
Turn-taking in conversation depends heavily on this signal. Listeners begin to plan their reply before the speaker stops, and a fall at the end of a clause is one of the strongest signs that the turn is ending. Speakers who use a fall at the end of every clause are often interrupted, because their listeners hear each fall as an invitation to speak. Speakers who never fall can seem not to come to an end, so that the listener is not sure when to reply. The non-final fall-rise of GB has a further use: on a single word or phrase it often implies "but …", a reservation or a contrast that is not said. This is taken up in [[connected-speech/intonation-meaning]].
:::

::: example Splitting a long sentence {#ex-unfinished}
Divide each sentence into tone units with |, mark the nuclei, and give the tones (GB).

1. *When the meeting ended we went out for lunch.*
2. *Usually I take the bus but today I walked.*
3. *If it rains tomorrow we'll stay at home.*
::: solution
1. *When the ˈmeeting ↘↗**end**ed | we ˈwent ˈout for ↘**lunch**.*
2. *↘↗**Us**ually | I ˈtake the ↘**bus**, | but to↘**day** | I ↘**walked**.* — also possible: *↘↗**Us**ually I take the bus | but to↘**day** I walked*, with *today* and *walked* in one tone unit. Here *usually* contrasts with *today*, so the fall-rise is especially natural.
3. *If it ˈrains to↘↗**mor**row | we'll ˈstay at ↘**home**.*

In GA, each fall-rise could be a rise: *When the ˈmeeting ↗**end**ed | we ˈwent ˈout for ↘**lunch**.*
:::
:::

### Uptalk

In many varieties of English — in Australia, New Zealand, the US, and increasingly among younger speakers in the UK — a **high rise** is used at the end of statements: *I've just ˈstarted at the uni↗**ver**sity? | I'm ˈstudying ↗**law**?* This is often called **uptalk** (or the *high rising terminal*). It is not a question: speakers use it to check that the listener is following, or to keep the turn while telling a story. Uptalk has been described as a feature of young women's speech, but studies have found it in the speech of men and older speakers too, and the attitudes to it differ from place to place. For learners, the important point is to recognise it: a statement with a rise is not always a question.

::: note English and Chinese
- **Questions with 吗 and 吧.** In Mandarin, a yes/no question is usually marked by a particle — *Nǐ shì xuésheng ma?* 你是学生吗？ — and the particle carries much of the work that a rise does in English. Learners therefore often say English yes/no questions with a fall. As this chapter shows, that is not wrong; but questions in statement form (*You're a student?*) have no grammatical marker in English, and **must** rise.
- **Tags and 吧.** A falling tag in English (*It's cold, ↘isn't it?*) is close in function to 吧 or 是吧 in Mandarin (*Jīntiān hěn lěng ba?* 今天很冷吧？), which invite the listener to agree; a rising tag is closer to a real question with 吗.
- **Questions without particles.** Mandarin can also ask a question without a particle, by intonation alone: *Nǐ míngtiān zǒu?* 你明天走？ 'You're leaving tomorrow?' The whole utterance, and especially the end, is said on a raised pitch, while each syllable keeps the shape of its own tone. In English the rise is a single movement starting on the nucleus. This difference is the subject of [[connected-speech/tones-and-intonation]].
- **"Every question rises".** Many learners were taught this rule and apply it to wh-questions as well. A routine *ˈWhere are you ↗**from**?* with a high rise can sound like an echo or a question to a child; the normal tone is a fall.
:::

::: history
English intonation has been described for learners for a hundred years. Harold Palmer's *English Intonation* (1922) introduced the idea of the nucleus and of a small set of tones. Lilias Armstrong and Ida Ward's *Handbook of English Intonation* (1926) described two basic "tunes" — Tune 1, falling, and Tune 2, rising — that became the basis of English teaching for decades. J. D. O'Connor and G. F. Arnold's *Intonation of Colloquial English* (1961) set out ten "tone groups" with their typical meanings, and M. A. K. Halliday (1967) placed tone beside tonality and tonicity as one of three choices. In the United States a different tradition developed: Janet Pierrehumbert's 1980 doctoral thesis analysed intonation as a sequence of high and low targets (H and L), and this led to the **ToBI** ("Tones and Break Indices") system of 1992, which is widely used in research. John Wells's *English Intonation* (2006) combined the British tradition with careful comparison of GB and GA.
:::

## Where this leads

The meanings of the tones — friendliness, surprise, reservation, impatience — and the importance of pitch range are the subject of [[connected-speech/intonation-meaning]]. How the habits of a tone language affect English intonation is discussed in [[connected-speech/tones-and-intonation]]. Using intonation to recognise the end of a turn is practised in [[listening-and-conversation/turn-taking]].

::: summary
- **Intonation** is the use of pitch over a tone unit; its central part is the **nuclear tone**, which begins on the nucleus. English tones belong to tone units, not to words ([[#def-tone]]).
- The main tones are the **fall** ↘, the **rise** ↗, the **fall-rise** ↘↗ and the **rise-fall** ↗↘.
- Statements and wh-questions normally **fall**; yes/no questions often **rise** but often fall; questions in statement form **must rise**; alternative questions rise, then fall on the last option ([[#rule-questions]]).
- Closed lists: rises, then a final fall. Tags: a **falling** tag asks for agreement, a **rising** tag asks a real question ([[#rule-tags]]).
- Non-final tone units have a rise or (especially in GB) a fall-rise; a fall signals that the speaker has finished ([[#rule-unfinished]]).
- **Uptalk** — a high rise on statements — is not a question.
:::
