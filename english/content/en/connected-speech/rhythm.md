Say these three sentences, clapping once on each word in capitals:

- *DOGS EAT BONES.*
- *The DOGS will EAT the BONES.*
- *The DOGS have been EATing the BONES.*

The first has three syllables, the second six, the third eight. Yet many English speakers say the three sentences in times that are much closer to each other than the syllable count suggests: the extra syllables are squeezed into the spaces between the claps. Try it: keep the claps at the same steady speed and fit the small words in between. Some teaching books go further and say that in English the stressed syllables come at **equal** intervals, whatever happens between them — that English is "stress-timed".

This chapter looks at what is true in that idea and what is not. It explains the rhythm of English as an alternation of strong and weak syllables, shows how weak forms and reduced vowels create it, compares it with the rhythm of Mandarin, and gives a way to practise it. It builds directly on [[connected-speech/sentence-stress]] and [[connected-speech/weak-forms]].

## Strong and weak beats

::: definition Rhythm {#def-rhythm}
The **rhythm** of speech is the pattern made in time by its strong and weak syllables. In English the strong syllables are the stressed syllables of the sentence ([[connected-speech/sentence-stress#rule-content]]); the weak ones are unstressed syllables, many of them with weak vowels or in weak forms.
:::

A useful way to see English rhythm is to divide speech into **feet**, as the phonetician David Abercrombie (1967) did.

::: definition Foot {#def-foot}
A **foot** (in the sense used for speech rhythm) begins with a stressed syllable and includes all the unstressed syllables after it, up to the next stressed syllable. Feet are marked with | : *| ˈDogs will | ˈeat the | ˈbones |*. Unstressed syllables at the very start of a phrase, before the first stress, are left outside the first foot: *The | ˈdogs have been | ˈeating the | ˈbones |*.
:::

In the third sentence of the introduction, the feet are of very different sizes: | ˈdogs have been | has three syllables, | ˈeating the | has three, | ˈbones | has one. If English were strictly stress-timed, each foot would take the same time, and the syllables of the longer feet would be crushed to fit.

::: example Dividing into feet {#ex-feet}
Mark the stressed syllables and divide into feet: *I'd like to buy a ticket to Shanghai for tomorrow morning.*
::: solution
First find the content words: *like*, *buy*, *ticket*, *Shanghai*, *tomorrow*, *morning*. Mark their stressed syllables, then draw | before each stress.

*I'd | ˈlike to | ˈbuy a | ˈticket to Shang | ˈhai for to | ˈmorrow | ˈmorning |*

- *I'd* comes before the first stress and stays outside.
- Feet cut across words: | ˈticket to Shang | contains the end of one word, a whole function word and the first syllable of another. This is normal: feet are units of rhythm, not of grammar.
- The feet have 2, 2, 4, 3, 2 and 2 syllables. *Shanghai* is stressed on its second syllable here, as English speakers usually say it (/ʃæŋˈhaɪ/).

In fluent speech the stress on *morrow* may be weaker than the others, or the speaker may make *morning* the only strong syllable of *tomorrow morning*; foot division depends on how the sentence is actually said.
:::
:::

## Is English "stress-timed"?

::: definition Stress-timed and syllable-timed {#def-timing}
In 1945 Kenneth Pike described English as **stress-timed**: the stressed syllables were said to recur at roughly equal intervals, so that unstressed syllables are compressed when there are many of them. He contrasted it with **syllable-timed** languages such as Spanish, in which each syllable was said to take roughly the same time. Abercrombie (1967) claimed that every language belongs to one type or the other: English, Russian and Arabic stress-timed; French, Spanish and Yoruba syllable-timed.
:::

The idea is attractive, and it captures something real about how English sounds. But when phoneticians measured speech, the strict version did not hold.

::: theorem What the measurements show {#rule-timing}
1. **The intervals between stresses in English are not equal.** A foot with more syllables takes longer than a foot with fewer, in English as in other languages; there is some compression of unstressed syllables, but nowhere near enough to make the intervals equal (Roach 1982).
2. **"Syllable-timed" languages do not have equal syllables either.** Syllables in French or Spanish vary in length, too.
3. **What differs between languages is how much syllables differ from each other.** English has a large difference between strong and weak syllables: long stressed syllables with full vowels and complex consonant clusters, and short unstressed syllables with reduced vowels. Spanish has much smaller differences: simpler syllables and few reduced vowels (Dauer 1983).
4. **Languages lie on a scale, not in two boxes.** Measures of how much the length of vowels and consonants varies place English, Dutch and German at one end, Spanish, Italian and French towards the other, and many languages in between (Ramus, Nespor and Mehler 1999; Grabe and Low 2002). The results depend a lot on the speaker, the speed and the sentences chosen (Arvaniti 2012).
:::

::: proof
Roach (1982) measured the time between stressed syllables in recordings of languages that Abercrombie had called stress-timed and syllable-timed. The "stress-timed" languages did not show more regular intervals between stresses than the others, and the "syllable-timed" ones did not show more regular syllables. Dauer (1983) found the same and proposed the explanation now widely accepted: the impression of stress-timing comes from the **structure** of English, not from a clock. English has vowel reduction, weak forms and many consonant clusters, so its stressed syllables are much longer and more prominent than its unstressed ones, and the ear hears the stresses as a beat. In a language with simple syllables and no reduction, every syllable sounds about equally important, which gives the impression of "syllable-timing". Later work tried to measure this with "rhythm metrics", but these turned out to be sensitive to the materials used, so they describe tendencies rather than fixed types.
:::

So the classroom rule "English stresses come at equal intervals" is an exaggeration. A better statement for learners is this:

::: theorem English rhythm for learners {#rule-rhythm}
1. **Make the stressed syllables strong**: longer, clearer, with a full vowel and a pitch movement.
2. **Make the unstressed syllables weak**: short, with reduced vowels and weak forms.
3. **Let the unstressed syllables between two stresses share the time**: the more there are, the quicker each one is. They do not have to fit an exact beat.
4. **Keep the rhythm going across word boundaries**: do not stop after each word, and do not add vowels to consonant clusters, which would create extra syllables (*sport* /spɔːt/, not ✗ /ˈspɔːtə/).
:::

::: proof
Rules 1 and 2 are the real basis of English rhythm, as the measurements in [[#rule-timing]] show: it is the contrast between strong and weak syllables that listeners hear. Rule 3 describes the tendency to compress, which is real even though it does not produce equal intervals. Rule 4 matters because English listeners use the rhythm to find word boundaries ([[connected-speech/hearing-fast-speech]]): a vowel added after a final consonant creates a new weak syllable, which can change the rhythm and even the words heard (*sport* /spɔːt/ heard as *sporter*). Clapping or tapping on the stresses, as in the introduction, is a useful exercise because it makes the speaker shorten the weak syllables; it is not a description of a clock inside English.
:::

::: warning Every syllable the same length
✗ *I* · *want* · *to* · *go* · *to* · *the* · *li* · *bra* · *ry* — nine equal syllables, each with a full vowel.
✓ *I ˈwant to ˈgo to the ↘**li**brary* — /aɪ ˈwɒnt tə ˈɡəʊ tə ðə ˈlaɪbrəri/ (GB): three strong syllables, the others short and weak.

Equal syllables are a typical feature of learners whose first language has little vowel reduction. The words are all there, but the listener cannot hear which ones matter, and the speech sounds slow and "staccato". The fix is not to speak faster overall, but to make the weak syllables shorter.
:::

::: example Three sentences, three lengths {#ex-compression}
Compare the three sentences of the introduction. Mark the feet, count the syllables in each foot, and explain why the third sentence does not take three times as long as the first.
::: solution
1. *| ˈDogs | ˈeat | ˈbones |* — three feet of one syllable each (3 syllables).
2. *| ˈDogs will | ˈeat the | ˈbones |* — feet of 2, 2, 1 syllables (5 syllables, with *The* outside: 6).
3. *The | ˈdogs have been | ˈeating the | ˈbones |* — feet of 3, 3, 1 syllables (7, plus *The*: 8).

All three have three stresses and three feet. The extra syllables in sentences 2 and 3 are function words in weak forms (*will* /l/ or /wəl/, *the* /ðə/, *have been* /əv bɪn/) and the unstressed *-ing*, all of them short. So the third sentence is longer than the first, but much less than three times as long: the weak syllables are compressed. They are not compressed to zero, and a careful measurement would show three different lengths, as [[#rule-timing]] says.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which statement is best supported by research on English rhythm?
- [ ] English stressed syllables come at exactly equal intervals.
- [ ] English and Spanish have the same rhythm.
- [x] English has a bigger difference between strong and weak syllables than languages like Spanish.
- [ ] Rhythm has nothing to do with vowel reduction.
::: solution
Measurements (Roach 1982; Dauer 1983) found no equal intervals between English stresses, so the first option is wrong. What makes English sound different from Spanish is the strong contrast between long stressed syllables and short unstressed ones with reduced vowels — so vowel reduction is central to rhythm, and the last option is wrong too.
:::
:::

## Rhythm in verse

English verse uses the strong–weak alternation of everyday speech and makes it regular. The most common pattern in English poetry, **iambic pentameter**, has five weak–strong pairs in a line. Here is the first line of Shakespeare's Sonnet 18 (published 1609):

> *Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?*

::: example Scanning a line {#ex-verse}
Mark the metrical beats of the line above, then say how a natural reading might differ.
::: solution
The metre asks for five weak–strong pairs:

*shall* **I** | *com* **PARE** | *thee* **TO** | *a* **SUM** | *mer's* **DAY**

A natural reading does not stress all five beats equally. *To* is a function word and would normally be weak; in reading verse it is given a light beat or none, and the line still sounds regular because the surrounding pattern is so clear. *I* may be stressed or not, depending on the reader's interpretation. The beats on *-pare*, *sum-* and *day* fall on the stressed syllables of content words, exactly where sentence stress would put them, and the nucleus of the question is on *day*: *Shall I comˈpare thee to a ˈsummer's ↗**day**?* (or with a fall). Verse is built from the same strong–weak contrast as speech; the poet arranges the words so that the contrast becomes regular. Metre is studied in detail in [[reading-literature/metre-rhythm]].
:::
:::

## Rhythm and listeners around the world

English is spoken with many rhythms. In many varieties of English used in Asia and Africa — Singapore English, Indian English and Nigerian English among them — unstressed vowels are less reduced and syllables more even than in GB or GA. Measurements have confirmed this for Singapore English (Low, Grabe and Nolan 2000). These are systematic features of established varieties, not errors ([[world-englishes/southeast-asian-englishes]]).

What does this mean for a learner's goals? Jennifer Jenkins (2000), studying conversations between speakers of English as a lingua franca, found that weak forms and "stress-timed" rhythm were **not** necessary for being understood by other non-native speakers, while the placement of the nucleus ([[connected-speech/sentence-stress]]) and clear consonants were. A reasonable conclusion: you need to **recognise** reduced syllables and weak forms to understand GB and GA speakers; and using them in your own speech makes your stressed words stand out more clearly, which helps every listener. But even rhythm with fewer reductions is fully intelligible if the stresses and the nucleus are in the right places.

::: example Thought groups {#ex-chunk}
Divide this short text into tone units ("thought groups"), mark the stressed syllables and the nucleus of each, and describe the rhythm of the second tone unit.

*When we got to the hotel, the receptionist told us that our room wasn't ready yet.*
::: solution
*When we ˈgot to the ho↘**tel**, | the reˈceptionist ˈtold us that our ˈroom | ˈwasn't ˈready ↘**yet**.*

or, in two units: *When we ˈgot to the ho↘**tel**, | the reˈceptionist ˈtold us that our ˈroom wasn't ˈready ↘**yet**.*

- The boundary after *hotel* comes at the end of the subordinate clause.
- In the second unit the stresses fall on *-cep-*, *told*, *room*, *wasn't*, *rea-* and *yet*. Between *told* and *room* there are three weak syllables (*us that our*, /əs ðət aʊə/ in GB, often /əs ðət ɑː/), so they are said quickly; between *wasn't* and *rea-* there are none, so the two stresses come close together.
- The nucleus is on *yet*, the last content word. *Yet* is an adverb and can carry the nucleus; many speakers would instead put the fall on *rea-* and say *yet* in the tail.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Mandarin rhythm.** In Mandarin most syllables carry a full tone and a full vowel, and their lengths are much more even than those of English syllables. This is why Mandarin is often grouped with the "syllable-timed" languages, though, as for English, the label describes a tendency, and measurements of Mandarin rhythm do not all agree. The exception is the **neutral tone** (轻声): neutral-tone syllables are short and weak, and they give Mandarin words like *dōngxi* 东西 and *xièxie* 谢谢 a strong–weak shape that is close to English rhythm.
- **Mandarin feet.** Chinese linguists, among them 冯胜利, have argued that Mandarin also groups syllables into feet, usually of two syllables. But a Mandarin foot does not reduce its second syllable unless it has the neutral tone; an English foot almost always does.
- **What transfers.** Learners who carry over Mandarin rhythm tend to give every English syllable a full vowel and similar length, to separate words with small pauses, and sometimes to add a vowel after a final consonant, making extra syllables. All of these flatten the contrast between strong and weak syllables.
- **What helps.** Practise feet rather than words: *ˈwant to*, *ˈgo to the*, *ˈtell her*, *ˈwhat do you* — one strong syllable followed by quick weak ones, as in a neutral-tone word.
- **Cantonese speakers** may face a similar difficulty: Cantonese has no neutral tone like Mandarin's, and all of its syllables keep their tones, so the English weak syllable may feel even more unusual.
:::

::: history
Attempts to describe English rhythm are old. In 1775 Joshua Steele, in *An Essay Towards Establishing the Melody and Measure of Speech*, divided English speech into bars like music, each beginning with a strong "pulse". In 1945 Kenneth Pike introduced the terms "stress-timed" and "syllable-timed", and David Abercrombie (1967) made them into a general claim about all languages. In the 1960s and 1970s many experiments looked for equal intervals and did not find them; Peter Roach (1982) and Rebecca Dauer (1983) showed that the difference between languages lies in syllable structure and vowel reduction. From 1999 onwards, researchers such as Franck Ramus, Marina Nespor and Jacques Mehler, and Esther Grabe and Ee Ling Low, proposed measures of rhythm based on the variability of vowels and consonants; their usefulness is still debated.
:::

## Where this leads

The weak syllables between the stresses are joined to the words around them by **linking** ([[connected-speech/linking]]) and lose or change sounds by **elision** and **assimilation** ([[connected-speech/elision-assimilation]]). Using the strong beats to find words in fast speech is the subject of [[connected-speech/hearing-fast-speech]]. Rhythm in poetry is developed in [[reading-literature/metre-rhythm]], and the rhythm of other varieties of English in [[world-englishes/southeast-asian-englishes]] and [[world-englishes/lingua-franca]].

::: summary
- The **rhythm** of English is the pattern of strong (stressed) and weak (unstressed) syllables; it can be described in **feet**, each starting with a stress ([[#def-rhythm]], [[#def-foot]]).
- English was called **stress-timed** (Pike 1945; Abercrombie 1967), but measurements show that the intervals between stresses are not equal ([[#def-timing]], [[#rule-timing]]).
- What makes English rhythm distinctive is the **large contrast** between long, full stressed syllables and short, reduced unstressed ones, together with complex syllables (Dauer 1983).
- Languages lie on a scale of rhythm, not in two types; the results of rhythm measures depend on speakers and materials.
- For learners: make stresses strong, weak syllables short and reduced, let the weak syllables share the time between stresses, and do not add vowels ([[#rule-rhythm]]).
- Verse makes the strong–weak alternation regular (iambic pentameter).
- Many varieties of English have more even rhythm; nucleus placement matters more for intelligibility than weak forms, but recognising weak forms is essential for listening.
:::

How many syllables are there in the foot | ˈcinema with my | in the previous exercise?

::: exercise Scan a line {level=2}
Mark the beats in this line from Christopher Marlowe's play *Doctor Faustus* (written around 1590), which is in iambic pentameter. Does each beat fall on a syllable that would also be stressed in ordinary speech?

> *Was this the face that launched a thousand ships*
::: solution
*was* **THIS** | *the* **FACE** | *that* **LAUNCHED** | *a* **THOU** | *sand* **SHIPS**. Yes: *this* is a demonstrative, which can be stressed in speech ('this face, the one before me'); *face*, *launched* and *ships* are content words; *thou-* is the stressed syllable of *thousand*. The weak positions — *was*, *the*, *that*, *a*, *-sand* — are all syllables that ordinary speech would leave unstressed. The line follows the rhythm of natural speech very closely, which is one reason it is so easy to remember.
:::

::: exercise Diagnose the rhythm {level=3}
A learner reads: *I · want · to · be · come · a · tea · cher · of · mu · sic*, with every syllable equal, and *become* said with a vowel after each consonant. Describe three problems with the rhythm and give a model version with stresses, weak forms and feet marked.
::: hint
Think about stress, weak forms and the number of syllables.
:::
::: solution
1. **No contrast between strong and weak syllables.** Every syllable has the same length, so the stresses on *want*, *-come*, *tea-* and *mu-* do not stand out.
2. **Strong forms of function words.** *to* /tuː/, *a* /eɪ/, *of* /ɒv/ should be weak: /tə/, /ə/, /əv/.
3. **The stress of *become*.** It is be**COME** /bɪˈkʌm/ — the first syllable is weak, and the word should not be split with extra vowels.

Model (GB): *I | ˈwant to be | ˈcome a | ˈteacher of | ↘**mu**sic.* /aɪ ˈwɒnt tə bɪ ˈkʌm ə ˈtiːtʃər əv ˈmjuːzɪk/ — note the linking /r/ in *teacher of*. Four feet, each with one strong syllable and two or three short weak ones (except the last).
:::

::: exercise Shadow a rhythm {level=3}
Choose a short recording of natural English (a podcast or the dialogue of a film) and pick two sentences. Write them down, mark the stresses and feet, then say them along with the recording, tapping the table on each stress. Report what you noticed about the weak syllables between the taps.
::: solution

It may also note that the taps were not perfectly regular — which is what the research in [[#rule-timing]] would predict.
