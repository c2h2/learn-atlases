Read this sentence aloud: *Lin didn't buy the red bike.* Now read it again six times, each time making a different word the strongest: ***LIN** didn't buy the red bike* (someone else did), *Lin **DIDN'T** buy the red bike* (whatever you heard), *Lin didn't **BUY** the red bike* (she borrowed it), *Lin didn't buy **THE** red bike* (not the famous one), *Lin didn't buy the **RED** bike* (she bought the blue one), *Lin didn't buy the red **BIKE*** (she bought the red scooter). The words are the same each time, but the message changes.

In a sentence, as in a word, some syllables are strong and others weak. One syllable is the strongest of all: it carries the main movement of the voice and tells the listener what is most important. English uses this much more than Mandarin does. This chapter explains which words are normally stressed, where the main stress — the **nucleus** — normally falls, and how moving it changes the meaning. It builds on the word stress of [[connected-speech/word-stress-patterns]].

## How sentence stress is marked

In this course a sentence is written in italics with two kinds of mark:

- ˈ before each **stressed syllable**, as in a dictionary: *I ˈwant to ˈbuy a ˈnew ↘**bike**.*
- ↘ (a fall) or ↗ (a rise) before the **nucleus**, the strongest syllable, which is also in **bold**: *↘**bike***. The arrow shows the direction of the pitch movement on the nucleus. Falls and rises are the subject of [[connected-speech/intonation-basics]]; in this chapter most nuclei are falls.

Only the stressed syllable of a long word is marked: *She's ˈstudying ↘**med**icine.*

## Content words and function words

::: definition Content words and function words {#def-content}
**Content words** carry the main meaning of a sentence: nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs, numbers, and also demonstratives (*this*, *that*), question words (*what*, *where*) and negative words (*not*, *never*, *didn't*, *can't*). **Function words** mainly show grammar: articles (*a*, *the*), prepositions (*to*, *at*, *for*), personal pronouns (*I*, *him*, *them*), auxiliary and modal verbs (*is*, *have*, *can*, *will*), the verb *be*, conjunctions (*and*, *but*, *that*), existential *there* and *some* meaning 'an amount of'.
:::

::: theorem Stressed and unstressed words {#rule-content}
In a neutral sentence, said without special emphasis:

1. **Content words are stressed** — more exactly, the stressed syllable of each content word is a stressed syllable of the sentence.
2. **Function words are unstressed**, and many of them are said in a weak form ([[connected-speech/weak-forms]]).

Some content words are often not stressed in quick speech, especially very common "empty" nouns such as *thing*, *people* and *stuff*, and adverbs such as *just* and *really* in the middle of a sentence. Some function words are stressed in special positions: a negative auxiliary is stressed (*I ˈcan't ˈcome*); a positive auxiliary at the end of a sentence has its strong form (*Yes, I ↘**can**.*); and any word can be stressed for contrast (see below).
:::

::: proof
The pattern follows from what listeners need. Content words carry new and unpredictable meaning, so they must be heard clearly. Function words can usually be guessed from the grammar: after *I want … go*, the listener expects *to*. A speaker saves time and effort by reducing what can be guessed, and the listener's attention goes to the strong syllables. This is why a sentence of stressed words alone — *ˈwant ˈbuy ˈnew ↘**bike*** — can often be understood, while a list of the function words — *I … to … a* — cannot. Roach (2009) and Cruttenden (2014) give the same division, and both note that it describes a tendency: emphasis, contrast and the position in the sentence can change it.
:::

| content words (usually stressed) | function words (usually unstressed) |
|---|---|
| nouns: *bike*, *station*, *Lin* | articles: *a*, *an*, *the* |
| main verbs: *buy*, *study*, *go* | pronouns: *I*, *you*, *him*, *them*, *it* |
| adjectives: *red*, *happy* | prepositions: *to*, *for*, *at*, *of*, *from* |
| adverbs: *quickly*, *often*, *here* | auxiliaries and modals: *do*, *have*, *can*, *will* |
| numbers: *three*, *twenty* | *be*: *am*, *is*, *are*, *was* |
| *this*, *that*; *what*, *where* | conjunctions: *and*, *but*, *or*, *that* |
| *not*, *never*, *didn't*, *can't* | *there* (as in *there is*), *some* ('an amount of') |

::: example Find the stressed words {#ex-content}
Mark the stressed syllables in these sentences, said in a neutral way.

1. *I'm going to the station to meet my sister.*
2. *Can you tell me where the library is?*
3. *We didn't have time for lunch.*
::: solution
Find the content words, then mark the stressed syllable of each.

1. *I'm ˈgoing to the ˈstation to ˈmeet my ˈsister.* — *going* (main verb), *station* (noun), *meet* (main verb), *sister* (noun). *I'm*, *to*, *the*, *my* are function words.
2. *Can you ˈtell me ˈwhere the ˈlibrary is?* — *tell* (main verb), *where* (question word), *library* (noun). *Is* at the end keeps its strong form /ɪz/ but is not stressed: the verb *be* is a function word.
3. *We ˈdidn't ˈhave ˈtime for ˈlunch.* — *didn't* (negative), *have* (here a main verb, 'have time'), *time*, *lunch* (nouns).

Notice that only one syllable of *library* and *sister* is marked: the stressed syllable of the word becomes a stressed syllable of the sentence; the others stay weak.
:::

## The nucleus

Not all stressed syllables are equal. Speech is divided into short stretches, each with one main point of prominence.

::: definition Tone unit and nucleus {#def-nucleus}
A **tone unit** (also called an *intonation phrase*) is a stretch of speech with one complete intonation pattern; a short sentence is usually one tone unit, and a long one is divided into several, marked | when we need to show the boundaries. In each tone unit, one stressed syllable is the most prominent. It is the **nucleus** (or *tonic syllable*): the voice makes its main movement — usually a fall or a rise — on it or starting from it. Syllables after the nucleus form the **tail**: they continue the pitch movement without new stresses.
:::

::: theorem The nucleus falls on the last content word {#rule-last}
In a neutral sentence, where everything is new to the listener, the nucleus is on the stressed syllable of the **last content word** of the tone unit:

- *I ˈwant to ˈbuy a ˈnew ↘**bike**.*
- *ˈLin ˈlives in ↘**Shang**hai.*
- *We ˈmet at the ˈstation | at ↘**six**.*

Anything after the last content word — pronouns, prepositions, *please* — is in the tail: *ˈWhat are you ↘**look**ing at?* *ˈCan you ˈhelp ↗**me**?* is not the neutral form; the neutral form is *ˈCan you ↗**help** me?*
:::

::: proof
Wells (2006) calls this the default position of the nucleus, and treats every other position as a choice that says something. It works because the end of a phrase usually brings the newest information: English tends to put the topic first and the new part last. The nucleus on the last content word does not mean that only that word is new. In *I want to buy a new ↘**bike***, the whole sentence can be new — for instance, as the answer to *Any plans for the weekend?* This is called **broad focus**. The nucleus on *bike* is simply where English puts the main prominence when nothing is singled out.
:::

The last content word is not always the nucleus. Three cases are common.

::: theorem When the last content word is not the nucleus {#rule-deaccent}
1. **Given information is not accented.** A word that repeats or refers to something just said loses its stress, and the nucleus moves to the last **new** content word: *A: Did you buy a bike? — B: I ↘**bor**rowed a bike.* (*bike* is given).
2. **Empty words.** Very general words such as *thing*, *something*, *people*, *person*, *place* and *stuff* are usually not the nucleus: *I've ↘**bought** something.* — *There were ↘**hun**dreds of people.*
3. **Event sentences.** A short sentence that announces a sudden event often has its nucleus on the **subject**: *The ↘**ket**tle's boiling.* *My ↘**phone**'s ringing.* *Your ↘**bag**'s open.*
:::

::: proof
All three follow from the same idea: the nucleus marks what is most worth the listener's attention. A given word is already in the listener's mind, so stressing it again would wrongly suggest it is new or contrastive; in Wells's (2006) terms it is **de-accented**. Empty words add little meaning: the important part is the verb or the number before them. Event sentences are different: here the verb (*boiling*, *ringing*) is the expected thing for the subject to do, and the news is which thing is doing it. Wells notes that in event sentences the subject is accented and the predicate is often de-accented. Speakers vary, especially with rule 3, and both *The ↘**ket**tle's boiling* and *The ˈkettle's ↘**boil**ing* are heard.
:::

::: example Where is the nucleus? {#ex-nucleus}
Mark the nucleus in B's answers and explain your choice.

1. *A: What do you do? — B: I teach English at a university.*
2. *A: Do you like English food? — B: I love English food.*
3. *A: What's that noise? — B: The baby's crying.*
4. *A: Did you see anyone at the party? — B: I didn't know anyone.*
::: solution
1. *I ˈteach ˈEnglish at a ˌuni↘**ver**sity.* — broad focus: the whole answer is new, so the nucleus goes on the last content word ([[#rule-last]]).
2. *I ↘**love** English food.* — *English food* is given (it was in the question), so it is de-accented and the nucleus moves to the last new word, *love* ([[#rule-deaccent]], rule 1).
3. *The ↘**ba**by's crying.* — an event sentence: the subject carries the nucleus (rule 3).
4. *I ˈdidn't ↘**know** anyone.* — *anyone* is an empty word and also refers back to *anyone* in the question; the nucleus goes on *know*.
:::

## Focus and contrast

::: definition Broad and narrow focus {#def-focus}
**Focus** is the part of an utterance that the speaker presents as new or important. In **broad focus** the whole tone unit is new, and the nucleus takes its default position ([[#rule-last]]). In **narrow focus** one word or phrase is singled out — to answer a question, to correct, or to contrast — and the nucleus moves to it. Everything after it becomes the tail.
:::

::: theorem Moving the nucleus {#rule-focus}
To give narrow focus to a word, put the nucleus on its stressed syllable and de-accent what follows:

1. **Answers**: the nucleus goes on the word that answers the question. *A: Who bought the bike? — B: ↘**Lin** bought it.*
2. **Corrections**: *A: You bought a red bike? — B: No, a ↘**blue** one.*
3. **Contrasts**: *I asked for ↘**tea**, | not ↘**cof**fee.*
4. **Emphasis on truth** (polarity): the nucleus goes on the auxiliary, which takes its strong form: *A: You're not listening. — B: I ↘**am** listening!* If there is no auxiliary, *do* is added: *I ↘**did** call you.*
5. **Function words** can take the nucleus when they are the point of the contrast: *The letter is ↘**from** her, | not ↘**to** her.*
:::

::: proof
This is how English marks focus: mainly by the position of the nucleus, while the order of the words stays the same. Other languages make more use of word order or particles for the same job (see the note on Chinese below). Because the nucleus has a default position, any other position is noticed and interpreted: a listener who hears *Lin didn't buy the ↘**red** bike* will think of a contrast with another colour, even if the speaker did not intend one. Bolinger (1972) showed that the position of the accent cannot be predicted from grammar alone; it depends on what the speaker wants to highlight. That is why the rules here are about meaning, not about word classes.
:::

::: warning Do not stress a pronoun at the end
✗ *I ˈlove ↘**you**.* ✗ *ˈCan you ˈhelp ↘**me**?* ✗ *I ˈsaw ↘**him** yesterday.* (said with no contrast)
✓ *I ↘**love** you.* ✓ *ˈCan you ↗**help** me?* ✓ *I ˈsaw him ↘**yes**terday.*

A stressed pronoun means a contrast: *I love ↘**you*** suggests 'you, not someone else' — perhaps as a reply to *I love you*. Learners who stress every final word often put the nucleus on a pronoun by accident, and the listener hears a contrast that was not intended.
:::

::: example One sentence, five meanings {#ex-contrast}
Each reply below continues *Lin didn't buy the red bike*. Mark the nucleus of the first sentence so that the reply makes sense.

1. *… Her brother did.*
2. *… She borrowed it.*
3. *… She bought the blue one.*
4. *… She bought the red car.*
5. *… Whatever Tom says.*
::: solution
1. *↘**Lin** didn't buy the red bike.* — the contrast is the person.
2. *Lin didn't ↘**buy** the red bike.* — the action.
3. *Lin didn't buy the ↘**red** bike.* — the colour.
4. *Lin didn't buy the red ↘**bike**.* — the thing; this is also the position of broad focus, so without the second sentence the listener might not hear a contrast. In speech the contrast is often made clearer with a higher, sharper fall.
5. *Lin ↘**did**n't buy the red bike.* — the truth of the sentence (polarity), with the nucleus on the negative auxiliary.

In each case, the words after the nucleus are in the tail: low, quick and without new stresses.
:::
:::

::: example Questions and answers {#ex-question}
Write B's answer to each question, then mark its nucleus.

1. *A: Where did you go on holiday? (B went to Yunnan.)*
2. *A: Did you go to Yunnan by train? (B flew.)*
3. *A: Did you go to Yunnan with your parents? (B went with friends.)*
::: solution
1. *We ˈwent to ↘**Yun**nan.* — the nucleus is on the word that answers *where*. It is also the last content word.
2. *No, we ↘**flew**.* or *No, we ˈwent by ↘**air**.* — the new information is the means of travel.
3. *No, I ˈwent with ↘**friends**.* — the contrast is *friends* against *parents*.

A useful test: the word that answers the question takes the nucleus. If you put the nucleus elsewhere, the answer will sound as if it answers a different question.
:::

- [ ] *No, she ↘**is** a nurse.*
- [x] *No, she's a ↘**nurse**.*
- [ ] *No, she's ↘**a** nurse.*

The new information, in contrast with *doctor*, is *nurse*, so it takes the nucleus. *She* refers to Wang Fang, who is given, and *is* is not in contrast (B is not saying that she *is* something after all, but what she is).

## Dividing speech into tone units

A long sentence is usually divided into two or more tone units, each with its own nucleus. The boundaries come at grammatical breaks: after a long subject, between clauses, around additions such as *however*, before and after a name used to address someone.

- *When I ˈgot to the ↘**sta**tion | the ˈtrain had ↘**left**.*
- *My ˈsister | who ˈlives in ↘**Can**ada | is ˈcoming to ↘**vis**it.* (She has only one sister.)
- *ˈThanks, ↘**Tom**.* — but *ˈThanks for ↘**ev**erything, Tom* (*Tom* in the tail).

Wells (2006), following Halliday (1967), calls these three choices **tonality** (how speech is divided into tone units), **tonicity** (where the nucleus goes) and **tone** (which pitch movement it carries). This chapter has been mostly about tonicity. Tone is the subject of [[connected-speech/intonation-basics]] and [[connected-speech/intonation-meaning]].

::: note English and Chinese
- **How focus is shown.** Mandarin also makes a focused word more prominent: its pitch range is widened and it is lengthened, and in Beijing Mandarin the pitch of the words after it is compressed (Xu 1999). But Mandarin also has other means that English uses less: the cleft *shì … de* (*Shì Lín mǎi de* 是林买的 'It was Lin who bought it'), and word order. English learners often rely on these and put the nucleus in its default place, so that *I didn't buy the ↘**bike*** is heard as broad focus when a contrast was intended.
- **Not all Chinese varieties are the same.** Research has reported that Hong Kong Cantonese marks focus without compressing the pitch of the following words (Wu and Xu 2010), so Cantonese speakers may find the low, flat English tail less natural than Beijing Mandarin speakers do.
- **Tones keep their shape.** In Mandarin every syllable keeps its tone in the tail, and the lexical tones of the de-accented words must still be recognisable. In English the tail has no tones of its own: it simply continues the movement started on the nucleus — low after a fall, rising after a rise. Practise saying tails quickly and quietly, as if they were part of the nucleus word.
- **Final pronouns.** Mandarin does not use stress to mark contrast on pronouns in the same way, and a learner who stresses *me* or *you* at the end of every request may not notice that the listener hears a contrast.
:::

::: history
The idea that the place of the nucleus is a choice, separate from the choice of tone, was made explicit by M. A. K. Halliday in *Intonation and Grammar in British English* (1967), who named the three systems tonality, tonicity and tone, and linked the nucleus to "new information". In a famous paper, "Accent is predictable (if you're a mind-reader)" (1972), Dwight Bolinger argued that no rule of grammar can predict where the accent falls, because it depends on what the speaker means. John Wells's *English Intonation* (2006) made these ideas practical for learners, with the "last content word" default and lists of the cases where it does not apply.
:::

## Where this leads

The unstressed words of a sentence are said in weak forms: [[connected-speech/weak-forms]]. The alternation of stressed and unstressed syllables gives English its rhythm: [[connected-speech/rhythm]]. The pitch movement on the nucleus — fall, rise or fall-rise — and what it means are in [[connected-speech/intonation-basics]] and [[connected-speech/intonation-meaning]]. Using the stressed words to understand fast speech is in [[connected-speech/hearing-fast-speech]], and stress in presentations in [[presentations-and-discussion/delivery]].

::: summary
- Mark stressed syllables with ˈ and the nucleus with ↘ or ↗ and bold: *I ˈwant a ˈnew ↘**bike**.*
- **Content words** (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs, negatives, question words) are usually stressed; **function words** (articles, pronouns, prepositions, auxiliaries, *be*, conjunctions) usually are not ([[#def-content]], [[#rule-content]]).
- Each **tone unit** has one **nucleus**, the most prominent syllable, where the main pitch movement happens; what follows is the **tail** ([[#def-nucleus]]).
- By default the nucleus falls on the **last content word** ([[#rule-last]]).
- Given information, empty words like *thing* and *people*, and the predicate of event sentences are usually **de-accented** ([[#rule-deaccent]]).
- To focus on a word — answering, correcting, contrasting — move the nucleus to it; any word can take it ([[#def-focus]], [[#rule-focus]]).
- Do not stress final pronouns unless you mean a contrast.
:::
