A learner listening to a colleague hears /ʃiːz ɪn ðə ˈneks ˈruːm/. She knows the words *next* and *room*, but she learned *next* as /nekst/, and there was no /t/ in what she heard. Later the same colleague says something that sounds like *tem pounds* and *wudja like a coffee?* There are no words *tem* or *wudja*. What happened?

In connected speech, sounds at word boundaries are not only joined ([[connected-speech/linking]]); they can also **disappear** or **change**. The /t/ of *next* is dropped between two consonants; the /n/ of *ten* turns into /m/ before the /p/ of *pounds*; the /d/ of *would* and the /j/ of *you* merge into /dʒ/. These processes — **elision** and **assimilation** — are normal in the speech of all English speakers, in both GB and GA, and in formal as well as informal speech. You do not have to use all of them, but you need to recognise them, because without them fast speech is very hard to follow. This chapter also covers the informal spoken forms *gonna*, *wanna* and *gotta*, which have their own rules.

## Elision: sounds that disappear

::: definition Elision {#def-elision}
**Elision** is the loss of a sound that a word has when it is said on its own. In *next day* /ˈneks ˈdeɪ/, the /t/ of *next* is elided; in *camera* /ˈkæmrə/, the middle vowel is elided.
:::

The most common elision affects /t/ and /d/.

::: theorem Elision of /t/ and /d/ {#rule-td}
A /t/ or /d/ that comes **between two other consonants** is often elided:

- *next day* /ˈneks ˈdeɪ/, *last week* /ˈlɑːs ˈwiːk/ (GB), *first thing* /ˈfɜːs ˈθɪŋ/ (GB), *don't know* /ˈdəʊn ˈnəʊ/ (GB)
- *old man* /ˈəʊl ˈmæn/ (GB), *hand bag* → *handbag* /ˈhænbæɡ/, *stand still*
- inside words: *exactly* /ɪɡˈzækli/, *mostly* /ˈməʊsli/ (GB), *facts* /fæks/, *friendship* /ˈfrenʃɪp/
- past-tense endings: *looked back* /ˈlʊk ˈbæk/, *mixed bag* /ˈmɪks ˈbæɡ/

It does **not** usually happen when a vowel follows: *next of kin* /ˈnekst əv ˈkɪn/, *last orders*, *old age*. And it is least likely when the /t/ or /d/ is followed by /h/ or /j/, or when the speaker is being careful or emphatic.
:::

::: proof
A /t/ or /d/ squeezed between two consonants is short and hard to hear even when it is said, because a stop needs a following vowel to make its release audible. Speakers therefore save the movement. Roach (2009) and Cruttenden (2014) both describe it as one of the most frequent elisions in English. When the elided /t/ or /d/ is a grammatical ending (*looked*, *mixed*), the meaning is usually clear from context: *Yesterday I looked back* cannot be present tense. Before a vowel, the stop can be released into the vowel and is easy to hear, so it is kept. This is one reason why linking ([[connected-speech/linking#rule-cv]]) and elision work together: *next of kin* links, *next day* elides.
:::

::: quiz
In which phrase is the /t/ most likely to be elided in fluent speech?
- [ ] *last of all*
- [x] *last night*
- [ ] *at last*
- [ ] *the last answer*
::: solution
In *last night* the /t/ comes between /s/ and /n/, two consonants, so it is very often dropped: [ˈlɑːs ˈnaɪt] (GB), [ˈlæs ˈnaɪt] (GA). In *last of all* and *the last answer* a vowel follows and the /t/ links to it; in *at last* it is at the end, before a pause, where it is kept (or glottalised).
:::
:::

Other elisions are common in fluent speech:

::: theorem Other common elisions {#rule-elision}
1. **Weak vowels in unstressed syllables** disappear, especially between a consonant and /r/, /l/ or /n/, or after /p t k/ at the start of a word: *camera* /ˈkæmrə/, *family* /ˈfæmli/, *chocolate* /ˈtʃɒklət/ (GB), *different* /ˈdɪfrənt/, *interesting* /ˈɪntrəstɪŋ/, *vegetable* /ˈvedʒtəbl/; *police* [pliːs], *correct* [krekt], *support* [spɔːt] (GB), *potato* [pʰˈteɪtəʊ] (GB), *today* [tʰˈdeɪ]. Many of these forms are now the usual dictionary pronunciations.
2. **The /v/ of *of*** is often lost before a consonant: *lots of them* /ˈlɒts ə ðəm/ (GB), *a cup of coffee* /ə ˈkʌp ə ˈkɒfi/ (GB).
3. **/h/ in weak forms** of *he*, *him*, *her*, *have* is lost in the middle of a sentence ([[connected-speech/weak-forms]]).
4. **/θ/ and /ð/ in clusters**: *months* /mʌnθs/ is often [mʌns] or [mʌnts]; *clothes* /kləʊðz/ (GB) is very often /kləʊz/, the same as the verb *close*.
:::

::: example Which sounds can be dropped? {#ex-elide}
In each phrase, say whether the /t/ or /d/ in bold is likely to be elided in fluent speech, and why.

1. *nex**t** week*
2. *nex**t** August*
3. *I wan**t** to go* (the /t/ of *want*)
4. *a col**d** day*
5. *a col**d** evening*
6. *She pass**ed** the test.* (the /t/ of *passed*)
::: solution
1. **Likely**: /kst/ + /w/ — the /t/ is between /s/ and /w/: /ˈneks ˈwiːk/.
2. **Unlikely**: a vowel follows, so the /t/ links to it: /ˈnekst ˈɔːɡəst/ (GB) [ˈneks ˈtɔːɡəst].
3. **Often merged rather than lost**: /nt/ + /t/ — the two /t/s become one: /ˈwɒn tə/ (GB). In GA this is often [ˈwɑːnə] — see *wanna* below.
4. **Likely**: /ld/ + /d/ — the /d/ of *cold* merges with the /d/ of *day*, or disappears: [ˈkəʊl ˈdeɪ] (GB).
5. **Unlikely**: a vowel follows: [ˈkəʊl ˈdiːvnɪŋ] (GB) — the /d/ links to *evening*.
6. **Likely in fast speech**: /pɑːst ðə/ → /ˈpɑːs ðə/ (GB). The past meaning is still clear from the context of a test.
:::
:::

## Assimilation: sounds that change

::: definition Assimilation {#def-assimilation}
**Assimilation** is a change in a sound that makes it more like a neighbouring sound. In *ten pounds*, the /n/ of *ten* becomes [m], made with the lips like the following /p/: [ˈtem ˈpaʊndz]. In English the sound that changes is usually the **last** sound of a word, and the sound that causes the change is the **first** sound of the next word.
:::

::: theorem Place assimilation of /t/, /d/ and /n/ {#rule-place}
The alveolar consonants /t/, /d/ and /n/ at the end of a word often take the place of articulation of a following consonant:

| before | /t/ becomes | /d/ becomes | /n/ becomes | examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| /p/, /b/, /m/ (lips) | [p] | [b] | [m] | *that man* [ˈðæp ˈmæn], *good boy* [ˈɡʊb ˈbɔɪ], *ten pounds* [ˈtem ˈpaʊndz], *in bed* [ɪm ˈbed] |
| /k/, /ɡ/ (soft palate) | [k] | [ɡ] | [ŋ] | *that case* [ˈðæk ˈkeɪs], *bad cold* [ˈbæɡ ˈkəʊld], *ten cups* [ˈteŋ ˈkʌps], *in case* [ɪŋ ˈkeɪs] |

/s/ and /z/ become [ʃ] and [ʒ] before /ʃ/ and /j/: *this shop* [ðɪʃ ˈʃɒp] (GB), *those shoes* [ðəʊʒ ˈʃuːz] (GB), *this year* [ðɪʃ ˈjɪə] (GB).

Consonants made with the lips or the back of the tongue (/p b m k ɡ ŋ/) do not change in this way: *bad cold* changes *bad*, but *big day* does not change *big*.
:::

::: proof
Assimilation comes from the overlapping of movements. To say *ten pounds*, the tongue must touch the alveolar ridge for /n/ and then the lips must close for /p/. In fluent speech the lips begin to close while the /n/ is still being made, and often the tongue movement is reduced or left out, so the nasal is heard as [m]. The alveolar consonants are the ones that change because the tongue tip is quick and its movement is easily hidden by the next sound; a lip or back-of-tongue closure is not hidden in the same way. In GB, a final /t/ before a consonant is also very often replaced by a glottal stop ([ˈðæʔ ˈmæn]), which competes with assimilation. Assimilation is optional and depends on speed and style, but it is not "careless": Cruttenden (2014) treats it as a normal feature of GB.
:::

Listeners are very good at undoing assimilation. In experiments by Gaskell and Marslen-Wilson (1996), English listeners recognised *lean* when they heard [liːm] before a /b/ (*leam bacon*), but much less when the same [m] came before a sound that could not have caused it. In other words, listeners use the next sound to work out what the speaker meant. Learners can learn to do the same: [tem] before /p/ is *ten*.

::: example Predicting assimilation {#ex-assim}
Show how the sounds at the boundary may change in fluent speech, and say which rule applies.

1. *green park* 2. *red car* 3. *that boy* 4. *one more* 5. *in Glasgow* 6. *nice shoes*
::: solution
1. /n/ + /p/ → [m]: [ˈɡriːm ˈpɑːk] (GB) — place assimilation ([[#rule-place]]).
2. /d/ + /k/ → [ɡ]: [ˈreɡ ˈkɑː] (GB).
3. /t/ + /b/ → [p]: [ˈðæp ˈbɔɪ], or in GB often [ˈðæʔ ˈbɔɪ] with a glottal stop.
4. /n/ + /m/ → [m]: [ˈwʌm ˈmɔː] (GB) — one long [mː].
5. /n/ + /ɡ/ → [ŋ]: [ɪŋ ˈɡlɑːzɡəʊ] (GB).
6. /s/ + /ʃ/ → [ʃ]: [ˈnaɪʃ ˈʃuːz] — one long [ʃː].

In each case the listener still hears *green*, *red*, *that*, *one*, *in*, *nice*: the change is predictable from the next sound.
:::
:::

::: theorem Coalescence with /j/ {#rule-coalescence}
When a word ending in /t/, /d/, /s/ or /z/ is followed by a word beginning with /j/ — above all *you*, *your* and *year* — the two sounds often **merge** into one:

- /t/ + /j/ → [tʃ]: *don't you* [ˈdəʊntʃu] (GB), *last year* [lɑːs ˈtʃɪə] (GB), *what you need* [ˈwɒtʃə ˈniːd] (GB)
- /d/ + /j/ → [dʒ]: *would you* [ˈwʊdʒu], *did you* [ˈdɪdʒu], *could you* [ˈkʊdʒu]
- /s/ + /j/ → [ʃ]: *I miss you* [aɪ ˈmɪʃ ju], *bless you* [ˈbleʃ ju]
- /z/ + /j/ → [ʒ]: *as you know* [əʒ ju ˈnəʊ] (GB), *is your* [ɪʒ jə]

This merging is called **coalescence** (or *yod coalescence*, from *yod*, a name for /j/).
:::

::: proof
Coalescence is the same kind of overlap: /j/ is made with the tongue near the hard palate, just behind the alveolar ridge, and when it overlaps with /t/ or /s/ the result is a sound made between the two — /tʃ/ or /ʃ/. The same process has happened inside many words in the history of English: *nature* /ˈneɪtʃə/ (GB) and *question* /ˈkwestʃən/ once had /tj/, and *sure* and *sugar* once had /sj/. It is still happening in GB in words like *tune* /tjuːn/, often said [tʃuːn], and *duty* /ˈdjuːti/, often [ˈdʒuːti]. GA has dropped the /j/ in these words altogether (*tune* /tuːn/, *duty* /ˈduːti/), so there is nothing to coalesce. Across word boundaries, coalescence with *you* is common in both GB and GA.
:::

::: example *Would you* and *don't you* {#ex-coalesce}
Write these questions as they are often said in fluent speech (GB and GA), and mark the stress.

1. *Would you like a coffee?* 2. *Did you see that?* 3. *Don't you think so?* 4. *What did you do?*
::: solution
1. GB [ˈwʊdʒə ˈlaɪk ə ˈkɒfi], GA [ˈwʊdʒə ˈlaɪk ə ˈkɑːfi] — *would* + *you* coalesce, and *you* is weak /jə/.
2. [ˈdɪdʒə ˈsiː ðæt] — /d/ + /j/ → [dʒ]. (*That* is a demonstrative pronoun here; it may also be the nucleus: *Did you see ↘**that**?*)
3. GB [ˈdəʊntʃə ˈθɪŋk səʊ], GA [ˈdoʊntʃə ˈθɪŋk soʊ] — /t/ + /j/ → [tʃ].
4. GB [ˈwɒdʒə ˈduː], GA [ˈwʌdʒə ˈduː] — *did* is reduced and coalesces with *you*; the /t/ of *what* is lost. In very fast GA speech this is often heard as *whaddya do?* [ˈwʌɾəjə ˈduː] or *whadja do?*

Notice that in all four the main verb keeps its full form and stress; only the function words change.
:::
:::

### Voicing

A third, smaller kind of assimilation affects voicing. In a few very common phrases, a voiced consonant becomes voiceless before a voiceless one: *have to* /ˈhæf tə/, *has to* /ˈhæs tə/, *used to* /ˈjuːs tə/, *supposed to* /səˈpəʊs tə/ (GB). These are now the normal pronunciations of these phrases, and *have to* /ˈhæf tə/ keeps it apart from *have two* /ˈhæv ˈtuː/.

### GB and GA

- **Glottal stops (GB and GA).** Before a consonant, a final /t/ is very often a glottal stop in both accents: *that man* [ˈðæʔ ˈmæn], *not now*, *football* ([[sounds-of-english/stops-fricatives]]). Between vowels, a glottal /t/ (*water* [ˈwɔːʔə]) is heard in many British accents and increasingly among younger speakers, but it is not yet considered typical of GB.
- **GA /nt/.** In GA, /t/ after /n/ and before an unstressed vowel is often dropped: *twenty* [ˈtweni], *winter* [ˈwɪnər], *internet* [ˈɪnərnet], *wanted* [ˈwɑːnɪd]. *Winter* and *winner* can sound the same.
- **GA tap.** A /t/ or /d/ between vowels is a tap in GA, inside words and across words: *a lot of* [ə ˈlɑːɾəv], *get it* [ˈɡeɾɪt] ([[connected-speech/linking#rule-cv]]).

## *Gonna*, *wanna* and other informal forms

Some combinations are reduced so often that they have their own informal spellings. They are not slang words: they are spellings of ordinary fast pronunciations.

| full form | informal form | pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| *going to* (future) | *gonna* | GB [ˈɡɒnə], GA [ˈɡɑːnə] or [ˈɡʌnə]; after *I'm*, often [ˈaɪmənə] |
| *want to* | *wanna* | GB [ˈwɒnə], GA [ˈwɑːnə] |
| *(have) got to* | *gotta* | GB [ˈɡɒtə], GA [ˈɡɑːɾə] |
| *have to*, *has to* | *hafta*, *hasta* | [ˈhæftə], [ˈhæstə] |
| *kind of*, *sort of* | *kinda*, *sorta* | [ˈkaɪndə], GB [ˈsɔːtə], GA [ˈsɔːrɾə] |
| *let me*, *give me* | *lemme*, *gimme* | [ˈlemi], [ˈɡɪmi] |
| *don't know* | *dunno* | GB [dəˈnəʊ], GA [dəˈnoʊ] |

::: theorem Where *gonna* and *wanna* are possible {#rule-gonna}
1. ***Gonna*** replaces *going to* only when it means the future and is followed by a verb: *I'm gonna call her.* It cannot replace *going to* + a place: ✗ *I'm gonna the station.* ✓ *I'm going to the station.*
2. ***Wanna*** replaces *want to* + verb: *I wanna go home.* It does not replace *wants to* (✗ *She wanna go*) or *want* + a noun (✗ *I wanna coffee* for *I want a coffee*).
3. *Wanna* is also impossible when the person who does the second action is not the subject of *want*. *Who do you want to visit?* can mean 'Which person do you want to visit?' — then *Who do you wanna visit?* is fine. But *Who do you want to win?* means 'Which person do you want to be the winner?' — here the winner is someone else, and ✗ *Who do you wanna win?* is not possible with that meaning.
4. **Register**: these forms belong to informal speech. In writing they appear in informal messages, in dialogue in fiction and in song titles, never in essays, reports or exams. Most speakers who say [ˈɡɒnə] write *going to*.
:::

::: proof
Rules 1 and 2 follow from the meaning: *gonna* is a reduction of the future construction *be going to* + verb, which is so frequent that it has become a single unit; *going to* + a place is the ordinary verb *go* + preposition, and is not reduced in the same way. Rule 3 has interested linguists since the 1970s (Chomsky and Lasnik 1977): it seems that *want* and *to* can merge only when nothing — not even an understood person — stands between them. In *Who do you want to win?* ('you want X to win'), the understood X is between *want* and *to*, and this blocks the merging. Rule 4 is a matter of convention: the informal spellings look careless in formal writing, although the pronunciations they represent are used by speakers of every background.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence could be said with *wanna*?
- [ ] *She wants to stay.*
- [ ] *I want a sandwich.*
- [x] *We want to stay.*
- [ ] *Who do you want to win the match?* (meaning 'Which team do you hope will win?')
::: solution
*We want to stay* has *want to* + verb, with the subject of *want* also doing the staying: *We wanna stay.* *Wants to* cannot become *wanna*; *want a* + noun is not *want to*; and in the last sentence the winner is someone other than *you*, which blocks *wanna* ([[#rule-gonna]], rule 3).
:::
:::

::: warning *Gonna* is not a word for writing
✗ *In this essay I'm gonna discuss the causes of climate change.*
✓ *In this essay I will discuss the causes of climate change.* / *This essay discusses …*

✗ *I'm gonna the library.* ✓ *I'm going to the library.*

Use the informal forms, if you want to, in casual speech and messages to friends. You do not need to say them at all to be understood; you need to **recognise** them, because they are everywhere in films, conversations and podcasts.
:::

::: example Can it be *gonna* or *wanna*? {#ex-gonna}
For each sentence, say whether the words in bold could be said and written informally as *gonna* or *wanna*.

1. *It's **going to** rain.*
2. *Are you **going to** the party?*
3. *I don't **want to** talk about it.*
4. *He **wants to** leave early.*
5. *Do you **want to** see it?*
6. *Who do you **want to** help you?*
::: solution
1. **Yes**: future + verb — *It's gonna rain.*
2. **No**: *going to* + a place (*the party*).
3. **Yes**: *I don't wanna talk about it.*
4. **No**: *wants to* — the *-s* prevents it. (In very fast speech the words may still be reduced, [ˈwɒnts tə], but not to *wanna*.)
5. **Yes**: *Do you wanna see it?* — often [dʒə ˈwɒnə ˈsiː ɪt] (GB).
6. **No**: the person who helps is someone else, between *want* and *to* in meaning ('you want X to help you'), as in [[#rule-gonna]], rule 3.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Changes Mandarin speakers know.** Mandarin also changes sounds at syllable boundaries in fast speech: in *miànbāo* 面包, the *-n* is often pronounced closer to [m] before *b*, and in casual Beijing speech *zěnme* 怎么 or *bù zhīdào* 不知道 are much reduced. Tone sandhi (the change of tone 3 before another tone 3, as in *nǐ hǎo* 你好) is another kind of change caused by a neighbour, though it concerns tone, not consonants. The idea that a sound changes because of the next one is therefore not new.
- **What is new.** English assimilation and elision affect **consonant clusters**, which Mandarin does not have. A learner who has worked hard to pronounce the /t/ of *next* may feel that dropping it is wrong. For your own speech, keeping it is fine; for listening, you must expect it to be missing.
- **Past tense.** Because Chinese does not mark tense on the verb, learners often fail to hear (and to say) *-ed*. Elision makes this harder: in *looked back* the /t/ is usually gone. Listen for other signs of the past (*yesterday*, *last week*) and do not worry if you cannot hear the ending.
- **Spelling forms in online English.** Learners meet *gonna*, *wanna* and *gotta* in songs, films and social media. They are useful to recognise; but in exam writing and formal English they should be written in full.
:::

::: history
Early phoneticians knew that sounds change in context — Henry Sweet already discussed assimilation in the nineteenth century — but systematic descriptions for learners came in the twentieth century. A. C. Gimson's *An Introduction to the Pronunciation of English* (1962) gave a detailed account of assimilation, elision and linking in RP, which, revised by Alan Cruttenden, remains a standard source (*Gimson's Pronunciation of English*, 8th edition 2014). The informal spellings *gonna* and *wanna* became common in print in the twentieth century; the restrictions on *wanna* became a famous problem in syntax in the 1970s, discussed among others by Noam Chomsky and Howard Lasnik (1977). In the 1990s psycholinguists such as Gareth Gaskell and William Marslen-Wilson showed experimentally how listeners use the following sound to undo assimilation.
:::

## Where this leads

Recognising elided and assimilated words in real speech is practised in [[connected-speech/hearing-fast-speech]]. The pitch of connected speech is the subject of the next three chapters, beginning with [[connected-speech/intonation-basics]]. Accents with different patterns of glottalisation, tapping and reduction are discussed in [[listening-and-conversation/accents]] and [[world-englishes/british-american]].

::: summary
- **Elision** is the loss of a sound in connected speech; **assimilation** is a change that makes a sound more like its neighbour ([[#def-elision]], [[#def-assimilation]]).
- /t/ and /d/ between two consonants are often elided: *next day*, *last week*, *looked back*; not before a vowel ([[#rule-td]]).
- Weak vowels are often lost (*camera*, *family*, *police*), and so are the /v/ of *of* before consonants and /h/ in weak forms ([[#rule-elision]]).
- Final /t d n/ take the place of a following consonant: *ten pounds* [tem], *ten cups* [teŋ], *good boy* [ɡʊb]; /s z/ become [ʃ ʒ] before /ʃ j/ ([[#rule-place]]).
- /t d s z/ + /j/ merge into [tʃ dʒ ʃ ʒ]: *would you* [ˈwʊdʒu], *last year* ([[#rule-coalescence]]).
- GA drops /t/ after /n/ (*twenty* [ˈtweni]) and taps /t/ between vowels; both accents glottalise /t/ before consonants.
- *Gonna* = future *going to* + verb; *wanna* = *want to* + verb, not *wants to*, and not when someone else does the action; both are informal speech ([[#rule-gonna]]).
:::

1. *Just a minute.* 2. *Just now.* 3. *I don't like it.* 4. *a blind man* 5. *the old office*
::: solution
1. Not likely: *just‿a* — a vowel follows, so the /t/ links. 2. Likely: [ˈdʒʌs ˈnaʊ]. 3. Likely: [aɪ ˈdəʊn ˈlaɪk ɪt] (GB) — the /t/ of *don't* is between /n/ and /l/. 4. Likely: [ə ˈblaɪn ˈmæn], and the /n/ may assimilate to [m]: [ə ˈblaɪm ˈmæn]. 5. Not likely: a vowel follows: *old‿office*.
:::

::: exercise Gonna or not? {level=1}
Which sentences can use *gonna*?

1. *We're going to be late.* 2. *We're going to Paris.* 3. *What are you going to do?* 4. *I was going to call you.*
::: solution
1, 3 and 4: future (or, in 4, past future) + verb: *We're gonna be late*, *What are you gonna do?*, *I was gonna call you*. Not 2: *going to* + a place.
:::

::: exercise Count the changes {level=2 check="3"}
*Did you find the green bag last week?* is said fluently in GB. How many of these changes are likely: (a) coalescence in *did you*; (b) assimilation of /n/ in *green bag*; (c) elision of /t/ in *last week*?
::: solution
All three: (a) [ˈdɪdʒə] — /d/ + /j/ merge ([[#rule-coalescence]]); (b) [ˈɡriːm ˈbæɡ] — /n/ before /b/ ([[#rule-place]]); (c) [ˈlɑːs ˈwiːk] — /t/ between /s/ and /w/ ([[#rule-td]]). The answer is 3.
:::
:::

::: exercise GB and GA {level=2}
Give the GB and GA pronunciations of *tune*, *duty* and *twenty* in fluent speech, and explain the differences.
::: solution
*tune*: GB /tjuːn/, often [tʃuːn] (coalescence inside the word); GA /tuːn/ (no /j/). *duty*: GB /ˈdjuːti/, often [ˈdʒuːti]; GA /ˈduːti/, with a tap [ˈduːɾi]. *twenty*: GB /ˈtwenti/; GA /ˈtwenti/, very often [ˈtweni] with the /t/ after /n/ dropped. GA has lost /j/ after /t d n/ in these words (yod-dropping), so it has nothing to coalesce; GB keeps /j/ and often merges it with the consonant.
:::

::: exercise Dictation from fast speech {level=3}
A friend leaves you this voice message, transcribed here as it was said (GB). Write it in ordinary spelling, then list every elision, assimilation and coalescence you can find.

[hiː | ɪts ˈsæm | ˈwʊdʒə ˈlaɪk tə ˈkʌm tə ðə ˈsɪnəmə ˈneks ˈwiːk | ðeə ˈʃəʊɪŋ ðə ˈnjuː ˈfɪlm | ɪŋ ˈkeɪs jər ˈɪntrəstɪd | ˈlemi ˈnəʊ | ˈtʃɪəz]
::: hint
The message has seven short parts, separated by |. Look for [dʒ], [ŋ] before /k/, and a missing /t/.
:::
::: solution
*Hi. It's Sam. Would you like to come to the cinema next week? They're showing the new film, in case you're interested. Let me know. Cheers.*

Changes:
- *would you* [ˈwʊdʒə]: coalescence of /d/ + /j/, with weak *you* ([[#rule-coalescence]]).
- *next week* [ˈneks ˈwiːk]: elision of /t/ between consonants ([[#rule-td]]).
- *in case* [ɪŋ ˈkeɪs]: /n/ assimilated to [ŋ] before /k/ ([[#rule-place]]).
- *interested* [ˈɪntrəstɪd]: a weak vowel elided, three syllables instead of four ([[#rule-elision]]).
- *let me* [ˈlemi]: the informal *lemme*, with the /t/ lost.
- Weak forms: *to* [tə] twice, *they're* [ðeə], *you're* [jər] (with linking /r/ before *interested*).

Read word by word, the transcription looks full of unknown words; once you expect these changes, the message is easy to understand.
:::
:::

::: exercise Your own fast speech {level=3}
Write five short sentences you might say to a friend (for example about plans for the weekend). Mark where elision, assimilation or coalescence is likely, then say each sentence twice: once carefully, once fluently. Which changes feel natural to you, and which would you rather avoid in your own speech? Explain in two or three sentences.
::: solution
Answers vary. A model: *Did you get my message?* (coalescence [ˈdɪdʒə]); *I'm going to the beach next Saturday* (elision of /t/ in *next*); *Do you want to come?* ([dʒə ˈwɒnə ˈkʌm]); *We can meet in Brighton* (/n/ → [m] before /b/); *Let me know by Friday* ([ˈlemi]). A good reflection might say: "Coalescence and the dropped /t/ feel natural; I would avoid *wanna* and *lemme* with people I don't know well, but I will listen for them." Any choice is acceptable, as long as the learner recognises all of the forms.
:::
