A learner who has studied English for years watches a film and hears a character say something like [aɪ ˈdʌnəʊ wɒts ˈɡɒnə ˈhæpm]. She does not recognise a single word. Then the subtitles appear: *I don't know what's going to happen.* She knows every word — she could have written that sentence in her first year of English. The problem was not vocabulary or grammar. It was that the words she knows, in the form she knows them, were not in what she heard.

This is the central problem of listening to natural English. The earlier chapters of this course described what happens to words in connected speech: unstressed syllables are reduced ([[connected-speech/weak-forms]]), words are joined ([[connected-speech/linking]]), sounds are dropped and changed ([[connected-speech/elision-assimilation]]), and pitch organises the stream into tone units with a nucleus ([[connected-speech/intonation-basics]]). This chapter turns that knowledge around: it shows how a listener can **use** these patterns to decode fast speech, and how to practise doing so.

## The segmentation problem

::: definition Segmentation {#def-segmentation}
In writing, words are separated by spaces. In speech there are no spaces: the sounds of one word run into the next without pauses, as [[connected-speech/linking]] showed. **Segmentation** is the listener's task of finding where one word ends and the next begins. Native listeners do it automatically and without effort; for learners it is one of the main reasons that speech they "know" sounds like a continuous blur.
:::

Pauses in speech come between tone units, not between words, and even there they are often absent. A sentence such as *Did you see where I put my keys?* is usually one unbroken stream: [ˈdɪdʒə ˈsiː weər aɪ ˈpʊt maɪ ˈkiːz]. The listener has to find six or more words in a string of sounds that contains no clear boundaries at all.

::: theorem Strong syllables mark word beginnings {#rule-strong}
In English, most **content words** begin with a **strong syllable** — a syllable with a full vowel (not /ə/ or a weak /ɪ/ or /ʊ/). Cutler and Carter (1987), counting words in a large corpus of spoken English, found that about nine out of ten content words begin in this way.

A useful listening strategy follows: **treat each strong syllable as the probable start of a content word**, and treat weak syllables as probable function words or as the unstressed parts of longer words. In [ðə ˈtiːtʃər ɪkˈspleɪnd ɪt ə ˈɡen], the strong syllables *tea-*, *-plained* and *-gain* suggest three content words. *Tea-* begins *teacher*. *-plained* does not begin a word — it ends *explained* — but listeners quickly recover from such a guess, and the weak syllables around the strong ones are mostly function words: *the*, *it*, *a-*.
:::

::: proof
Cutler and Norris (1988) called this the **metrical segmentation strategy**, and showed in experiments that English listeners do use it: they find it easier to spot a word that begins at a strong syllable than one that straddles a strong syllable. The strategy works because of the way English words are built (most common content words are stressed on the first syllable, or are monosyllables) and because function words are usually weak ([[connected-speech/weak-forms]]). It is a probability, not a rule: *exPLAIN*, *aBOUT* and *toDAY* are exceptions, and listeners use other knowledge to correct the guess. But it gives the listener a starting point: the strong syllables are the places to look.
:::

::: quiz
In fluent speech, the phrase *a cup of tea in the garden* has these syllables: *a · cup · of · tea · in · the · gar · den*. Which syllables are strong, and so are good guesses for the beginnings of content words?
- [ ] *a*, *of*, *in*, *the*
- [x] *cup*, *tea*, *gar-*
- [ ] *cup*, *tea*, *-den*
- [ ] all eight
::: solution
*Cup*, *tea* and *gar-* have full vowels (/ʌ/, /iː/, /ɑː/) and begin the three content words *cup*, *tea* and *garden*. *A*, *of*, *in* and *the* are function words in their weak forms (/ə/, /əv/ or /ə/, /ɪn/, /ðə/); *-den* /dən/ has a weak vowel and ends *garden*.
:::
:::

## Using stress and intonation to decode

::: theorem How to listen to a fast utterance {#rule-listen}
1. **Hear the stressed words first.** The content words carry the stresses and are the clearest part of the signal; the message can usually be reconstructed from them. In *I ˈthought you were ˈgoing to ˈcall me last ˈnight*, the stressed words *thought*, *going*, *call* and *night* give most of the meaning.
2. **Fill in the grammar.** The weak syllables between the stresses are mostly pronouns, auxiliaries, prepositions and articles. You know which ones are possible: after *I thought* and before *going*, [jə wə] can only be *you were* (or *you're*).
3. **Use the nucleus.** The nucleus of each tone unit falls on the most important new information ([[connected-speech/sentence-stress#rule-last]]). If you catch nothing else, catch the nuclei.
4. **Use the tone.** A fall usually marks a finished statement or the end of a turn; a rise or fall-rise marks something unfinished or a question ([[connected-speech/intonation-basics]]). Tone tells you whether to wait or to reply.
5. **Use context and expectation.** Listeners constantly predict what is likely to be said. When a sequence could be divided in more than one way, choose the one that makes sense.
:::

::: proof
This strategy reflects how listening works for native speakers too. Psycholinguistic research shows that listeners do not process speech sound by sound and then word by word; they make rapid guesses from partial information and correct them as more arrives (Field 2008 gives an accessible account for language teachers). Function words, said in isolation, are often unrecognisable even to native listeners: cut out of a recording, [ə] could be *a*, *of*, *are*, *or* or *her*. They are recognised from the context — the stressed words around them and the grammar. Learners who try to hear every word clearly before understanding the sentence are trying to do something that native listeners do not do.
:::

::: example Decoding one sentence {#ex-decode}
A speaker says, at normal speed (GB): [ˈwɒdʒə ˈθɪŋk əv ðə ˈfɪlm]. Work out the sentence step by step.
::: solution
1. **Stressed syllables**: [ˈwɒ], [ˈθɪŋk], [ˈfɪlm]. *Think* and *film* are clear content words. [ˈwɒdʒə] is a stressed syllable followed by [dʒə].
2. **Reduced chunk**: [dʒə] is very often *did you* or *do you*, coalesced ([[connected-speech/elision-assimilation#rule-coalescence]]). [ˈwɒdʒə] is therefore *what did you* or *what do you*, with the /t/ of *what* lost.
3. **Grammar**: *What d'you think of the film?* — [əv] is *of*, [ðə] is *the*.
4. **Tense**: *What did you think* (asking about a film already seen) or *What do you think* (a film being discussed). Context — e.g. coming out of a cinema — decides.

The full sentence: *What did you think of the film?* Only the stressed words were heard clearly; everything else was reconstructed.
:::
:::

::: example Catching the nuclei {#ex-nuclei}
A station announcement is fast and the loudspeaker is poor. The tone units and nuclei are marked here (GB). Which words would you need to catch, and why?

*The ˈtrain now ˈarriving at ˈplatform ↘↗**four** | is the ˈten fif↘**teen** service | to ↘**Man**chester, | ˈcalling at ↗**Stoke**, | ↗**Mac**clesfield | and ↘**Stock**port.*
::: solution
The nuclei are *four*, *fifteen*, *Manchester*, *Stoke*, *Macclesfield* and *Stockport* — exactly the information a traveller needs: the platform, the time, the destination and the stops. The rest (*the train now arriving at*, *is the*, *service to*, *calling at*) is predictable and is said quickly in the run-up to each nucleus. The tones help too: the fall-rise on *four* and the rises in the list show that more is coming; the fall on *Stockport* marks the end of the list and of the announcement ([[connected-speech/intonation-basics#rule-tags]]). A listener who catches the six nuclei has understood the announcement.
:::
:::

## Reduced chunks

Some combinations are reduced so often that it is easier to learn them as **chunks** — single units with their own sound shape — than to work them out each time. Most were described in [[connected-speech/weak-forms]] and [[connected-speech/elision-assimilation]]; here they are collected for recognition.

| you hear | it means | example |
|---|---|---|
| [dʒə] | *do you*, *did you* | *[dʒə] want one?* |
| [ˈwɒdʒə], [ˈwʌdʒə] (GA [ˈwʌɾəjə]) | *what do you*, *what did you* | *[ˈwɒdʒə] mean?* |
| [ˈwʊdʒə], [ˈkʊdʒə] | *would you*, *could you* | *[ˈkʊdʒə] help me?* |
| [ˈdəʊntʃə] | *don't you* | *[ˈdəʊntʃə] like it?* |
| [ˈɡɒnə], [ˈwɒnə], [ˈɡɒtə] (GA [ˈɡʌnə], [ˈwɑːnə], [ˈɡɑːɾə]) | *going to*, *want to*, *got to* | *I'm [ˈɡɒnə] be late.* |
| [dəˈnəʊ], [ˈdʌnəʊ] | *don't know* | *I [dəˈnəʊ].* |
| [ˈkaɪndə], [ˈsɔːtə] | *kind of*, *sort of* | *It's [ˈkaɪndə] strange.* |
| [ˈlemi], [ˈɡɪmi] | *let me*, *give me* | *[ˈlemi] see.* |
| [əm], [ðəm] | *them* | *Tell [əm] I'm coming.* |
| [kəz], [kɒz] | *because* | *I left [kəz] I was tired.* |
| [ˈɪnɪt] (informal British) | *isn't it* | *Cold today, [ˈɪnɪt]?* |
| [ˈwɒtʃə] | *what are you*, *what you* (GB) | *[ˈwɒtʃə] doing?* |

::: example Chunks in a conversation {#ex-chunks}
Write this overheard exchange (GB, informal) in standard spelling.

**A:** [ˈwɒtʃə ˈdʊɪŋ təˈnaɪt]
**B:** [aɪ dəˈnəʊ | ˈprɒbli ˈnʌθɪŋ | ˈwaɪ]
**A:** [ˈwɒnə ˈkʌm tə ðə ˈpʌb]
**B:** [ˈʃʊə | ˈlemi ˈtel ˈsæm | hiː ˈmaɪt ˈwɒnə ˈkʌm ˈtuː]
::: solution
**A:** *What are you doing tonight?*
**B:** *I don't know. Probably nothing. Why?*
**A:** *(Do you) want to come to the pub?*
**B:** *Sure. Let me tell Sam — he might want to come too.*

Notes: [ˈwɒtʃə] = *what are you*; [dəˈnəʊ] = *don't know*; [ˈprɒbli] — *probably* with a weak syllable elided ([[connected-speech/elision-assimilation#rule-elision]]); [ˈwɒnə] = *want to*, and A's question has lost *do you* altogether, which is common in informal speech; [ˈlemi] = *let me*.
:::
:::

## When boundaries are ambiguous

Because there are no pauses between words, some strings of sounds can be divided in more than one way. [[connected-speech/linking#ex-boundary]] gave examples such as *grey tape* and *great ape*. The figure shows how a listener considers both.

::: widget graph
nodes: heard /ɡreɪteɪp/@0,3; grey + tape@-3,1.5; great + ape@3,1.5; context - a parcel to wrap@-3,0; context - a zoo@3,0; the speaker meant grey tape@-3,-1.5; the speaker meant great ape@3,-1.5
edges: heard /ɡreɪteɪp/>grey + tape; heard /ɡreɪteɪp/>great + ape; grey + tape>context - a parcel to wrap; great + ape>context - a zoo; context - a parcel to wrap>the speaker meant grey tape; context - a zoo>the speaker meant great ape
caption: Two ways of dividing one string of sounds. The listener hears /ɡreɪteɪp/ and, automatically, considers more than one division: *grey + tape* or *great + ape*. Small phonetic details can help — the /t/ of *tape* is aspirated, the /eɪ/ of *great* is shorter, and in GA *great ape* may have a tap — but in fast speech the decision is usually made by context: wrapping a parcel or visiting a zoo. You can drag the boxes.
:::

Misdivisions happen to native speakers too, especially with song lyrics, where context is weak and music hides the rhythm of speech. Such mishearings are called **mondegreens**, after a Scottish ballad line *and laid him on the green*, which the American writer Sylvia Wright had heard as a child as *and Lady Mondegreen*.

::: warning Don't wait for every word
✗ Stopping the recording at every word you didn't catch, or trying to hear each function word clearly before moving on.
✓ Listening for the stressed words and the nuclei, getting the gist, and then filling in.

✗ Using only slow, carefully spoken learner materials.
✓ Mixing them with natural speech — conversations, podcasts, films — even if you understand less at first.

Function words in fast speech are not heard clearly by anyone. A listener who stops at each one loses the sentence. And materials recorded slowly for learners, with every word in its strong form, do not train the ear for the reduced forms of real speech.
:::

## Practising

::: theorem Four ways to practise {#rule-practice}
1. **Dictation.** Listen to a short, natural recording (one or two sentences) several times, and write down exactly what was said. Then compare with the transcript. Mistakes show which reductions you do not yet recognise.
2. **Dictogloss.** Listen to a short passage (a paragraph) twice at normal speed, noting only the key words; then reconstruct the text, alone or with a partner, as accurately as possible, and compare with the original (Wajnryb 1990). This trains you to hear stressed words and rebuild grammar.
3. **Shadowing.** Speak along with a recording, a fraction of a second behind, copying rhythm, reductions and intonation. Producing the reduced forms yourself makes them easier to hear.
4. **Transcript after, not before.** Listen first without text; then read the transcript and listen again, noticing exactly where what you heard differs from what is written; finally, listen once more without text.
:::

::: proof
Each technique targets a different skill. Dictation forces attention to every word, including weak forms, and so shows exactly where segmentation fails. Dictogloss trains the opposite skill — understanding from the stressed words — and combines it with grammar. Shadowing links perception and production: learners who can say [ˈwɒdʒə] fluently are better at recognising it. Listening first without a transcript prevents reading from replacing listening; the transcript afterwards makes the gap between written and spoken forms visible. Teachers have used dictation for centuries; its value for connected speech is that the mistakes are informative.
:::

::: example A dictation and its mistakes {#ex-dictation}
A learner hears, in a podcast: *I've been meaning to ask you about that for ages.* She writes: *I've been mean to ask you a bout that for age.* Explain each mistake with the processes of this course.
::: solution
- *mean to* for *meaning to*: in fast, informal speech *-ing* is often [ɪn] rather than [ɪŋ], and *to* is weak: [ˈmiːnɪn tə]. The weak syllable [ɪn] was lost in the stream, and the learner chose a verb form she knows.
- *a bout* for *about*: the first syllable of *about* is a weak /ə/, which sounds the same as the article *a* ([[#rule-strong]]: *-bout* is the strong syllable, and the word begins before it — an exception the learner must learn).
- *for age* for *for ages*: the /z/ of *ages* was weak at the end of the tone unit, perhaps devoiced; but the grammar (*for* + a plural expression of time) could have restored it.
- *ask you* was correct: the learner may have heard [ˈɑːsk jə] or [ˈɑːskjuː] — linking of /k/ to /j/.

Each error is a place where the learner relied on the sound alone; grammar and the common chunk *for ages* would have corrected two of them.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Every Chinese syllable counts.** In Mandarin almost every syllable is a morpheme with a full vowel and a tone, and listeners expect each syllable to be clear. English weak syllables are not clear, and learners may think they have "missed" something when in fact there was little to hear.
- **Strong syllables, not characters.** Chinese readers are used to dividing text into characters, one syllable each. A more useful unit for English listening is the **foot** ([[connected-speech/rhythm#def-foot]]): a strong syllable and the weak syllables that follow it. Listen for the strong syllables, as in [[#rule-strong]].
- **Exam listening and real listening.** Many listening tests use clearly read scripts, and learners who do well on them may still find films and conversations very hard. Both are useful, but only natural speech trains recognition of reduced forms.
- **Subtitles.** Chinese subtitles help you follow the story but let you skip the English. English subtitles are more useful for connecting sounds to words; best of all is listening first without subtitles, then with English subtitles, as in rule 4 of [[#rule-practice]].
:::

::: history
Systematic attention to listening to natural, reduced speech in language teaching began in the 1970s. Gillian Brown's *Listening to Spoken English* (1977; 2nd edition 1990) described the reductions of conversational English for teachers and showed how different it is from the careful speech of teaching materials. In the 1980s, Anne Cutler and her colleagues studied how listeners segment speech: Cutler and Carter (1987) found the predominance of strong initial syllables in English content words, and Cutler and Norris (1988) proposed the metrical segmentation strategy. Ruth Wajnryb's *Grammar Dictation* (1990) made the dictogloss widely known. John Field's *Listening in the Language Classroom* (2008) argued that teachers should train the decoding processes of listening directly, rather than only testing comprehension. The word *mondegreen* was coined by Sylvia Wright in an essay in *Harper's Magazine* in 1954.
:::

::: summary
- Speech has no spaces; **segmentation** — finding word boundaries — is a main difficulty for learners ([[#def-segmentation]]).
- Most English content words begin with a **strong syllable**; treat strong syllables as likely word beginnings (Cutler and Carter 1987; [[#rule-strong]]).
- Listen for stressed words and nuclei first, then fill in the function words from grammar and context; use the tone to know whether the speaker has finished ([[#rule-listen]]).
- Learn common **reduced chunks** — [dʒə], [ˈwɒdʒə], *gonna*, *dunno*, *lemme* — as units.
- Ambiguous divisions (*grey tape* / *great ape*) are resolved mainly by context.
- Practise with **dictation**, **dictogloss**, **shadowing**, and **transcript-after** listening ([[#rule-practice]]).
:::

Underline (mentally) the strong syllables and say which content words they begin.

1. *a glass of water* 2. *in the morning* 3. *She's at the station.*
::: solution
1. *glass*, *wa-* (*water*). 2. *mor-* (*morning*). 3. *sta-* (*station*). (*She's*, *at* and *the* are weak in normal speech.)

Write in standard spelling.

1. [dʒə ˈwɒnə ˈkʌm] 2. [aɪ dəˈnəʊ] 3. [ˈlemi ˈsiː] 4. [ˈkʊdʒə ˈhelp mi]
::: solution
1. *Do you want to come?* 2. *I don't know.* 3. *Let me see.* 4. *Could you help me?*
:::

::: exercise Two ways to divide {level=1}
Each sound string can be divided in two ways. Give both.

1. [ə ˈnaɪs ˈmæn] 2. [sʌ ˈmʌðəz] 3. [ˈaɪs ˈkriːm]
::: solution
1. *a nice man* or *an ice man*. 2. *some others* (the /m/ linked to the vowel, [[connected-speech/linking#rule-cv]]) or *some mothers* (two /m/s said as one, [[connected-speech/linking#rule-cc]]); a longer [mː] points to *mothers*. 3. *ice cream* or *I scream* (the /k/ of *cream* is aspirated; in *scream* it is not).
:::

::: exercise Count the strong syllables {level=2 check="3"}
In *The teacher explained it again* said at normal speed, how many strong syllables are there?
::: solution
*tea-* (/iː/), *-plained* (/eɪ/) and *-gain* (/e/). *The*, *-cher*, *ex-*, *it* and *a-* are weak. The answer is 3.
:::
:::

::: exercise Reconstruct from stresses {level=2}
You catch only the stressed words: *... ˈthought ... ˈmeeting ... ˈTuesday ... ˈmoved ... ˈFriday.* Reconstruct a likely sentence and mark its tone units and nuclei.
::: solution
*I ˈthought the ˈmeeting was on ↘↗**Tues**day, | but it's been ˈmoved to ↘**Fri**day.* Other reconstructions are possible (*We thought the meeting on Tuesday had been moved to Friday*); a good answer uses the stressed words in order and fills in plausible function words. The nucleus *Friday* is the new information.
:::
:::

::: exercise What did she hear? {level=2}
A learner wrote *I got a go* for what was said: *I've got to go.* Explain the mistake.
::: solution
*Got to* is often reduced to [ˈɡɒtə] (GB) or [ˈɡɑːɾə] (GA), and *I've* to [aɪv] or even [aɪ] before /ɡ/. The learner heard [aɪ ˈɡɒtə ˈɡəʊ] and divided [ˈɡɒtə] as *got a*, which is a real English sequence. The grammar (*I've got to* + verb) and context (someone leaving) show that *I've got to go* is meant. *Gotta* is a chunk to learn ([[connected-speech/elision-assimilation#rule-gonna]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Dictation project {level=3}
Choose a 30-second clip of natural English (a conversation in a podcast or film, not a news reading). Write down everything you hear, listening as many times as you need. Then compare with a transcript or subtitles and classify your mistakes: weak forms, linking, elision/assimilation, chunks, vocabulary, other.
::: hint
Choose a clip with a transcript available. Mark each difference first, then decide which chapter of this course explains it.
:::
::: solution
Answers vary. A typical result is that most mistakes involve weak forms (missed *of*, *to*, *have*), chunks (*gonna*, *d'you*) and linking across boundaries, and fewer involve unknown words. A good answer gives two or three specific examples and links each to a process, e.g. "I wrote *I want to* for *I wanted to*: the *-ed* of *wanted* was lost before *to* — elision".
:::
:::

::: exercise Shadowing diary {level=3}
For one week, shadow the same one-minute clip for five minutes a day. On day 1 and day 7, record yourself and note: Which reductions could you produce? Where did you lose the speaker? What changed?
::: solution
Answers vary. Learners usually report that on day 1 they fall behind at reduced chunks and long tails, and that by day 7 they can follow the rhythm and produce most weak forms. A good diary names particular phrases (e.g. *what are you gonna do* [ˈwɒtʃə ˈɡɒnə ˈduː]) and notices that the nucleus and the overall melody become easier to copy than individual words.
:::
