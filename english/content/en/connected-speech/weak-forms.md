Here is a sentence every learner knows: *I want to go to the shop for some bread.* Spelled out, it has eleven words. Said at a normal speed by a speaker of General British, it sounds more like this:

/aɪ ˈwɒnt tə ˈɡəʊ tə ðə ˈʃɒp fə səm ˈbred/

Look at the small words. *To* is not /tuː/ but /tə/; *for* is not /fɔː/ but /fə/; *the* is /ðə/ and *some* is /səm/. None of these is a mistake or a "lazy" pronunciation. They are the normal forms of these words in an unstressed position, and an English speaker who said /tuː/, /fɔː/ and /sʌm/ here would sound strange — careful, slow and rather emphatic. These reduced pronunciations are called **weak forms**. They are a big part of why learners who read English well often cannot hear it.

This chapter lists the weak forms of the most common words, explains when the strong forms are used instead, and shows how contractions such as *I'm* and *she'd* write some weak forms in ordinary spelling. It builds on the content and function words of [[connected-speech/sentence-stress]] and on schwa, the weak vowel of [[sounds-of-english/short-vowels]].

## Strong and weak forms

::: definition Strong form and weak form {#def-weak}
Some English function words have two or more pronunciations. The **strong form** is the pronunciation with a full vowel, used when the word is stressed or said on its own: *to* /tuː/, *can* /kæn/, *her* /hɜː/ (GB). The **weak form** is a reduced pronunciation used when the word is unstressed: its vowel becomes schwa /ə/ (or sometimes /ɪ/, /i/, /u/), and some sounds may disappear: *to* /tə/, *can* /kən/, *her* /ə/ (GB).
:::

About forty very common words have weak forms. They are almost all function words: articles, prepositions, pronouns, auxiliary and modal verbs, and conjunctions. In frequency lists made from large corpora, such as the British National Corpus (Leech, Rayson and Wilson 2001), the most frequent words of English are almost all function words — *the*, *and*, *of*, *to*, *a*, *I*, *you*, *it*. So weak forms are not a detail: they occur in nearly every sentence, many times.

Many function words have **no** weak form, because their vowel is already short or because they are rarely unstressed: *in* /ɪn/, *on*, *up*, *with*, *it*, *if*, *this*, *not*. Content words never have weak forms.

Three notes on the table:

- **The** before a vowel. The traditional rule gives /ði/ before a vowel. Many younger speakers, especially in GA, now also use /ðə/ there (*the end* /ðə ˈend/); both are heard.
- **/h/ in pronouns and *have*.** *He*, *him*, *his*, *her*, *have*, *has* and *had* usually lose their /h/ when unstressed in the middle of a sentence (*tell him* /ˈtel ɪm/), but keep it at the start (*He's here* /hiz ˈhɪə/, GB). This is normal in both GB and GA, and it is different from the dropping of /h/ in stressed words (*'ouse* for *house*) heard in some regional accents.
- ***Been*** is a GB–GA difference: GB has a strong form /biːn/ and a weak form /bɪn/; GA uses /bɪn/ for both.

::: example Finding the weak forms {#ex-mark}
Write these sentences in phonemic transcription (GB), with weak forms where they belong.

1. *Can you give them a call?*
2. *I was waiting for her at the station.*
3. *She's older than her brother.*
::: solution
First mark the stresses ([[connected-speech/sentence-stress]]); then reduce the unstressed function words.

1. *ˈCan you ˈgive them a ↗**call**?* — /kən ju ˈɡɪv ðəm ə ˈkɔːl/. *Can* is at the start of a question but unstressed, so it is weak /kən/; *them* /ðəm/; *a* /ə/.
2. *I was ˈwaiting for her at the ↘**sta**tion.* — /aɪ wəz ˈweɪtɪŋ fər ər ət ðə ˈsteɪʃn/. *Was* /wəz/; *for* /fə/ — but here a vowel follows, so GB pronounces the /r/: /fər/ (linking r, [[connected-speech/linking]]); *her* /ə/ (with /r/ before the vowel of *at*: /ər/); *at* /ət/; *the* /ðə/.
3. *She's ˈolder than her ↘**broth**er.* — /ʃiz ˈəʊldə ðən ə ˈbrʌðə/. *Is* is contracted to /z/; *than* /ðən/; *her* /ə/.

In GA: (1) /kən ju ˈɡɪv ðəm ə ˈkɔːl/ (/ˈkɑːl/ for speakers who say *caught* like *cot*); (2) /aɪ wəz ˈweɪɾɪŋ fər ər ət ðə ˈsteɪʃn/; (3) /ʃiz ˈoʊldər ðən ər ˈbrʌðər/. Notice how many syllables have schwa: in sentence 2, five of the ten.
:::
:::

## When the strong form is used

Weak forms are the rule in unstressed positions. The strong form appears in a small number of clear situations.

::: theorem When to use the strong form {#rule-strong}
Use the **strong form** of a word that has a weak form:

1. **When it is stressed** for emphasis or contrast: *I said ↘**to** him, | not ↘**from** him.* — *You ↘**must** come.* — *I ↘**can** do it!*
2. **At the end of a sentence or phrase**, for prepositions and auxiliaries: *Where are you ↘**from**?* /frɒm/ (GB); *What are you looking ↘**at**?* — more often *What are you ↘**look**ing at?*, still with strong /æt/; *Yes, I ↘**can**.* /kæn/; *I think she ↘**has**.* /hæz/.
3. **When it is quoted** as a word: *"The" is the most common word in English.* /ðiː/.
4. **When two function words are joined by *and* or *or***: *with ˈor without ˈsugar*, *I travel ˈto and ˈfrom ↘**work** by bus* — both *to* and *from* are strong.

A preposition at the end of a sentence followed only by a pronoun may be weak or strong: *I was looking at him* — /ət ɪm/ or /æt ɪm/.
:::

::: proof
Rule 1 follows from the definition: a weak form is an unstressed form, so a stressed word cannot have it. Rules 2 and 4 are less obvious, because the word is often still not the nucleus (in *What are you looking at?* the nucleus is on *look-*). The reason is rhythmic and historical: a weak vowel needs a following word to lean on. At the end of a phrase, a preposition or auxiliary has nothing after it, and English keeps its full vowel — compare the contracted form, which is impossible at the end: *Yes, I am* /æm/, never ✗ *Yes, I'm*. Roach (2009) gives these rules, with the same examples of final prepositions and coordinated function words; Cruttenden (2014) adds that the strong form is also usual when a word is said slowly and deliberately, for instance in dictation.
:::

::: warning Do not end a sentence with a weak form
✗ *Yes, I /kən/.* ✗ *Who's it /fə/?* ✗ *That's what I'm looking /ət/.* ✗ *Yes, I'm.*
✓ *Yes, I /kæn/.* ✓ *Who's it /fɔː/?* (GB) ✓ *That's what I'm looking /æt/.* ✓ *Yes, I am.*

A weak form at the end of a sentence sounds cut off, as if the speaker had stopped in the middle. The same rule explains why contractions (*I'm*, *she's*, *we've*) cannot come last: *Is she ready? — Yes, she is.* (not ✗ *Yes, she's*).
:::

::: example Strong or weak? {#ex-strong}
For each word in bold, say whether it is strong or weak, and why.

1. *What are you waiting **for**?*
2. *I'm waiting **for** a taxi.*
3. *Are you coming? — Yes, I **am**.*
4. *I **am** coming — just give me a minute!*
5. *Is it a present **for** you or **from** you?*
::: solution
1. Strong /fɔː/ (GB), /fɔːr/ (GA): a preposition at the end ([[#rule-strong]], 2).
2. Weak /fə/ (GB), /fər/ (GA): unstressed, with *a taxi* after it.
3. Strong /æm/: an auxiliary at the end of the sentence (rule 2). The weak form /əm/ or the contraction *I'm* is impossible here.
4. Strong /æm/, and it is the nucleus: the speaker insists that what the listener doubts is true (rule 1; this is emphasis on polarity, [[connected-speech/sentence-stress#rule-focus]]).
5. Strong, both of them: they are contrasted (rule 1): *Is it a ˈpresent ↗**for** you | or ↘**from** you?*
:::
:::

## Contractions: weak forms in spelling

Some weak forms are so common that they have a written form, the **contraction**, with an apostrophe in place of the missing sounds.

| full form | contraction | pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| *I am*, *you are*, *he is* | *I'm*, *you're*, *he's* | /aɪm/, /jɔː/ (GB) or /jə/, /jʊr/ (GA), /hiːz/ |
| *I have*, *she has* | *I've*, *she's* | /aɪv/, /ʃiːz/ |
| *I will*, *they would*, *we had* | *I'll*, *they'd*, *we'd* | /aɪl/, /ðeɪd/, /wiːd/ |
| *is not*, *do not*, *cannot*, *will not* | *isn't*, *don't*, *can't*, *won't* | /ˈɪznt/, /dəʊnt/ (GB), /doʊnt/ (GA), /kɑːnt/ (GB), /kænt/ (GA), /wəʊnt/ (GB), /woʊnt/ (GA) |

Two contractions are ambiguous: *'s* can be *is* or *has*, and *'d* can be *would* or *had*. The next word tells you which.

- *She's working* (*is* + *-ing*) — *She's worked* (*has* + past participle).
- *He'd go* (*would* + bare infinitive) — *He'd gone* (*had* + past participle) — *He'd better go* (*had better*).

Contractions are normal in speech in every register, and in informal writing. In formal writing (essays, reports) the full forms are usual; but even when a text is written with full forms, it is often read aloud with weak forms or contractions: *It is important* → /ɪts ɪmˈpɔːtnt/ (GB), or with a weak /ɪz/.

::: theorem *Can* and *can't* {#rule-can}
In a positive sentence, unstressed *can* is weak /kən/; the main verb after it is stressed: *I can ˈswim.* Negative *can't* is **stressed** and has a full vowel: /kɑːnt/ (GB), /kænt/ (GA): *I ˈcan't ˈswim.* The difference is therefore one of stress and vowel, not only of the final /t/:

- GB: *I can swim* /aɪ kən ˈswɪm/ — *I can't swim* /aɪ ˈkɑːnt ˈswɪm/
- GA: *I can swim* /aɪ kən ˈswɪm/ — *I can't swim* /aɪ ˈkænt ˈswɪm/, often with the /t/ replaced by a glottal stop: [ˈkæ̃nʔ]
:::

::: proof
In GA the strong forms *can* /kæn/ and *can't* /kænt/ differ only in the final /t/, and before a consonant that /t/ is often glottalised or barely audible. Listeners therefore rely on the first part of the word: a weak /kən/ means *can*; a strong, stressed /kæn-/ in a positive sentence is rare, so a stressed /kæn/ before a verb usually means *can't*. In GB the vowel is also different (/ɑː/ in *can't*), which makes the contrast easier. A learner who says strong /kæn/ in *I can swim* risks being heard as saying *can't*, especially by GA listeners. Using the weak form is therefore not only a matter of sounding natural; it carries meaning.
:::

::: example *Can* or *can't*? {#ex-can}
Wang Lei is in a meeting with colleagues who speak GA. He hears these sentences. Use stress and vowel to decide whether each contains *can* or *can't*.

1. /aɪ kən ˈmiːt ju ət ˈθriː/
2. /wi ˈkænt ˈfɪnɪʃ ɪt baɪ ˈfraɪdeɪ/
3. /ʃi kən ˈspiːk ˈtʃaɪˈniːz/
4. /ju ˈkæn ˈpɑːrk ˈhɪr/
::: solution
1. *can* — weak /kən/, and the stress is on *meet*: *I can meet you at three.*
2. *can't* — /ˈkænt/ is stressed, with a full vowel: *We can't finish it by Friday.*
3. *can* — weak /kən/: *She can speak Chinese.*
4. Probably *can't*: /ˈkæn/ is stressed with a full vowel in a positive-looking sentence, and the /t/ may be glottalised before /p/. If the speaker meant an emphatic *can* (*You ↘**can** park here!* — 'it really is allowed'), the context would show it. In real conversation, when it matters, ask: *Sorry — can or can't?*
:::
:::

::: example Reading a dialogue with weak forms {#ex-dialogue}
Mark the weak forms in this short dialogue, and give their pronunciation (GB).

**Chen Jie:** *Are you going to the party on Saturday?*
**Ana:** *I'd like to, but I've got to work. What time does it start?*
**Chen Jie:** *At eight. You could come after work.*
::: solution
**Chen Jie:** *Are* /ə/ *you* /ju/ *going* *to* /tə/ *the* /ðə/ *party on Saturday?* — *ˈAre you ˈgoing to the ˈparty on ↗**Sat**urday?* (In a question beginning with *Are*, the auxiliary is unstressed and weak.)

**Ana:** *I'd* /aɪd/ *like to* /tuː/ — **strong**: the verb after *to* has been left out, so *to* is the last word of its phrase (*I'd ˈlike ↘**to***) … *but* /bət/ *I've* /aɪv/ *got to* /tə/ *work.* *What time does* /dəz/ *it start?*

**Chen Jie:** *At* /ət/ *eight.* *You* /ju/ *could* /kəd/ *come after work.*

*To* in *I'd like to* is a common trap: when the verb after it is left out, *to* keeps a full vowel (*Do you want to come? — I'd ˈlove ↘**to**.*). In *At eight*, a vowel follows, so the /t/ links to it: /ət ˈeɪt/ ([[connected-speech/linking]]).
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **A familiar idea.** Mandarin has its own "weak words": grammatical particles such as *de* 的, *le* 了, *ma* 吗 and *zhe* 着 are said in the neutral tone (轻声) — short, light and without a tone of their own. English weak forms are similar: grammatical words lose their full vowel and their stress. Thinking of *to*, *of* and *for* as "neutral-tone words" is a good start.
- **What is different.** Mandarin neutral-tone syllables keep their vowel more or less, and most Mandarin function words (*zài* 在, *gěi* 给, *hé* 和) keep their full tone. In English the reduction is stronger — the vowel becomes schwa and consonants can disappear (*and* → /n/) — and it affects almost every function word. Learners who give *to*, *for* and *can* full vowels make every word equally strong, and the stressed words no longer stand out.
- **Spelling pronunciation.** Many learners first learned these words from their written form and their dictionary pronunciation, which is usually the strong form. Learn both forms, and learn the weak form as part of a phrase: *a cup of tea* /ə ˈkʌp əv ˈtiː/, *I can swim* /aɪ kən ˈswɪm/.
- **Listening.** If you listen for /tuː/ and /ɒv/, you will not find them. Expect /tə/ and /əv/, and use grammar to fill them in: after *want*, *to* is almost certain.
:::

::: history
Weak forms are old: the indefinite article *a* was originally a reduced form of *one*, and spellings such as *'tis*, *I'll* and *o'* (for *of*) are common in the plays of Shakespeare's time. But for a long time grammars and dictionaries described only the "full" pronunciation of words. In the late nineteenth century Henry Sweet insisted that the spoken language should be taught as it is actually spoken; his *Elementarbuch des gesprochenen Englisch* (1885) gave everyday English in phonetic transcription, weak forms included. Daniel Jones's *An Outline of English Phonetics* (1918) set out strong and weak forms for learners, and lists of them have appeared in every pronunciation course since then. Modern pronouncing dictionaries such as LPD give both forms in the entry for each word.
:::

## Where this leads

Weak forms are what make English **rhythm**: stressed syllables stand out because the syllables between them are weak ([[connected-speech/rhythm]]). Weak forms join the words around them through **linking** ([[connected-speech/linking]]), and some lose further sounds by **elision** ([[connected-speech/elision-assimilation]]). Hearing weak forms in fast speech is practised in [[connected-speech/hearing-fast-speech]]. Function words as a part of vocabulary are discussed in [[core-vocabulary/function-words]].

::: summary
- Many function words have a **strong form** (full vowel, used when stressed) and a **weak form** (usually with schwa, used when unstressed) ([[#def-weak]]).
- About forty very common words have weak forms: *the*, *a*, *and*, *but*, *that*, *than*, *as*, *to*, *for*, *of*, *from*, *at*, *some*, *there*, *can*, *must*, *have*, *do*, *be*, *was*, pronouns and others.
- GB and GA differ mainly in /r/ (*for* /fə/, /fər/) and in *been* (GB /biːn/, /bɪn/; GA /bɪn/).
- The strong form is used when the word is stressed or contrasted, at the end of a phrase, when quoted, and when two function words are joined by *and* or *or* ([[#rule-strong]]).
- Contractions (*I'm*, *she's*, *they'd*, *can't*) are weak forms in spelling; *'s* = *is* or *has*, *'d* = *would* or *had*.
- *Can* is weak /kən/; *can't* is always strong and stressed — the stress and vowel tell them apart ([[#rule-can]]).
- Using weak forms is part of being understood, not careless speech.
:::

1. *I'd like a cup **of** tea.* 2. *What's it made **of**?* 3. *He **can** play the piano.* 4. *Can you play? — Yes, I **can**.* 5. *Give it **to** me.* 6. *Who did you give it **to**?*
::: solution
1. weak /əv/ 2. strong /ɒv/ (GB), /ʌv/ (GA) — last in the sentence 3. weak /kən/ 4. strong /kæn/ — last 5. weak /tə/ 6. strong /tuː/ — last

::: exercise Count the schwas {level=2 check="5"}
How many function words in *I was hoping to see some of the photos* are said with a schwa (GB, normal speed)?
::: solution
*I* /aɪ/ *was* /wəz/ *hoping* *to* /tə/ *see* *some* /səm/ *of* /əv/ *the* /ðə/ *photos*. The function words with schwa are *was*, *to*, *some*, *of* and *the*. *I* has no weak form with schwa, and *hoping* and *photos* are content words (their unstressed syllables have /ɪ/ and /əʊ/, GB). The answer is 5.
:::

::: exercise Strong, weak or both? {level=2}
Mark the strong forms in this dialogue and explain each one.

**A:** *Who are these flowers for?*
**B:** *They're for you!*
**A:** *For me? Thank you! But what are they for?*
**B:** *For your birthday. You thought I'd forgotten, didn't you? Well, I hadn't.*
::: solution
- *Who are these flowers **for**?* — strong /fɔː/: last in the sentence.
- *They're **for** you!* — weak /fə/: unstressed (the nucleus is on *you*, which is new and emphatic: *They're for ↘**you**!*).
- ***For** me?* — strong: *For me?* is a short echo question, and *for* begins it with a stress, since *me* is given. Both are often stressed: *ˈFor ↗**me**?*
- *But what are they **for**?* — strong: last.
- ***For** your birthday.* — weak /fə/: the nucleus is on *birthday*.
- *I **hadn't**.* — strong, and the nucleus: a negative auxiliary at the end. Earlier, *I'd forgotten* is a contraction of *had* (+ past participle).
:::
:::

::: exercise *Can* and *can't* {level=2}
Transcribe (GB and GA) and mark the stress: *I can come, but I can't stay long.*
::: solution
GB: /aɪ kən ˈkʌm | bət aɪ ˈkɑːnt ˈsteɪ ˈlɒŋ/. GA: /aɪ kən ˈkʌm | bət aɪ ˈkænt ˈsteɪ ˈlɔːŋ/. *Can* is weak and unstressed; *can't* is strong and stressed. In GA the /t/ of *can't* before /s/ may be glottalised, so the stress and the full vowel are the main clues.
:::
:::

::: exercise Rewrite in transcription {level=3}
Write this text in phonemic transcription (GB or GA — say which), using weak forms where they belong. Mark the stressed syllables.

*I've been trying to call you all afternoon. Where were you? I wanted to ask if you could help us with the move on Sunday.*
::: hint
Find the content words first. *Been*, *to*, *you*, *were*, *could*, *us*, *with*, *the*, *on* all have weak forms or are unstressed.
:::
::: solution
GB: /aɪv bɪn ˈtraɪɪŋ tə ˈkɔːl ju ˈɔːl ˌɑːftəˈnuːn | ˈweə wə ju | aɪ ˈwɒntɪd tu ˈɑːsk ɪf ju kəd ˈhelp əs wɪð ðə ˈmuːv ɒn ˈsʌndeɪ/

GA: /aɪv bɪn ˈtraɪɪŋ tə ˈkɔːl jə ˈɔːl ˌæftərˈnuːn | ˈwer wər ju | aɪ ˈwɑːntɪd tu ˈæsk ɪf jə kəd ˈhelp əs wɪð ðə ˈmuːv ɑːn ˈsʌndeɪ/

Notes: *to* before the vowel of *ask* is /tu/; *were* in *Where were you?* may be weak, as here (*↘**Where** were you?*), or strong and accented (*ˈWhere ↘**were** you?*, /wɜː/ GB, /wɜːr/ GA), a very common pattern in short questions like this; *on* has no weak form; *Sunday* may end in /i/ (/ˈsʌndi/). Both versions accept small variations, such as /ˈwɒnɪd/ (GB) or /ˈwɑːnɪd/ (GA) for *wanted*.
:::

::: exercise Record and compare {level=3}
Choose four sentences from the table of weak forms in this chapter. Say each one twice into a recorder: first with every word in its strong form, then with weak forms. Listen to both. Write two or three sentences describing the differences you hear in length, rhythm and clarity of the stressed words.
::: solution
Answers vary. A typical observation: "The version with strong forms took longer and every word sounded equally important; the stressed words (*cup*, *tea*) did not stand out. With weak forms, the sentence was shorter and had a clear beat on the stressed syllables, and it sounded more like the English I hear in films and podcasts." A good answer notices that the stressed syllables become **clearer**, not weaker, when the function words are reduced.
