Listen to an English speaker say *Pick it up.* You will not hear three separate words, *pick* · *it* · *up*. You will hear something closer to *pi-ki-tup*: the /k/ of *pick* begins the next syllable, and so does the /t/ of *it*. In the same way *an apple* sounds like *a-napple*, *turn off* like *tur-noff*, and *go out* has a small *w* between the two vowels: *go-wout*.

This joining of words is called **linking**. It is one reason why English speech sounds smooth, and one reason why learners find it hard to hear where one word ends and the next begins. A learner who keeps every word separate — often with a tiny stop of the throat before each vowel — sounds choppy and slow, even when every sound is correct. This chapter explains the main kinds of linking in GB and GA: consonant to vowel, vowel to vowel with a glide /j/ or /w/, linking and intrusive /r/, and the joining of two consonants. It builds on the rhythm of [[connected-speech/rhythm]] and the weak forms of [[connected-speech/weak-forms]], which are almost always linked to the words around them.

## Consonant to vowel

::: definition Linking {#def-linking}
**Linking** is the smooth joining of the last sound of one word to the first sound of the next, with no pause and no glottal stop between them. It happens inside a tone unit ([[connected-speech/sentence-stress#def-nucleus]]), not across a real pause.
:::

::: theorem Consonant + vowel {#rule-cv}
When a word ends in a consonant and the next word begins with a vowel, the consonant is joined to the vowel, as if it began the next syllable:

- *pick it up* /ˈpɪk ɪt ˈʌp/ → [ˈpɪ kɪ ˈtʌp]
- *an apple* /ən ˈæpl/ → [ə ˈnæpl]
- *turn off* → [tɜː ˈnɒf] (GB)
- *look at it* → [ˈlʊ kə tɪt]
- *first of all* → [ˈfɜː stə ˈvɔːl] (GB)
- *Is it ready?* → [ɪ zɪt ˈredi]

In GA, a /t/ that ends a word and stands between two vowels is usually a quick tap [ɾ], like a short *d*: *get up* [ɡe ˈɾʌp], *a lot of* [ə ˈlɑː ɾəv], *what about* [ˈwʌ ɾə ˈbaʊt], *put it on* [ˈpʊ ɾɪ ˈɾɑːn].
:::

::: proof
English syllables prefer to begin with a consonant, so a final consonant before a vowel is naturally pulled into the next syllable. The joining is not complete, however: English speakers still keep some traces of the word boundary. Compare *a name* and *an aim*, or *I scream* and *ice cream*. In *ice cream* the /k/ starts a stressed syllable and has a puff of air (aspiration, [kʰ]), while in *I scream* the /k/ comes after /s/ and has none; and the vowel /aɪ/ is shorter in *ice*, before the voiceless /s/ of the same word ([[sounds-of-english/long-vowels-diphthongs#rule-clipping]]). These small differences, studied since the 1950s, help listeners — but in fast speech they are often too small to hear, and context decides. The GA tap follows the same pattern that produces the tap in *water* and *city* ([[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet#rule-rhotic]]): a /t/ between vowels, not at the start of a stressed syllable.
:::

::: example Marking consonant–vowel links {#ex-cv}
Mark every consonant–vowel link with ‿ and say how the phrase sounds.

1. *Come in and sit down.*
2. *I've got an idea.*
3. *Not at all.*
::: solution
1. *Come‿in‿and sit down.* — [kʌ mɪ nən ˈsɪt ˈdaʊn]. *And* is weak /ən/, and the /n/ of *in* runs into it. *Sit down* has no link: /t/ + /d/ are both consonants (see "Two consonants" below).
2. *I've got‿an‿idea.* — GB [aɪv ˈɡɒ tə naɪ ˈdɪə]; GA [aɪv ˈɡɑː ɾə naɪ ˈdiːə]. There is no link between *I've* and *got*: /v/ + /ɡ/ are both consonants.
3. *Not‿at‿all.* — GB [ˈnɒ tə ˈtɔːl]; GA [ˈnɑː ɾə ˈɾɑːl] or [ˈnɑː ɾə ˈɾɔːl], with two taps. In GB many speakers use a glottal stop for the first /t/ in casual speech, but the second /t/, at the start of the stressed *all*, is linked: [ˈnɒʔ ə ˈtɔːl].

Check each boundary: a consonant–vowel link needs a consonant **before** it and a vowel **after** it.
:::
:::

## Vowel to vowel

When one word ends in a vowel and the next begins with a vowel, English does not let the two vowels meet with a gap or a glottal stop. It fills the gap with a short glide.

::: theorem Vowel + vowel {#rule-vv}
1. After a vowel that ends high and front — /iː/, /i/, /ɪ/ (in /eɪ/, /aɪ/, /ɔɪ/) — a short **/j/** glide appears: *see it* [ˈsiː ʲɪt], *the end* [ði ʲˈend], *I agree* [aɪ ʲəˈɡriː], *say again* [ˈseɪ ʲəˈɡen], *enjoy it* [ɪnˈdʒɔɪ ʲɪt].
2. After a vowel that ends high and back with rounded lips — /uː/, /u/, /ʊ/ (in /əʊ/ GB, /oʊ/ GA, /aʊ/) — a short **/w/** glide appears: *do it* [ˈduː ʷɪt], *go out* [ˈɡəʊ ʷˈaʊt] (GB), *how old* [ˈhaʊ ʷˈəʊld] (GB), *two eggs* [ˈtuː ʷˈeɡz], *you are* [ju ʷə].
3. After the other vowels — /ə/, /ɑː/, /ɔː/, /ɜː/, /ɪə/, /eə/ — GB uses **/r/** ([[#rule-r]]). GA, which pronounces every written *r* anyway, simply runs the vowels together when there is no *r* in the spelling: *the idea of it* [ði aɪˈdiːə əv ɪt].
:::

::: proof
The glides are not extra phonemes added by the speaker; they are the natural result of moving from one vowel position to another without stopping the voice. At the end of /iː/ or /aɪ/ the tongue is high and front, which is the position of /j/; at the end of /uː/ or /aʊ/ the tongue is high and back and the lips are rounded, which is the position of /w/. As the voice moves on to the next vowel, it passes through these positions. This is why phoneticians often write the glides small, [ʲ] and [ʷ], or in brackets. The /j/ and /w/ glides are the same in GB and GA. Roach (2009) and Cruttenden (2014) both describe them; Cruttenden notes that they are weaker than the full consonants /j/ and /w/, so that *I earn* and *I yearn* stay different.
:::

::: quiz
Which glide joins the words in *Do you know Anna?* between *know* and *Anna*?
- [ ] /j/
- [x] /w/
- [ ] /r/ in GB only
- [ ] no glide: a glottal stop
::: solution
*Know* ends in /əʊ/ (GB) or /oʊ/ (GA), whose last part is high, back and rounded, so a /w/ glide forms: [ˈnəʊ ʷˈænə]. A glottal stop would make the words sound separated and emphatic.
:::
:::

## Linking /r/ and intrusive /r/

GB is non-rhotic: a written *r* after a vowel is not pronounced before a consonant or a pause ([[sounds-of-english/nasals-approximants#rule-r]]). But when the next word begins with a vowel, the /r/ comes back.

::: theorem Linking and intrusive /r/ in GB {#rule-r}
1. **Linking /r/.** A word spelled with final *r* or *re* is pronounced with /r/ when the next word begins with a vowel: *far* /fɑː/ but *far away* /ˌfɑːr əˈweɪ/; *more often* /ˈmɔːr ˈɒfn/; *here it is* /ˈhɪər ɪt ɪz/; *after all* /ˌɑːftər ˈɔːl/; *for example* /fər ɪɡˈzɑːmpl/.
2. **Intrusive /r/.** Many GB speakers also put /r/ between two vowels where there is no *r* in the spelling, after /ə/, /ɑː/, /ɔː/ and /ɪə/: *the idea of it* /ði aɪˈdɪər əv ɪt/; *law and order* /ˌlɔːr ən ˈɔːdə/; *China and Japan* /ˈtʃaɪnər ən dʒəˈpæn/; *Africa and Asia*. It also occurs inside words: *drawing* [ˈdrɔːrɪŋ].
3. **GA** pronounces /r/ wherever there is an *r* in the spelling, so linking /r/ is simply its normal /r/. Intrusive /r/ is not part of GA.
:::

::: proof
Linking and intrusive /r/ come from the history of GB. When /r/ was lost after vowels in south-eastern England in the eighteenth century, it was kept where a vowel followed, so pairs like *far* /fɑː/ and *far away* /fɑːr əˈweɪ/ appeared. Speakers then came to treat /r/ as a sound that appears between certain vowels, and extended it to words that never had an *r*: *idea of* now behaves exactly like *ear of*. Intrusive /r/ has long been criticised as "incorrect", especially when it is spelled out in writing, yet it is used in fluent speech by most GB speakers, including many who believe they do not use it (Wells 2008 in LPD; Cruttenden 2014). Learners using a GB model should use linking /r/; intrusive /r/ is optional — you need to recognise it, but you can link with a glide or with no glide instead.
:::

::: example Linking or intrusive? {#ex-r}
In GB, which of these phrases have a linking /r/, which have an intrusive /r/, and which have none? What happens in GA?

1. *Where is it?* 2. *Where did it go?* 3. *Asia and Europe* 4. *saw it* 5. *a pair of shoes* 6. *a sofa bed*
::: solution
1. **Linking /r/**: *where* is spelled with *r*, and *is* begins with a vowel: /ˈweər ɪz ɪt/. GA: /ˈwer ɪz ɪt/, its ordinary /r/.
2. **None**: *did* begins with a consonant: /ˈweə dɪd ɪt ˈɡəʊ/. GA: /ˈwer/.
3. **Intrusive /r/** (for many speakers): no *r* in *Asia*, but /ə/ + vowel: /ˈeɪʒər ən ˈjʊərəp/. GA: /ˈeɪʒə ən ˈjʊrəp/, no /r/.
4. **Intrusive /r/** (for many speakers): /ˈsɔːr ɪt/. GA: /ˈsɑː ɪt/ or /ˈsɔː ɪt/, no /r/.
5. **Linking /r/**: /ə ˈpeər əv ˈʃuːz/. GA: /ə ˈper əv ˈʃuːz/.
6. **None**: *bed* begins with a consonant: /ə ˈsəʊfə bed/.
:::
:::

::: warning Do not separate words with glottal stops
✗ *pick* [ʔ] *it* [ʔ] *up* — ✗ *I* [ʔ] *am* [ʔ] *an* [ʔ] *engineer* — with a tiny catch in the throat before each vowel.
✓ *pick‿it‿up* [ˈpɪ kɪ ˈtʌp] — ✓ *I‿am‿an‿engineer* [aɪ ʲəm ə ˌnendʒɪˈnɪə] (GB).

A glottal stop [ʔ] is a short closing of the vocal folds. English speakers do use it before a vowel — but mainly for emphasis (*It's [ʔ]↘**aw**ful!*) or after a pause. Before every vowel-initial word it makes speech sound jerky and over-careful, and it hides the weak forms, which need to lean on the words around them.
:::

## Two consonants

Linking also affects two consonants that meet at a word boundary.

::: theorem Consonant + consonant {#rule-cc}
1. **The same consonant twice** is said once, held a little longer — not twice with a break: *bus stop* [ˈbʌsːtɒp], *this side*, *big game*, *black cat*, *good day*, *some more*. For /p t k b d ɡ/ there is one closure, held longer, and one release.
2. **Two different stops** (*hot dog*, *good boy*, *that place*, *big cat*): the first stop is usually **not released** — the tongue or lips make the closure, but the air is not let out until the second stop: *hot dog* [ˈhɒt̚ dɒɡ] (GB).
3. **No vowel is added** between the consonants: *bus stop*, not ✗ [ˈbʌsə ˈstɒpə]; *good boy*, not ✗ [ˈɡʊdə ˈbɔɪ].
:::

::: proof
These rules follow from keeping the voice and the mouth moving: releasing every stop and starting the next one fresh would mean a small break at every boundary. Rule 2 is the same as inside words: in *doctor* and *actor* the /k/ is not released before the /t/. A released stop, with a little puff or a vowel after it, is one of the most common sources of extra syllables in learners' English ([[sounds-of-english/syllables-clusters#rule-practice]]). Elision and assimilation, the next chapter, describe what happens when the consonants at a boundary change or disappear.
:::

::: example Where do the words divide? {#ex-boundary}
These pairs sound very similar in connected speech. For each, say what (if anything) helps a listener to hear the difference, and how context decides when nothing does.

1. *ice cream* — *I scream*
2. *a name* — *an aim*
3. *grey tape* — *great ape*
::: solution
1. In *ice cream* the /k/ begins the stressed syllable *cream*, so it is aspirated [kʰ]; in *I scream* it follows /s/ inside *scream* and is unaspirated. The /aɪ/ is also shorter in *ice* (before /s/ in the same syllable) than in *I*. These cues are real but small.
2. Almost no difference: [ə ˈneɪm] for both. Some speakers make the /n/ slightly longer or add a glottal stop in *an aim* when they want to be clear. In practice, context decides (*What's your name?* — *a name*; *Our aim is …* — *an aim*).
3. In *great ape*, the /t/ ends *great*; in GB it may be glottalised or weakly released, and the /eɪ/ of *great* is shorter (before voiceless /t/). In *grey tape* the /t/ begins the stressed *tape* and is aspirated [tʰ]. In GA, *great ape* may have a tap [ˈɡreɪ ɾeɪp], while *grey tape* never does — a clearer cue.

English listeners usually resolve such ambiguities without noticing, because only one reading makes sense in context. For learners, the lesson is: when you hear a string of sounds, try more than one way of dividing it ([[connected-speech/hearing-fast-speech]]).
:::

::: example A linked dialogue {#ex-dialogue}
Mark the links (‿) in this dialogue and note the kind of each (C–V, /j/, /w/, /r/). Use GB.

**Liu Yang:** *Are you OK? You look a bit upset.*
**Sam:** *I'm all right. I just lost my umbrella on the way in.*
::: solution
**Liu Yang:** *Are you‿OK? You look‿a bit‿upset.*
- *Are you*: no link — in GB the *r* of weak *are* /ə/ is not pronounced before the consonant /j/. *you‿OK*: /u/ + /əʊ/ → /w/ glide [ju ʷəʊˈkeɪ].
- *look‿a*: C–V [ˈlʊ kə]; *bit‿upset*: C–V [bɪ tʌpˈset] (in GB often with a glottal stop for /t/ in casual speech, [bɪʔ ʌpˈset]).

**Sam:** *I'm‿all right. I just lost my‿umbrella‿on the way‿in.*
- *I'm‿all*: C–V [aɪ ˈmɔːl].
- *my‿umbrella*: /aɪ/ + /ʌ/ → /j/ glide [maɪ ʲʌmˈbrelə].
- *umbrella‿on*: /ə/ + /ɒ/ → intrusive /r/ for many GB speakers [ʌmˈbrelər ɒn]; a glide-less link is also fine.
- *way‿in*: /eɪ/ + /ɪ/ → /j/ glide [ˈweɪ ʲɪn].
- *just lost*: in casual speech the /t/ of *just* is usually dropped before /l/ — this is elision ([[connected-speech/elision-assimilation]]).
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Mandarin keeps syllables apart.** In Mandarin a syllable boundary is kept even when a consonant meets a vowel: *Xī'ān* 西安 is two syllables, *xi* + *an*, and the Pinyin apostrophe exists to show that it is not *xiān* 先. *Tiān'ānmén* 天安门 is not said *tiā-nān-mén*. English does the opposite: *an apple* is said *a-napple*. A Mandarin speaker's instinct to keep words apart therefore works against English linking.
- **The glottal stop.** Mandarin syllables with no initial consonant (*ān* 安, *ài* 爱, *ō* 噢) are often begun with a glottal stop or a similar catch in the throat. Learners carry this into English and say [ʔ] before every vowel-initial word (*pick [ʔ]it [ʔ]up*). Practise by moving the consonant: say *pi-ki-tup*, *a-napple*, *tur-noff*, then speed up.
- **Final consonants.** Mandarin syllables end only in a vowel, *-n*, *-ng* (or *-r*), so English final stops are a challenge ([[sounds-of-english/sounds-for-chinese-speakers#rule-finals]]). Linking actually helps here: a final consonant before a vowel moves into the next syllable, where it is easier to say. *Get‿up* is easier than *get* alone.
- **Linking /r/ and the Mandarin *-r*.** The Mandarin rhotic final (*-r* in *nǎr* 哪儿) is not the same as English linking /r/, but learners who use the *-r* suffix are used to an *r*-colour at the end of a syllable, which can help with GA final /r/.
- **Southern speakers** whose dialects merge /n/ and /l/ should take care with linked /n/: *in it* [ɪ nɪt] must not become [ɪ lɪt].
:::

::: history
The study of how word boundaries are marked in speech began with the American structuralists. George Trager and Henry Lee Smith, in *An Outline of English Structure* (1951), proposed a "plus juncture" phoneme to mark the boundary in pairs like *a name* and *an aim*. Ilse Lehiste (1960) made acoustic measurements of such pairs and showed that the boundary is signalled not by a special sound but by small differences in the allophones around it — aspiration, length, glottalisation. In British phonetics, linking and intrusive /r/ were described by Daniel Jones and A. C. Gimson; Gimson's *Introduction to the Pronunciation of English* (1962) and its later editions, now *Gimson's Pronunciation of English* (Cruttenden 2014), treat intrusive /r/ as a normal feature of the accent rather than an error.
:::

## Where this leads

Linking joins sounds; [[connected-speech/elision-assimilation]] describes how sounds at word boundaries also disappear and change. Hearing linked words as separate words is practised in [[connected-speech/hearing-fast-speech]]. Linking is also part of everyday fluency in [[listening-and-conversation/how-listening-works]] and of the differences between accents in [[listening-and-conversation/accents]].

::: summary
- **Linking** joins the last sound of one word to the first sound of the next, with no pause or glottal stop ([[#def-linking]]).
- A final consonant before a vowel moves on to it: *pick‿it‿up* [ˈpɪ kɪ ˈtʌp]; in GA, /t/ between vowels becomes a tap: *get‿up* [ɡe ˈɾʌp] ([[#rule-cv]]).
- Between vowels, a /j/ glide follows /iː/ and diphthongs ending in /ɪ/; a /w/ glide follows /uː/ and diphthongs ending in /ʊ/ ([[#rule-vv]]).
- GB uses **linking /r/** where the spelling has *r* (*far away*) and, for many speakers, **intrusive /r/** where it does not (*law and order*); GA has neither as a separate process ([[#rule-r]]).
- Two identical consonants are said once, held longer; the first of two stops is usually unreleased; no vowel is added ([[#rule-cc]]).
- Glottal stops before vowel-initial words make speech choppy; use them only for emphasis.
- Mandarin keeps syllables apart (*Xī'ān*); English joins them (*a‿napple*).
:::

::: solution
Problems: glottal stops before the vowel-initial words *asked*, *if*, *eat*, *it*, *all*; extra vowels after *ask* and *could*, which create extra syllables; the past-tense /t/ of *asked* is lost.

Model (GB): *I‿asked‿if‿I could‿eat‿it‿after‿all.* — [aɪ ʲˈɑːsk tɪ faɪ kʊ ˈdiː tɪ ˈtɑːftə ˈrɔːl]: a /j/ glide after *I*; C–V links in *asked‿if* (the /t/ moves on), *if‿I*, *could‿eat*, *eat‿it*, *it‿after*; a linking /r/ in *after‿all*. *Could* is often weak /kəd/: [kə ˈdiːt].
:::

::: exercise Link a short text {level=3}
Write three or four sentences about your morning (for example, what you ate and how you got to school or work). Mark all the links (C–V, /j/, /w/, GB /r/), then read the text aloud twice — once slowly, once at normal speed — keeping every link.
::: solution
Answers vary. A model: *I got‿up‿at seven‿and had‿an‿egg‿and some toast. Then I walked to the station‿in the rain‿and caught‿a train‿at‿eight.* Links: C–V in *got‿up*, *up‿at*, *seven‿and*, *had‿an*, *an‿egg*, *egg‿and*, *station‿in*, *rain‿and*, *caught‿a*, *train‿at*, *at‿eight*. A good answer finds every boundary where a consonant meets a vowel, and reads without glottal stops between them.
