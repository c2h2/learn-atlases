Two hotel receptionists say exactly the same words to a guest: *Good morning. How can I help you?* The first speaker's voice rises high on *mor-* and falls far down; on *help* it moves again, clearly. The second speaker's voice hardly moves at all — every syllable on almost the same pitch. Both are grammatical, both are correctly pronounced, and both use the "right" tones. Yet most English listeners would say that the first receptionist is friendly and the second is bored, tired or even rude.

The previous chapter, [[connected-speech/intonation-basics]], showed how the choice of tone — fall, rise, fall-rise — helps to mark statements and questions, lists and tags, and the end of a turn. This chapter is about the other main job of intonation in English: showing the speaker's **attitude** and feelings — interest, surprise, politeness, doubt, impatience. Much of this depends not on which tone is used but on **pitch range**: how far the voice moves. This is one of the areas where learners of English are most easily misjudged, because listeners react to intonation without noticing that they are doing so.

## Pitch range

::: definition Pitch range {#def-range}
A speaker's **pitch range** at any moment is the distance between the highest and lowest pitches they are using. A speaker can use a **wide** range (large movements, high peaks, low ends) or a **narrow** range (small movements, close to a middle pitch). Range is relative to each speaker's own voice: what matters is how much of it is being used.

Range can change from one tone unit to the next, and it can be changed on a single nucleus: a **high fall** starts near the top of the range, a **low fall** starts in the middle.
:::

::: theorem What pitch range suggests {#rule-range}
In English (both GB and GA):

1. A **wider** range than usual suggests **involvement**: interest, enthusiasm, surprise, warmth, or strong feeling of any kind — including anger. *That's a ˈgreat i↘**dea**!* with a high fall sounds enthusiastic.
2. A **narrower** range than usual suggests **lack of involvement**: routine, boredom, tiredness, sadness — or, if it is unexpected, unfriendliness. The same *That's a great i↘**dea**.* with a low, small fall can sound polite but unconvinced, or sarcastic.
3. Starting a tone unit **higher** than the one before marks something as **new, contrastive or surprising**; starting **lower** marks it as an addition or an aside: *I ˈmet ↘**Sam** — | (lower) you ˈknow, my ˈold ↘**neigh**bour.*

These are tendencies, not fixed meanings. A narrow range is normal in a formal announcement or a list of numbers, and it is not rude there.
:::

::: proof
Why should a wide range sound interested? One explanation, proposed by Carlos Gussenhoven (*The Phonology of Tone and Intonation*, 2004), is the "effort code": a speaker who puts more effort into speaking produces larger and more precise pitch movements, and listeners interpret that effort as importance or emotional involvement. Such links between pitch and meaning are found in many languages. But each language also has its own conventions about how much range is normal. Listeners judge a speaker against the norm of their own language, so a range that is normal in one language can sound flat — or exaggerated — in another. Cruttenden (2014) and Wells (2006) both stress that English uses a fairly wide range in friendly conversation, and that a narrow range in a situation that calls for warmth is heard as an attitude, not as an accent.
:::

::: example Same words, different range {#ex-range}
How would most English listeners hear each version of the reply? The tone is a fall each time.

**A:** *I've got the job!*
1. **B:** *That's ↘**won**derful.* (narrow range, low)
2. **B:** *That's ↘**won**derful!* (wide range, high fall)
3. **B:** *Oh, ↘**real**ly. | That's ↘**won**derful.* (both narrow, slow)
::: solution
1. Polite but not very interested — or, depending on the relationship, slightly sarcastic or jealous. The words say "wonderful", but the pitch does not.
2. Genuinely pleased and enthusiastic. This is what A hopes to hear.
3. Bored or even unfriendly: *Oh, really* with a narrow fall suggests "I'm not interested", and a narrow *That's wonderful* confirms it.

In each case the listener believes the pitch more than the words. This is why learners who speak with a narrow range can be misunderstood even when everything they say is correct.

## What the tones suggest

The tones of [[connected-speech/intonation-basics]] do not have fixed meanings, but each has typical effects that most descriptions agree on.

::: theorem Typical effects of the tones {#rule-attitude}
1. **Fall** ↘ — definite, complete, matter-of-fact. A **high fall** adds emphasis or emotion: *It's ↘**beau**tiful!*; a **low fall** is calm or detached.
2. **Low rise** ↗ — open, encouraging, non-committal; often friendly in greetings and routine exchanges: *Good ↗**mor**ning* (cheerful); *↗**Thanks*** (casual, for a small favour).
3. **High rise** ↗ (starting high, ending very high) — a request to repeat, or disbelief: *She's ↗**what**?* *↗**Sor**ry?* (GB, meaning "Pardon?").
4. **Fall-rise** ↘↗ — reservation, contrast or something implied: *It's ↘↗**good** …* (but not very good); a polite correction or partial agreement: *↘↗**Wed**nesday, | I think* (not Tuesday); a warning: *↘↗**Care**ful!*
5. **Rise-fall** ↗↘ — impressed, or strongly committed; it can also sound challenging or sarcastic: *↗↘**Love**ly!* (very impressed, or ironic); *Of ↗↘**course**.* (obviously).
:::

::: proof
These effects can be understood from the basic contrast between "finished" (fall) and "open" (rise). A fall closes the matter, so it sounds definite. A rise leaves it open for the listener, so it sounds less assertive — encouraging, tentative or questioning. The fall-rise begins as if closing the matter but then reopens it: the speaker says something and at the same time signals that there is more to it, which is why it so often implies "but …". The rise-fall is a fall with extra build-up, so it sounds more committed than a plain fall — impressed, or, if the content does not deserve that commitment, ironic. The fall-rise is especially frequent in GB, where it carries much of the work of politeness and implication; GA speakers use it too, but less often (Wells 2006).
:::

::: example *Thank you* and *Sorry* {#ex-thanks}
Describe the effect of each tone (GB).

1. *↘**Thank** you.* (high fall) 2. *↗**Thank** you.* (low rise) 3. *↘**Sor**ry.* 4. *↗**Sor**ry?* (high rise) 5. *↘↗**Sor**ry, | but …*
::: solution
1. A sincere thank-you, for something that matters: a gift, real help.
2. A routine acknowledgement — to a shop assistant handing you your change, or a bus driver. Not rude, but light. (Using it for a big favour sounds too casual.)
3. An apology: *I'm sorry.*
4. A request to repeat: *Pardon? What did you say?* (In GA, *Excuse me?* or *Sorry?* with a rise is used in the same way.)
5. A polite introduction to disagreement or a refusal: *↘↗Sorry, | but I don't ↘think that's right.*

In *thank you* the nucleus is on *thank*, and *you* is in the tail; learners who put the nucleus on *you* (*thank ↘**you***) sound as if they are thanking one person rather than another.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A colleague asks *Did you like the presentation?* and you answer *The ↘↗**slides** were good.* What does your colleague probably understand?
- [ ] You liked everything about the presentation.
- [x] You liked the slides, but not something else — perhaps the talk itself.
- [ ] You did not hear the question.
- [ ] You are asking whether the slides were good.
::: solution
The fall-rise on *slides* gives them a contrast: the slides were good — implying that something else was not. With a fall (*The ˈslides were ↘**good***) the answer would be a plain positive judgement. The fall-rise lets English speakers criticise politely, without saying the criticism.
:::
:::

## Politeness

::: theorem Intonation in requests, offers and commands {#rule-polite}
1. **Requests** and **offers** in question form usually sound more polite with a **rise**: *Could you ˈopen the ↗**win**dow?* *Would you ˈlike some ↗**more**?* With a fall, the same request can sound like an order: *Could you ˈopen the ↘**win**dow.*
2. **Imperatives** with a **fall** are commands: *ˈSit ↘**down**.* With a **fall-rise** or a **rise** they become invitations or gentle encouragement: *ˈSit ↘↗**down*** (please — make yourself comfortable); *ˈDon't ↗**wor**ry.*
3. **Short answers and responses** sound friendly with a wide range and abrupt with a narrow one: *↘**Yes**!* (high fall, pleased) vs *↘**Yes**.* (low and short, curt).
4. **Wh-questions** are made gentler with a rise: *ˈWhat's your ↗**name**?* (to a child or a nervous person) vs *ˈWhat's your ↘**name**?* (neutral — or, said slowly with a narrow range, like an official form).
:::

::: proof
In English, politeness is to a large extent a matter of giving the listener room: making a request sound like a real question that can be refused, or an instruction sound like a suggestion. A rise leaves the matter open, so it gives that room; a fall closes it, so it does not. Grammar does part of this work (*Could you …?* instead of *Open …*), but intonation can undo the effect of polite grammar. *Could you open the window* with a low, narrow fall is grammatically polite but is often heard as impatient.
:::

::: example Shop and office {#ex-polite}
Mark the tones that would make each utterance friendly and polite, and then the tones that would make it sound impatient (GB).

1. *Can I help you?* (a shop assistant)
2. *Take a seat.* (a receptionist)
3. *Next, please.* (at a counter)
::: solution
1. Friendly: *Can I ↗**help** you?* (wide range, rise). Impatient: *Can I ↘**help** you.* (narrow, low fall — it can even mean "What are you doing here?").
2. Friendly: *ˈTake a ↘↗**seat**.* (fall-rise) or *ˈTake a ↗**seat**.* Impatient or cold: *ˈTake a ↘**seat**.* (narrow, low).
3. Friendly: *↘**Next**, | ↗**please**.* (or a wide fall on *Next* with *please* rising). Impatient: *↘**Next**. | ↘**Please**.* (two short, low falls).
:::
:::

::: warning Correct words, wrong attitude
✗ *Could you ˈhelp me with ˈthis ↘**form**.* — said with a narrow range and a low fall: grammatically polite, but it sounds like an instruction.
✓ *Could you ˈhelp me with ˈthis ↗**form**?* — a wider range and a rise.

✗ *↘**Thanks**.* — short, low and flat after someone has spent an hour helping you: it can sound ungrateful.
✓ *↘**Thank** you ˈso ↘**much**!* — a wider range and a high fall.

English listeners are rarely aware that they are reacting to pitch. They do not think "that was a narrow fall"; they think "she's unfriendly". A learner who suspects this is happening should first try **widening the pitch range** on the important words.
:::

## Meanings are not one-to-one

It is tempting to make a list — "fall-rise = doubt", "rise-fall = surprise" — and learn it by heart. Most writers on English intonation warn against this. O'Connor and Arnold (1961) gave each of their tone patterns a list of attitudes, but later writers (among them Crystal 1969, Brazil 1985/1997, Cruttenden 2014 and Wells 2006) pointed out that the same tone can express different attitudes in different contexts, and that the attitude is often carried more by pitch range, loudness, speed, voice quality and facial expression than by the tone alone. *Oh, ↗↘**love**ly* can be sincere delight or bitter irony; only context decides. The safest generalisations are the broad ones in this chapter: falls close, rises open, fall-rises imply, and wide range shows involvement.

::: example An unfriendly-sounding learner {#ex-learner}
A Chinese student working in a London café is told by her manager that customers think she is unfriendly. She is surprised: she always says *Hello*, *What would you like?* and *Thank you*. A recording shows that she says every phrase with a short fall in a narrow range near the middle of her voice. What would you advise?
::: solution
The words are right, and the falls are not wrong in themselves. The problem is the **range**: narrow falls on greetings and thanks sound routine or uninterested to English listeners ([[#rule-range]]). Advice:

- Start greetings higher and use a wider fall, or a rise: *↘**Hel**lo!* (high fall) or *Hel↗**lo**.*
- Ask *What would you ↗**like**?* with a rise, or a wide fall starting high.
- Use a high fall on *↘**Thank** you* for customers, and keep the nucleus on *thank*.
- Practise with exaggeration: it will feel too much at first, but what feels exaggerated to the speaker usually sounds normal to the listener.

The cause is very probably not her "Chinese intonation" — Mandarin has large pitch movements on every syllable — but nervousness, concentration on correct words, and lack of practice with English pitch habits ([[#rule-range]] and the note below).
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Mandarin has a wide pitch range — on tones.** The four tones of Mandarin use the full pitch range of the voice, and focus and emotion in Mandarin also widen the range (Xu 1999). So a narrow range in a Chinese speaker's English is not a direct copy of Mandarin. More likely causes are concentration on words and grammar, reading aloud, nervousness, and the fact that in Mandarin the pitch is already "used up" by the lexical tones, so speakers are not used to moving it freely across a whole phrase. [[connected-speech/tones-and-intonation]] looks at this in more detail.
- **Particles do the work in Mandarin.** Much of the attitude that English shows with tone and range is shown in Mandarin by sentence-final particles: *a* 啊 (softening, liveliness), *ba* 吧 (suggestion, seeking agreement), *ne* 呢 (continuing interest), *ma* 嘛 (obviousness). A learner who has no particles in English must use pitch to do the same job.
- **Politeness forms.** Mandarin requests are made polite with words such as *qǐng* 请 and *máfan nǐ* 麻烦你. English also has *please* and *could you*, but, as this chapter shows, they do not guarantee politeness if the intonation is flat or falls low.
- **Cantonese speakers** use particles even more (*aa3*, *laa1*, *wo3* …), and the same advice applies: the attitude carried by the particle must move into the pitch of the English sentence.
:::

::: history
The idea that intonation expresses attitude is old, but its systematic description for learners began with J. D. O'Connor and G. F. Arnold's *Intonation of Colloquial English* (1961), which gave each tone pattern a set of attitudinal meanings illustrated in dialogues. David Crystal's *Prosodic Systems and Intonation in English* (1969) showed how pitch range, loudness, tempo and rhythm combine with tone, and was sceptical about fixed attitudinal labels. David Brazil, in *The Communicative Value of Intonation in English* (1985; Cambridge University Press edition 1997), developed **discourse intonation**, which explains tones by what the speaker treats as shared ("referring" tones such as the fall-rise) or new ("proclaiming" tones such as the fall), and treats pitch "key" — high, mid and low — as a separate system. Carlos Gussenhoven (2004) related the meanings of pitch to biological "codes" found across languages. Wells (2006) brought these ideas together in a practical description for learners.
:::

## Where this leads

How Mandarin tones influence English intonation — and how to practise wider English pitch movements — is the subject of [[connected-speech/tones-and-intonation]]. Using intonation to agree and disagree politely in conversation is developed in [[listening-and-conversation/opinions]], and asking someone to repeat in [[listening-and-conversation/clarification]].

::: summary
- **Pitch range** is how far the voice moves; English uses a fairly wide range in friendly conversation ([[#def-range]]).
- A **wide** range suggests involvement (interest, enthusiasm, surprise); a **narrow** one suggests routine, boredom or unfriendliness ([[#rule-range]]).
- Typical effects: **fall** definite, **low rise** open or friendly, **high rise** "Pardon?" or disbelief, **fall-rise** reservation or implication, **rise-fall** impressed or ironic ([[#rule-attitude]]).
- Requests and offers sound more polite with a rise; imperatives with a fall are commands, with a fall-rise invitations ([[#rule-polite]]).
- Tones do not have one-to-one meanings; context, range, loudness and speed all play a part.
- A narrow range in learners' English usually comes from concentration and nerves, not from Mandarin itself; widen the range on the important words.
:::

::: exercise Interested or not? {level=1}
Which reply sounds more interested?

1. (a) *ˈReally?* with a high rise. (b) *ˈReally.* with a low, narrow fall.
2. (a) *That's ↘**nice**.* low and short. (b) *That's ↘**nice**!* with a high fall.
::: solution
1. (a) — the high rise shows surprise and interest; (b) sounds bored. 2. (b) — the high fall and wide range show enthusiasm; (a) sounds routine or unimpressed.
:::

::: exercise Which tone? {level=1}
Choose the tone (fall, low rise, high rise, fall-rise or rise-fall) that best fits.

1. You did not hear what someone said: *Sorry?* 2. Warning someone about a wet floor: *Careful!* 3. Thanking a friend who drove you to the airport: *Thank you!* 4. You are very impressed by a friend's new flat: *Lovely!*
::: solution
1. High rise: *↗**Sor**ry?* 2. Fall-rise: *↘↗**Care**ful!* (a high fall is also possible if it is urgent). 3. Fall, high and wide: *↘**Thank** you!* 4. Rise-fall: *↗↘**Love**ly!* (or a high fall).
:::

::: exercise Implied meaning {level=1}
What is implied by the fall-rise in each?

1. *The ↘↗**food** was good.* 2. *I can ↘↗**try**.* 3. *↘↗**Some** of them are right.*
::: solution
1. The food was good — but something else (the service, the price) was not. 2. I can try — but I don't promise to succeed. 3. Some are right — but not all of them.
:::

::: exercise Polite or not? {level=2 check="2"}
How many of these four requests are likely to sound polite to an English listener?

(a) *Could you ˈpass the ↗**salt**?* (b) *Could you ˈpass the ↘**salt**.* (narrow, low fall) (c) *ˈPass the ↘**salt**.* (narrow, low fall) (d) *Would you ˈmind ˈpassing the ↗**salt**?*
::: solution
(a) and (d): requests in question form with a rise. (b) is grammatically polite, but the narrow, low fall makes it sound like an order; (c) is a plain command. The answer is 2.
:::

::: exercise Mark the attitude {level=2}
Mark the nucleus and choose the tone and range to express each attitude, using the words *It's ten o'clock.*

1. A neutral statement of the time. 2. Surprise — you thought it was much earlier. 3. Reproach — your teenage son has come home late. 4. Reservation — it's ten, but the shop may still be open.
::: solution
1. *It's ˈten o'↘**clock**.* — a normal fall, mid range. (*O'clock* is the last content word.)
2. *It's ˈten o'↗**clock**?* — a high rise, wide range. Or *It's ↘**ten** o'clock!* with a high fall on *ten*.
3. *It's ↘**ten** o'clock.* — a high fall on *ten* (the time is what matters), slow and deliberate. Or a rise-fall: *It's ↗↘**ten** o'clock.*
4. *It's ˈten o'↘↗**clock** …* — a fall-rise: but perhaps there's still time.
:::
:::

::: exercise GB and GA {level=2}
A British speaker says *I ↘↗**like** it* (fall-rise on *like*). How would you interpret it, and how might a GA speaker express the same meaning?
::: solution
The fall-rise implies a reservation: *I like it — but …* (perhaps "but it's too expensive", or "but I don't love it"). GA speakers also use the fall-rise this way, but often less frequently; they may use a rise or a level ending, add words (*I like it, but …*) or make the contrast explicit. The meaning is understood in both accents.
:::

::: exercise Rescue a cold conversation {level=3}
This exchange is grammatically perfect, but B sounds unfriendly. Mark B's lines again with tones and range that would sound warm, and explain your choices.

**A:** *Hi! I'm Kate, I'm new in the department.*
**B:** *↘Hello. | I'm ↘Wang Lei. | ↘Welcome.* (all low and narrow)
**A:** *Do you know where the printer is?*
**B:** *It's on the ↘second floor.* (low and narrow)
::: hint
Think about range first, then tone. Which of B's tone units could rise or fall-rise?
:::
::: solution
**B:** *↘**Hel**lo! | I'm ˈWang ↘**Lei**. | ↘↗**Wel**come!* — or *↘**Wel**come!* with a high fall. A high start and a wide fall on *Hello* show interest; a fall-rise or high fall on *Welcome* sounds warm.
**B:** *It's on the ↘**sec**ond floor* — with a high start and a wide fall; or friendlier still, *It's on the ↘**sec**ond floor | — I can ↗**show** you, if you like?* The main change is range: the same tones in a wider range already sound friendlier. The rise on the offer leaves A room to accept or refuse.
:::

::: exercise Record and compare {level=3}
Choose five everyday phrases (for example *Good morning*, *Thank you*, *Could you help me?*, *That's great*, *See you tomorrow*). Record yourself saying each in your normal way, then again with a deliberately wider range. Listen to both. Which sounds more natural to you, and which do you think an English listener would find friendlier? Write two or three sentences about what you notice.
::: solution
Answers vary. Most learners find that the wider version feels exaggerated when speaking but sounds natural when played back, and that the normal version sounds flatter than expected. A good answer notices where the nucleus is in each phrase (*Good ↘**mor**ning*, *↘**Thank** you*, *Could you ↗**help** me?*, *That's ↘**great***, *See you to↘**mor**row*) and whether the widening happened on the nucleus. If possible, compare the recording with a recording of a native speaker saying the same phrases.
