Chinese can stack several words before a noun: 我的这三本新书, 另外两个学生, 这么好的天气. A word-for-word translation produces ✗ *my these three new books*, ✗ *other two students* and ✗ *so good weather* — understandable, but not English. English has its own rules about which words can come before a noun, how many of them, and in what order: *these three new books of mine*, *the other two students* (or *another two students*), *such good weather*.

This lesson covers the determiners that have not yet had a lesson of their own: the **demonstratives** *this*, *that*, *these* and *those*; *other* and *another*; *such* and exclamative *what*; and, finally, the order of determiners when several come together. It builds on the articles ([[nouns-and-articles/a-and-the]]), the quantifiers ([[nouns-and-articles/quantifiers]]) and the possessives ([[nouns-and-articles/possessives-and-of]]).

## *This*, *that*, *these* and *those*

::: definition Demonstrative {#def-demonstrative}
The **demonstratives** are *this* and *that* (singular) and *these* and *those* (plural). They point to something and show whether it is **near** (*this*, *these*) or **further away** (*that*, *those*) — in space, in time, or in the conversation. They can be determiners (*this book*) or pronouns (*I like this*).
:::

| | near | further away |
|---|---|---|
| singular and uncountable | *this* /ðɪs/ | *that* /ðæt/ |
| plural | *these* /ðiːz/ | *those* /ðəʊz/ (GA /ðoʊz/) |

::: theorem Choosing *this* or *that* {#rule-this-that}
1. **Number.** *This* and *that* go with singular and uncountable nouns; *these* and *those* with plural nouns: *this chair*, *this water*, *these chairs*. *Kind* and *sort* follow the same rule: *this kind of book*, *these kinds of books*.
2. **Space.** *This*/*these* for things near the speaker; *that*/*those* for things further away, or near the listener: *This bag is mine — is that one yours?*
3. **Time.** *This* for the present or the time we are in (*this week*, *this morning*, *these days* ‘nowadays’); *that* for a time in the past or future that is not now (*that day*, *in those days*, *at that time*).
4. **Conversation and text.** *That* usually refers back to what has just been said, especially by the other person: *That's a great idea.* *This* can refer back to what the speaker has just said or forward to what is coming: *Listen to this.* *This is what we'll do: …*
5. **Feeling.** *That* can express distance or disapproval; *this* closeness or interest: *I don't like that man.* *I love this song!*
:::

::: proof
The basic meaning is distance from the speaker, and the other uses extend it: what is "now" is near in time, what is past or hypothetical is further away, what I am saying is "mine" and near, what you have said is "yours" and a little further. Swan (2016) and Quirk et al. (1985) give the same uses. Two conventions are worth knowing. On the telephone, British speakers traditionally use *this* for themselves and *that* for the other person (*Hello, this is Li Wei. Is that Anna?*), while American speakers use *this* for both (*Is this Anna?*). And in informal storytelling, *this* can introduce a new person or thing, like *a*: *There was this man on the bus, and he started singing.* Do not use this informal *this* in formal writing.
:::

- *Is **this** seat free?* (the one next to me)
- *Can you see **that** tower over there?*
- ***These** days most people pay by phone.* (nowadays)
- *I was a student in Wuhan then. **Those** were good years.*
- ***A:** Let's take the train. — **B:** **That**'s a good idea.*
- *Do you like **this kind of** music?* / *I don't like **these kinds of** films.*

::: warning *This* and *these*
✗ *I like this shoes.* ✓ *I like these shoes.*

✗ *These information is useful.* ✓ *This information is useful.*

✗ *This people are very kind.* ✓ *These people are very kind.*

✗ *Those kind of questions are difficult.* ✓ *That kind of question is difficult.* or *Those kinds of questions are difficult.* (In informal speech *those kind of* is common, but it is avoided in careful writing.)

There are two sources of this mistake. One is grammar: Chinese 这 works with any noun (这本书, 这些书), so the singular–plural choice is new. The other is pronunciation: *this* /ðɪs/ has a short vowel and *these* /ðiːz/ a long one ending in /z/, a difference many learners do not make in speech. Practise the pair aloud: *this shoe*, *these shoes*.
:::

::: example *This* or *that*? {#ex-this-that}
Choose and explain. (a) *(This / That) soup is delicious — try some!* (said while eating) (b) *Do you remember (this / that) restaurant in Xiamen where we ate oysters?* (c) *A: I've lost my passport. B: Oh no, (this / that)'s terrible.* (d) *Hello, (this / that) is Wang Fang speaking.* (e) *Who are (these / those) people outside the window?* (f) *(These / Those) days I work from home on Fridays.*
::: solution
Use [[#rule-this-that]]: ask whether the thing is near or far in space, in time, or in the conversation, and whether the noun is singular or plural.

(a) *This*: the soup is in front of the speaker.

(b) *that*: a past time and a distant place.

(c) *that*: refers back to what A has just said (rule 4).

(d) *this*: the speaker introducing herself on the phone (BrE and AmE).

(e) *those*: plural, further away (outside).

(f) *These*: plural, the present time, ‘nowadays’.
:::
:::

## *Other* and *another*

::: theorem *Other*, *another*, *the other*, *others* {#rule-other}
1. ***Another*** (one word: *an* + *other*) + singular countable noun = ‘one more’ or ‘a different one’: *Can I have another cup of tea?* *Let's meet another day.* With numbers and plural expressions of quantity, *another* means ‘more’: *another three days*, *another 20 minutes*.
2. ***Other*** + plural or uncountable noun = ‘different’ or ‘additional’: *other people*, *other ideas*, *other information*.
3. ***The other*** + singular = ‘the second of two’, ‘the remaining one’: *I've lost the other glove.* ***The other*** + plural = ‘the rest of a group’: *The other students have gone home.*
4. As **pronouns**: *another* (one more), *others* (more, different ones), *the other* (the second of two), *the others* (the rest). *Some people like it; others don't.* *Where are the others?*
5. *Other* as a determiner never takes *-s*: *other students*, not ✗ *others students*.
:::

::: proof
*Another* is the indefinite article *an* joined to *other*, so it has the grammar of *a*: it goes with a singular countable noun and means "one, not identified". *The other* has the grammar of *the*: the listener can identify which one, because only one (or one group) remains. *Other* without an article is like the zero article: plural or uncountable, not identified. The pattern *another* + number + plural (*another three days*) looks irregular, but the number phrase is treated as one amount; Swan (2016) and Huddleston & Pullum (2002) both note it. *Every other* means ‘alternate’: *every other day* = on Monday, Wednesday, Friday …
:::

| | not identified (‘a’) | identified (‘the’) |
|---|---|---|
| singular | *another book* / *another* | *the other book* / *the other* (one) |
| plural | *other books* / *others* | *the other books* / *the others* |

::: example *Other* or *another*? {#ex-other}
Complete with *another*, *other*, *others*, *the other* or *the others*. (a) *This shirt is too small. Do you have ___ one?* (b) *I have two brothers. One lives in Nanjing and ___ lives in Paris.* (c) *Some students walk to school; ___ take the bus.* (d) *Only Kenji came. ___ were ill.* (e) *We need ___ two chairs.* (f) *Are there any ___ questions?*
::: solution
For each gap, ask: singular or plural? With a noun or alone? Identified (the remaining ones) or not ([[#rule-other]])?

(a) *another*: one more, a different one, not identified.

(b) *the other*: the second of two brothers, identified.

(c) *others*: different students, not identified, used alone.

(d) *The others*: the rest of an identified group.

(e) *another*: with a number = ‘two more’. (*Two other chairs* is also correct, meaning two different chairs.)

(f) *other*: plural noun, ‘additional’.
:::
:::

::: warning *Another*, *other* and *others*
✗ *Can I have an other coffee?* ✓ *Can I have another coffee?*

✗ *We visited another cities.* ✓ *We visited other cities.*

✗ *The others students left early.* ✓ *The other students left early.*

✗ *I have two sisters. One is a nurse and other is a teacher.* ✓ *… and the other is a teacher.*

The Chinese words map loosely: 另一个 → *another*, 别的 / 其他的 → *other*, 另外那个 → *the other*, 其他人 → *the others* or *other people*. 另外两个 can be *another two* (two more) or *the other two* (the remaining two), depending on the meaning.
:::

## *Such*, *what*, *so* and *quite*

::: theorem *Such* and exclamative *what* {#rule-such-what}
1. ***Such a/an*** + (adjective) + singular countable noun: *It was such a nice day.* *She's such a good teacher.*
2. ***Such*** + (adjective) + plural or uncountable noun, with no article: *They're such kind people.* *We had such good weather.*
3. ***So*** goes with an adjective or adverb alone: *The day was so nice.* *She speaks so quickly.* (*So* + adjective + *a* + noun — *so nice a day* — exists but is formal.)
4. **Exclamations:** ***What a/an*** + (adjective) + singular countable noun: *What a beautiful view!* ***What*** + (adjective) + plural or uncountable: *What lovely flowers!* *What terrible weather!* ***How*** + adjective: *How beautiful!*
:::

::: proof
*Such* and exclamative *what* come before the article, so they are predeterminers (see [[#rule-det-order]]); this is why the order is *such a*, *what a*, never ✗ *a such*. The rule about *a* is the usual one: *a* only with singular countable nouns, so *such a day* but *such weather* (✗ *such a weather*, ✗ *what a weather*). *So* modifies adjectives and adverbs, not nouns, which explains ✗ *so good weather*. In both British and American English *quite* also comes before *a*: *quite a long time*, *quite a few people*; British English also uses *rather a* (*rather a long walk*), which in American English is less common.
:::

::: example *So* or *such*? {#ex-such-so}
Complete with *so*, *such* or *such a*, and explain. (a) *The film was ___ boring that I fell asleep.* (b) *It was ___ boring film that I fell asleep.* (c) *They have ___ beautiful garden!* (d) *I've never seen ___ beautiful flowers.* (e) *Why are you ___ late?* (f) *We had ___ fun at the party.*
::: solution
Look at what follows the gap: an adjective alone (*so*), or an adjective + noun (*such*, with *a* only before a singular countable noun) ([[#rule-such-what]]).

(a) *so*: adjective alone (*boring*).

(b) *such a*: adjective + singular countable noun (*boring film*).

(c) *such a*: singular countable (*beautiful garden*).

(d) *such*: plural (*flowers*), no article.

(e) *so*: adjective alone (*late*).

(f) *such*: *fun* is uncountable, so no *a* (✗ *such a fun*). (In informal American English, *fun* is also used as an adjective — *It was so fun* — but this is avoided in formal writing.)
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *It was so good weather that we went to the beach.*
- [ ] *What a lovely weather!*
- [x] *It was such good weather that we went to the beach.*
- [ ] *It was a such good day.*
::: solution
*Weather* is uncountable, so *such* (not *so*, not *such a*) and *What lovely weather!* (no *a*). *Such* comes before the article: *such a good day*, not ✗ *a such good day*.
:::
:::

## The order of determiners

::: definition Predeterminer, central determiner and postdeterminer {#def-det-slots}
Following Quirk et al. (1985), the determiners of English can be sorted into three positions before the noun:

- **predeterminers**, which come before the article: *all*, *both*, *half*, *double*, *twice*, *three times*, *such*, exclamative *what*, *quite*, *rather*;
- **central determiners**, of which a noun phrase can have only **one**: *a*/*an*, *the*, *this*, *that*, *these*, *those*, possessives (*my*, *Li Wei's*), *some*, *any*, *no*, *every*, *each*, *either*, *neither*, *which*, *whose*, *what*, *enough*;
- **postdeterminers**, which come after the central determiner: numbers (*two*, *first*, *next*, *last*), *other*, *many*, *few*, *little*, *several*, *same*.
:::

::: theorem The order of determiners {#rule-det-order}
1. The order is **predeterminer + central determiner + postdeterminer + adjectives + noun**: *all the other new students*, *both my older brothers*, *half the time*, *twice the price*, *my first two years*, *the next three stops*, *the many people who helped*, *these few days*.
2. Only **one central determiner** is possible: ✗ *the my book*, ✗ *this my friend*, ✗ *a this house*. To combine a demonstrative or *a* with a possessive, use *of* + possessive: *this book of mine*, *a friend of Wang Fang's* ([[nouns-and-articles/possessives-and-of#rule-double-genitive]]).
3. Among postdeterminers, **ordinals come before cardinals**: *the first three chapters*, *the last two days*, *the next five years*.
:::

::: proof
The positions are a description of what English actually allows, worked out by Quirk et al. (1985) and used in most teaching grammars; Huddleston & Pullum (2002) describe the same facts with different terms. The important point for learners is rule 2: the central slot holds one word, so *the*, *this* and *my* compete for it. Rule 3 follows from meaning: *the first three chapters* are chapters 1–3, which is what people usually want to say. *The three first chapters* is sometimes heard, but it is unusual and can sound like ‘the first chapters of three different books’. Some words belong to more than one slot: *many* is a postdeterminer in *my many friends* and a quantifier on its own in *many friends*; *every* can take a number after it (*every two weeks*, *every few minutes*).
:::

::: widget graph

nodes: predeterminer@0,2; central determiner@3,2; postdeterminer@6,2; adjectives@9,2; noun@12,2; all@0,0; the@3,0; other@6,0; new@9,0; students@12,0; both@0,-1.5; my@3,-1.5; older@9,-1.5; brothers@12,-1.5
edges: predeterminer>central determiner; central determiner>postdeterminer; postdeterminer>adjectives; adjectives>noun; all>the; the>other; other>new; new>students; both>my; my>older; older>brothers
caption: The order of words before a noun. Top row: the slots. Middle row: *all the other new students*. Bottom row: *both my older brothers*, with the postdeterminer slot left empty. Each slot can be empty, but the order cannot change, and the central slot holds only one word — *the*, *this*, *my*, *some*, *every* … — never two. You can drag the words.
:::

- ***All my** friends came.* (predeterminer + central)
- ***Both the** buses were late.* / ***Both** buses …* / ***Both of the** buses …*
- *She earns **twice the** salary I do.*
- *I spent **half the** day on the phone.* (BrE and AmE; also *half of the day*)
- ***The first two** weeks were difficult.*
- ***My many** visits to Xi'an …* (formal)
- *We need **another two** volunteers.*

Note *half an hour* (BrE, also AmE) and *a half hour* (AmE): the predeterminer *half* normally comes before *a*, but American English also allows *a half* before a noun of time.

::: example Putting determiners in order {#ex-order}
Put the words in the right order to make a noun phrase, and say which slot each word fills. (a) *students / all / other / the* (b) *my / both / sisters / older* (c) *two / the / weeks / first* (d) *the / price / double* (e) *few / the / days / last* (f) *book / this / mine / of / new*
::: solution
Use [[#rule-det-order]]: predeterminer + central + postdeterminer + adjective + noun.

(a) *all the other students*: *all* (pre), *the* (central), *other* (post).

(b) *both my older sisters*: *both* (pre), *my* (central), *older* (adjective).

(c) *the first two weeks*: *the* (central), *first* then *two* (post: ordinal before cardinal).

(d) *double the price*: *double* (pre), *the* (central).

(e) *the last few days*: *the* (central), *last* then *few* (post).

(f) *this new book of mine*: *this* is the central determiner, so the possessive moves after the noun with *of*.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which phrase is correct?
- [ ] *the my two cousins*
- [ ] *two my cousins*
- [x] *both my cousins*
- [ ] *my both cousins*
::: solution
*Both* is a predeterminer and comes before the central determiner *my*: *both my cousins*. *The my* has two central determiners. ‘Two of my cousins’ is *two of my cousins*, and ‘my two cousins’ (all of them) is *my two cousins*.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Demonstrative + classifier.** Chinese says 这本书, 这些书, 这三本书. English has no classifier and changes the demonstrative for number: *this book*, *these books*, *these three books*.
- **Possessive + demonstrative.** Chinese freely combines them: 我的这本书, 我这三本书. English allows only one central determiner, so the choice is *this book of mine*, *these three books of mine*, or simply *my three books* / *my book*.
- **别的, 另一个, 其他.** 另一个 is usually *another*; 别的 and 其他 before a plural noun are *other*; 其他的人 referring to the rest of a group is *the others* or *the other people*. 另外两个 is *another two* (two more) or *the other two* (the remaining two).
- **这么 / 那么 + adjective + noun.** 这么好的天气 is *such good weather*, 这么好的人 *such a good person* — not ✗ *so good weather*. 这么好 alone is *so good*.
- **Time.** 那时候 and 当时 are *at that time*, *then* or *in those days*; 这几天 is *these days* (nowadays) or *the last few days* (recently), depending on the meaning.
:::

::: history
English once had three demonstratives. Beside *this* and *that*, older English used *yon* and *yonder* for something far away and visible (‘that one over there’); they survive in Scots and some northern English dialects and in poetic language (*yonder hills*). The plurals have an odd history. In Old English the plural of *þes* ‘this’ was *þās*, and the plural of *þæt* ‘that’ was *þā*. In Middle English, *þās* developed into *those* and became the plural of *that*, while a new plural, *these*, was formed for *this*. So *those* historically comes from the old plural of ‘this’. The classification of determiners into predeterminers, central determiners and postdeterminers is modern: it was set out in the descriptive grammars of Randolph Quirk and his colleagues (1972, 1985), which aimed to describe the order of English words as speakers actually use them.
:::

## Where this leads

The words that come after the determiners — adjectives in order, nouns used as modifiers — and the phrases and clauses that follow the noun are the subject of [[nouns-and-articles/noun-phrases]]. *This* and *that* as pronouns that link sentences (*This shows that …*) are important in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]] and [[reading-skills/following-the-thread]]. *So* and *such* with *that*-clauses of result (*so tired that …*) are part of [[complex-sentences/adverbial-clauses]].

::: summary
- *This*/*these* for things near in space, time or the conversation; *that*/*those* for things further away, for the past, and for what the other person has just said ([[#rule-this-that]]).
- *This* and *that* go with singular and uncountable nouns, *these* and *those* with plurals: *this information*, *these people*, *this kind of book*.
- On the phone: BrE *This is Li Wei. Is that Anna?*; AmE *Is this Anna?*
- *Another* + singular (and + number: *another two days*); *other* + plural; *the other* = the remaining one(s); *others*, *the others* as pronouns ([[#rule-other]]).
- *Such a* + adjective + singular noun, *such* + adjective + plural/uncountable noun, *so* + adjective alone; *What a lovely day!*, *What lovely weather!* ([[#rule-such-what]]).
- Order: predeterminer (*all*, *both*, *half*) + one central determiner (*the*, *this*, *my*) + postdeterminer (*other*, *first*, *two*) + adjectives + noun; ordinals before cardinals ([[#rule-det-order]]).
- Only one central determiner: ✗ *this my book* → *this book of mine*.
:::
