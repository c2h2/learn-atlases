Compare two sentences that Chinese would translate in the same way, 我喜欢音乐:

- *I like music.*
- *I like the music.*

The first is about music in general — all kinds, everywhere. The second is about some particular music that both speakers can identify: the music in a film you have just watched, or the music playing in a café. In the same way, *Life is short* is a general truth, while ✗ *The life is short* sounds as if the speaker means a particular life and forgot to say whose. Chinese uses the same bare noun for both meanings, so learners who have just learned *the* often add it where English wants nothing at all.

This lesson is about that "nothing" — the **zero article** — and about **generic reference**, the ways English talks about a whole class: *Tigers are endangered*, *The tiger is endangered*, *A tiger is a large cat*. It also collects the fixed expressions in which a singular noun has no article (*go to school*, *by bus*, *at night*), with their British and American differences. It builds on [[nouns-and-articles/a-and-the]].

## No article: plural and uncountable nouns

::: definition Zero article {#def-zero}
A noun phrase has the **zero article** when a plural or uncountable noun stands without any determiner: *books*, *people*, *music*, *rice*. The zero article is indefinite, like *a*: it shows that the speaker does not expect the listener to identify particular things or a particular amount.
:::

Since *a* cannot go with plural or uncountable nouns ([[nouns-and-articles/a-and-the#def-articles]]), the zero article is what these nouns use where a singular countable noun would use *a*. So the choice for plural and uncountable nouns is between *the* and nothing.

::: theorem *The* or no article with plural and uncountable nouns {#rule-zero-the}
With a **plural** or **uncountable** noun:

1. use **no article** when you mean the thing **in general** — all of it, or that kind of thing: *Music helps me relax.* *Cats sleep a lot.* *Rice is grown in the south.*
2. use **no article** (or unstressed *some*) for an **indefinite amount or number**: *There are cats in the garden.* *We need milk.* *I've bought (some) apples.*
3. use ***the*** when you mean **particular** things or a particular amount that the listener can identify, for the reasons in [[nouns-and-articles/a-and-the#rule-the-uses]]: *The music in this film is beautiful.* *The cats next door are noisy.* *Where's the milk I bought?*
:::

::: proof
The rule is the same identifiability test as for singular nouns, applied to nouns that cannot take *a*. *The* says "you can tell which ones"; with general statements nothing is to be picked out, so no article is used. Swan (2016) and Quirk et al. (1985) both stress that English, unlike French, Spanish or Italian, does **not** use the definite article for general statements: French *J'aime la musique* but English *I like music*. *Some* and the zero article overlap in case 2; *some* suggests a limited amount (*I'd like some coffee* — a cup), while the zero article is vaguer and is preferred for habits and general facts (*I drink coffee every morning*). Quantifiers such as *some* are treated in [[nouns-and-articles/quantifiers]].
:::

- ***Children** learn languages quickly.* (children in general) — ***The children** are in the garden.* (our children)
- *I don't eat **meat**.* — ***The meat** in this dish is chicken.*
- ***Teachers** in this country work long hours.* (teachers here, in general) — ***The teachers** at my school are friendly.*
- *She's interested in **history** and **politics**.* — *She's writing a book on **the history** of Hangzhou.*

The last pair shows a useful tendency. An adjective in front does not usually make a general noun specific, but an *of*-phrase after it usually does:

| general (no article) | specified by *of* (*the*) |
|---|---|
| *Chinese history* | *the history of China* |
| *modern art* | *the art of the twentieth century* |
| *student life* | *the life of a student* |
| *English grammar* | *the grammar of English* |

::: warning *The* with general abstract and plural nouns
✗ *The life is not easy.* ✓ *Life is not easy.*

✗ *I'm very interested in the nature.* ✓ *I'm very interested in nature.*

✗ *The money can't buy the happiness.* ✓ *Money can't buy happiness.*

✗ *The smartphones have changed our lives.* ✓ *Smartphones have changed our lives.*

The sentences on the left are understandable, and *the* is right as soon as a particular case is meant: *the life of Lu Xun*, *the nature of the problem*, *the money in my wallet*. But for general statements, English uses no article with plural and uncountable nouns. Speakers of French, Spanish, German and many other languages make the same error, because their languages use the definite article here.
:::

::: example General or specific? {#ex-general-specific}
Complete with *the* or nothing (–), and explain. (a) *___ water boils at 100 °C.* (b) *Don't drink ___ water from that tap.* (c) *___ people who live upstairs are very quiet.* (d) *___ people usually sleep less as they get older.* (e) *I love ___ Italian food.* (f) *___ food at the party was delicious.*
::: solution
For each noun ask: is it plural or uncountable (so *a* is impossible)? Then: does it mean the thing in general, or particular things the listener can identify ([[#rule-zero-the]])?

(a) –: water in general. (b) *the*: the water that comes from that tap (identified by *from that tap*). (c) *The*: particular people, identified by *who live upstairs*. (d) –: people in general. (e) –: a general kind of food; the adjective *Italian* does not make it particular. (f) *The*: the food at one particular party.
:::

## Talking about a whole class: generic reference

::: definition Generic reference {#def-generic}
A noun phrase has **generic reference** when it refers to a whole class or kind of thing, not to particular members of it: *Tigers are endangered.* *A tiger is a large wild cat.* *The tiger lives in Asia.* A noun phrase that refers to particular things is **specific**: *The tiger in this zoo is old.*
:::

::: theorem Three forms of generic reference {#rule-generic}
English has three ways of talking about a class with a countable noun:

1. **Plural with no article** — the most common and the most neutral: *Tigers are endangered.* *Computers are cheaper than they used to be.*
2. ***A*/*an* + singular** — **any typical member**: used for definitions and for statements that are true of each individual: *A tiger is a large wild cat.* *A teacher needs patience.* It cannot be used for something true only of the class as a whole: ✗ *A tiger is endangered.*
3. ***The* + singular** — **the class as a type**, in formal, scientific and technical style, especially for animals and plants, inventions and machines, musical instruments and parts of the body: *The tiger is endangered.* *The telephone was invented in the nineteenth century.* *The heart pumps blood round the body.*

With uncountable nouns, generic reference uses no article: *Water boils at 100 °C.* *Gold is a soft metal.*
:::

::: proof
The three forms look at the class from different angles. The plural speaks of the members together, so it fits almost every general statement and is by far the most frequent in conversation (Biber et al. 1999). *A* picks out one example and invites the listener to generalise: "take any tiger — it is a large cat", which is why it suits definitions but not facts about the whole species, such as being endangered, extinct, widespread or invented, which are not true of any single animal or machine. *The* + singular treats the whole class as one identifiable thing, a type; this is formal, and with ordinary nouns of everyday life it can sound odd or textbook-like (*The student works hard* does not mean ‘students work hard’). Quirk et al. (1985), Huddleston & Pullum (2002) and Swan (2016) all describe these three forms and the same limits on *a*.
:::

| statement | *tigers* | *a tiger* | *the tiger* |
|---|---|---|---|
| definition: *… is/are a large wild cat* | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ (formal) |
| true of each one: *… can swim well* | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ (formal) |
| true only of the species: *… is/are endangered* | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ (formal) |
| everyday conversation | ✓ most natural | possible | sounds like a textbook |

::: theorem *The* + adjective and nationality words for groups of people {#rule-the-adjective}
1. ***The* + adjective** can refer to a group of people in general, with a **plural** verb: *the rich*, *the poor*, *the young*, *the elderly*, *the unemployed*, *the homeless*. *The young **are** often more optimistic.* Do not add *-s* (✗ *the poors*), and do not use it for one person (*a homeless man*, not ✗ *a homeless*).
2. **Nationality words in *-sh*, *-ch* and *-ese*** work the same way: *the British*, *the French*, *the Dutch*, *the Chinese*, *the Japanese*, *the Vietnamese* = the people of that nation as a whole. For one person, use a noun or *a … man/woman/person*: *a Chinese person*, *a Frenchwoman*, *a Briton* (formal).
3. **Other nationality words are ordinary nouns**, with a plural *-s*: *Americans*, *Canadians*, *Germans*, *Indians*, *Thais*. For the nation as a whole, both *Canadians* and *the Canadians* are used.
:::

::: proof
These expressions are generic, but they refer to people rather than to a type, so the verb is plural. Some of them have become sensitive: many writers now prefer *older people* to *the elderly*, *disabled people* to *the disabled*, and *people experiencing homelessness* in some official contexts, because the bare adjective can seem to reduce people to one characteristic. A group expression such as *the Chinese* or *the British* states a generalisation about a whole nation, and in careful writing it is often better to say *many Chinese people* or *people in Britain*, which are more accurate.
:::

::: example Choosing a generic form {#ex-generic-forms}
Which of the three generic forms ([[#rule-generic]]) can complete each sentence? Use the noun in brackets. (a) *___ was invented in China.* (*compass*) (b) *___ is a person who designs buildings.* (*architect*) (c) *___ are becoming more popular in cities.* (*electric bike*) (d) *___ has four strings.* (*violin*) (e) *___ are now extinct in the wild.* (*this kind of bird*, with the noun *bird*)
::: solution
Ask whether the statement is a definition, true of each member, or true only of the class as a whole.

(a) True only of the class (an invention): *The compass was invented in China.* (formal, most usual). The plural *Compasses were invented in China* is possible. ✗ *A compass was invented in China* would mean one particular compass.

(b) A definition: *An architect is a person who designs buildings.* is the usual dictionary style; *Architects are people who design buildings* is also correct. *The architect* would refer to a particular architect.

(c) True of the class as a whole (popularity is not a property of one bike): *Electric bikes are becoming more popular in cities.* *The electric bike is becoming more popular* is possible in formal style; *an electric bike* is not.

(d) True of each one: *The violin has four strings.* (instruments often take *the*), *A violin has four strings.*, and *Violins have four strings.* are all correct.

(e) Extinction is true only of the class: *Birds of this kind are now extinct in the wild.* — *a bird of this kind* is impossible. (With a species name, *the* is also usual: *The crested ibis was once nearly extinct.*)
:::
:::

## Singular nouns with no article: fixed expressions

A singular countable noun normally needs a determiner ([[nouns-and-articles/a-and-the]]). But in many fixed expressions it stands alone. Most of these nouns name places, times or means that are thought of as an activity or a function rather than a thing.

::: theorem Fixed expressions with no article {#rule-zero-fixed}
1. **Institutions used for their main purpose:** *go to school*, *be at school*, *in class*, *go to church*, *be in prison*, *go to bed*, *be at work*, *go home*, *be at home*. BrE *in hospital* / AmE *in the hospital*; BrE *at university* / AmE *in college*, *go to college*.
2. **Means of transport and communication with *by*:** *by bus*, *by train*, *by car*, *by bike*, *by plane*, *by sea*, *by email*, *by phone*, *by post* (BrE) / *by mail* (AmE). But *on foot*; and with other prepositions the article returns: *on the bus*, *in the car*, *on a plane*.
3. **Meals:** *have breakfast*, *at lunch*, *before dinner*, *What's for dinner?*
4. **Times of day and night, days, months and relative times:** *at night*, *at noon*, *at midnight*, *at dawn*, *on Monday*, *in July*, *in 2030*, *next week*, *last year*. But *in the morning*, *in the afternoon*, *in the evening*; seasons with or without *the*: *in (the) winter*.
5. **Sports, games, school subjects and languages:** *play football*, *play chess*, *study physics*, *speak Chinese*.
6. **Pairs and repetitions:** *arm in arm*, *face to face*, *day after day*, *from top to bottom*, *husband and wife*, *step by step*.
7. **A unique role after *be*, *become*, *as* or *elect*:** *She was elected president of the club.* *He works as head of department.*
:::

::: proof
These expressions are fixed by usage, but there is a shared idea behind most of them: the noun names a function, not a building or an object. *She's in hospital* (BrE) means she is a patient; *She's at the hospital* means she is at the building, perhaps visiting. *He's at school* means he is a pupil there or attending lessons; *I went to the school to meet his teacher* is about the building. *By bus* names a means of travel, not a bus; *I left my bag on the bus* is about one bus. When an adjective or another description makes the noun a particular thing, the article comes back: *have lunch* but *a quick lunch*, *the lunch was excellent*; *at night* but *a cold night*, *the night we met*. The British and American differences are in all the major grammars and learner's dictionaries (Swan 2016; Carter & McCarthy 2006): American English uses *the hospital*, and *college* rather than *university* for undergraduate study (*She's in college*), while *at university* is British. Note too BrE *in future* (‘from now on’) beside *in the future*, which American English uses in both meanings.
:::

| British English | American English |
|---|---|
| *She's in hospital.* | *She's in the hospital.* |
| *He's at university.* / *He goes to university.* | *He's in college.* / *He goes to college.* |
| *In future, please knock.* (‘from now on’) | *In the future, please knock.* |
| *I've got flu.* (also *the flu*, informal) | *I have the flu.* |
| *She plays the piano.* | *She plays the piano.* / *She plays piano.* |
| *go to the cinema* | *go to the movies* |

Illnesses vary. Most take no article (*cancer*, *diabetes*, *measles*), common minor ones take *a* (*a cold*, *a headache*, *a sore throat*, *a fever*), and *flu* takes no article or *the*, as in the table.

::: warning Adding *the* to fixed expressions
✗ *I go to the school by the bus.* (as a pupil) ✓ *I go to school by bus.*

✗ *We had the dinner at eight.* ✓ *We had dinner at eight.*

✗ *I'll call you at the night.* ✓ *I'll call you at night.* — but ✓ *I'll call you in the evening.*

✗ *She speaks the Chinese very well.* ✓ *She speaks Chinese very well.* (also ✓ *the Chinese language*)

✗ *I play the basketball every Friday.* ✓ *I play basketball every Friday.* — but instruments: ✓ *I play the guitar.*
:::

::: example Function or building? {#ex-institutions}
Complete with *the* or nothing (–) and explain. (a) *My little brother starts ___ school in September.* (b) *There's a meeting for parents at ___ school tonight.* (c) *Grandma has been in ___ hospital for a week.* (BrE) (d) *I'm going to ___ hospital to visit her.* (e) *Ali is at ___ university in Manchester.* (BrE) (f) *Is ___ university far from the station?*
::: solution
Ask whether the noun names the institution's function (being a pupil, a patient, a student) or a particular building or organisation ([[#rule-zero-fixed]]).

(a) –: he will be a pupil; *start school* is fixed. (b) *the*: the building, where parents (not pupils) go for a meeting. (c) –: she is a patient (BrE; AmE *in the hospital*). (d) *the*: the speaker is a visitor, going to the building. (e) –: he is a student there (BrE; AmE *in college* or *at the University of …*). (f) *the*: one particular university, as a place.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **One bare noun, several English forms.** A Chinese bare noun can be general, specific or indefinite: 狗是忠实的动物 (*Dogs are loyal animals*), 狗在院子里 (*The dog is in the garden*), 我想养狗 (*I'd like to have a dog*). English must choose between zero article, *the* and *a* each time. For general statements the usual answer is the plural with no article.
- **Generic sentences.** Chinese generic sentences usually use a bare noun as topic (老虎是猫科动物, 电话是十九世纪发明的). Learners translating them often write ✗ *Tiger is a kind of cat* (no article with a singular countable noun). The safest English choice is the plural: *Tigers are members of the cat family*; in formal writing *The tiger* is also possible.
- **Over-use of *the*.** Once learners know that English needs articles, many add *the* to general abstract and plural nouns (✗ *The education is important*), perhaps because *the* has been linked with 这/那 or because "every noun needs an article". The rule to remember is the opposite of that intuition: general plural and uncountable nouns take nothing.
- **Fixed expressions as chunks.** 坐公交车上学, 在家, 吃晚饭, 晚上 correspond to *go to school by bus*, *at home*, *have dinner*, *at night* — with no article — but 早上 is *in the morning*, with one. These are best learned as whole phrases, not worked out each time.
:::

::: example Correcting a short text {#ex-correct-text}
Correct the article mistakes in this paragraph from a student's essay, and explain each change.

*The pandas are one of the most famous animals in the world. Panda eats bamboo for about fourteen hours a day. In the past, the people believed that the pandas were in great danger, but the conservation has helped them, and the number of pandas in wild has increased.*
::: solution
Work sentence by sentence; for each noun ask: generic or specific? countable or uncountable? singular or plural?

1. *The pandas are …* → *Pandas are one of the most famous animals in the world.* General statement about the class: plural with no article ([[#rule-generic]], form 1). (*The panda is …* would also be correct in formal style.)
2. *Panda eats …* → *A panda eats bamboo …* or *Pandas eat bamboo …* (or formal *The panda eats …*). A singular countable noun cannot stand alone; here *a* is fine because eating bamboo is true of each panda.
3. *the people believed* → *people believed*: people in general.
4. *the pandas were in great danger* → *pandas were in great danger*: still general.
5. *the conservation has helped* → *conservation has helped*: an uncountable noun in a general sense ([[#rule-zero-the]]).
6. *in wild* → *in the wild*: a fixed expression with *the* (like *in the country*, *in the dark*).

*The number of pandas* is correct: *of pandas* identifies which number.

The corrected paragraph: *Pandas are one of the most famous animals in the world. A panda eats bamboo for about fourteen hours a day. In the past, people believed that pandas were in great danger, but conservation has helped them, and the number of pandas in the wild has increased.*
:::
:::

::: history
Generic sentences have puzzled logicians since Aristotle. *Birds fly* is true, although penguins and ostriches do not; *Mosquitoes carry malaria* is true, although most mosquitoes never do; and *Ducks lay eggs* is true, although only adult female ducks lay eggs — yet *Ducks are female* is false, even though there are more female ducks than egg-laying ones. So a generic sentence is not simply a statement about *all*, *most* or *some* members. In his doctoral dissertation *Reference to Kinds in English* (1977), the linguist Gregory Carlson proposed that a bare plural such as *birds* is like a name for a kind, and much later work on genericity, collected in *The Generic Book* (edited by Carlson and Francis Jeffry Pelletier, 1995), developed and debated that idea. For learners the practical lesson is simpler: English marks general statements by leaving the article out of plural and uncountable nouns, and keeps *a* for "any one" and *the* + singular for the formal language of types.
:::

## Where this leads

Names of people and places have their own conventions, including the zero article for most of them (*China*, *Lake Taihu*, *Mount Tai*): see [[nouns-and-articles/articles-with-names]]. *Some*, *any*, *much*, *many* and the other words that can stand where the zero article stands are in [[nouns-and-articles/quantifiers]]. General statements and how to qualify them (*Most tigers …*, *Tigers tend to …*) are important in academic writing: see [[academic-writing/academic-style]] and [[modality/hedging-boosting]].

::: summary
- Plural and uncountable nouns take **no article** when they mean the thing in general or an indefinite amount, and *the* when particular things are meant ([[#rule-zero-the]]).
- English does not use *the* for general statements: *Life is short*, *I love nature*, *Smartphones have changed our lives*.
- An adjective does not usually make a general noun particular (*Chinese history*), but an *of*-phrase usually does (*the history of China*).
- Generic reference has three forms: plural (most common), *a* + singular (any member — not for facts about the whole class, ✗ *A tiger is endangered*), and *the* + singular (the type, formal) ([[#rule-generic]]).
- *The* + adjective (*the young*, *the rich*) and nationality words in *-sh*, *-ch*, *-ese* (*the Chinese*) refer to groups with a plural verb ([[#rule-the-adjective]]).
- Many fixed expressions have no article: *go to school*, *by bus*, *have dinner*, *at night*, *play football* — but *in the morning*, *play the piano* ([[#rule-zero-fixed]]).
- British and American English differ: *in hospital* / *in the hospital*, *at university* / *in college*, *in future* / *in the future*, *flu* / *the flu*.
:::

::: solution
*Paper is one of the most important inventions in history. Paper was invented in China about two thousand years ago. Before paper, people wrote on bamboo and silk. Bamboo was heavy and silk was expensive, so the invention of paper changed education and government.*

The seven corrections: (1) *in the history* → *in history* (history in general); (2) *The paper was invented* → *Paper was invented* (the material in general, uncountable); (3) *the people* → *people*; (4) *the bamboo* → *bamboo*; (5) *the silk* → *silk*; (6) *the invention of the paper* → *the invention of paper* (*the invention* is right, identified by *of paper*, but *paper* is general); (7) *the education and the government* → *education and government* (general). (*Changed education and government* means ‘changed how education and government worked everywhere’; *changed the government* would mean one particular government.)
:::

::: exercise Describe an animal {level=3}
Write a short paragraph (six to eight sentences) about an animal you know well, for a reader of a nature magazine. Use at least one generic plural, one generic *a* in a definition, one formal generic *the*, one particular noun phrase with *the*, and one fixed expression with no article (for example *at night*, *in the wild*, *by day*).
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

*The giant salamander is the largest amphibian in the world. A salamander is an animal that lives both in water and on land, but giant salamanders spend almost all their time in cold mountain rivers. They hunt at night and hide under rocks by day. Adults can be more than a metre long. Like many river animals, they are threatened by pollution and loss of habitat. The salamanders in our local nature reserve are protected, and visitors can see them in a special pool near the entrance.*

Check: generic *the giant salamander* (formal); generic *a salamander* in a definition; generic plurals *giant salamanders*, *adults*, *river animals*; uncountable *pollution*; fixed expressions *at night*, *by day*; particular *the salamanders in our local nature reserve*, *the entrance*.
