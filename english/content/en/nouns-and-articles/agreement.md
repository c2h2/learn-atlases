A Chinese verb never changes for its subject: 他工作, 他们工作, 每个人都工作. An English verb in the present tense does: *he works*, *they work*, *everyone works*, and the verb *be* changes in the past too (*was*, *were*). The basic rule — add *-s* for a third person singular subject — was set out in [[core-grammar/present-simple#rule-agreement]], and *there is* / *there are* in [[core-grammar/be-and-have#def-there]]. Most learners can apply the rule in short sentences such as *My sister works in a bank*. The mistakes come in longer and less obvious subjects:

- ✗ *The price of the tickets have gone up.*
- ✗ *Everyone in my family like spicy food.*
- ✗ *A number of students has complained.*
- ✗ *The news are good.*

This lesson deals with these harder cases. The key, as in [[nouns-and-articles/noun-phrases]], is to find the head of the subject.

## Agreement with the head

::: definition Agreement {#def-agreement}
**Subject–verb agreement** means that the verb has the singular or plural form that matches its subject. It is visible only in a few places: the *-s* of the present simple (*works*/*work*), the present and past of *be* (*is*/*are*, *was*/*were*), and *has*/*have* and *does*/*do*. Modal verbs (*can*, *must*) and past tenses of other verbs (*worked*) have one form for all subjects.
:::

::: theorem The verb agrees with the head of the subject {#rule-head}
1. The verb agrees with the **head noun** of the subject noun phrase, not with a noun inside a postmodifier, even if that noun is closer to the verb: *The **price** of the tickets **has** gone up.* *The **students** in my class **are** from six countries.* *The **box** of old letters **was** in the attic.*
2. ***One of*** + plural noun takes a singular verb, because the head is *one*: *One of my friends **lives** in Chongqing.*
3. Phrases such as *as well as*, *together with*, *along with* and *like* do not make the subject plural: *The **teacher**, together with her students, **is** visiting the museum.*
4. The verb in a relative clause agrees with the noun the clause describes: *the **people** who **live** upstairs*; *a **student** who **speaks** five languages*. In *one of the **people** who **work** here*, the clause describes *people*, so the verb is plural in careful English; the singular (*who works*) is also heard.
:::

::: proof
The head is the noun that the whole subject is about (see [[nouns-and-articles/noun-phrases#def-noun-phrase]]): *the price of the tickets* is a price, so it takes *has*. When a plural noun stands next to the verb, speakers are often pulled towards a plural verb; grammarians call this **attraction** or **proximity** agreement. It is a common slip even among native speakers in speech, but it is treated as a mistake in writing (Quirk et al. 1985; Swan 2016). For *one of the people who work here*, Huddleston & Pullum (2002) and Swan (2016) both note that the plural is the grammatically expected form and that the singular is common; in formal writing, use the plural, or rewrite (*one of the people working here*).
:::

- *The **quality** of these photos **is** excellent.*
- *The **cost** of living in big cities **has** risen.*
- *The **results** of the survey **were** surprising.*
- *Each **one** of these apartments **has** a balcony.*
- *A **list** of the winners **is** on the website.*
- *The **manager**, as well as two of her assistants, **was** at the meeting.*

::: example Find the head {#ex-head}
Choose the verb and name the head noun. (a) *The keys to the front door (is / are) on the hook.* (b) *The noise from the trucks (keeps / keep) me awake.* (c) *One of the windows (was / were) broken.* (d) *The number on the doors (is / are) hard to read.* (e) *My brother, along with his two sons, (has / have) gone fishing.* (f) *Students who (lives / live) on campus get a discount.*
::: solution
Find the head of the subject and ignore nouns in phrases after it ([[#rule-head]]).

(a) *are*: head *keys* (plural); *door* is inside the postmodifier.

(b) *keeps*: head *noise* (uncountable); *trucks* is inside *from the trucks*.

(c) *was*: head *one*.

(d) *is*: head *number* (singular). (If each door has its own number: *The numbers on the doors are …*)

(e) *has*: head *brother*; *along with* does not add to the subject.

(f) *live*: the relative clause describes *students* (plural); the main verb *get* also agrees with *students*.
:::
:::

::: warning Plural nouns near the verb
✗ *The price of the tickets have gone up.* ✓ *The price of the tickets has gone up.*

✗ *The quality of the hotels in this area are poor.* ✓ *The quality … is poor.*

✗ *One of my classmates speak Korean.* ✓ *One of my classmates speaks Korean.*

✗ *Everyone in my family like spicy food.* ✓ *Everyone in my family likes spicy food.*

Before you write the verb, go back to the beginning of the subject and ask: what is this sentence about? A price, a quality, one person, everyone. Ignore the *of*-phrase and other postmodifiers.
:::

## *And*, *or* and words like *everyone*

::: theorem Coordinated subjects {#rule-coordination}
1. Two subjects joined by ***and*** usually take a plural verb: *My brother and my sister **live** in Dalian.*
2. But a singular verb is used when the two nouns are **one thing or one person**: *Fish and chips **is** a British dish.* *Bread and butter **was** all we had.* *My friend and colleague, Dr Lin, **has** just arrived.* (one person)
3. *Each* or *every* before nouns joined by *and* keeps the verb singular: *Every boy and girl **has** a seat.*
4. With ***or***, ***either … or*** and ***neither … nor***, the verb agrees with the **nearer** subject: *Either my parents or my brother **is** coming.* *Neither the teacher nor the students **were** ready.* When this sounds awkward, put the plural subject second.
:::

::: theorem *Everyone*, *each*, *none*, *neither* {#rule-indefinite}
1. ***Everyone***, ***everybody***, ***someone***, ***no one***, ***nobody***, ***everything***, ***nothing*** take a singular verb, though the pronoun that refers back to them is often *they*: *Everyone **has** their own opinion.*
2. ***Each*** and ***every*** + noun, and ***each of***, ***every one of*** + plural noun, take a singular verb: *Each room **has** a TV.* *Each of the rooms **has** a TV.* But when *each* comes after a plural subject, the verb is plural: *The rooms each **have** a TV.*
3. ***None of*** + plural noun: singular (more formal) or plural (more common in conversation), both correct: *None of my friends **is**/**are** coming.* With an uncountable noun, singular: *None of the money **was** stolen.*
4. ***Neither of*** and ***either of*** + plural noun: singular in formal writing, often plural in informal speech: *Neither of the answers **is**/**are** correct.*
:::

::: proof
*Everyone* refers to many people but is grammatically singular, as the basic rule in [[core-grammar/present-simple#rule-agreement]] showed; the plural *they* that follows it is not a contradiction but the normal English way of referring back to a person of unknown sex (see [[core-grammar/nouns-pronouns#rule-he-she]]). The words in rules 3 and 4 are where **grammatical** agreement (the form of the word) and **notional** agreement (the idea of number) pull in different directions. *None* comes historically from *no one*, and many people were taught that it must be singular, but the plural has been used for centuries and both reference grammars and usage guides accept it (Quirk et al. 1985; Swan 2016). In an exam or formal writing, the singular is the safer choice for *neither of* and *either of*; for *none of*, both are safe.
:::

::: example *And*, *or*, *each* and *none* {#ex-coordination}
Choose the verb and explain. (a) *Salt and pepper (is / are) on the table.* (b) *Rice and beans (is / are) a popular dish in Brazil.* (c) *Neither my sister nor my parents (eats / eat) meat.* (d) *Each of the students (gets / get) a certificate.* (e) *None of the information (was / were) new.* (f) *Everybody in the two classes (passes / pass) the test.*
::: solution
Decide whether the subject is two things or one, which subject is nearer, or what the indefinite word requires ([[#rule-coordination]], [[#rule-indefinite]]).

(a) *are*: two separate things (two containers).

(b) *is*: one dish (rule 2 of [[#rule-coordination]]). *Are* is possible if you think of the two foods separately, but *a popular dish* makes the singular natural.

(c) *eat*: nearer subject *my parents* (plural).

(d) *gets*: *each of* + plural = singular.

(e) *was*: *none of* + uncountable = singular.

(f) *passes*: *everybody* is singular, however many people there are.
:::
:::

## Numbers, amounts and parts

::: theorem Quantities {#rule-quantities}
1. ***A number of*** + plural noun = ‘several, many’, plural verb: *A number of students **have** complained.* ***The number of*** + plural noun = the figure itself, singular verb: *The number of students **has** risen.*
2. With ***a lot of***, ***lots of***, ***plenty of***, ***most of***, ***some of***, ***all of***, ***half of***, ***the rest of***, ***the majority of***, fractions (*two thirds of*) and percentages (*40 per cent of*), the verb agrees with the noun after *of*: *A lot of **people** **are** …*, *A lot of **money** **is** …*; *Half of the **cake** **has** gone*, *Half of the **guests** **have** gone*; *60% of the **students** **are** women.*
3. An **amount** of money, time, distance or weight seen as one quantity takes a singular verb, even though the noun is plural: *Ten dollars **is** too much.* *Three weeks **is** a long time.* *Five kilometres **is** a long way to walk.*
4. ***More than one*** + singular noun takes a singular verb: *More than one student **has** asked.*
:::

::: proof
In all these cases the meaning decides. *A number of* has become a quantifier like *several*, so the head of *a number of students* is in effect *students*; *the number* is a figure. In *a lot of people* and *half of the guests*, the important noun is the one after *of*, and the verb follows it — the reverse of the *price of the tickets* pattern in [[#rule-head]], which is why the two must be learned together. Swan (2016) and Biber et al. (1999) describe the same behaviour. In rule 3, the plural form names a single sum: we pay *ten dollars*, we wait *three weeks*, as one amount. With *the majority of* + plural, the plural verb is usual; with *the majority* alone, British English allows both (*The majority **is**/**are** in favour*). After a percentage + collective or uncountable noun the verb is usually singular, though British English also allows the plural with collectives: *40% of the population **lives** in cities.*
:::

::: example Amounts and parts {#ex-quantities}
Choose the verb. (a) *A number of flights (has / have) been cancelled.* (b) *The number of flights (has / have) doubled.* (c) *Two thirds of the land (is / are) desert.* (d) *Two thirds of the workers (is / are) under 30.* (e) *Twenty minutes (is / are) enough for lunch.* (f) *Most of the furniture (was / were) old.*
::: solution
Ask whether the verb follows the noun after *of*, the figure itself, or a single amount ([[#rule-quantities]]).

(a) *have*: *a number of* = several flights.

(b) *has*: *the number* is the figure.

(c) *is*: agrees with *land* (uncountable).

(d) *are*: agrees with *workers* (plural).

(e) *is*: one period of time.

(f) *was*: agrees with *furniture* (uncountable; see [[nouns-and-articles/countable-uncountable#rule-uncountable-list]]).
:::
:::

## Collective nouns and other special nouns

::: theorem Collective nouns: British and American {#rule-collective}
1. A **collective noun** names a group: *team*, *family*, *government*, *staff*, *committee*, *class*, *audience*, *company*, *band*, *jury*.
2. In **American English** it usually takes a singular verb: *The team **is** playing well.* *The government **has** decided.*
3. In **British English** it can take a singular verb (the group as one unit) or a plural verb (the members as individuals): *The team **is**/**are** playing well.* *The government **has**/**have** decided.* The plural is more common in speech, and especially with sports teams named after a place: *England **are** two goals down.* *Arsenal **have** signed a new player.*
4. Whichever you choose, **be consistent** within the sentence: *The committee **has** made **its** decision* or *The committee **have** made **their** decision*, not ✗ *The committee has made their decision* in careful writing.
:::

::: proof
The choice reflects whether the speaker thinks of the group as a whole or as a set of people (notional agreement). Corpus studies have found that the plural verb is much more frequent in British than in American English, and more frequent in British speech than in British newspapers (Biber et al. 1999). American English does use plural verbs with team names that are plural in form: *The Lakers **are** …*. With *family*, *staff* and *couple*, both varieties use plural verbs when individual actions are meant: *The staff **are** all on holiday* (BrE), *The couple **were** married in 1990*. For learners, the simplest choice is the singular, which is correct everywhere; but recognise the plural in British texts as correct, not a mistake.
:::

| always plural | singular, though they end in *-s* | either |
|---|---|---|
| *police*, *people*, *cattle* | *news*, *physics*, *mathematics*, *economics* | *data*, *media* |
| *The police **are** investigating.* | *The news **is** good.* | *The data **is**/**are** clear.* |
| *People **say** …* | *Physics **is** hard.* | *The media **has**/**have** …* |
| *clothes*, *trousers*, *scissors* | *the United States*, *the Philippines* (country) | *politics* (see below) |

*Politics* is singular as a subject of study (*Politics **is** …*) and often plural when it means someone's political views (*His politics **are** quite conservative*); see [[nouns-and-articles/countable-uncountable#rule-plural-only]]. *Data* is plural in Latin, and the plural (*these data show*) is common in scientific writing; in everyday and much professional English, it is treated as uncountable (*this data shows*). Both are correct; follow the style of your field.

Finally, a **clause** as subject — an *-ing* clause, a *to*-infinitive, a *that*-clause or a *what*-clause — takes a singular verb: *Learning languages **takes** time.* *To win both games **was** a surprise.* *That he resigned **doesn't** surprise me.* *What we need **is** more time.* With a *what*-clause before a plural noun, the plural is also used: *What we need **are** more volunteers.*

Say whether each sentence is acceptable in British English, American English, or both. (a) *The government are planning new taxes.* (b) *The government is planning new taxes.* (c) *My family are all doctors.* (d) *The police is coming.* (e) *Real Madrid have won again.* (f) *The audience was enormous.*
::: solution
Use [[#rule-collective]] and the table of special nouns.

(a) BrE: the members of the government seen as individuals. In AmE the singular is normal.

(b) Both: the singular is correct everywhere.

(c) Mainly BrE. *All* and *doctors* make the plural natural, since each member is a doctor; AmE usually rephrases: *Everyone in my family is a doctor.*

(d) Neither: *police* is always plural: *The police are coming.*

(e) BrE (a team named after a place, plural verb); AmE would usually say *Real Madrid has won again*.

(f) Both: one large group, so the singular.

::: note English and Chinese
- **No agreement in Chinese.** Chinese verbs do not change for person or number, and Chinese nouns usually do not show number either: 学生 can be one student or many, and 们 is optional and is not used after a number (✗ 三个学生们) (Li & Thompson 1981). An English verb therefore carries information Chinese does not mark, and a mistake in it is noticeable.
- **都 is not a plural signal.** 每个人都喜欢 contains 都, which suggests "all", but *everyone* is singular: *Everyone likes it.* Similarly 我们班每个人都有 is *Everyone in our class has …*.
- **The head comes first in English.** In Chinese the head is at the end of the noun phrase (票的价格 — 价格 last, next to the verb: 票的价格涨了). In English the head can be far from the verb (*the price of the tickets … has*), so the plural noun next to the verb is a trap that does not exist in Chinese.
- **Amounts.** 十块钱太贵了 — Chinese treats the amount as one thing, and so does English: *Ten yuan is too much.* Here Chinese intuition helps.
- **Collective nouns.** Chinese 政府, 球队, 家人 do not raise the question at all. Use the singular (correct in all varieties) and recognise the British plural when you read it.
:::

::: history
English verbs once had more agreement than they do now. Old English had separate forms for singular and plural, and the present plural ended in *-aþ* (*hīe singaþ* ‘they sing’). In medieval northern English and Scots a different system developed, still found in some northern dialects today and known to linguists as the **Northern Subject Rule**: the verb took *-s* with any subject except a personal pronoun right next to it, so speakers said *they sing* but *the birds sings*. Early Modern English writers were also freer than modern grammar books allow; in Shakespeare's *Julius Caesar*, Cassius says of Brutus, "Three parts of him / Is ours already", with a singular verb for a plural amount. The idea that agreement should follow strict grammatical form, with fixed rules for *none*, *either* and collective nouns, grew in the school grammars of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries; modern descriptive grammars (Quirk et al. 1985; Huddleston & Pullum 2002) describe instead how grammatical and notional agreement interact, and record British and American practice as it is.
:::

## Where this leads

Agreement with *there is* and *there are*, including the informal *there's* before a plural, is in [[core-grammar/be-and-have#def-there]]. Pronouns that refer back to *everyone* and to collective nouns (*their*, *its*) are part of keeping a text consistent, a topic of [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]]. Checking agreement in long sentences is one of the steps in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/editing]]. Differences between British and American English in general are surveyed in [[world-englishes/british-american]].

::: summary
- The verb agrees with the **head** of the subject, not the nearest noun: *The price of the tickets **has** gone up*; *One of my friends **lives** …* ([[#rule-head]]).
- *X and Y* is plural, unless it is one thing (*Fish and chips is*); with *or* and *neither … nor*, the nearer subject decides ([[#rule-coordination]]).
- *Everyone*, *each*, *every* take a singular verb; *none of* and *neither of* + plural can take either, the singular being more formal ([[#rule-indefinite]]).
- *A number of* + plural verb, *the number of* + singular; with *a lot of*, *half of*, *most of*, percentages, follow the noun after *of*; amounts are singular (*Ten dollars is …*) ([[#rule-quantities]]).
- Collective nouns: AmE singular (*the team is*); BrE singular or plural (*the team is/are*, *England are*) — be consistent ([[#rule-collective]]).
- Always plural: *police*, *people*; singular: *news*, *physics*, *the United States*, clauses as subjects (*Learning languages takes time*).
:::
