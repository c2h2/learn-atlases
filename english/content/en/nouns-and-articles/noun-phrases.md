In Chinese, everything that describes a noun comes before it, usually joined by 的: 我昨天在书店买的那本书, 坐在窗边的那个穿红衣服的女孩. However long the description, the noun comes last. English does it differently. Short descriptions go before the noun, but anything longer than a word or two — a phrase, a clause — goes **after** it:

- 我昨天在书店买的那本书 → *the book I bought at the bookshop yesterday*
- 坐在窗边的那个穿红衣服的女孩 → *the girl in the red coat sitting by the window*

This is one of the biggest structural differences between the two languages, and one of the main reasons why English written by Chinese speakers can feel heavy or hard to follow at the start of a sentence. This lesson shows how an English noun phrase is built: the words before the noun, the noun itself, and the words after it. It builds on the determiners ([[nouns-and-articles/other-determiners]]) and on the noun + noun structure ([[nouns-and-articles/possessives-and-of#rule-noun-noun]]). The basic order of adjectives (*a beautiful old house*) was set out in [[core-grammar/adjectives-adverbs]] and is not repeated here.

## The parts of a noun phrase

::: definition Noun phrase, head and modifier {#def-noun-phrase}
A **noun phrase** is a group of words built around a noun, its **head**. The other words describe or identify the head; they are its **modifiers**. A full noun phrase has four parts, in this order:

1. **determiners**: *the*, *a*, *my*, *all*, *these two* …;
2. **premodifiers**: adjectives and nouns before the head: *new*, *leather*, *running*;
3. the **head** noun: *shoes*;
4. **postmodifiers**: phrases and clauses after the head: *in the window*, *that I bought last week*.
:::

All four appear in *all the other new students from Japan who arrived yesterday*:

| determiners | premodifiers | head | postmodifiers |
|---|---|---|---|
| *all the other* | *new* | *students* | *from Japan who arrived yesterday* |
| *my* | *old leather* | *bag* | — |
| *a* | — | *book* | *about pandas* |
| — | *Chinese* | *students* | *studying abroad* |

Only the head is required; any of the other parts can be empty. The head decides the grammar of the whole phrase: in *the box of chocolates is on the table*, the head is *box*, so the verb is singular. This matters for subject–verb agreement, the subject of [[nouns-and-articles/agreement]].

## Before the noun

The premodifiers are mostly adjectives, in the order described in [[core-grammar/adjectives-adverbs#rule-adj-position]], and nouns used as modifiers.

::: theorem Nouns before nouns {#rule-modifier-noun}
1. A noun used as a modifier is usually **singular**, even when the meaning is plural: *a shoe shop* (it sells many shoes), *a book club*, *a toothbrush*, *a ticket office*.
2. This includes measurements with a number, which are joined with hyphens: *a ten-year-old girl*, *a five-minute break*, *a two-hour flight*, *a 20-dollar note* (AmE *bill*). Compare *The girl is ten years old* and *The flight takes two hours*, where the noun is plural because it is not a modifier.
3. A few modifier nouns are plural, usually because the singular has a different meaning or does not exist: *a sports car*, *a clothes shop*, *a sales manager*, *a savings account*, *the arms trade*, *a goods train* (BrE; AmE *freight train*). British English uses plural modifiers a little more often than American: BrE *a drugs problem*, AmE more often *a drug problem*.
4. Adjectives come **before** modifier nouns: *a new leather bag* (✗ *a leather new bag*), *an expensive Japanese sports car*, *a small plastic toothbrush holder*.
:::

::: proof
A modifier noun works like an adjective: it classifies the head (what kind of shop?), and English adjectives have no plural, so the modifier noun loses its plural too. The measurement phrases in rule 2 show this clearly: *ten years* becomes *ten-year-old*, a single compound adjective, and like any adjective it does not take *-s* (✗ *a ten-years-old girl*) (Swan 2016; Quirk et al. 1985). The plural exceptions are fixed expressions, and dictionaries list them; they cannot be predicted from a rule. Rule 4 follows the general principle of the adjective order in [[core-grammar/adjectives-adverbs]]: the closer to the head, the more permanent and defining the feature, and the modifier noun, which names the type, is the closest of all.
:::

In speech, a noun + noun compound is usually stressed on the **first** noun: *a ˈcoffee cup*, *a ˈshoe shop*, *the ˈcar park*. An adjective + noun phrase is usually stressed on the noun: *a hot ˈcup*, *a new ˈshop*. This is how listeners tell *a ˈgreenhouse* (a glass building for plants) from *a green ˈhouse* (a house that is green). Stress is covered in [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics]].

::: quiz
Which phrase is correct?
- [ ] *a two-weeks holiday*
- [x] *a two-week holiday*
- [ ] *a two weeks' holiday's*
- [ ] *a holiday two-week*
::: solution
A measurement used as a modifier is hyphenated and singular: *a two-week holiday* (AmE *vacation*). *Two weeks' holiday* (without *a*) is also correct, using the possessive of time from [[nouns-and-articles/possessives-and-of#rule-s-or-of]].
:::
:::

::: example Nouns as modifiers {#ex-modifiers}
Make one noun phrase with a modifier noun. (a) a shop that sells books (b) a girl who is twelve years old (c) a break of ten minutes (d) a room where you can wait (e) a car built for sport (f) a bag made of leather, which is new
::: solution
The modifier noun goes directly before the head and is normally singular; adjectives come before it ([[#rule-modifier-noun]]).

(a) *a bookshop* (AmE *a bookstore*): singular *book*, one word.

(b) *a twelve-year-old girl*: hyphens, and *year* without *-s*.

(c) *a ten-minute break*: as (b).

(d) *a waiting room*: an *-ing* form used as a modifier noun (‘a room for waiting’), stressed on *waiting*.

(e) *a sports car*: one of the fixed plural exceptions.

(f) *a new leather bag*: the adjective *new* before the modifier noun *leather*.
:::
:::

::: warning Too many words before the noun
✗ *a ten-years-old boy* ✓ *a ten-year-old boy*

✗ *a books shop* ✓ *a bookshop* (AmE *bookstore*)

✗ *a full of water bottle* ✓ *a bottle full of water*

✗ *the interested in art students* ✓ *the students interested in art*

✗ *my yesterday bought shoes* ✓ *the shoes I bought yesterday*

An adjective or a noun alone can go before the head; an adjective with words after it (*full of water*, *interested in art*), a time or place phrase, or a clause goes after the head ([[#rule-postmodifiers]]). Strings of three or more modifier nouns (*city council housing policy review*) are common in headlines and official writing but hard to read; in your own writing, unpack them with *of* or *for*: *a review of the city council's housing policy*.
:::

## After the noun

::: theorem What can follow the noun {#rule-postmodifiers}
The main postmodifiers, from shortest to longest:

1. a **prepositional phrase**: *the girl **in the red coat***, *a book **about pandas***, *the road **to the airport***, *people **from all over the world***;
2. an **adjective with words after it**: *a bag **full of books***, *students **interested in art***, *a country **rich in oil***;
3. an ***-ing* participle phrase** (active meaning): *the man **standing at the door***, *students **living on campus***;
4. an ***-ed* participle phrase** (passive meaning): *the photos **taken in Tibet***, *a language **spoken by millions***;
5. a ***to*-infinitive**: *a good place **to eat***, *the first person **to arrive***, *nothing **to do***;
6. a **relative clause**: *the book **(that) I bought yesterday***, *the people **who live upstairs***, *the town **where I grew up***.
:::

::: proof
English follows a general principle that heavy material comes at the end — the principle of **end weight** (Quirk et al. 1985). Inside a noun phrase, this means that the head comes early, so the listener knows what the phrase is about, and the long description follows. Postmodifiers are especially frequent in written English: Biber et al. (1999) found that prepositional phrases are by far the most common postmodifiers, and that noun phrases with postmodifiers are much more common in news and academic writing than in conversation, where most noun phrases are short (often just a pronoun). Participle phrases (3, 4) are reduced relative clauses: *the man standing at the door* = *the man who is standing at the door*. Relative clauses are covered in detail in [[complex-sentences/relative-clauses]], and participle and infinitive clauses in [[complex-sentences/participle-infinitive-clauses]].
:::

::: example Kinds of postmodifier {#ex-postmod-types}
Name the postmodifier in each phrase (prepositional phrase, adjective phrase, *-ing* phrase, *-ed* phrase, *to*-infinitive or relative clause). (a) *a letter written in 1937* (b) *the woman whose car was stolen* (c) *a jar full of coins* (d) *the last train to leave tonight* (e) *a view of the lake* (f) *children waiting for the bus*
::: solution
Look at the first word after the head and at what kind of phrase it begins ([[#rule-postmodifiers]]).

(a) *-ed* participle phrase (passive: the letter was written).

(b) relative clause, introduced by *whose*.

(c) adjective phrase: *full* + *of coins*.

(d) *to*-infinitive, after *last*, as after *first*.

(e) prepositional phrase, the most common type.

(f) *-ing* participle phrase (active: the children are waiting).
:::
:::

Some single words also go after the noun:

- *something / anything / nothing / someone* + adjective: *something **interesting***, *anyone **new***, *nothing **special*** (✗ *interesting something*);
- *available*, *possible*, *involved*, *concerned*, *present* in certain meanings: *the only seats **available***, *the best solution **possible***, *the people **involved*** (‘who take part’), *the people **present*** (‘who are here’);
- a few fixed phrases, mostly from French or law: *the Secretary **General***, *the president **elect***, *Asia **Pacific***.

Compare *the people concerned* (‘the people this is about’) with *concerned people* (‘worried people’), and *the present members* (‘today's members’) with *the members present* (‘those who are here’). The position changes the meaning.

::: example From Chinese order to English order {#ex-chinese-order}
Translate, putting the head noun early. (a) 我昨天在书店买的那本书 (b) 一个关于熊猫的很有意思的纪录片 (c) 坐在窗边的那个穿红衣服的女孩 (d) 一个没有人认识的人 (e) 去机场最快的路 (f) 第一个到的人
::: solution
Find the head noun in the Chinese (the last word), then put short modifiers before it and phrases and clauses after it ([[#rule-postmodifiers]]).

(a) *the book (that) I bought at the bookshop yesterday*: head *book*; the clause follows; *that* can be left out because *I* is the subject of the clause.

(b) *a very interesting documentary about pandas*: the adjective before, the prepositional phrase after.

(c) *the girl in the red coat sitting by the window*: two postmodifiers; the shorter one (*in the red coat*) usually comes first. *The girl in red* is also common.

(d) *someone nobody knows* or *a person (who) nobody knows*.

(e) *the quickest way to the airport*: superlative before, prepositional phrase after.

(f) *the first person to arrive*: *to*-infinitive after *first*.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which noun phrase is correct?
- [ ] *a near the station hotel*
- [ ] *an available seat only*
- [x] *a hotel near the station*
- [ ] *a hotel near the station's*
::: solution
A place phrase goes after the head: *a hotel near the station*. The adjective *near* followed by *the station* cannot go before the noun.

## Apposition

::: theorem Apposition {#rule-apposition}
A noun phrase can be placed next to another to say who or what it is: *my brother, a doctor in Chengdu, …*; *Wang Fang, our new manager, …*; *the capital, Beijing*. With commas the second phrase adds information (non-restrictive); without commas it identifies (restrictive): *my friend Lin* (the one called Lin), *the word "article"*, *the year 1949*.
:::

::: proof
In apposition the two noun phrases refer to the same person or thing, so the second could replace the first. The comma rule is the same as for relative clauses: extra information is marked off by commas, identifying information is not (Quirk et al. 1985; Swan 2016). In news writing a title is often placed before a name without an article: *President Lincoln*, *Chinese Premier Zhou Enlai*; this style is more common in American English, and British English often prefers *the Chinese Premier, Zhou Enlai*. Ordinary jobs do not work this way in standard English: say *the writer Mo Yan* or *Mo Yan, the writer*, not ✗ *writer Mo Yan*, which is newspaper style.
:::

::: example Combining with apposition {#ex-apposition}
Combine the sentences using apposition, with or without commas. (a) *Lu Xun wrote "A Madman's Diary". He was a writer.* (b) *I'll be with my cousin. Her name is Yu.* (I have several cousins.) (c) *We visited Suzhou. It is a city famous for its gardens.* (d) *Dr Chen will see you now. She is our dentist.*
::: solution
If the added phrase identifies which one, use no commas; if it only adds information, use commas ([[#rule-apposition]]).

(a) *The writer Lu Xun wrote "A Madman's Diary".* or *Lu Xun, the writer, …*; the first is more natural, because *the writer* identifies which Lu Xun is meant only for readers who don't know him.

(b) *I'll be with my cousin Yu.* No commas: *Yu* identifies which of several cousins.

(c) *We visited Suzhou, a city famous for its gardens.* Commas: extra information; note the postmodifier *famous for its gardens* after *city*.

(d) *Dr Chen, our dentist, will see you now.* Commas: extra information. (*Dr* without a full stop in BrE; *Dr.* in AmE.)
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Modifiers before vs after.** In Chinese all modifiers precede the head noun, including whole clauses, usually linked by 的 (Li & Thompson 1981 call these "nominalizations" with 的). In English only determiners, adjectives and nouns come before the head; phrases and clauses follow it. This is the reason for the most common long-sentence error, a heavy subject with the noun at the end: ✗ *The yesterday in the library met student is from Korea.* → *The student I met in the library yesterday is from Korea.*
- **的 as a signal.** 朱德熙 (1982) treats the 的-phrase as a unit that marks a modifier, and Chinese can pile several 的-phrases before one noun. English has no such marker before the noun, so the reader needs the head early; translate long 的-phrases into postmodifiers.
- **Clause before or after.** Because the Chinese relative clause comes first, a Chinese reader meets the description before knowing what it describes. An English reader meets the noun first and then the description. When reading English, learn to hold the head noun in mind and treat what follows as its description: *the man [who sold me the bike]*.
- **Noun + noun works similarly.** 汽车门, 咖啡杯, 鞋店: Chinese and English both put a classifying noun before the head. The English modifier noun stays singular (*a shoe shop*), which is natural for Chinese speakers, since Chinese nouns do not show number.
- **Number + measure.** 一个十岁的女孩 → *a ten-year-old girl* (or *a girl of ten*); 两个小时的飞行 → *a two-hour flight*. The hyphens and the singular *year*/*hour* have no Chinese equivalent.
:::

::: history
English has always been able to put one noun before another — Old English compounds such as *dægweorc* ‘day-work’ or *bōchūs* ‘book-house, library’ were common — but long strings of modifier nouns are a modern habit, especially of newspapers, where space is short. Headline style, which drops articles and piles nouns together, can produce unexpected readings; American copy editors in 2009 named such ambiguous headlines "crash blossoms", after the headline *Violinist Linked to JAL Crash Blossoms*: it meant that a violinist connected with a plane crash was now doing well, but many readers first took *crash blossoms* as a noun + noun phrase. A different development took place in academic writing. Douglas Biber and Bethany Gray, in *Grammatical Complexity in Academic English* (2016), argued that modern academic prose has become complex not mainly through long subordinate clauses but through dense noun phrases, with many premodifying nouns and prepositional phrases after the head: *the effect of temperature on enzyme activity*. Learning to read and build such phrases is a key skill for academic English.
:::

## Where this leads

Agreement between a long subject and its verb depends on finding the head noun: see [[nouns-and-articles/agreement]]. Relative clauses — *who*, *which*, *that*, and when the relative word can be left out — are covered in [[complex-sentences/relative-clauses]]; participle and infinitive phrases in [[complex-sentences/participle-infinitive-clauses]]. Compounds such as *toothbrush* and *bus stop* are a topic of [[word-formation/compounds]]. Dense noun phrases in academic texts are taken up in [[academic-reading/dense-sentences]].

::: summary
- A noun phrase has determiners + premodifiers + **head** + postmodifiers: *all the other new students from Japan who arrived yesterday* ([[#def-noun-phrase]]).
- Before the head: determiners, adjectives (in the usual order) and modifier nouns; modifier nouns are normally singular (*a shoe shop*, *a ten-year-old girl*), with fixed exceptions (*a sports car*, *a clothes shop*) ([[#rule-modifier-noun]]).
- After the head: prepositional phrases, adjectives with complements, participle phrases, *to*-infinitives and relative clauses (*a bag full of books*, *the book I bought yesterday*) ([[#rule-postmodifiers]]).
- *Something interesting*, *the people involved*, *the only seats available*: some single words follow the head.
- Chinese puts all modifiers before the noun with 的; English puts long ones after it, so translate 的-phrases into postmodifiers.
- Apposition: *my cousin Yu* (identifies) vs *Suzhou, a city famous for its gardens* (adds information) ([[#rule-apposition]]).
:::

::: exercise Unpack and rebuild {level=3}
The following sentence is hard to read. Rewrite it so that each noun phrase has its head early, then write two sentences of your own that each contain one noun phrase with at least two postmodifiers.

*The by our team last year designed new student online course registration system has many in the first week reported problems.*
::: solution
Model rewrite: *The new online system for course registration that our team designed last year has many problems, which students reported in the first week.* or *Students reported many problems with the new online course registration system that our team designed last year in its first week.*

The main heads are *system* and *problems*. *New online* and *course registration* stay before *system* (short premodifiers); *that our team designed last year* and *reported in the first week* move after their heads.

Own sentences will vary. Models: *I'd like a room with a view of the sea that isn't too expensive.* *She is the only person in our office who can fix the printer.*
