Wang Fang is moving to Manchester to study. She writes to the university:

> *Dear Sir or Madam, I would be grateful for some advices and informations about accommodations near the campus. I also have two luggages and some furnitures to send.*

Every word is spelled correctly and the meaning is clear, yet an English reader notices four mistakes at once: *advices*, *informations*, *luggages* and *furnitures*. In English these nouns have no plural. (*Accommodations* is fine in American English but not in British English, which makes the case more interesting still.) Nothing about advice or furniture in the real world explains this. Whether a noun can be counted is a fact about the English word, and English sorts its nouns more strictly than most learners expect.

The first look at countable and uncountable nouns was in [[core-grammar/nouns-pronouns]]: the definitions ([[core-grammar/nouns-pronouns#def-countable]], [[core-grammar/nouns-pronouns#def-uncountable]]), the main uncountable nouns and the unit nouns *a piece of*, *a cup of*. This lesson goes further. It gives you tests for countability, the nouns that cause most trouble, the many ways of counting the uncountable, nouns with two uses, and nouns that are always plural or that end in *-s* but are singular. Everything later in this course — *a* and *the*, *some* and *any*, *much* and *many* — depends on this distinction.

## Countable and uncountable uses

::: definition Countable and uncountable use {#def-count-use}
A noun has a **countable use** when it names separate units that can be counted: *a chair*, *two chairs*. It has an **uncountable use** (also called a *mass* or *non-count* use) when it names a substance, a mass or an idea as an undivided whole: *water*, *furniture*, *happiness*. Most nouns have one use only; many have both, usually with a change of meaning (*a paper* / *paper*).
:::

We speak of *uses*, not just of *countable nouns* and *uncountable nouns*, because a dictionary entry often has both. *Coffee* is uncountable in *I don't drink coffee* and countable in *Two coffees, please*. Learner's dictionaries mark each meaning [C] (countable) or [U] (uncountable); some also use [C, U] for meanings that are found both ways.

::: theorem Four tests for countability {#rule-count-tests}
A noun in a given meaning is **countable** if it can:

1. take *a* or *an* in the singular: *a chair*, *an idea*;
2. take a plural ending: *chairs*, *ideas*;
3. follow a number: *three chairs*;
4. follow *many*, *few*, *a few*, *several* and *these*: *many ideas*, *a few chairs*.

It is **uncountable** if instead it has no plural, takes no *a*/*an* and no number, takes a singular verb, and follows *much*, *little*, *a little* and *this*: *much furniture*, *a little information*, *this advice*.
:::

::: proof
The tests work because they are the grammar of counting. *A* comes from the word *one* (Old English *ān*), so it fits only things that come in ones; numbers and *many* need units to count; and a plural ending says "more than one unit". An uncountable noun names something without built-in units, so it has none of these. Some words fit both kinds of noun and do not help you decide: *the*, *some*, *any*, *no*, *a lot of*, *lots of*, *plenty of*, *enough*, *more* and *most* work with plural countable nouns and with uncountable nouns alike (*a lot of chairs*, *a lot of furniture*). Swan (2016) and the *Cambridge Grammar of English* (Carter & McCarthy 2006) give the same tests.
:::

| | countable | uncountable |
|---|---|---|
| *a* / *an* | *a suitcase* | ✗ *a luggage* |
| plural | *two suitcases* | ✗ *two luggages* |
| quantity words | *many*, *few*, *a few*, *several* | *much*, *little*, *a little* |
| how to ask | *How many suitcases?* | *How much luggage?* |
| verb | *The suitcases **are** heavy.* | *The luggage **is** heavy.* |
| both | *some*, *any*, *no*, *the*, *a lot of*, *enough*, *more* | |

## The nouns that cause trouble

Most nouns behave as you would expect: *table*, *student* and *idea* are countable; *water*, *rice* and *happiness* are uncountable. The trouble comes from nouns that are uncountable in English but countable in the learner's mind — because the thing seems countable, or because the word is countable in another language. For Chinese speakers, the pattern is clear: Chinese says 一条建议, 一个消息, 一件家具, so *advice*, *news* and *furniture* feel countable.

::: theorem Common uncountable nouns {#rule-uncountable-list}
These nouns are **uncountable** in their usual meanings, in both British and American English unless marked:

| area | nouns |
|---|---|
| information and ideas | *advice*, *information*, *news*, *knowledge*, *evidence*, *research*, *feedback*, *proof* |
| study and work | *homework*, *housework*, *work* (‘effort, a job in general’), *progress*, *practice*, *permission* |
| things in a group | *furniture*, *luggage* (BrE) / *baggage* (esp. AmE), *equipment*, *clothing*, *money*, *cash*, *mail* / *post* (BrE), *rubbish* (BrE) / *garbage*, *trash* (AmE) |
| the world around us | *weather*, *traffic*, *scenery*, *pollution*, *damage* |
| food | *bread*, *toast*, *rice*, *meat*, *pasta*, *spaghetti*, *fruit* (usually) |
| others | *travel*, *behaviour*, *fun*, *accommodation* (BrE) |
:::

::: proof
The list comes from the usage notes of Swan (2016) and the [C]/[U] labels of the Oxford and Cambridge learner's dictionaries, which agree on all of them. A few need a comment. *Accommodation* is uncountable in British English (*We need accommodation for two nights*), but American English often uses the plural *accommodations* (*The hotel offers luxury accommodations*). *Fruit* is usually uncountable (*I eat a lot of fruit*), but *fruits* is used for different kinds (*tropical fruits*). *Research* is uncountable in most writing (*a lot of research*, *a piece of research*); the plural *researches* is rare and is best avoided. *Work* is uncountable for effort or employment (*I have a lot of work*), but *a work* is a book, painting or piece of music (*the works of Lu Xun*). *Damage* is uncountable; *damages* (plural) is a legal word for money paid as compensation.
:::

The practical answer to an uncountable noun is often a **countable partner**: another word, with a similar meaning, that can be counted.

| uncountable | countable partner |
|---|---|
| *advice* | *a tip*, *a suggestion* |
| *information* | *a fact*, *a detail* |
| *news* | *a news story*, *a report* |
| *furniture* | *a chair*, *a table*, *a sofa* |
| *luggage* | *a suitcase*, *a bag* |
| *work* | *a job*, *a task* |
| *travel* | *a trip*, *a journey* |
| *homework* | *an exercise*, *an assignment* |
| *research* | *a study*, *an experiment* |
| *money* | *a coin*, *a note* (BrE) / *a bill* (AmE), *a dollar*, *a yuan* |

- *He gave me some useful advice.* = *He gave me three useful tips.*
- *I need some information.* = *I need a few details.*
- *We had a lot of travel this year.* sounds odd; *We went on three trips this year.* is natural.
- *Have you got any work?* (employment in general) / *Have you found a job?* (one position)

::: warning Plural endings and *a* on uncountable nouns
✗ *Thank you for your feedbacks.* ✓ *Thank you for your feedback.*

✗ *We need some more equipments.* ✓ *We need some more equipment.*

✗ *What a terrible weather!* ✓ *What terrible weather!*

✗ *She has made a great progress.* ✓ *She has made great progress.*

✗ *I'm looking for a work.* ✓ *I'm looking for work.* or *I'm looking for a job.*

These errors are understandable — in many languages, including Chinese with its 一个反馈, 一个进步 and 一份工作, the meaning is countable. But in English they are marked as errors by every examiner and editor, and they are easy to fix once you know the list.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *Can you give me an advice about my essay?*
- [ ] *There were many traffic on the bridge this morning.*
- [x] *We had some very good news yesterday.*
- [ ] *The new equipments have arrived.*
::: solution
*News* is uncountable and takes *some*, which works with uncountable nouns. *Advice* takes *some advice* or *a piece of advice*; *traffic* takes *a lot of* or *much* (*There was a lot of traffic*); *equipment* has no plural and takes a singular verb (*The new equipment has arrived*).
:::
:::

::: example Choosing the right quantity word {#ex-quantity-word}
Complete each sentence with *much* or *many*, and say how you decided. (a) *How ___ luggage are you taking?* (b) *How ___ bags are you taking?* (c) *There isn't ___ news about the flight.* (d) *We don't have ___ chairs.* (e) *I haven't done ___ research on this topic.*
::: solution
Step 1: find the noun after the gap. Step 2: decide whether it is countable in this meaning ([[#rule-count-tests]]). Step 3: plural countable → *many*; uncountable → *much*.

(a) *luggage* is uncountable → *How **much** luggage are you taking?*

(b) *bags* is plural countable → *How **many** bags are you taking?*

(c) *news* is uncountable (the *-s* is not a plural ending) → *There isn't **much** news about the flight.*

(d) *chairs* is plural countable → *We don't have **many** chairs.*

(e) *research* is uncountable → *I haven't done **much** research on this topic.*

Note that (a) and (b) ask about the same suitcases. The choice depends on the word, not on the things.
:::
:::

## Counting the uncountable

::: definition Unit noun (partitive) {#def-partitive}
A **unit noun**, also called a **partitive**, is a countable noun used with *of* to divide an uncountable noun into countable amounts: *a **piece** of advice*, *two **bottles** of water*, *a **slice** of bread*. The unit noun takes the plural; the uncountable noun does not change.
:::

Unit nouns fall into a few groups. Learn them with their usual partners, because the partners are fixed by usage.

| kind | unit nouns and partners |
|---|---|
| general | *a piece of* (*advice*, *information*, *news*, *furniture*, *equipment*, *luggage*, *music*, *research*, *paper*); *a bit of* (informal: *a bit of luck*, *a bit of help*); *an item of* (*news*, *clothing*, *furniture*), more formal |
| shape | *a slice of* (*bread*, *cake*, *meat*); *a loaf of bread*; *a sheet of paper*; *a bar of chocolate* / *soap*; *a lump of sugar*; *a grain of rice* / *sand*; *a drop of* (*water*, *rain*, *milk*) |
| container | *a cup of tea*; *a glass of water*; *a bottle of* (*milk*, *wine*); *a bowl of* (*rice*, *soup*); *a box of*; *a jar of jam*; *a packet of* (BrE) / *a pack of* (AmE); *a tin of* (BrE) / *a can of* |
| measure | *a kilo of* (*rice*, *apples*); *a litre of milk* (AmE spelling *liter*); *a metre of cloth*; *a spoonful of sugar* |
| special | *a flash of lightning*; *a clap* / *a crash of thunder*; *a stroke of luck*; *a fit of anger*; *a game of chess*; *a round of applause* |

::: theorem Using unit nouns {#rule-unit-nouns}
1. The pattern is **number or *a* + unit noun + *of* + uncountable noun**: *a piece of advice*, *three pieces of advice*.
2. Only the unit noun becomes plural: *two slices of bread*, not ✗ *two slice of bread* or ✗ *two slices of breads*.
3. The verb agrees with the unit noun: *Two pieces of furniture **were** damaged.*
4. With measures and containers, the uncountable noun keeps no article: *a bottle of water*, not ✗ *a bottle of the water* — unless a particular water is meant (*a bottle of the water from the spring*).
:::

::: proof
The unit noun does the counting, so it is the part that carries number. This is why rule 3 follows: in *two pieces of furniture* the noun that the number counts is *pieces*, so the verb is plural (agreement is treated fully in [[nouns-and-articles/agreement]]). *A piece of* is the most general unit noun, but it is not used with everything: we say *a piece of cake* and *a slice of cake*, but not usually *a piece of water* or *a piece of rice*, because water is measured by containers and rice by grains, bowls or kilos. In conversation, speakers often avoid unit nouns altogether by using *some*, *a lot of* or a countable partner: *Can I give you some advice?* is more common than *Can I give you a piece of advice?*
:::

::: example Translating with unit nouns {#ex-unit-nouns}
Translate into English: (a) 两条建议 (b) 一张纸 (c) 三件家具 (d) 一条新闻 (e) 两瓶牛奶
::: solution
For each phrase: (1) find the English noun; (2) check whether it is countable ([[#rule-count-tests]]); (3) if it is uncountable, choose a unit noun that fits it, and make only the unit noun plural ([[#rule-unit-nouns]]).

(a) *advice* is uncountable → *two pieces of advice*, or with a countable partner, *two suggestions*.

(b) *paper* (the material) is uncountable → *a piece of paper* or *a sheet of paper*.

(c) *furniture* is uncountable → *three pieces of furniture* (or name the things: *a table and two chairs*).

(d) *news* is uncountable → *a piece of news*, *an item of news* (formal), or *a news story*.

(e) *milk* is uncountable; the measure word 瓶 is already a container → *two bottles of milk*.

The Chinese classifier 条 appears in both (a) and (d), but it gives no help with English: what matters is the English noun.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Classifiers and measure words.** Chinese needs a word between a number and every noun: 一**本**书, 两**张**桌子, 三**杯**水. Li & Thompson (1981) distinguish **classifiers** (个, 本, 张, 条, 只), which go with countable things and add little meaning, from **measure words** (杯, 瓶, 斤, 碗), which name a container or a quantity. English has no classifiers: *a book*, *two tables*, with nothing between the number and the noun. But English unit nouns correspond closely to Chinese measure words: 三杯水 → *three glasses of water*, 一碗米饭 → *a bowl of rice*, 两斤苹果 → *a kilo of apples* (1 斤 is half a kilo).
- **Where the two systems disagree.** A Chinese classifier does not tell you whether the English noun is countable. 一张桌子 is *a table* (countable, no unit noun), but 一张纸 is *a piece of paper* or *a sheet of paper* (uncountable *paper*). 一条消息 is *a piece of news*, 一条建议 *a piece of advice* or *a suggestion*, 一件家具 *a piece of furniture*, 一件衣服 *a piece of clothing* or simply *a shirt*, *a dress* …
- **Number is not marked in Chinese.** 书 means ‘a book’ or ‘books’ according to the context, and 们 is used only with people and is often left out. So nothing in Chinese prepares a learner to ask, for every English noun, "Is it countable here?" The habit has to be built: check new nouns in a learner's dictionary, and note [C] or [U] together with the meaning.
- **Quantity words.** 很多 covers both *many* and *much*, 一些 both *some* + plural and *some* + uncountable, 多少 both *how many* and *how much*. English splits the first and the last according to countability.
:::

## Nouns with two uses

Many common nouns have both uses, and the meaning changes in a regular way.

::: theorem Regular shifts between countable and uncountable {#rule-two-uses}
1. **Material and object.** The uncountable noun names a material; the countable noun names an object made of it. *paper* / *a paper* (‘a newspaper, an essay’); *glass* / *a glass*; *iron* / *an iron* (for clothes); *stone* / *a stone*; *hair* (on the head) / *a hair* (one single hair).
2. **Substance and animal or item.** *chicken* (meat) / *a chicken* (the bird); *cake* / *a cake*; *lamb* / *a lamb*.
3. **Substance and portion or kind.** In everyday speech a drink or food can be counted as a serving: *a coffee* (= a cup of coffee), *two teas*, *three beers*. It can also be counted as a kind: *a cheese from France*, *the wines of this region*.
4. **General idea and single instance.** The uncountable noun is the idea in general; the countable noun is one case of it. *experience* (‘knowledge from doing something’) / *an experience* (‘something that happened to you’); *time* / *a time* (‘an occasion’: *three times*); *noise* / *a noise*; *light* / *a light* (‘a lamp’); *room* (‘space’) / *a room*; *business* (‘trade’) / *a business* (‘a company’); *life* / *a life*; *success* / *a success*.
:::

::: proof
The pattern is general enough that new pairs are easy to understand: if a café sells a new kind of tea, you can order *two oolongs*. But the shift is not free in every direction. Shift 3 is mostly informal and spoken (*Two coffees, please* in a café; *two cups of coffee* in writing). Shift 4 depends on the word: *a success* and *a pleasure* are normal, but ✗ *an advice* and ✗ *a progress* are not, because *advice* and *progress* have no countable use in standard English. Quirk et al. (1985) and Huddleston & Pullum (2002) both describe these shifts as regular processes rather than lists of exceptions; the dictionary tells you which individual nouns allow them.
:::

- *I don't have **time** to cook.* (time in general) — *I've been to Shanghai **three times**.* (occasions)
- *Do you have any **experience** of teaching?* — *Living abroad was **an** amazing **experience**.*
- *The house has two large bedrooms.* (countable rooms) — *Is there **room** for one more?* (space)
- *There is too much **noise** in this street.* — *I heard **a** strange **noise** last night.*
- *She works in **business**.* — *She runs **a** small **business**.*

::: theorem *A* with abstract nouns described by an adjective {#rule-a-abstract}
Some uncountable abstract nouns take *a*/*an* when an adjective or a following phrase limits them to one kind or one experience: *a good education*, *a sound knowledge of grammar*, *a love of music*, *a great help*, *a pity*, *a shame*. Others never do, even with an adjective: *good advice*, *great progress*, *terrible weather*, *useful information*.
:::

::: proof
*A good education* means ‘one particular kind of education, a good one’, and English allows the article because the adjective creates a type. The nouns that refuse it — *advice*, *progress*, *weather*, *information*, *news*, *furniture* — are the same nouns that are on the list of strictly uncountable nouns ([[#rule-uncountable-list]]). There is no reliable way to predict which abstract nouns allow *a* + adjective; Swan (2016) lists *a knowledge of*, *a good education*, *a help* and *a relief* among the common ones. When in doubt, leave *a* out and check in a dictionary.
:::

::: example Explaining a pair {#ex-two-uses}
Explain the difference in meaning in each pair. (a) *I bought **a paper**.* / *I bought **paper**.* (b) *We had **chicken** for dinner.* / *We had **a chicken** in the garden.* (c) *There's **a hair** in my soup.* / *She has long **hair**.* (d) *This job needs **experience**.* / *The trip was **an experience**.*
::: solution
Use [[#rule-two-uses]] and ask, for each sentence, whether the noun names a material or idea in general (uncountable) or one thing (countable).

(a) *a paper*: one newspaper (countable, shift 1). *paper*: the material, for example for a printer (uncountable).

(b) *chicken*: the meat (uncountable). *a chicken*: a living bird (countable, shift 2).

(c) *a hair*: one single hair (countable). *hair*: all the hair on her head, as a mass (uncountable). ✗ *She has long hairs* would suggest a few long separate hairs.

(d) *experience*: knowledge and skill gained over time (uncountable, shift 4). *an experience*: one event that affected you (countable).
:::
:::

Which sentence means ‘I went to Beijing on three occasions’?
- [ ] *I spent three time in Beijing.*
- [ ] *I had a lot of time in Beijing.*
- [x] *I've been to Beijing three times.*
- [ ] *I've been to Beijing three time.*
::: solution
*Time* in the sense ‘an occasion’ is countable, so it takes the plural after *three*: *three times*. *A lot of time* uses the uncountable meaning, ‘a period of time’.

## Always plural, or *-s* but singular

Some nouns cannot be counted in the usual way because they exist only in the plural. Others look plural but are singular.

::: theorem Plural-only nouns and singular nouns in *-s* {#rule-plural-only}
1. **Plural only, plural verb:** *clothes*, *goods*, *belongings*, *thanks*, *congratulations*, *stairs*, *outskirts*, *surroundings*, *savings*, *earnings*, *premises*; the two-part things of [[core-grammar/nouns-pronouns]] (*trousers*, *jeans*, *scissors*, *glasses*). *My clothes **are** wet.* *Many thanks.*
2. **Plural without *-s*:** *people*, *police*, *cattle*. *The police **are** on their way.* (One member: *a police officer*.)
3. **Singular in *-s*:** *news*; subjects in *-ics* (*mathematics*, *physics*, *economics*, *linguistics*, *athletics*); *means*, *series*, *species* (which have the same form in the plural). *Physics **is** my favourite subject.* *The news **was** a surprise.*
:::

::: proof
Rule 1 and rule 2 nouns name groups that are not divided into units in everyday thinking, so *a clothes* or *two polices* make no sense; for one item we use another word (*a piece of clothing*, *a garment*, *a police officer*). Rule 3 nouns have a final *-s* that is not, or is no longer, a plural ending. *News* was once used with plural verbs; in modern English it is always singular (Swan 2016). Some *-ics* words have a second meaning that can be plural: *Politics **is** a difficult career*, but *His politics **are** very conservative* (‘his political opinions’). *Statistics* is singular as a subject (*Statistics is taught in the first year*) and plural when it means numbers (*These statistics are out of date*).
:::

::: warning *People* and *police*
✗ *There are many peoples in the square.* ✓ *There are many people in the square.*

✗ *The police has arrived.* ✓ *The police have arrived.*

✗ *My brother is a police.* ✓ *My brother is a police officer.* (also *a policeman*, *a policewoman*)

*Peoples* exists only in the meaning ‘nations, ethnic groups’: *the peoples of Asia*.
:::

::: example Singular or plural verb? {#ex-plural-only}
Choose the verb and give the reason. (a) *Mathematics (is / are) hard for me.* (b) *My new jeans (is / are) too long.* (c) *The goods (was / were) delivered on Monday.* (d) *The news (is / are) on at nine.* (e) *Two pieces of luggage (is / are) missing.*
::: solution
Decide which group of [[#rule-plural-only]] the subject belongs to, or which noun does the counting.

(a) *is*: a school subject in *-ics* is singular.

(b) *are*: *jeans* is a two-part, plural-only noun.

(c) *were*: *goods* is plural only.

(d) *is*: *news* is singular despite the *-s*.

(e) *are*: the subject counted is *pieces*, a plural unit noun ([[#rule-unit-nouns]]).
:::
:::

::: history
The line between countable and uncountable has moved over time. In older English, *advice* could be countable in the sense ‘a report, a piece of news’: merchants and diplomats wrote of *advices* from abroad, and the sense is recorded in the *Oxford English Dictionary*. *News* itself began as the plural of *new* (‘new things’), and older writers sometimes gave it a plural verb; it has been treated as singular for a long time now. Changes are still happening. *Data* is now often uncountable (*much data*, *the data is*) as well as plural (*the data are*), as [[nouns-and-articles/agreement]] explains, and *feedback* and *input* are sometimes heard with *-s* in business English, though dictionaries still mark them uncountable. For learners, the useful step came with learner's dictionaries: in the tradition of A. S. Hornby's *Idiomatic and Syntactic English Dictionary* (Tokyo, 1942), which became the *Oxford Advanced Learner's Dictionary*, they mark nouns as countable or uncountable, so a learner can check a word instead of guessing.
:::

## Where this leads

Countability is the first question in choosing an article, as [[nouns-and-articles/a-and-the]] shows: *a* goes only with singular countable nouns, and no article is possible only with plurals and uncountable nouns ([[nouns-and-articles/zero-article-generic]]). The full system of quantity words — *some* and *any*, *much* and *many*, *few* and *a little* — is in [[nouns-and-articles/quantifiers]], and verb agreement with unit nouns and plural-only nouns in [[nouns-and-articles/agreement]]. Learning words together with their countability and partners is discussed in [[core-vocabulary/knowing-a-word]].

::: summary
- A noun is countable in a given meaning if it takes *a*/*an*, a plural, numbers and *many*; uncountable if it takes none of these, a singular verb, and *much* or *a little* ([[#rule-count-tests]]).
- *Advice*, *information*, *news*, *furniture*, *luggage*, *equipment*, *research*, *evidence*, *feedback*, *homework*, *progress*, *weather* and *traffic* are uncountable; *accommodation* is uncountable in BrE, often plural in AmE ([[#rule-uncountable-list]]).
- Use a countable partner (*a tip*, *a suitcase*, *a job*, *a trip*) or a unit noun (*a piece of*, *a slice of*, *a cup of*) to count; only the unit noun takes the plural ([[#rule-unit-nouns]]).
- Many nouns have two uses with a regular change of meaning: material/object, meat/animal, drink/serving, idea/instance ([[#rule-two-uses]]).
- Some abstract nouns take *a* with an adjective (*a good education*), but *advice*, *progress* and *weather* never do ([[#rule-a-abstract]]).
- *Clothes*, *people*, *police* and *goods* are plural; *news*, *physics* and *mathematics* are singular ([[#rule-plural-only]]).
- Chinese measure words correspond to English unit nouns, but Chinese classifiers do not show whether an English noun is countable: check the dictionary's [C]/[U].
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Countable or uncountable? {level=1}
Write C (countable) or U (uncountable) for the usual meaning of each noun: (a) *suitcase* (b) *luggage* (c) *suggestion* (d) *advice* (e) *job* (f) *work* (g) *trip* (h) *travel* (i) *coin* (j) *money* (k) *fact* (l) *information*
::: solution
(a) C; (b) U; (c) C; (d) U; (e) C; (f) U (but *a work* of art is C); (g) C; (h) U; (i) C; (j) U; (k) C; (l) U. The countable words in this list are countable partners of the uncountable ones next to them.
:::
:::

::: exercise *Much* or *many*? {level=1}
Complete with *much* or *many*. (a) *How ___ homework do you have?* (b) *There weren't ___ people at the meeting.* (c) *I don't eat ___ bread.* (d) *How ___ times have you seen this film?* (e) *She doesn't have ___ experience of this work.* (f) *How ___ pieces of luggage can I take?*
::: solution
(a) *much*; (b) *many* (*people* is plural); (c) *much*; (d) *many* (*times* = occasions, countable); (e) *much*; (f) *many* (the noun counted is *pieces*).
:::
:::

::: exercise Unit nouns {level=1}
Complete with a suitable unit noun. (a) *a ___ of bread* (b) *a ___ of soap* (c) *a ___ of paper* (d) *a ___ of lightning* (e) *a ___ of jam* (f) *two ___ of chocolate* (g) *a ___ of applause* (h) *a ___ of luck*
::: solution
(a) *loaf* or *slice* (or *piece*); (b) *bar*; (c) *sheet* or *piece*; (d) *flash*; (e) *jar*; (f) *bars* (or *pieces*); (g) *round*; (h) *stroke* (or informal *bit*).
:::
:::

::: exercise Correct the mistakes {level=2}
Correct the sentences that are wrong. One is correct. (a) *Thank you for all your helps.* (b) *I'd like two coffees and a tea, please.* (c) *The police is looking for the driver.* (d) *We bought some new furnitures for the living room.* (e) *Economics are a popular subject.* (f) *She gave me a very useful information.*
::: solution
(a) *Thank you for all your help.* (b) Correct: in a café, *a coffee* and *a tea* mean ‘a cup of’ (informal, normal in speech). (c) *The police are looking for the driver.* (d) *We bought some new furniture for the living room.* (e) *Economics is a popular subject.* (f) *She gave me some very useful information.* or *a very useful piece of information.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Countable partners {level=2}
Rewrite each sentence with a countable partner instead of the uncountable noun, changing other words as necessary. (a) *I've got a lot of homework this week.* (b) *She gave me some advice about the interview.* (c) *He's looking for work.* (d) *We had a lot of luggage.* (e) *Their research shows that sleep matters.*
::: solution
(a) *I've got a lot of exercises / assignments this week.* (b) *She gave me some tips / a few suggestions about the interview.* (c) *He's looking for a job.* (d) *We had a lot of bags / suitcases.* (e) *Their study shows that sleep matters.* or *Their studies show …* (the verb follows the new subject). Other partners with the same meaning are accepted.
:::
:::

::: exercise Two uses {level=2}
Complete each pair with the noun in brackets, with *a*/*an*, with a plural, or with neither. (a) *(glass)* *The window is made of special ___.* / *Could I have ___ of water?* (b) *(room)* *There isn't enough ___ in the car for five.* / *Our hotel has forty ___.* (c) *(iron)* *Spinach contains ___.* / *I need ___ for my shirt.* (d) *(business)* *He wants to start ___.* / *She's in Shanghai on ___.* (e) *(light)* *Plants need ___.* / *Turn off ___, please.*
::: solution
(a) *glass* / *a glass*; (b) *room* / *rooms*; (c) *iron* / *an iron*; (d) *a business* / *business* (‘for work’); (e) *light* / *the light* (one particular lamp in the room; *the* is explained in [[nouns-and-articles/a-and-the]]; *a light* would mean ‘some lamp or other’).
:::
:::

::: exercise Correct an email {level=3}
Correct Wang Fang's email from the start of the lesson, and the rest of it below, for British English. There are eight mistakes with countable and uncountable nouns in total.

*Dear Sir or Madam, I would be grateful for some advices and informations about accommodations near the campus. I also have two luggages and some furnitures to send. Could you tell me how many money I will need for the first month? I have read many good news about your university. Thank you for your helps.*
::: hint
Check every noun against [[#rule-uncountable-list]]. *Many* and *much* depend on the noun after them.
:::
::: solution
*Dear Sir or Madam, I would be grateful for some **advice** and **information** about **accommodation** near the campus. I also have two **suitcases** (or *two pieces of luggage*) and some **furniture** to send. Could you tell me how **much** money I will need for the first month? I have read **a lot of** good news (or *much good news*, more formal) about your university. Thank you for your **help**.*

The eight corrections: (1) *advices* → *advice*; (2) *informations* → *information*; (3) *accommodations* → *accommodation* (BrE; the plural is acceptable in AmE); (4) *two luggages* → *two suitcases* / *two pieces of luggage*; (5) *furnitures* → *furniture*; (6) *many money* → *much money*; (7) *many good news* → *a lot of good news*; (8) *helps* → *help*.
:::
:::

::: exercise Packing for a trip {level=3}
Write six to eight sentences about what you would pack for a week's trip and what you would buy when you arrive. Use at least four uncountable nouns (for example *luggage*, *clothing*, *money*, *food*, *equipment*), at least three unit nouns, at least one countable partner, and both *How much …?* and *How many …?* once.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

*I always try to travel with very little luggage: one small suitcase and a backpack. I pack enough clothing for four days and wash it on the way. How many shirts do I need? Three is enough. I take a little cash and a bank card, because I don't like carrying much money. When I arrive, I buy a bottle of water, a loaf of bread and a bar of chocolate for the first evening. If I'm going hiking, I also take some equipment: a map, a torch and a few plasters. How much food should I carry? Not much — there are shops everywhere.*

Check: uncountable *luggage*, *clothing*, *cash*, *money*, *water*, *bread*, *chocolate*, *equipment*, *food* without *a* or plural; unit nouns *a bottle of*, *a loaf of*, *a bar of*; countable partners *a suitcase*, *shirts*; *How many shirts* and *How much food*. (*Torch* is BrE; AmE *flashlight*. *Plasters* is BrE; AmE *Band-Aids*.)
:::
:::
