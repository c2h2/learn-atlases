Two sentences differ by one small word:

- *I have a few friends in Shanghai.*
- *I have few friends in Shanghai.*

The first is good news: there are some friends there, enough to call on. The second is sad: there are hardly any. Chinese makes the same distinction in a different place — 我在上海有几个朋友 and 我在上海没几个朋友 — so the meaning is familiar, but the English form is easy to miss. Words like *a few*, *few*, *some*, *any*, *much*, *many*, *each*, *every*, *all* and *both* are **quantifiers**. They are among the most frequent words in English, and each has its own grammar: which nouns it goes with, whether it is used in questions and negatives, and whether it can be followed by *of*.

This lesson builds directly on [[nouns-and-articles/countable-uncountable]], because the first question for almost every quantifier is whether the noun is countable.

## What quantifiers are

::: definition Quantifier {#def-quantifier}
A **quantifier** is a word or phrase that says how much or how many: *some*, *any*, *no*, *much*, *many*, *a lot of*, *few*, *a few*, *little*, *a little*, *several*, *enough*, *all*, *both*, *each*, *every*, *either*, *neither*, *most*, *half* and numbers. Quantifiers are determiners when they come before a noun (*some water*, *every day*); most can also stand alone as pronouns (*Some are broken*), and most can be followed by *of* + a definite noun phrase (*some of the water*).
:::

::: theorem Which quantifiers go with which nouns {#rule-quantifier-nouns}
| with singular countable nouns | with plural countable nouns | with uncountable nouns |
|---|---|---|
| *each*, *every*, *either*, *neither*, *another*, *a*/*one* | *many*, *few*, *a few*, *several*, *both*, *a number of*, numbers from *two* | *much*, *little*, *a little*, *a great deal of*, *an amount of* |
| | **with both plural and uncountable nouns:** *some*, *any*, *no*, *all*, *most*, *a lot of*, *lots of*, *plenty of*, *enough*, *more* | |
:::

::: proof
The table follows from countability ([[nouns-and-articles/countable-uncountable#rule-count-tests]]): words that count separate units (*many*, *several*, *both*, numbers) need countable nouns, and words for amounts (*much*, *little*) need uncountable ones; *each*, *every*, *either* and *neither* look at members one at a time, so they take a singular noun even though they are about a group. The words in the bottom row describe quantity without counting, so they fit both kinds. *No* also goes with singular countable nouns (*There's no bus today*). The table is the same in Swan (2016), Murphy (2019) and Quirk et al. (1985).
:::

## *Some*, *any* and *no*

::: theorem *Some* and *any* {#rule-some-any}
1. Use ***some*** in **positive statements**: *I've bought some bread.* *There are some letters for you.*
2. Use ***any*** in **negatives** and in **neutral questions**: *I haven't got any money.* *Are there any questions?* *Any* also follows *if*, *without*, *never*, *hardly*: *Ask me if you need any help.*
3. Use ***some*** in **questions that expect or invite "yes"**, especially offers and requests: *Would you like some tea?* *Could I have some water, please?* *Did you buy some milk?* (I think you did.)
4. **Stressed *any*** in a positive statement means ‘it doesn't matter which’: *Any bus from here goes to the station.* *Come any time.* It goes with singular nouns too.
5. **Stressed *some*** /sʌm/ means ‘certain ones, not all’: ***Some** people like durian; others hate it.* With a singular noun it means ‘an unknown’: *Some woman called for you.*
:::

::: proof
Unstressed *some* (/səm/) and *any* are both indefinite; the difference is whether the speaker is committed to the existence of the thing. In a positive statement or an offer the speaker assumes it exists, so *some*; in a negative or an open question the speaker does not, so *any*. This is why *Did you buy some milk?* and *Did you buy any milk?* are both correct but different: the first expects "yes" (I hope or think you did), the second is neutral. Swan (2016) and Carter & McCarthy (2006) present the same picture and warn against the school rule "*some* in statements, *any* in questions", which fails for offers, requests and stressed *any*. The compounds follow the same pattern: *someone*, *something*, *somewhere* / *anyone*, *anything*, *anywhere*: *Is anyone there?* *Would you like something to eat?*
:::

::: theorem *No*, *none* and *not any* {#rule-no-none}
1. ***No*** + noun = *not a* / *not any*. It is used with a positive verb and is more emphatic or more formal: *There's no milk.* = *There isn't any milk.* *I have no idea.*
2. ***None*** is the pronoun, used alone or with *of*: *How many tickets are left? — None.* *None of my friends came.*
3. Use only **one** negative word in a clause in standard English: *I don't have any money* or *I have no money*, not ✗ *I don't have no money*.
:::

::: proof
English marks the negative once in the standard variety. *I don't have no money* is not "illogical" — the same pattern (negative concord) is normal in many languages, was common in older English (Chaucer used it freely), and is regular in many present-day dialects of English. But it is non-standard, and it is not accepted in exams or formal writing. *No* before a subject is the usual way to negate it: *No students came* (not ✗ *Any students didn't come*). The verb after *none of* + plural noun can be singular or plural: see [[nouns-and-articles/agreement]].
:::

::: example *Some*, *any* or *no*? {#ex-some-any}
Choose and explain. (a) *Would you like (some / any) cake?* (b) *There aren't (some / any) buses after midnight.* (c) *I've got (some / any) good news!* (d) *You can borrow (some / any) book you like.* (e) *Have you got (some / any) brothers or sisters?* (f) *We've got (no / any) bread left.*
::: solution
Use [[#rule-some-any]] and [[#rule-no-none]]: decide whether the sentence is positive, negative, a neutral question or an offer, and whether a stressed meaning is intended.

(a) *some*: an offer, expecting "yes" (rule 3).

(b) *any*: negative (rule 2).

(c) *some*: positive statement; *news* is uncountable, so *some* (not *a*) is right (rule 1).

(d) *any*: stressed, ‘it doesn't matter which’, with a singular noun (rule 4).

(e) *any*: a neutral question; the speaker does not know (rule 2).

(f) *no*: a positive verb (*have got*) with *no* = *not any*. (*We haven't got any bread left* says the same thing.)
:::
:::

## *Much*, *many* and *a lot of*

::: theorem *Much*, *many*, *a lot of* {#rule-much-many}
1. ***Much*** goes with uncountable nouns, ***many*** with plural countable nouns ([[#rule-quantifier-nouns]]).
2. In everyday English, *much* and *many* are used mainly in **questions** and **negatives**: *How much time do we have?* *There aren't many people here.*
3. In **positive statements**, everyday English prefers ***a lot of*** or ***lots of*** (informal), with both kinds of noun: *We have a lot of time.* *There were lots of people at the concert.*
4. *Much* and *many* are normal in positive statements **after *so*, *too*, *as* and *very*** (*too much noise*, *so many questions*, *as many as possible*), and in **formal** writing (*Many studies have shown …*, *Much has been written about this*).
5. Other common expressions: *plenty of* (‘more than enough’), *a great deal of* + uncountable and *a large number of* + plural (both formal).
:::

::: proof
✗ *I have much money* is not ungrammatical in the strict sense, but it sounds stiff or old-fashioned in conversation; speakers say *I have a lot of money* or *I've got plenty of money*. In academic and official writing, *many* and *much* are common in positive statements, and *a lot of* sounds too informal. So the choice is a matter of register, not correctness (Swan 2016; Carter & McCarthy 2006). Note that *a lot of* contains *a* but takes a plural verb with a plural noun: *A lot of people **are** waiting.* *Lots of* is the same, more informal.
:::

::: warning *Much* with countable nouns, and *a lot of* without *of*
✗ *There are much cars in the city centre.* ✓ *There are a lot of cars …* or *… many cars …*

✗ *I don't have many time.* ✓ *I don't have much time.*

✗ *She has a lot friends.* ✓ *She has a lot of friends.* (But *a lot* stands alone without *of*: *Thanks a lot.* *I like it a lot.*)

✗ *How many money does it cost?* ✓ *How much money does it cost?* or just *How much does it cost?*
:::

## *Few*, *a few*, *little*, *a little*

::: theorem *Few* and *a few*, *little* and *a little* {#rule-few-little}
1. ***A few*** (+ plural) and ***a little*** (+ uncountable) mean ‘some, a small number or amount’. They are **positive**: *I've got a few ideas.* *There's a little rice left.*
2. ***Few*** and ***little*** without *a* mean ‘not many’, ‘not much’, ‘almost none’. They are **negative**, and they often suggest "less than we want or expect": *Few people came to the meeting.* *There's little hope of finding the keys.*
3. *Few* and *little* without *a* are rather **formal**. In conversation, people usually say *not many*, *not much*, *hardly any* or *only a few*: *Not many people came.* *There isn't much hope.*
4. ***Quite a few*** means ‘a fairly large number’: *Quite a few students failed.*
:::

::: proof
The article makes the difference. *A few* and *a little* treat the small quantity as something that exists, like *a* + noun; without *a*, *few* and *little* stress how small it is, and behave like negatives — they take positive question tags, for example: *Few people know that, **do** they?* (Quirk et al. 1985). The difference is one of attitude as much as of quantity: in *I have a few friends* and *I have few friends* the number may be the same, three or four, but the first speaker is content and the second is not. The adjective *little* (‘small’: *a little house*) is a different use of the same word.
:::

| positive (some) | negative (not many, not much) |
|---|---|
| *I have a few friends here.* | *I have few friends here.* / *I don't have many friends here.* |
| *She speaks a little French.* | *She speaks little French.* / *She doesn't speak much French.* |
| *There's a little milk in the fridge.* | *There's little milk in the fridge.* / *There's hardly any milk.* |

::: example *Few* or *a few*? {#ex-few}
Complete with *few*, *a few*, *little* or *a little*, and explain. (a) *Don't worry — we still have ___ time before the train leaves.* (b) *The film was boring, and ___ people stayed until the end.* (c) *Can you lend me ___ money until Friday?* (d) *He's very shy and has ___ friends.* (e) *I know ___ words of Japanese — enough to say hello.* (f) *There's ___ point in arguing; he never changes his mind.*
::: solution
First decide countable (*few*/*a few*) or uncountable (*little*/*a little*). Then decide whether the meaning is positive (‘some’) or negative (‘not enough, almost none’) ([[#rule-few-little]]).

(a) *a little*: *time* is uncountable; "don't worry" shows a positive meaning.

(b) *few*: *people* is plural; "boring" shows the negative meaning (almost nobody stayed).

(c) *a little*: uncountable, positive (a small amount).

(d) *few*: plural, negative (shy, not many friends). In conversation: *doesn't have many friends*.

(e) *a few*: plural, positive — "enough to say hello".

(f) *little*: *point* here is uncountable (‘use, purpose’); negative. *There's little point in …* is a fixed phrase.
:::
:::

Which sentence means ‘Some students passed, a small number’?

- [x] *A few students passed the exam.*
- [ ] *Little students passed the exam.*
- [ ] *A little students passed the exam.*

*A few* + plural noun is positive: some students passed. *Few students passed* means almost none did. *Little* and *a little* go with uncountable nouns, so they cannot be used with *students* in this sense (*little students* would mean ‘small students’).

## *All*, *every*, *each* and *both*, *either*, *neither*

::: theorem *All*, *every* and *each* {#rule-all-every-each}
1. ***All*** goes with plural and uncountable nouns: *All children need sleep.* *All the money has gone.* With a singular noun of time, *all* means ‘the whole’: *all day*, *all week*, *all summer*.
2. ***Every*** goes with a singular countable noun and a singular verb, for three or more things seen as a group: *Every student **has** a locker.* It is also used for regular intervals: *every day*, *every two weeks*, *every few minutes*.
3. ***Each*** goes with a singular countable noun and a singular verb, for two or more things seen **one by one**: *Each student **was** given a different question.* *Each* can also stand alone (*They cost five yuan each*), be followed by *of* (*each of the students*), and follow a plural subject (*The students each have a locker*).
4. ***Every*** cannot stand alone or be followed directly by *of*: use *every one of* or *each of*: *Every one of the rooms was full.*
:::

::: proof
*Every* and *each* both look at members one at a time, which is why they take a singular noun and verb, as Chinese 每 does not require. The difference is one of emphasis: *every* sees the members together, close to *all* (*Every seat was taken*), and needs at least three; *each* sees them separately (*Each seat has its own screen*) and can be used for two (*She had a bag in each hand*, not ✗ *every hand*). *All day* and *every day* mean different things: *I studied all day* = the whole of one day; *I study every day* = on each day. Huddleston & Pullum (2002) and Swan (2016) describe the same differences.
:::

- *I've been waiting all morning.* (the whole morning)
- *I go running every morning.* (each morning)
- *Every room in the hotel has a balcony.*
- *The teacher spoke to each student in turn.*
- *All my friends were there.* / *All of my friends were there.*

::: theorem *Both*, *either* and *neither* {#rule-both-either}
These words are used for **two** things.

1. ***Both*** + plural noun = ‘the one and the other’: *Both sisters are doctors.* *Both (of) the sisters …* *Both of them …*
2. ***Either*** + singular noun = ‘one or the other’, or ‘each of the two’: *You can take either bus.* *There are trees on either side of the road.*
3. ***Neither*** + singular noun = ‘not one and not the other’: *Neither answer is correct.*
4. With *of*, all three take a determiner + plural noun or a pronoun: *both of my parents*, *either of the buses*, *neither of them*.
:::

::: proof
*Either* and *neither* take a singular noun because, like *each*, they pick out one member at a time. After *either of* and *neither of* + plural noun, the verb is singular in formal English (*Neither of them **is** ready*) and often plural in conversation (*Neither of them **are** ready*); the full rule is in [[nouns-and-articles/agreement]]. For more than two, use *any*, *none* and *all*: *Neither of my two brothers smokes*, but *None of my three brothers smokes*. The pairs *both … and*, *either … or* and *neither … nor* join two words or phrases: *Both Ali and Mei were invited.* *You can pay either by card or in cash.*
:::

::: example *All*, *every*, *each*, *both* {#ex-all-every}
Choose the correct word and explain. (a) *She works (all / every) day from nine to five.* (b) *It rained (all / every) day on Sunday — we couldn't go out.* (c) *The two teams shook hands after the game. (Every / Each) player said "Well played".* (d) *(Both / Either) my parents are teachers.* (e) *(Every / Each) of the children got a present.* (f) *I've asked (all / every) my friends.*
::: solution
(a) *every*: each working day, a regular habit ([[#rule-all-every-each]], rule 2).

(b) *all*: the whole of one day (rule 1).

(c) *Each*: the players are seen one by one, as they shake hands. *Every* is possible too, if the speaker sees them as one group; *each* fits the picture of individual handshakes better.

(d) *Both*: two parents, ‘the one and the other’ ([[#rule-both-either]]).

(e) *Each*: *every* cannot be followed directly by *of* (rule 4). (*Every one of the children* is also correct.)

(f) *all*: *every* needs a singular noun with no other determiner; *all my friends* (or *every one of my friends*, *each of my friends*).
:::
:::

## Quantifier + *of*

::: theorem Quantifier + *of* + determiner {#rule-of-pattern}
1. Use ***of*** after a quantifier when the noun has a **determiner** (*the*, *my*, *these* …) or is a **pronoun**: *some of the students*, *many of my friends*, *most of these books*, *all of them*.
2. Do **not** use *of* when the noun has no determiner: *most people*, *some students*, not ✗ *most of people*, ✗ *some of students*.
3. After *all* and *both*, *of* is optional before a determiner: *all (of) the money*, *both (of) my sisters* — but needed before a pronoun: *all of us*, *both of them*. *Every*, *no* and *a/an* cannot take *of* directly: use *every one of*, *none of*, *one of*.
:::

::: proof
The two patterns mean different things. *Most students* means most students in general; *most of the students* means most of a particular group the listener can identify — the students in this class, at this school. The *of* + determiner pattern picks a part out of an identified whole, which is why it needs *the*, *my* or another determiner after *of*. This is the most common quantifier error in learners' writing (Swan 2016 lists ✗ *most of people* among typical mistakes), and it is easy to check: if there is no *the* or *my* after the quantifier, there should be no *of*.
:::

::: warning *Most of* and *most*
✗ *Most of people in my city use public transport.* ✓ *Most people in my city use public transport.*

✗ *The most students passed.* ✓ *Most students passed.* / *Most of the students passed.*

✗ *Some of students were late.* ✓ *Some students were late.* / *Some of the students were late.*

✗ *All of students must register.* ✓ *All students must register.* / *All (of) the students must register.*

*The most* is a superlative (*the most beautiful city*, *Who scored the most goals?*), not a quantifier meaning ‘the majority’.
:::

::: example Part of a group or the group in general? {#ex-of}
Explain the difference in each pair. (a) *Most students work part-time.* / *Most of the students work part-time.* (b) *Some people were angry.* / *Some of the people were angry.* (c) *All children like stories.* / *All of my children like stories.*
::: solution
Apply [[#rule-of-pattern]]: *of* + determiner picks a part out of a group the listener can identify; without *of*, the quantifier speaks about the class in general.

(a) *Most students*: students in general (in this country, say). *Most of the students*: most of a particular group, such as the students in this class.

(b) *Some people*: some people, not identified. *Some of the people*: some of a group already mentioned or present (*the people at the meeting*).

(c) *All children*: a general statement about every child. *All of my children*: a particular family; *All my children* is equally correct.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *Most of Chinese students learn English at school.*
- [ ] *Every of my friends has a car.*
- [x] *Neither of the two hotels has a pool.*
- [ ] *Each students has a laptop.*
::: solution
*Neither of* + determiner + plural noun is correct, with a singular verb in formal English. The others should be *Most Chinese students …* (no determiner, no *of*), *Every one of my friends …* or *Each of my friends …*, and *Each student has a laptop*.
:::

::: remark
***Less* and *fewer*.** The traditional rule is *fewer* with plural nouns and *less* with uncountable ones: *fewer cars*, *less traffic*. Careful writers and most examiners follow it, and it is a good rule for formal writing. But *less* with plural nouns is common in speech (*less people*), and it is normal before numbers and amounts treated as a single quantity: *less than 20 minutes*, *less than 50 kilometres*, *in less than three years*. Supermarket signs saying *10 items or less* are therefore not simply "wrong"; *10 items or fewer* is the more formal choice.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **一些 and *some*/*any*.** Chinese uses 一些 in statements and 什么 or nothing in questions: 你有什么问题吗? → *Do you have any questions?* 冰箱里有一些牛奶 → *There's some milk in the fridge.* The English choice depends on whether the sentence is positive, negative or a question, which Chinese does not mark in the quantifier.
- **很多 and *much*/*many*/*a lot of*.** 很多 covers all three. English asks two questions: countable or not (*many*/*much*), and positive statement or question/negative (*a lot of*/*much*, *many*).
- **几个 and *a few*/*few*.** 有几个 corresponds to *a few*, 没几个 or 很少 to *few* / *not many*: 我有几个朋友 → *I have a few friends*; 我没几个朋友 → *I don't have many friends* (formal: *I have few friends*).
- **每 and 都.** 每个学生都有电脑 → *Every student has a computer* (singular noun, singular verb), or *All the students have computers*. ✗ *Every students have …* mixes the two. 都 is placed before the verb in Chinese; English *all* can come before the noun (*All the students passed*) or after the subject (*The students all passed*).
- **两个都 and *both*.** 两个都很好 → *Both are good* / *Both of them are good*; 两个都不行 → *Neither is any good* / *Neither of them works*, not ✗ *Both are not good*, which suggests ‘not both’.
:::

::: history
*Few* and *little* are among the oldest words of English: Old English had *fēawe* ‘few’ and *lȳtel* ‘little’. The rule that *fewer* must be used with plural nouns, and *less* only with uncountable ones, is much younger. It is usually traced to the grammarian Robert Baker, who in his *Reflections on the English Language* (1770) said that *fewer* seemed to him more elegant than *less* with numbers, and later usage guides turned the preference into a rule. *Less* had been used with countable nouns for many centuries before that; *Merriam-Webster's Dictionary of English Usage* (1989) traces such uses back to Old English, in a text from the time of King Alfred. Like many usage rules, the *fewer*/*less* rule is a description of careful formal style that is presented as a law; learning it is useful, but so is knowing that it is not how everyone speaks.
:::

## Where this leads

The agreement of the verb after quantifiers — *Each of them is*, *None of them is/are*, *A number of people are*, *The number of people is* — is in [[nouns-and-articles/agreement]]. The order of quantifiers with other determiners (*all the other students*, *my many friends*, *the few people who came*) is in [[nouns-and-articles/other-determiners]]. Quantifiers are also central to cautious academic claims (*most*, *many*, *some*, *a few*): see [[modality/hedging-boosting]] and [[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]].

::: summary
- Each quantifier goes with particular nouns: *many*, *few*, *several*, *both* with plurals; *much*, *little* with uncountables; *each*, *every*, *either*, *neither* with singulars; *some*, *any*, *all*, *a lot of*, *enough* with both plurals and uncountables ([[#rule-quantifier-nouns]]).
- *Some* in positive statements, offers and requests; *any* in negatives and neutral questions, and stressed for ‘it doesn't matter which’ ([[#rule-some-any]]); *no* = *not any*, with one negative per clause in standard English ([[#rule-no-none]]).
- *Much* and *many* are used mainly in questions, negatives, after *so*/*too*/*as*, and in formal writing; *a lot of* in everyday positive statements ([[#rule-much-many]]).
- *A few*, *a little* = some (positive); *few*, *little* = not many, not much (negative, rather formal) ([[#rule-few-little]]).
- *Every* and *each* take a singular noun and verb; *all day* ≠ *every day* ([[#rule-all-every-each]]); *both*, *either*, *neither* are for two ([[#rule-both-either]]).
- Use *of* only before a determiner or pronoun: *most people*, *most of the people*, ✗ *most of people* ([[#rule-of-pattern]]).
:::

(a) *There are a few biscuits left.* (b) *Few tourists visit this village.* (c) *I speak a little Spanish.* (d) *We have little time.* (formal; the original is more natural in speech) (e) *Few people know the answer.* (f) *There's a little soup in the pot.*

::: exercise *Of* or no *of*? {level=2}
Complete with *of* or nothing (–). (a) *Most ___ my classmates live on campus.* (b) *Most ___ people enjoy holidays.* (c) *Some ___ the photos are blurry.* (d) *All ___ us were tired.* (e) *Both ___ my grandmothers live in Fujian.* (f) *Many ___ young people use this app.* (g) *Each ___ them has a key.* (h) *None ___ the shops were open.*
::: solution
(a) *of*; (b) –; (c) *of*; (d) *of* (before a pronoun); (e) *of* or – (both are correct before *my*); (f) –; (g) *of*; (h) *of*.
:::

Correct the mistakes. (a) *Every students in our class has a dictionary.* (b) *I have much homework today.* (c) *Most of people like music.* (d) *I don't have no time.* (e) *Neither of answers is right.* (f) *She has a lot friends in Beijing.* (g) *There is few milk in the fridge.*

::: exercise A short report {level=3}
A teacher asked 40 students how they travel to school. Write five or six sentences reporting the results below. Use at least five different quantifiers, including one with *of* and one of *few*/*a few*. Results: bus 22; bike 10; on foot 5; car 3; train 0. Every student answered.
::: hint
Think about the size of each group: 22 out of 40 is more than half (*most of the students*? *over half*?); 3 is *a few*; 0 is *none* or *no students*.
:::
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

*All 40 students answered the survey. Just over half of them take the bus to school, which makes it the most common way to travel. Quite a few students cycle — ten in total. A few walk, and only three come by car. None of the students comes by train, probably because there is no station near the school. So most students use public transport or travel under their own power, and very few depend on a car.*

Check: *all*, *half of them* (with *of* + pronoun), *quite a few*, *a few*, *none of the students*, *no station*, *most students*, *very few*. *None of the students comes* (formal) and *come* (informal) are both correct.
:::
:::

::: exercise Correct a paragraph {level=3}
Correct the quantifier mistakes in this paragraph. There are seven.

*In my hometown there are much small restaurants, and the most of them serve noodles. Every restaurants has its own recipe. I have tried a few of them, but not all. Last week I went to a new place with both my friends Li Na, Ahmed and Kenji. We didn't have some money left after the trip, so we only ordered a little dishes. Few minutes later the food arrived — they were the best noodles I've ever had.*
::: hint
Check the noun after each quantifier: countable or uncountable, singular or plural? Then check *of*, *both* (two only) and *some*/*any*.
:::
::: solution
*In my hometown there are **a lot of** (or *many*) small restaurants, and **most** of them serve noodles. **Every restaurant** has its own recipe. I have tried a few of them, but not all. Last week I went to a new place with **three of my friends** (or *my friends*) Li Na, Ahmed and Kenji. We didn't have **any** money left after the trip, so we only ordered **a few** dishes. **A few** minutes later the food arrived — they were the best noodles I've ever had.*

The seven corrections: (1) *much small restaurants* → *a lot of / many small restaurants* (plural countable); (2) *the most of them* → *most of them*; (3) *Every restaurants* → *Every restaurant* (singular after *every*); (4) *both my friends* with three names → *three of my friends* or *my friends* (*both* is for two); (5) *some money* → *any money* (negative); (6) *a little dishes* → *a few dishes* (plural countable); (7) *Few minutes later* → *A few minutes later* (positive meaning, ‘after a short time’). *A few of them* and *not all* are correct.
:::
:::
