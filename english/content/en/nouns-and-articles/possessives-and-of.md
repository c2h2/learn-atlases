Chinese has one small word, 的, for a very wide range of relations: 我爸爸的车, 桌子的腿, 今天的报纸, 咖啡的杯子, 一个朋友的建议. English has three main structures where Chinese has 的, and they are not interchangeable:

- *my father's car* — the possessive *'s*
- *the legs of the table* — a phrase with *of*
- *the car door*, *a coffee cup* — one noun used to describe another

A learner who uses only one of them writes sentences that are understandable but odd: ✗ *the table's legs* sounds strange in most contexts, ✗ *the car of my father* sounds like a translation, and ✗ *a cup of coffee* for an empty cup says the wrong thing. This lesson explains when English uses each structure. The basic forms of *'s* and *s'*, and the possessive pronouns *my*/*mine*, were introduced in [[core-grammar/nouns-pronouns]] ([[core-grammar/nouns-pronouns#rule-possessive]]); here we build on them.

## The possessive *'s*

::: definition Possessive *'s* (genitive) {#def-genitive}
The **possessive** (also called the **genitive**) is a noun phrase with the ending ***'s*** (or only an apostrophe after a plural *-s*) placed before another noun: *Li Wei's phone*, *my parents' house*, *the company's profits*. The phrase with *'s* is the **possessor**; the noun after it is the **head** of the whole phrase.
:::

The forms were set out in [[core-grammar/nouns-pronouns]]. In short:

| possessor | written | example |
|---|---|---|
| singular noun | *'s* | *my sister's room*, *the boss's office* |
| regular plural in *-s* | *'* only | *my sisters' room* (two sisters), *the students' books* |
| irregular plural | *'s* | *the children's toys*, *people's opinions*, *women's football* |
| name ending in *-s* | *'s* or *'* | *James's car* or *James' car* |

::: theorem The possessive is a determiner {#rule-genitive-determiner}
1. A possessive phrase takes the place of the article before the head noun: *Tom's car*, not ✗ *the Tom's car* or ✗ *Tom's the car*.
2. Any article in the phrase belongs to the **possessor**, not to the head: in *the teacher's desk*, *the* goes with *teacher* (‘the desk of the teacher’). *A friend's house* means ‘the house of a friend’.
3. The *'s* is added to the **end of the whole possessor phrase**, even when the last word is not its main noun: *the King of Spain's visit*, *my brother-in-law's car*, *someone else's bag*, *the man next door's dog* (informal).
4. With two possessors: *Li Wei and Wang Fang's flat* = one flat that they share; *Li Wei's and Wang Fang's flats* = two flats, one each.
:::

::: proof
A possessive identifies the head noun, as *the* does: *Tom's car* is a particular car that the listener can find (the one belonging to Tom). That is why it fills the determiner position and leaves no room for another article (Huddleston & Pullum 2002; Quirk et al. 1985). It is also why *'s* attaches to a whole phrase: it marks the end of the possessor, wherever that is, so that the listener knows where the head noun begins. This "group genitive" is normal with short phrases (*the King of Spain's*, *someone else's*); with long ones it becomes clumsy, and English prefers *of* (see [[#rule-s-or-of]]): *the dog of the man who lives next door*.
:::

::: example Where does the *'s* go? {#ex-group}
Make one noun phrase with *'s* or *'*. (a) *the car — my brother-in-law* (b) *the wedding — my two cousins* (one wedding for both) (c) *the bags — the passengers* (d) *the opinion — the head of department* (e) *the bikes — Ali and Ming* (one bike each) (f) *the coat — someone else*
::: solution
Find the whole possessor phrase, add *'s* (or *'* after a plural *-s*) at its end, and drop *the* before the head noun ([[#rule-genitive-determiner]]).

(a) *my brother-in-law's car* — *'s* at the end of the compound.

(b) *my two cousins' wedding* — plural *cousins*, apostrophe only; one wedding.

(c) *the passengers' bags* — plural possessor; *the* belongs to *passengers*.

(d) *the head of department's opinion* — group genitive; *the opinion of the head of department* is also natural.

(e) *Ali's and Ming's bikes* — separate possession, so each name takes *'s*.

(f) *someone else's coat* — *'s* on *else*, the last word of the phrase.
:::
:::

::: warning *The* and *a* with possessives
✗ *I borrowed the Tom's bike.* ✓ *I borrowed Tom's bike.*

✗ *This is a my friend.* ✓ *This is a friend of mine.* or *This is my friend.*

✗ *She is the John's sister.* ✓ *She is John's sister.*

✗ *I met one my colleague at the airport.* ✓ *I met one of my colleagues at the airport.* or *a colleague of mine*

The possessive replaces the article. To say ‘one of several’ with a possessive, use *a … of mine* or *one of my …*: see [[#rule-double-genitive]].
:::

## Choosing between *'s* and *of*

Both *Li Wei's address* and *the address of Li Wei* are grammatical, and both *the end of the film* and *the film's end* can be found. But in most cases one of them is much more natural. The choice depends on what the possessor is and how long it is.

::: theorem *'s* or *of*: the usual choice {#rule-s-or-of}
**Usually *'s*** when the possessor is:

1. a **person** or an **animal**: *my mother's job*, *the doctor's advice*, *the cat's bowl*;
2. a **group of people**, an **organisation**, a **country** or a **place**: *the team's coach*, *the company's profits*, *China's economy*, *the city's museums*;
3. a **time** expression: *today's newspaper*, *last year's results*, *next week's meeting*, and lengths of time: *a week's holiday*, *two hours' drive*, *ten minutes' walk*.

**Usually *of*** when the possessor is:

4. a **thing** or an **idea**: *the end of the film*, *the top of the page*, *the name of the street*, *the cause of the problem*;
5. **long**, especially when it has a phrase or clause after it: *the name of the woman who lives upstairs*, *the address of the hotel we stayed at*.
:::

::: proof
These are tendencies, not strict rules, and the reference grammars present them as such (Quirk et al. 1985; Biber et al. 1999; Swan 2016). Two forces work together. One is **animacy**: English prefers *'s* for possessors that are human or close to human (animals, organisations, nations), and *of* for lifeless things. The other is **length**: English likes long, heavy phrases at the end of a noun phrase, so a long possessor goes after the head with *of* (*the son of a farmer from Gansu*), while a short one goes before it with *'s* (*the farmer's son*). Register matters as well. Biber et al. (1999) found that the *'s* possessive is especially common in news writing, which favours compact phrases (*the government's decision*, *the region's farmers*), while *of*-phrases are far more frequent in academic prose. Where both fit, both are correct: *the government's decision* and *the decision of the government* say the same thing, the second a little more formally.
:::

- *Have you seen **Amina's** new flat?* (a person)
- *The **museum's** collection is famous.* or *The collection **of the museum** …* (an organisation: both)
- *I read about it in **yesterday's** paper.* (time)
- *It's **an hour's** drive from here.* (length of time)
- *Write your name at the top **of the page**.* (a thing)
- *What's the title **of the book you lent me**?* (a long possessor)

::: example *'s* or *of*? {#ex-s-or-of}
Choose the more natural phrase and explain. (a) *the window's handle* / *the handle of the window* (b) *my uncle's farm* / *the farm of my uncle* (c) *this month's bills* / *the bills of this month* (d) *the boy's name who won* / *the name of the boy who won* (e) *the problem's cause* / *the cause of the problem* (f) *Shenzhen's population* / *the population of Shenzhen*
::: solution
Ask: is the possessor a person, organisation, place or time (*'s*), or a thing or idea (*of*)? Is it long (*of*)? ([[#rule-s-or-of]])

(a) *the handle of the window* — a thing (or the noun + noun *the window handle*, see below).

(b) *my uncle's farm* — a person.

(c) *this month's bills* — a time expression.

(d) *the name of the boy who won* — the possessor *the boy who won* is long, and the clause *who won* belongs to *boy*, so it must stay next to it. ✗ *the boy's name who won* separates them.

(e) *the cause of the problem* — an abstract thing.

(f) Both are natural: a city takes *'s* easily (rule 2), and *the population of Shenzhen* is common in more formal writing.
:::

## Noun + noun: *the car door*

::: theorem A noun used as a modifier {#rule-noun-noun}
When the first noun says **what kind** of thing the second is — its type, purpose, material, place or time — English often puts the two nouns side by side with no *'s* and no *of*: *the car door*, *a coffee cup*, *a kitchen table*, *the bedroom window*, *a tennis ball*, *a gold ring*, *a city map*, *the evening news*. The first noun is usually singular: *a shoe shop*, *a toothbrush*.
:::

::: proof
In a noun + noun phrase, the first noun is not a possessor but a classifier: *a coffee cup* is a kind of cup, the kind for coffee. That is the difference from *a cup of coffee*, which is a cup containing coffee, and from *Tom's cup*, which belongs to Tom. Noun + noun is very common for parts of things and for everyday objects (*the car door*, *the table leg*, *the window handle*), where *of* is possible but heavy, and *'s* is odd because the possessor is not a person. Many such pairs have become compounds, some written as one word (*toothbrush*, *bookshop*), some with a space (*bus stop*, *credit card*). Nouns used as modifiers, including the plural exceptions (*a sports car*, *a clothes shop*), are treated further in [[nouns-and-articles/noun-phrases]].
:::

| *'s* (belongs to) | *of* (part, content, relation) | noun + noun (kind) |
|---|---|---|
| *the driver's seat* (also a fixed term) | *the back of the car* | *the car seat* |
| *my sister's cup* | *a cup of coffee* (full) | *a coffee cup* (for coffee) |
| *the farmer's land* | *a piece of land* | *a land dispute* |
| *China's history* | *the history of China* | *a history book* |

::: example Three structures {#ex-noun-noun}
Translate with the most natural English structure and say which one you chose. (a) 一瓶葡萄酒 (full) (b) 葡萄酒瓶 (empty, for wine) (c) 王老师的办公室 (d) 卧室的门 (e) 明天的天气 (f) 网球拍
::: solution
For each phrase ask: does the first part own the second (*'s*), contain or relate to it (*of*), or say what kind it is (noun + noun)?

(a) *a bottle of wine* — content, *of*.

(b) *a wine bottle* — kind of bottle, noun + noun.

(c) *Ms Wang's office* (or *Mr Wang's*, *Professor Wang's*) — belongs to a person, *'s*. (*Teacher Wang* is not usual in English; see [[nouns-and-articles/articles-with-names]] and [[everyday-english/greetings-introductions]].)

(d) *the bedroom door* — the kind of door (the one belonging to the bedroom as a part), noun + noun. *The door of the bedroom* is possible but heavier; ✗ *the bedroom's door* is unusual for a thing.

(e) *tomorrow's weather* — time, *'s*.

(f) *a tennis racket* (AmE also *racquet*) — kind of racket, noun + noun.
:::

## *A friend of mine* and *at the dentist's*

::: theorem The double possessive {#rule-double-genitive}
To combine a possessive with *a*, *this*, *that*, *some* or a number, use **determiner + noun + *of* + possessive pronoun or *'s***: *a friend of mine*, *two colleagues of hers*, *that dog of yours*, *a cousin of Li Wei's*, *some friends of my parents'*. ✗ *a my friend* and ✗ *this my book* are not possible.
:::

::: proof
English allows only one central determiner before a noun ([[nouns-and-articles/other-determiners]]), so *a* and *my* cannot both come first. The *of* + possessive pattern moves the possessor after the noun. *A friend of mine* means ‘one of my friends’, without the suggestion that I have only one; *my friend* usually suggests someone already known to the listener or my only close friend in the context. With a noun, both *a friend of my father's* and *a friend of my father* are used; the second is more common in many contexts and is fully correct (Swan 2016). With *this* and *that*, the pattern often adds feeling: *that brother of yours* is usually critical or affectionate.
:::

::: theorem Possessive without a following noun {#rule-independent}
A possessive can stand without a head noun when the noun is clear from the context: *Is this umbrella yours or Amina's?* It is also used for **shops, businesses and homes**: *at the dentist's*, *at the hairdresser's*, *at the butcher's*, *at Wang Fang's* (= at her home). In this use, *at the dentist* and *at the hairdresser* (without *'s*) are also common, especially in American English.
:::

- *My phone is old, but my sister's is brand new.* (= my sister's phone)
- *We stayed at my grandparents' over the holiday.* (= their home)
- *I've got an appointment at the dentist's / the dentist on Monday.*
- *St Paul's* (= St Paul's Cathedral), *McDonald's* — see [[nouns-and-articles/articles-with-names#rule-buildings]].

::: example From 的 to English {#ex-de}
Translate, and explain the structure you chose. (a) 我的一个邻居 (b) 他们俩合住的公寓 (李伟和王芳的公寓) (c) 楼下那家新开的餐馆的老板 (d) 这是你的伞还是安娜的?
::: solution
(a) *a neighbour of mine* or *one of my neighbours* (AmE *neighbor*) — 我的一个 needs the double possessive or *one of* ([[#rule-double-genitive]]); ✗ *a my neighbour* is impossible.

(b) *Li Wei and Wang Fang's flat* (AmE *apartment*) — one flat shared by both, so *'s* once, at the end of the whole possessor ([[#rule-genitive-determiner]], rule 4).

(c) *the owner of the new restaurant downstairs* — the possessor is long and is a place described by a phrase, so *of* ([[#rule-s-or-of]], rule 5). *The new restaurant downstairs's owner* is not acceptable.

(d) *Is this your umbrella or Anna's?* or *Is this umbrella yours or Anna's?* — *Anna's* stands without a noun because *umbrella* is clear from the context ([[#rule-independent]]).
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **One 的, three English structures.** 我爸爸的车 → *my father's car* (*'s*); 桌子的腿 → *the leg of the table* or *the table leg* (*of* or noun + noun); 今天的报纸 → *today's paper* (*'s*, time); 一杯咖啡 → *a cup of coffee*, but 咖啡杯 → *a coffee cup*. Chinese marks all of these with 的 or with nothing; English must choose.
- **Order.** The *'s* possessive has the same order as Chinese (possessor first: 李伟的手机 → *Li Wei's phone*), so it feels natural. The *of*-phrase reverses it (房间的门 → *the door of the room*), which is why learners avoid *of* and over-use *'s* with things: ✗ *the room's door*, ✗ *the problem's solution*.
- **Long possessors.** Chinese puts even a long possessor first: 住在楼上的那个女人的名字. English moves it after the head with *of*: *the name of the woman who lives upstairs*. This is part of a general difference — English puts long modifiers after the noun — which is the subject of [[nouns-and-articles/noun-phrases]].
- **我的一个朋友.** Chinese puts the possessor before the numeral and classifier: 我的一个朋友, 他的两个同事. English uses *a friend of mine*, *two colleagues of his*, or *one of my friends*, never ✗ *a my friend* or ✗ *one my friend*.
- **No 的 in Chinese, *'s* in English.** Close relations often omit 的 in Chinese: 我妈妈, 我们学校. English still needs the possessive determiner: *my mother*, *our school*.
:::

::: history
The *'s* ending goes back to the Old English genitive case ending *-es*: Old English *cyninges* meant ‘of the king’. Most other case endings disappeared in Middle English, but this one survived and spread to almost all nouns. The apostrophe is much later, a printers' convention of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, at first used to show that a letter had been left out; for a time writers even believed that *'s* was a short form of *his*, and phrases such as *for Jesus Christ his sake*, in the Book of Common Prayer, show the idea. The apostrophe after plurals (*the students' books*) became established only in the eighteenth century. The ending also changed its place: in older English it was usually attached to the main noun of the possessor, with the rest of the phrase following the head noun, and the modern group genitive, with *'s* at the end of the whole possessor phrase (*the King of Spain's visit*), became usual in Early Modern English.
:::

## Where this leads

The possessive is one of several determiners, and its place among them — before numbers (*my two brothers*), after *all* and *both* (*all my friends*) — is in [[nouns-and-articles/other-determiners]]. Noun + noun phrases and other modifiers, before and after the noun, are the subject of [[nouns-and-articles/noun-phrases]]. Apostrophes in general, including *it's* and *its*, are covered in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/punctuation]].

::: summary
- English has three structures where Chinese has 的: *'s* (*my father's car*), *of* (*the end of the film*) and noun + noun (*the car door*).
- A possessive takes the place of the article; *'s* goes at the end of the whole possessor phrase: *the King of Spain's visit*, *Li Wei and Wang Fang's flat* ([[#rule-genitive-determiner]]).
- *'s* is usual for people, animals, organisations, places and times (*China's economy*, *today's news*, *a week's holiday*); *of* for things, ideas and long possessors (*the top of the page*, *the name of the woman who lives upstairs*) ([[#rule-s-or-of]]).
- Noun + noun says what kind: *a coffee cup* (for coffee) vs *a cup of coffee* (containing coffee) ([[#rule-noun-noun]]).
- Use *a friend of mine* or *one of my friends*, never ✗ *a my friend* ([[#rule-double-genitive]]).
- A possessive can stand alone for homes and shops: *at Wang Fang's*, *at the dentist's* (also *at the dentist*) ([[#rule-independent]]).
:::
