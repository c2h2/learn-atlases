Li Wei is telling a friend about her weekend:

> *On Saturday I went to a small café near the station. A man at the next table was reading a book in Chinese. The man looked up and asked me where I was from, and the book turned out to be a novel by Lu Xun.*

Why *a man* the first time and *the man* the second? Why *the station* when the friend has never been told about any station? And why *a novel* when the book has already been mentioned? Chinese tells this story without a single article: 我去了车站附近的一家小咖啡馆。邻桌有个男人在看一本中文书。那个人抬起头…… English must choose between *a* and *the* every time a singular countable noun appears, and the choice is not random. It depends on one question: **can the listener tell which one I mean?**

This lesson explains that question and the main situations in which the answer is yes or no. It builds on [[nouns-and-articles/countable-uncountable]], because *a* goes only with singular countable nouns. Two further lessons complete the system: [[nouns-and-articles/zero-article-generic]] (no article, and talking about whole classes) and [[nouns-and-articles/articles-with-names]].

## Form: *a*, *an* and *the*

::: definition Articles {#def-articles}
The **articles** are the determiners *a*/*an* and *the*. ***The*** is the **definite article**; it is used with singular, plural and uncountable nouns: *the book*, *the books*, *the water*. ***A*** and ***an*** are two forms of the **indefinite article**; they are used only with singular countable nouns: *a book*, *an apple*. When a noun phrase has no article or other determiner (*books*, *water*), we speak of the **zero article**.
:::

::: theorem *A* or *an*: the first sound decides {#rule-a-an}
Use ***a*** before a word that begins with a **consonant sound**, and ***an*** before a word that begins with a **vowel sound**. What counts is the sound of the next word, not the first letter and not the noun itself.
:::

| *a* (consonant sound) | *an* (vowel sound) |
|---|---|
| *a book*, *a cat*, *a house* | *an apple*, *an egg*, *an orange* |
| *a university* /ˌjuː-/, *a European* /ˌjʊə-/ (GA /ˌjʊr-/), *a uniform* | *an umbrella* /ʌm-/, *an uncle* |
| *a one-way ticket* /wʌn-/, *a once-famous actor* | *an hour* /aʊə/ (GA /aʊr/), *an honest man*, *an heir* (silent *h*) |
| *a UFO* /ˌjuː ef ˈəʊ/ | *an MP* /em-/, *an X-ray* /eks-/, *an FAQ* (said as letters) |
| *a big apple* (the next word is *big*) | *an old book* (the next word is *old*) |

::: proof
*A* and *an* are the same word. Its older form was *an* (from Old English *ān* ‘one’); before a consonant the *n* was dropped, because *a* + consonant is easier to say, while *an* + vowel avoids two vowels meeting. So the rule is about pronunciation, and spelling misleads in two directions: written *u* and *eu* often begin with the consonant /j/ (*a university*), and written *h* is silent in a few common words (*an hour*, *an honest*, *an honour*, *an heir*). Abbreviations depend on how they are said: *an MBA* (letters: /em-/), but *a NATO meeting* (a word: /ˈneɪtəʊ/). With *historic* and *hotel*, older and some formal British usage has *an* (*an historic event*), but *a historic*, with the *h* pronounced, is now normal in both British and American English (Swan 2016).
:::

The definite article also changes with the next sound, but only in pronunciation: it is usually /ðə/ before a consonant sound (*the book* /ðə bʊk/) and /ði/ before a vowel sound (*the apple* /ði ˈæpl/), though many speakers use /ðə/ in both places. When it is stressed for emphasis, it is /ðiː/: *This is **the** place to eat in Chengdu* (‘the best, the famous one’).

::: example *A* or *an*? {#ex-a-an}
Choose *a* or *an* and give the first sound of the next word: (a) ___ useful tip (b) ___ hour later (c) ___ easy question (d) ___ university student (e) ___ SMS message (f) ___ unusual name (g) ___ house (h) ___ European city
::: solution
Say the next word aloud and listen to its first sound ([[#rule-a-an]]).

(a) *a useful tip*: /j/, a consonant sound. (b) *an hour later*: /aʊ/, the *h* is silent. (c) *an easy question*: /iː/. (d) *a university student*: /j/. (e) *an SMS message*: the letters are said /es em es/, beginning with the vowel /e/. (f) *an unusual name*: /ʌ/. (g) *a house*: /h/ is pronounced. (h) *a European city*: /j/.
:::
:::

## What *the* means

::: definition Definite and indefinite noun phrases {#def-definite}
A noun phrase is **definite** when the speaker assumes that the listener can **identify** which thing or things are meant. It is **indefinite** when the speaker does not assume this — because the thing is new to the listener, or because it does not matter which one. *The* marks a noun phrase as definite; *a*/*an* (and the zero article) mark it as indefinite.
:::

Identifying does not mean having seen the thing before. If I say *The woman who lives next door is a doctor*, you may never have heard of her, but the words *who lives next door* tell you exactly which woman I mean. And it does not mean that only one such thing exists in the world: there are millions of doors, but in *Close the door* only one is relevant here and now.

::: theorem When the listener can identify the thing: use *the* {#rule-the-uses}
Use ***the*** when the listener can tell which thing you mean. The usual reasons are:

1. **It has been mentioned before.** *I bought **a** shirt and **a** jacket. **The** jacket was cheap.*
2. **It is in the situation we share.** *Can you pass **the** salt?* *Close **the** window, please.*
3. **It is known in our wider world** — our town, our country, our home: ***the** station*, ***the** government*, ***the** kitchen*, ***the** police*.
4. **There is only one** (in the world or in the context): ***the** sun*, ***the** moon*, ***the** sky*, ***the** internet*, ***the** environment*, ***the** capital*, ***the** president* (of a country already in mind).
5. **It belongs to something already mentioned.** *We went to **a** wedding. **The** bride was a friend from school.* (A wedding has a bride.)
6. **The words after the noun identify it.** ***The** man **who called yesterday** …*, ***the** top **of the hill***, ***the** book **on the table***, ***the** capital **of Japan***.
7. **The words before the noun make it unique:** superlatives and *only*, *same*, *first*, *second*, *next*, *last* (in a series): ***the** best restaurant*, ***the** only way*, ***the** same day*, ***the** first time*, ***the** next stop*.
:::

::: proof
The seven cases are seven ways in which one condition is met: the listener has enough information to pick out the referent. Christophersen (1939) called this *familiarity*; Hawkins (1978) described it as locating the referent in a set shared by speaker and listener; Huddleston & Pullum (2002) use the word *identifiable*. The reference grammars list the same situations (Quirk et al. 1985; Swan 2016), sometimes with different names: case 1 is often called **anaphoric** (‘referring back’), case 2 **situational**, case 5 **associative** (or *bridging*). Case 7 works because a superlative or *only* says that one thing alone fits the description — so the listener can identify it, even without knowing it. Note what *the* does **not** depend on: it does not depend on the thing being near (that is *this*), on the thing being important, or on the noun being singular.
:::

::: intuition
Think of *the* as an instruction to the listener: "You can find this one — look in what we have said, around us, or in what we both know." *A* is the opposite instruction: "Don't look for it; I'm giving you something new" — or "any one will do".
:::

::: example Why *the*? {#ex-the-reasons}
Say which reason in [[#rule-the-uses]] explains each *the*. (a) *Turn off **the** lights when you leave.* (b) *I read an article about plastic. **The** writer says we should stop using bags.* (c) *Who was **the** first person to climb Everest?* (d) *I ordered a pizza and a salad. **The** salad never arrived.* (e) *We need to protect **the** environment.* (f) *Do you know **the** girl in the red coat?*
::: solution
Ask, for each *the*, where the listener finds the information.

(a) Reason 2: the lights of the room the speakers are in.

(b) Reason 5: an article has a writer, so *the writer* means ‘the writer of that article’.

(c) Reason 7: *first* makes it unique.

(d) Reason 1: *a salad* was mentioned in the first sentence.

(e) Reason 4: there is one environment we all share.

(f) Reason 6: *in the red coat* identifies which girl.
:::
:::

- [ ] *Music was wonderful.*

A concert has music, so *the music* refers to the music of that concert (reason 5, associative use). *A music* is impossible because *music* is uncountable; *Music was wonderful* would be about music in general; *A concert was wonderful* introduces a new concert instead of continuing about the one already mentioned.

## What *a* means

::: theorem When to use *a*/*an* {#rule-a-uses}
Use ***a*/*an*** with a **singular countable noun** when:

1. **the thing is new to the listener** (first mention): *There's **a** cat in the garden.* *I've got **an** idea.*
2. **it does not matter which one** (any one): *Can I borrow **a** pen?* *She wants to buy **a** bike.*
3. **you say what kind of thing something is** — especially after *be* and *as*, for jobs, roles and descriptions: *She's **an** engineer.* *He works as **a** waiter.* *It's **a** good idea.* *What **a** beautiful day!*
4. **you mean ‘one’ or ‘each’** in numbers and rates: ***a** hundred*, ***a** dozen*, *twice **a** week*, *60 kilometres **an** hour*, *five yuan **a** kilo*.
:::

::: proof
*A* says that the listener cannot, or need not, identify the thing. In case 1 the speaker knows which cat but the listener does not; in case 2 even the speaker has no particular pen in mind. Case 3 is a different job: *She's an engineer* does not introduce an engineer, it puts her into the class of engineers. English requires the article here, while Chinese does not (她是工程师), which makes case 3 one of the most frequent places for a missing article. Case 4 continues the old meaning ‘one’; *per* is a more formal equivalent (*60 kilometres per hour*). *A* and *one* differ: *one* is a number and is stressed, used when the number matters (*I said **one** coffee, not two*), while *a* is unstressed and simply introduces the noun.
:::

- *I'm looking for **a** flat near the university.* (any flat that fits — I don't have one in mind yet)
- *I'm looking for **the** flat that we saw last week.* (one particular flat we both know)
- *My sister is **a** nurse, and her husband is **a** police officer.*
- *We meet twice **a** month.*

::: remark
Sometimes *a* is ambiguous between case 1 and case 2. *Wang Fang wants to marry a doctor* can mean that she has a particular doctor in mind (whom you do not know), or that she would like her future husband to be a doctor, whoever he is. Context usually decides; you can make the first reading clear with *a doctor she met in Beijing*, and the second with *any doctor*.
:::

::: warning A singular countable noun without an article
✗ *I am student.* ✓ *I am a student.*

✗ *She bought new phone yesterday.* ✓ *She bought a new phone yesterday.*

✗ *Please close door.* ✓ *Please close the door.*

✗ *He is very good teacher.* ✓ *He is a very good teacher.*

A singular countable noun in English almost always needs a determiner in front of it: an article, or a word like *my*, *this* or *every*. A useful habit when you edit: find each singular countable noun and check that something stands before it — and remember that the article comes before the adjective (*a new phone*, not ✗ *new a phone*). The exceptions are fixed expressions such as *by bus*, *at home* and *go to school*, which are listed in [[nouns-and-articles/zero-article-generic]].
:::

## *A* and *the* together in a text

In a text, things are usually introduced with *a* and then followed with *the* (or a pronoun). This is the pattern of Li Wei's story at the start of the lesson. But the change from *a* to *the* is not automatic, because *the* is chosen only when the listener can identify the thing, and *a* can be used again for a different, new thing of the same kind.

::: example Tracking a story {#ex-story}
Complete the story with *a*, *an* or *the*, and give a reason for each choice. *Last week Kenji bought (1) ___ old bicycle from (2) ___ neighbour. (3) ___ bicycle was cheap, but (4) ___ brakes didn't work. He took it to (5) ___ repair shop in our street. (6) ___ man in (7) ___ shop said it was (8) ___ easy job.*
::: solution
(1) *an*: the bicycle is new to the reader; *an* because *old* begins with a vowel sound ([[#rule-a-uses]], case 1).

(2) *a*: one of his neighbours, not identified.

(3) *The*: the bicycle already mentioned (reason 1).

(4) *the*: a bicycle has brakes, so the reader knows which brakes (reason 5, associative).

(5) *a*: the shop is new to the reader. (*the repair shop in our street* would also be possible if there is only one, because *in our street* would then identify it — reason 6.)

(6) *The*: the shop has someone working in it; *in the shop* identifies him (reasons 5 and 6).

(7) *the*: the shop mentioned in the previous sentence (reason 1).

(8) *an*: *easy job* says what kind of job it was (case 3); *an* before the vowel sound of *easy*.
:::
:::

::: warning *The* is not a translation of 那 or 这
✗ *I went to the Shanghai last year and visited the famous museum there.* (when the listener does not know which museum)

✓ *I went to Shanghai last year and visited a famous museum there.*

Learners who have been taught that *the* means 这/那 sometimes put *the* before anything that seems definite to the speaker. But the test is what the listener can identify. *A famous museum* is right if the listener does not know which one; *the Shanghai Museum* is right because the name identifies it. (*Shanghai* itself takes no article: see [[nouns-and-articles/articles-with-names]].) English *this* and *that* are closer to 这 and 那; *the* is weaker and much more frequent.
:::

Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *My father is engineer in a big company.*
- [ ] *She is the best student in a class.*
- [x] *Is there a bank near here?*
- [ ] *Can you open a window? It's hot in here.*

*Is there a bank near here?* asks about any bank, new to both speakers. *My father is **an** engineer* needs *an* (case 3). *The best student in **the** class* — the class is identified by the situation (the speakers' class). *Open **the** window* refers to a window in the room the speakers share; *a window* is possible only if the room has several windows and any one will do.

::: note English and Chinese
- **No articles.** Mandarin has no words that correspond to *a* and *the* (Li & Thompson 1981). A bare noun such as 书 can be definite, indefinite or general, and the listener works out which from the context: 书在桌子上 (*The book is on the table*), 我想买书 (*I want to buy a book / some books*).
- **Word order does part of the job.** Chinese tends to put definite, known things before the verb and new, indefinite things after it. 朱德熙 (1982) describes a strong tendency for the subject to be definite and the object indefinite; Li & Thompson (1981) discuss the same contrast. Compare 客人来了 (*The guests have arrived* — guests we were expecting) with 来客人了 (*Some guests have come* — new to the listener). English keeps the same order in both and uses the article instead.
- **一 + classifier is close to *a*.** 我买了一本书 → *I bought a book*. 有 introduces new things, much like English *there is* + *a*: 门口有一个人 → *There's a man at the door*. But Chinese often leaves out 一个 where English must use *a*: 他是老师 → *He is a teacher*; 我有车 → *I have a car*.
- **这 and 那 are not *the*.** 那本书 can be *the book* or *that book*, but English uses *the* in many places where Chinese needs no demonstrative at all: 关门 → *Close the door*; 太阳出来了 → *The sun has come out*. Learners therefore both leave *the* out (where Chinese has a bare noun) and put it in too often (where they think "那").
- **What to do.** Because nothing in Chinese forces the choice, the decision has to become a conscious habit: for each common noun, ask whether it is countable, whether it is singular, and whether the listener can identify it. Reading with attention to articles, and copying whole phrases (*in the morning*, *on the left*, *a lot of*), helps the habit become automatic.
:::

::: example From Chinese to English {#ex-translate}
Translate, choosing articles, and explain each choice. (a) 我姐姐是医生。 (b) 请把灯打开。 (c) 我昨天买了一件毛衣，可是毛衣太小了。 (d) 外面有一只狗。 (e) 月亮今晚很亮。
::: solution
For each noun ask: countable? singular? can the listener identify it?

(a) *My sister is a doctor.* — *doctor* is singular countable, and says what kind of person she is (case 3 of [[#rule-a-uses]]). Chinese has no 一个 here; English needs *a*.

(b) *Please turn on the light.* — the light in the room the speakers are in (reason 2 of [[#rule-the-uses]]).

(c) *I bought a sweater yesterday, but it was too small.* or *… but the sweater was too small.* — first mention *a*, second mention *the* (reason 1); a pronoun (*it*) is the more natural way to refer back.

(d) *There's a dog outside.* — 有 introduces something new: *there is* + *a* (case 1).

(e) *The moon is very bright tonight.* — only one moon (reason 4).
:::
:::

::: history
Old English had no true articles. It used a demonstrative, *se* (masculine), *sēo* (feminine), *þæt* (neuter), with many case forms, where modern English uses *that* or *the*; and the numeral *ān* ‘one’ was beginning to be used for introducing new things. In late Old English and early Middle English the demonstrative was gradually replaced, in its weak article use, by an unchanging form *þe*, the ancestor of *the*, while *þæt* survived as the demonstrative *that*. In Middle English *ān* split into the stressed numeral *one* and the unstressed article *an*, which lost its *n* before consonants. The boundary between *a* and *an* even moved inside some words: *a napron* became *an apron*, *a nadder* became *an adder*, and *a noumpere* became *an umpire*, because listeners heard the *n* as part of the article. Many languages, including Latin, Russian, Japanese and Chinese, have no articles; others, like English, French and German, developed them from demonstratives and from the numeral ‘one’.
:::

## Where this leads

This lesson covered singular countable nouns, where the choice is between *a* and *the*. Plural and uncountable nouns add a third possibility, no article, and talking about whole classes (*Cats sleep a lot*, *The tiger is endangered*) has its own rules: both are in [[nouns-and-articles/zero-article-generic]]. Names of people and places follow conventions of their own ([[nouns-and-articles/articles-with-names]]). *This*, *that* and the other determiners that can replace *the* are in [[nouns-and-articles/other-determiners]], and the way *a* and *the* help a reader follow who is who across a text is part of [[reading-skills/following-the-thread]].

::: summary
- *A*/*an* goes only with singular countable nouns; *the* goes with any noun ([[#def-articles]]).
- Choose *a* or *an* by the first **sound** of the next word: *a university*, *an hour*, *an MP* ([[#rule-a-an]]).
- Use *the* when the listener can identify which one: mentioned before, present in the situation, known to both, unique, linked to something mentioned, identified by the words after it, or made unique by a superlative, *only*, *first*, *same* or *next* ([[#rule-the-uses]]).
- Use *a*/*an* for something new to the listener, for "any one", for saying what kind of thing something is (*She's an engineer*), and for ‘one’ or ‘per’ ([[#rule-a-uses]]).
- A singular countable noun almost always needs a determiner: ✗ *I am student*.
- *The* is not a translation of 这/那: the test is what the listener can identify, not what the speaker knows.
- Chinese signals definiteness through word order, 一 + classifier, 有 and demonstratives; English uses the article in every noun phrase.
:::

::: exercise Tell a short story {level=3}
Write a short story of six to eight sentences about something that happened on a journey (a train, a bus, a plane). Introduce at least three people or things with *a*/*an*, refer back to each with *the*, and use *the* at least once for something linked to what you have mentioned (a train has a driver, a ticket has a price). Then underline each article and write the reason for it.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

*Last month I took a night train from Beijing to Xi'an. A woman in my compartment was carrying a large box. The box started to move during the night, and the woman laughed and said it was a cat. The cat was going to live with her daughter. In the morning the conductor brought us tea, and the cat finally fell asleep. When the train arrived, the daughter was waiting on the platform with a big smile.*

Reasons: *a night train*, *a woman*, *a large box*, *a cat*, *a big smile* — new to the reader. *The box*, *the woman*, *the cat* — mentioned before. *The night*, *the morning* — parts of the journey already in the story. *The conductor*, *the platform* — linked to the train (associative). *The daughter* — linked to *her daughter* already mentioned. *The train* — mentioned before.
