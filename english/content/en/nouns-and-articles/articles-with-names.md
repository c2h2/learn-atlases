Zhang Wei is planning a trip and writes to a friend:

> *Next summer I'm going to the United States and Canada. I want to see the Rocky Mountains, sail on Lake Michigan, walk across the Golden Gate Bridge and visit Yellowstone National Park. On the way back I'll stop in the Philippines.*

There are four *the*'s here and five names without any article. Nothing in the last two lessons explains the difference: every one of these places is unique, so the listener can identify all of them. Names follow conventions of their own. The good news is that the conventions are regular enough to learn as a small number of patterns — *the* with plural names, with rivers and seas, with names built on words like *republic* or *museum*, and with *of*-phrases; no article with most other names — plus a list of exceptions that you meet again and again.

This lesson covers the names of people, places, buildings and organisations. It builds on [[nouns-and-articles/a-and-the]] and [[nouns-and-articles/zero-article-generic]]. As in those lessons, "no article" is written as –.

## Names of people

::: definition Proper noun and name {#def-proper}
A **proper noun** is a noun that names one particular person, place or thing and is written with a capital letter: *Li Wei*, *Shanghai*, *Monday*. A **name** may consist of a proper noun alone (*Shanghai*) or of several words, some of them ordinary nouns (*the Yellow River*, *Peking University*).
:::

::: theorem Articles with people's names {#rule-people}
1. People's names take **no article**, with or without a title: *Li Wei*, *Dr Chen*, *Professor Smith*, *President Lincoln*, *Aunt Mei*. A title alone, without the name, is an ordinary noun and takes *the*: *the president*, *the professor*.
2. **The + plural family name** means the whole family: *the Wangs*, *the Johnsons*. *We're having dinner with the Wangs.*
3. ***The*** is used when a name needs to be distinguished: *Is that the Li Wei who studied with us, or a different one?* Stressed *the* /ðiː/ means ‘the famous one’: *You met Yao Ming? Not **the** Yao Ming?*
4. ***A*** means ‘a person called’, for someone the speaker does not know: *There's a Ms Patel on the phone for you.*
:::

::: proof
A name already identifies its person in the context, so *the* would add nothing (Quirk et al. 1985; Swan 2016). The exceptions are the cases in which the name stops working as a unique label and becomes an ordinary noun again: a family is a group of people with the same name (*the Wangs*), several people may share a name (*the Li Wei who …*), and a name the speaker has never heard of is just "someone with this name" (*a Ms Patel*). Titles used with a name (*Dr*, *Mr*, *Ms*, *President*, *Uncle*) are part of the name; British English writes *Mr*, *Mrs*, *Ms*, *Dr* without a full stop, American English usually with one (*Mr.*, *Dr.*).
:::

::: remark
**Chinese names in English.** Chinese names are written family name first: *Li Wei*, *Wang Fang*. In English this order is usually kept for people from China, so the polite form uses the family name: *Mr Li*, *Ms Wang*, not ✗ *Mr Wei* (that would be his given name). Some Chinese people living or working abroad reverse the order (*Wei Li*) or use an English given name (*David Li*), so the safest course is to ask, or to follow the way the person writes their own name.
:::

::: example Articles with people {#ex-people}
Explain the article (or the lack of one) in each sentence. (a) *We're spending Sunday with the Lius.* (b) *A Dr Moreau rang while you were out.* (c) *Doctor Lin will see you now.* (d) *The doctor will see you now.* (e) *She works with a Wang Fang — not the Wang Fang who was in our class.*
::: solution
Start from the default ([[#rule-people]], rule 1): a person's name has no article. Then ask whether the name is being used as an ordinary noun.

(a) *the Lius*: plural family name = the Liu family (rule 2).

(b) *A Dr Moreau*: the speaker does not know him; ‘someone called Dr Moreau’ (rule 4).

(c) *Doctor Lin*: title + name, no article (rule 1).

(d) *The doctor*: a title without a name is an ordinary noun; the listener can identify which doctor from the situation (the clinic they are in).

(e) *a Wang Fang*: one person with this name, new to the listener (rule 4); *the Wang Fang who was in our class*: the relative clause identifies a particular person with the name (rule 3).
:::
:::

## Names of places

The table summarises the usual patterns. It is followed by the rules that explain most of them.

| no article | *the* |
|---|---|
| continents: *Asia*, *Africa*, *Europe* | plural names: *the United States*, *the Netherlands*, *the Philippines* |
| most countries: *China*, *Japan*, *Brazil*, *Ukraine* | names with *republic*, *kingdom*, *union*, *states*, *emirates*: *the People's Republic of China*, *the United Kingdom*, *the Czech Republic*, *the United Arab Emirates* |
| states, provinces, cities: *Guangdong*, *Texas*, *Chengdu*, *London* | rivers: *the Yangtze*, *the Yellow River*, *the Thames*, *the Amazon* |
| single mountains: *Mount Everest*, *Mount Tai*, *Kilimanjaro* | mountain ranges: *the Himalayas*, *the Alps*, *the Rocky Mountains* (*the Rockies*) |
| single islands: *Hainan*, *Sicily*, *Taiwan* | groups of islands: *the Canary Islands*, *the Maldives* |
| lakes: *Lake Geneva*, *Lake Baikal*, *Qinghai Lake* | seas, oceans, canals, deserts: *the Pacific (Ocean)*, *the South China Sea*, *the Grand Canal*, *the Gobi (Desert)* |
| streets and squares: *Nanjing Road*, *Oxford Street*, *Times Square* | regions named by direction: *the Middle East*, *the north of China* |
| parks, stations, airports: *Hyde Park*, *Beijing West Station*, *Heathrow Airport* | names with *of*: *the Temple of Heaven*, *the Great Wall of China*, *the University of Hong Kong* |

::: theorem Places that take *the* {#rule-places-the}
Use ***the*** with a place name when:

1. **the name is plural:** *the Netherlands*, *the Alps*, *the Bahamas*, *the Great Lakes*.
2. **the name contains an ordinary noun for a political unit or a group** — *republic*, *kingdom*, *union*, *states*, *federation*, *emirates*: *the Republic of Korea*, *the United Kingdom*, *the European Union*.
3. **it is a river, sea, ocean, canal or desert** (even when the common noun is left out): *the Mekong*, *the Red Sea*, *the Atlantic*, *the Panama Canal*, *the Sahara*.
4. **the name contains *of***: *the Gulf of Mexico*, *the Bay of Bengal*, *the Cape of Good Hope*.
5. **it is a region named with a direction or a general noun:** *the Far East*, *the south of France*, *the Arctic*, *the Mediterranean*.
:::

::: theorem Places that take no article {#rule-places-zero}
Use **no article** with continents, most countries, states and provinces, cities and towns, streets and squares, single mountains (*Mount* + name), single islands, lakes (*Lake* + name), and parks, stations and airports named after a place or person.
:::

::: proof
The two rules follow one tendency. A name that is a proper noun alone (*China*, *Everest*, *Hainan*) or that combines a proper noun with a word like *Mount*, *Lake*, *Street*, *Park* or *Station* placed **before** or **after** it as a label (*Mount Tai*, *Lake Geneva*, *Oxford Street*) behaves like a person's name: no article. A name that is grammatically a description — a plural (*the United States*, literally ‘the united states’), a common noun with modifiers (*the United Kingdom*, *the Czech Republic*), or a phrase with *of* (*the Bay of Bengal*) — keeps the *the* that such a description would have. Rivers, seas and oceans are a convention: they take *the* even when the common noun is dropped (*the Yangtze*). These patterns are tendencies, not laws; Swan (2016) and Quirk et al. (1985) give them with their exceptions, and the safe way to check a particular name is to see how it is written in a dictionary or atlas.
:::

A few frequent names are exceptions, or have changed:

- *the Hague* (the Dutch city; written *The Hague* with a capital *T*), *the Bund* (in Shanghai), *the Strand* (in London), *the High Street* (BrE, the main shopping street of a town).
- *the Gambia* keeps *the*, which is part of the country's official English name.
- *Ukraine* and *Sudan* were once often called *the Ukraine* and *the Sudan*; the forms without *the* are now standard, and *the Ukraine* is considered dated or incorrect.
- *the Congo* (the river, and in some uses the country), but *the Democratic Republic of the Congo* and *the Republic of the Congo* by rule 2.

::: warning *The* with countries and cities
✗ *I'm from the China.* ✓ *I'm from China.*

✗ *She lives in the Shanghai.* ✓ *She lives in Shanghai.*

✗ *We visited United States last year.* ✓ *We visited the United States last year.*

✗ *He studied in the UK and the Japan.* ✓ *He studied in the UK and Japan.*

The first two errors come from treating *the* as "known, unique"; the third from treating *United States* like *China*. *The UK* and *the US* (or *the USA*) keep *the* because they are short forms of *the United Kingdom* and *the United States*.
:::

## Buildings, institutions and organisations

::: theorem Buildings and organisations {#rule-buildings}
1. **Many famous buildings, museums, theatres, hotels and landmarks take *the*:** *the Forbidden City*, *the Great Wall*, *the Eiffel Tower*, *the British Museum*, *the Louvre*, *the Peace Hotel*, *the Globe Theatre*, *the Golden Gate Bridge*.
2. **Names in the form *place or person* + *noun* often take no article**, especially for universities, stations, airports, parks, streets, cathedrals and palaces: *Peking University*, *Harvard University*, *Pudong Airport*, *Central Park*, *Buckingham Palace*, *Westminster Abbey*. Compare the *of* form, with *the*: *the University of Hong Kong*, *the Palace of Westminster*.
3. **Names with a possessive *'s*** take no article: *St Paul's Cathedral*, *McDonald's*, *Harrods* (originally *Harrod's*).
4. **Organisations:** names that are descriptions take *the* (*the United Nations*, *the World Health Organization*, *the Red Cross*); abbreviations **said as letters** keep *the* (*the BBC*, *the UN*, *the EU*, *the WHO*); abbreviations **said as words** take no article (*NASA*, *UNESCO*, *NATO*, *FIFA*). Company names take no article: *Huawei*, *Toyota*, *Microsoft*.
5. **Newspapers** usually take *the*, often as part of the title: *The Times*, *The Guardian*, *The New York Times*. Many magazines and journals do not: *Nature*, *Science*, *Time*.
:::

::: proof
Rule 1 is the descriptive pattern again: *the Forbidden City* is a city described as forbidden; *the British Museum* is a museum described as British. Rule 2 is the label pattern: *Peking* or *Harvard* is a name, and *University* a label after it, so the whole behaves like a proper noun, while *the University of Hong Kong* is a description with *of*. Rule 3 works because a possessive is itself a determiner, which leaves no room for another ([[nouns-and-articles/possessives-and-of]]). In rule 4, an abbreviation said as letters still feels like the description it stands for (*the B*ritish *B*roadcasting *C*orporation), while one said as a word (*NASA* /ˈnæsə/) has become a name. There are exceptions in every group — the official name of a museum or hotel may or may not include *the* — so for a particular name follow the way the institution writes it.
:::

::: example *Of* or no *of*? {#ex-of-names}
Give each name the right article. Then rewrite it in the other form where English allows it. (a) *___ Tsinghua University* (b) *___ University of Cambridge* (c) *___ Museum of Modern Art* (d) *___ Shanghai Museum* (e) *___ Bank of China* (f) *___ Tate Modern*
::: solution
Use [[#rule-buildings]]: a name + label takes no article; a description with *of*, or a descriptive name, takes *the*.

(a) *Tsinghua University* (name + label). An *of* form is not used for this name.

(b) *the University of Cambridge* (description with *of*). The other form, *Cambridge University*, is also widely used, with no article.

(c) *the Museum of Modern Art* (with *of*); no other form.

(d) *the Shanghai Museum* — a descriptive name (a museum described as Shanghai's), which is how it is usually written in English, unlike rule 2's universities. This shows why individual names must be checked.

(e) *the Bank of China* (with *of*).

(f) *Tate Modern* — the gallery's own name has no *the* (it is a name + label; the Tate galleries are named after Henry Tate, who founded the first one). In speech *the Tate Modern* is also heard, but the gallery itself writes *Tate Modern*.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Names with built-in generic words.** Chinese place names usually contain their generic word: 长江 (*jiāng* ‘river’), 泰山 (*shān* ‘mountain’), 太湖 (*hú* ‘lake’). In English the generic word is often translated and the article rule follows the English word: 长江 → *the Yangtze* or *the Yangtze River* (also *the Chang Jiang*), 黄河 → *the Yellow River*, 泰山 → *Mount Tai*, 青海湖 → *Qinghai Lake*. Official English names sometimes keep the Chinese generic word and add an English one (*Mount Huangshan*, *the Huangpu River*), which looks doubled but is common and accepted.
- **No article in Chinese, one rule in English.** Chinese never marks names with an article, so the default for Chinese speakers is no article — which is right for people, most countries, cities, provinces, mountains and lakes. The places to add *the* are the plural and descriptive names and the rivers and seas: *the United States*, *the People's Republic of China*, *the Yellow Sea*.
- **Translated landmark names.** Many Chinese landmarks have descriptive English names and therefore take *the*: 故宫 → *the Forbidden City* (or *the Palace Museum*), 长城 → *the Great Wall*, 天坛 → *the Temple of Heaven*, 外滩 → *the Bund*. Names that are transliterated, or name + label, do not: 天安门广场 → *Tiananmen Square*, 北京大学 → *Peking University*, 颐和园 → *the Summer Palace* (descriptive again).
- **Name order.** 李伟 is *Li Wei* in English, family name first; 李先生 is *Mr Li*. See the remark under [[#rule-people]].
:::

::: example Correcting a travel blog {#ex-travel}
Correct the article mistakes in this paragraph from a travel blog. There are seven.

*Last spring I travelled around the Yunnan with my friend. We flew from the Beijing to Kunming and then took a train to Dali, near Erhai Lake. From there we drove north towards Himalayas and saw Jinsha River, which later becomes Yangtze. On the way home we spent two days in the Hong Kong and visited Hong Kong Museum of History.*
::: solution
Check each name against [[#rule-places-the]], [[#rule-places-zero]] and [[#rule-buildings]].

1. *the Yunnan* → *Yunnan*: a province, no article.
2. *the Beijing* → *Beijing*: a city.
3. *towards Himalayas* → *towards the Himalayas*: a mountain range, plural.
4. *saw Jinsha River* → *saw the Jinsha River*: a river.
5. *becomes Yangtze* → *becomes the Yangtze*: a river.
6. *in the Hong Kong* → *in Hong Kong*: a city.
7. *visited Hong Kong Museum of History* → *visited the Hong Kong Museum of History*: a descriptive name with *of*.

*Kunming*, *Dali* and *Erhai Lake* are correct as they stand (two cities and a lake). (AmE spelling: *traveled*.)
:::

::: history
Several names show the history of the article. *The Hague* translates the Dutch name *Den Haag*, short for *'s-Gravenhage* ‘the count's hedge’; the article came from Dutch and is kept with a capital *T*. Some countries were long named with *the* in English because they were thought of as regions rather than states: *the Argentine* for Argentina, *the Lebanon*, *the Sudan* and *the Ukraine* all appear in older British writing. As these countries became, or became widely recognised as, independent states, English followed their own usage, and the forms without *the* became standard. *The Gambia* went the other way: the country kept *the* in its official English name, which is often explained as a way of distinguishing it from the river of the same name. Rivers, on the other hand, have taken *the* in English for centuries (*the Thames*, *the Severn*), and that convention has not changed.
:::

## Where this leads

Possessive names such as *St Paul's* and *McDonald's* are part of the larger story of *'s* in [[nouns-and-articles/possessives-and-of]]. ::: summary
- People's names take no article (*Li Wei*, *Dr Chen*); *the* + plural family name means the family (*the Wangs*); *a* means ‘someone called’ (*a Ms Patel*) ([[#rule-people]]).
- Chinese names keep family name first in English: *Li Wei*, *Mr Li*.
- No article with continents, most countries, provinces, cities, streets, single mountains, lakes, parks and airports: *China*, *Mount Tai*, *Lake Geneva* ([[#rule-places-zero]]).
- *The* with plural names, names with *republic*/*kingdom*/*union*, rivers, seas, oceans, canals, deserts, mountain ranges, island groups and names with *of*: *the United States*, *the Yangtze*, *the Alps* ([[#rule-places-the]]).
- Descriptive building names take *the* (*the Forbidden City*, *the British Museum*); name + label often does not (*Peking University*, *Central Park*); compare *the University of Hong Kong* ([[#rule-buildings]]).
- Abbreviations said as letters take *the* (*the BBC*); those said as words do not (*NASA*); company names take no article.
- These are tendencies with exceptions (*the Hague*, *the Bund*, *the Gambia*): check individual names in a dictionary or atlas.
:::
