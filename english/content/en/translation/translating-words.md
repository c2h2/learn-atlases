A Chinese–English dictionary gives *opinion*, *view*, *idea*, *suggestion*, *objection* and *complaint* as English equivalents of 意见. Now look at four sentences:

- 你有什么意见？ — at the end of a presentation: *Do you have any comments?* / *What do you think?*
- 我对这个方案有意见。 — said with a frown: *I have some objections to this plan.*
- 邻居对我们很有意见。 — *The neighbours are very unhappy with us.* / *The neighbours have been complaining about us.*
- 请大家提意见。 — on a feedback form: *Please give us your feedback.* / *We welcome your suggestions.*

Not one of these is best translated by *opinion*, the first word in the dictionary. And the four English translations are not interchangeable: *Do you have any objections?* at the end of a presentation would sound hostile, and *Please give us your complaints* on a feedback form would sound strange. The Chinese word covers a range of meaning that English divides among several words, and the context — who is speaking, to whom, in what situation, with what tone — decides which part of the range is meant.

This lesson is about translating at the level of the word: finding the meaning in context, choosing among near-equivalents, translating collocations as units, keeping the positive or negative colouring of a word, dealing with words that have no equivalent, and handling terminology. It draws on Mona Baker's *In Other Words* (3rd edition, 2018), whose first chapters are about exactly this problem, and on the techniques of 词义的选择、引申和褒贬 taught in Zhang Peiji's 《英汉翻译教程》 (1980).

## Meaning in context

::: definition Sense, denotation and connotation {#def-sense}
A word usually has several **senses** (meanings), and a dictionary lists them separately. In a given context, normally only one sense is active. Within a sense, the **denotation** (概念意义) is what the word refers to, and the **connotation** (内涵意义) is the attitude, register and associations it carries: *slim* and *skinny* have the same denotation and different connotations ([[collocation-and-register/connotation]]).
:::

::: theorem Translate the sense, not the word {#rule-sense}
1. **Identify the active sense** of each word from its context: the sentence, the text, the situation and the type of text.
2. **Find the target word or phrase that expresses that sense** with the same connotation and register — which may be a word that the bilingual dictionary does not list.
3. **Check the choice in the target language**: in a monolingual dictionary, in a corpus, or by asking whether a native writer would use it in that sentence.
:::

::: proof
Words in two languages rarely divide meaning in the same way. A bilingual dictionary entry lists the equivalents of a word in its various senses, but it cannot list every context, and its order reflects frequency or history rather than what fits the sentence in front of the translator ([[core-vocabulary/using-a-dictionary]]). Baker (2018) calls the problem **non-equivalence at word level**, and points out that it is the normal case rather than the exception, even for everyday words. Step 3 matters most when translating into a foreign language, where the translator's sense of what sounds natural is least reliable.
:::

::: example One English word, many Chinese words {#ex-run}
Translate the verb *run* in each sentence. (a) *She runs every morning.* (b) *He runs a small restaurant.* (c) *The buses run every ten minutes.* (d) *The play ran for two years.* (e) *My nose is running.* (f) *Don't leave the tap running.*
::: solution
1. **Find the sense** from the subject and object: a person moving fast; a person managing a business; a vehicle operating; a performance continuing; liquid flowing; a tap left on.
2. **Choose the Chinese verb** that collocates with the subject:

(a) 她每天早上跑步。 (b) 他经营着一家小餐馆。 / 他开了一家小餐馆。 (c) 公交车每十分钟一班。 — no verb at all: the Chinese uses a frequency expression. (d) 这部戏连演了两年。 (e) 我流鼻涕了。 (f) 别让水龙头一直开着。 / 别忘了关水龙头。

The English verb is the same in every sentence; the Chinese never repeats one. Note (c) and (f): the best Chinese is not a verb meaning ‘run’ at all.
:::
:::

## Collocation

::: definition Collocation {#def-collocation}
A **collocation** is a combination of words that occur together more often than chance would predict and that native speakers feel as the normal way of saying something: *heavy rain*, *strong tea*, *make a mistake*, *a sharp rise* ([[collocation-and-register/what-is-collocation]]). Many collocations cannot be predicted from the meanings of their parts, and they differ between languages.
:::

::: theorem Translate collocations as units {#rule-collocation}
Translate a collocation **as a whole**, by the collocation the target language uses for the same meaning, not word by word:

| Chinese | ✗ word by word | ✓ English collocation |
|---|---|---|
| 浓茶 | *thick tea* | *strong tea* |
| 大雨 | *big rain* | *heavy rain* |
| 学习知识 | *learn knowledge* | *acquire knowledge*, *learn* |
| 提高水平 | *raise the level* | *improve* (one's English), *raise standards* |
| 犯错误 | *commit a mistake* | *make a mistake* |
| 严重的问题 | ✓ *a serious problem* (here word by word works) | |
| 开会 | *open a meeting* | *have / hold a meeting* |

And in the other direction: *make a decision* is 做决定 or 作出决定, but *make a speech* is 发表讲话 or 讲话, *make progress* is 取得进步, *make a fortune* is 发财.
:::

::: proof
Baker (2018) lists collocation among the problems of **equivalence above word level**: the translator must notice that a pair of words is a collocation (not a free combination), and then find the target collocation, which may use quite different words or none. Collocational errors are the most common kind of unnaturalness in translations by learners, because each word is individually correct and the combination is still wrong; a reader notices that something is "not English" or "不地道" without being able to say what. Corpora are the best check: if *learn knowledge* is rare in a large corpus of English and *acquire knowledge* common, the corpus has answered the question ([[collocation-and-register/using-a-corpus]]).
:::

::: example Correcting collocations in a draft {#ex-collocation}
A student's English translation of a school report reads: *This term Wang Lei made big progress. He studied a lot of knowledge, his level of English was raised, and he joined many activities. He should pay more efforts to maths.* Find the collocation errors and correct them.
::: solution
1. **Find the combinations** that are word-for-word renderings: *made big progress* (取得了很大进步), *studied a lot of knowledge* (学到了很多知识), *his level of English was raised* (英语水平提高了), *joined many activities* (参加了很多活动), *pay more efforts* (付出更多努力).
2. **Replace each with the English collocation** for the meaning:

*This term Wang Lei **made great progress**. He **learned a great deal**, his English **improved**, and he **took part in** many activities. He should **put more effort into** maths.*

Notes: *progress* collocates with *great*, *good* or *real*, not *big*; *knowledge* is rarely the object of *study* or *learn* — English says *learn a lot* or *gain knowledge*; *his English improved* is the natural way to say 水平提高了; *join* takes a club or a group (*join the choir*), while *take part in* takes activities; *effort* is usually uncountable after *put … into*.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which English phrase best translates 浓雾 in a weather report?
- [ ] *strong fog*
- [x] *thick fog* (or *dense fog*)
- [ ] *heavy fog* only in spoken English
- [ ] *deep fog*
::: solution
English says *thick* or *dense fog*; *heavy fog* also occurs, especially in American English. *Strong* collocates with *tea*, *coffee* and *wind*, not with *fog*. Note that 浓 is *strong* with tea but *thick* with fog: the Chinese word is the same, the English collocations differ.
:::
:::

## Specific and general words

The two languages also differ in **how finely** they divide a field of meaning. Chinese kinship terms are a well-known example: English *brother*, *sister*, *uncle*, *aunt* and *cousin* each correspond to several Chinese words that specify age relative to the speaker, and whether the relation is on the father's or the mother's side.

| English | Chinese |
|---|---|
| *brother* | 哥哥 (older), 弟弟 (younger) |
| *uncle* | 伯伯 / 伯父 (father's older brother), 叔叔 (father's younger brother), 舅舅 (mother's brother), 姑父, 姨父 (husbands of aunts) |
| *cousin* | 堂哥, 堂弟, 堂姐, 堂妹 (father's brother's children), 表哥, 表弟, 表姐, 表妹 (other cousins) |

In other fields English is the more specific language. English distinguishes *rice* (plant and grain), *cooked rice* and *a meal*, where Chinese has 稻, 米, 饭; but English has *borrow* and *lend* where Chinese uses 借 for both, and *teach* and *learn* where 学 and 教 match — while Chinese 借 and 租 (*rent*, *hire*, *let*) and English *wear*, *put on*, *carry* and *hold* (穿, 戴, 拿, 带) divide differently again.

::: theorem Translating between specific and general words {#rule-specific}
1. **Into the more general language**, use the general word unless the distinction matters in the context; if it matters, add it: 我表哥 → *my cousin* or, where it matters, *my cousin on my mother's side*, *my older cousin*.
2. **Into the more specific language**, the translator must choose, and may need information the source does not give: *my brother* → 哥哥 or 弟弟? Use context; if the text does not say, look elsewhere in it, ask the client or author, or use a neutral form (兄弟, 我家兄弟) where one exists.
3. **Do not invent precision.** If the source is vague, and the target language allows vagueness, keep it vague.
:::

::: proof
Baker (2018) lists **translation by a more general word (superordinate)** as the most common strategy for non-equivalence, and it works because the general word is accurate: a 表哥 is a cousin. Going the other way is harder, because the translator must decide something the original author did not state. English novels translated into Chinese constantly face this: was Elizabeth Bennet's sister Jane older or younger? (Older — the text says so; but for many characters it never does, and translators choose 姐姐 or 妹妹 by guesswork or by reading the whole book.) Point 3 is a warning against "improving" the source.
:::

::: example *Brother* and *uncle* in a story {#ex-kinship}
Translate into Chinese: *Tom was staying with his uncle for the summer. His brother had gone to France with their parents.* What do you need to know, and what can you do if the text does not tell you?
::: solution
1. ***Uncle***: father's or mother's side? older or younger than the father? A Chinese translator needs 叔叔, 伯伯, 舅舅, 姑父 or 姨父. If the story later shows that the uncle is the mother's brother, use 舅舅; if the text never says, the general 叔叔 is the usual choice for an uncle of unknown relation in translated fiction, although it suggests the father's side.
2. ***Brother***: older or younger? If Tom is ten and his brother is at university, 哥哥. If unknown, 兄弟 avoids the choice but sounds less natural in narration; translators often read ahead.
3. ***Their parents*** — 父母 (no possessive needed).

A model: 那年夏天，汤姆住在舅舅家。他哥哥跟着父母去了法国。 Note also the reordering of the time phrase to the front ([[translation/english-chinese-compared#rule-order]]).
:::
:::

## Words with no equivalent

::: definition Culture-specific and non-lexicalised concepts {#def-nonequivalence}
Baker (2018) lists several common kinds of non-equivalence at word level, among them: **culture-specific concepts** (上火, 红包, 户口; *Thanksgiving*, *pub*, *sixth form*); source concepts that are **not lexicalised** in the target language — understood, but with no single word (English *siblings* ‘brothers and sisters’; Chinese 孝 is lexicalised in Chinese and has no exact English word); differences in **expressive meaning** (connotation); and differences in **form** (a word formed with an affix such as *-ish* or 老-, with no equivalent word-formation pattern in the target language).
:::

::: theorem Strategies for non-equivalence {#rule-strategies}
The strategies Baker describes, which she found in the work of professional translators, include:

1. a **more general word** (superordinate): 馒头 → *steamed bun*;
2. a **more neutral or less expressive word**: 老百姓 → *ordinary people*;
3. **cultural substitution**, replacing the item with a target-culture item with a similar effect: 月下老人 → *Cupid* (used sparingly — it changes the culture of the text);
4. a **loan word**, alone or **with an explanation**: *guanxi*, *hongbao (a red envelope with money given as a gift)*;
5. **paraphrase** using related or unrelated words: 上火 → *suffer from what Chinese medicine calls "internal heat"*;
6. **omission**, if the item is not vital to the meaning;
7. **illustration**, in texts with pictures.

The choice depends on how important the item is in the text, how often it recurs, and the brief.
:::

::: proof
Each strategy trades accuracy against ease of reading. A loan word keeps the concept exactly but tells the reader nothing unless explained; once explained, it can be used again freely, which makes it the best choice for a term that recurs. A paraphrase explains but lengthens the text and cannot be repeated every time. A general word is short and accurate but loses detail. Cultural substitution reads easily but moves the text into another culture, which is acceptable in advertising and some children's books and usually wrong in news, scholarship or literature ([[translation/idioms-culture]]). Omission is legitimate only when the item does no work in the sentence: a translator of a tourist leaflet may drop a decorative reference that would need a paragraph to explain.
:::

::: example 上火 in three texts {#ex-shanghuo}
Translate 上火 in each context, and name the strategy. (a) A friend's message: 最近吃太多火锅，上火了，嗓子疼。 (b) A leaflet for a herbal tea sold abroad: 清热降火，预防上火。 (c) An anthropology article on Chinese ideas of the body.
::: solution
(a) The friend means the symptoms: *I've been eating too much hotpot lately and now I've got a sore throat.* **Omission** of the concept, keeping the effect — for a casual message the idea of "heat" is unnecessary. (Or, if the reader knows some Chinese culture: *… and it's given me "internal heat" — my throat hurts.*)

(b) The product depends on the concept, so it must be kept: *A traditional herbal tea that helps cool "internal heat"* (**paraphrase with a loan concept**). Claims on health products are regulated in many countries; a translator should translate the claim exactly and not strengthen it.

(c) **Loan word with explanation** on first use, then the loan word: *shanghuo (上火, literally ‘rising fire’), a state of excess "heat" in the body in Chinese medical thinking, associated with symptoms such as sore throats and mouth ulcers*; afterwards *shanghuo*. A scholarly reader needs the exact concept, not an English approximation.
:::
:::

## Colour and attitude: 褒 and 贬

::: theorem Keep the evaluative colouring {#rule-colouring}
Many words are **positive** (褒义), **negative** (贬义) or **neutral** (中性), and equivalents in the two languages often differ in colouring. Choose the target word that keeps the attitude of the source — and where the target language has only a coloured word, choose by context:

- *ambitious* → 有抱负的, 有雄心的 (positive) or 野心勃勃的 (negative), by context;
- *propaganda* is negative in modern English; 宣传 is neutral and is usually *publicity*, *promotion* or *campaign* (宣传部 of a company → *marketing* or *publicity department*);
- 农民 → *farmers* in most modern contexts; *peasants* is now used mainly for historical and sociological descriptions and can sound condescending;
- 个人主义 is usually negative in Chinese political language; *individualism* is often neutral or positive in English. Translating either way, check what the author means.
:::

::: proof
Colouring is part of meaning: changing it changes what the text says about its subject, and can change a translation from praise into criticism. The danger is greatest with words whose dictionary equivalents look exact. Zhang Peiji's textbook treats 褒贬 as one of the three main problems of word choice for this reason. Colouring also changes over time — *propaganda* was much more neutral in English a century ago than it is now — so the translator of an old text must ask what the word meant then.
:::

::: warning Colouring that changes the message
A company's English website says: ✗ *Our propaganda department will contact you.* The reader hears political manipulation. ✓ *Our marketing team will contact you.*

A translated biography says of a scientist: ✗ 她从小就野心勃勃。 (for *She was ambitious from an early age*). The Chinese reader hears ruthlessness. ✓ 她从小就很有抱负。
:::

## Terminology

::: definition Term {#def-term}
A **term** is a word or phrase with a fixed meaning in a specialised field, defined by its place in a system of concepts: *myocardial infarction* (心肌梗死), *force majeure* (不可抗力), *operating system* (操作系统). Unlike ordinary words, terms are not chosen by the translator for effect: the translator must find and use the **established equivalent**.
:::

::: theorem Find the term, then keep it {#rule-term}
1. **Never coin or paraphrase a term** when an established equivalent exists. Check authoritative sources: standard terms published by the relevant body (in mainland China, the 全国科学技术名词审定委员会), specialised dictionaries, standards, and parallel texts written by experts in the target language.
2. **Use the same term for the same concept throughout** a text and a project. Variation that is good style in ordinary prose (*the city … the capital*) is confusing in technical text: the reader assumes that a different term means a different concept.
3. **Distinguish the term from the everyday word**: *stress* in engineering is 应力, in psychology 压力; 功 in physics is *work*.
4. **Record your choices** in a glossary or termbase ([[translation/specialised-translation]]).
:::

::: proof
Terms belong to systems: the meaning of *stress* in engineering is fixed by its relation to *strain*, *load* and *force*, and the Chinese 应力 has the same relations to 应变, 载荷 and 力. A paraphrase or a near-synonym breaks the system, and a specialist reader will either misunderstand or lose confidence in the translation. Yan Fu, who coined many terms for Western concepts, described the difficulty in the 译例言 of 1898: 一名之立，旬月踟蹰 — establishing a single term could take him a month of hesitation. Many of his coinages, such as 计学 for economics and 群学 for sociology, were later replaced by terms borrowed from Japanese (经济学, 社会学), a reminder that a term is established by use, not by the merits of the coinage.
:::

::: quiz
In a computer manual, the source text uses *folder* in chapter 1 and *directory* in chapter 5 for the same thing. What should the Chinese translator do?
- [ ] Translate both as 文件夹 without comment, since they mean the same thing.
- [ ] Translate them differently, 文件夹 and 目录, as the source does.
- [x] Check whether the source intends a difference; if not, consult the client, and follow the client's terminology and the conventions of the target operating system.
- [ ] Choose whichever sounds better in each sentence.
::: solution
In computing, *folder* and *directory* are sometimes used for the same thing and sometimes distinguished (the graphical interface and the file system). The translator cannot know which without checking, and a silent decision either way may be wrong. Consistency with the client's existing Chinese materials and with the interface of the software matters more than the translator's preference.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Chinese compounds are transparent; English words often are not.** 心肌梗死 is built from 心 ‘heart’, 肌 ‘muscle’, 梗 ‘block’ and 死 ‘death’, and a Chinese reader understands it; *myocardial infarction* is Latin and Greek, and many English speakers say *heart attack*. A translator into English must decide whether the reader needs the technical term or the everyday one ([[style-and-rhetoric/readers-and-media]]).
- **English often has a pair of everyday and learned words** (*heart attack* / *myocardial infarction*, *bleeding* / *haemorrhage*), which Chinese often renders with a single term. Choose by register.
- **Abbreviations.** Chinese abbreviates by taking one character from each word (北京大学 → 北大, 高等考试 → 高考); English abbreviates by initials (*UN*, *GDP*). Never translate a Chinese abbreviation into an English one that does not exist: 两会 is *the Two Sessions* (with an explanation the first time), not ✗ *TS*.
- **Dictionaries mislead with order.** The first equivalent listed is not the most likely one in your sentence; read the whole entry, and check the English word in an English learner's dictionary ([[core-vocabulary/using-a-dictionary]]).
:::

::: history
Bilingual lexicography between English and Chinese began with missionaries. Robert Morrison's *A Dictionary of the Chinese Language*, published in parts in Macau between 1815 and 1823, was the first large Chinese–English and English–Chinese dictionary. Yan Fu's struggles with terminology in the 1890s were part of a wider effort to name Western concepts in Chinese, in which many terms coined in Japan using Chinese characters (科学, 哲学, 经济, 社会) were eventually adopted. Lin Yutang's *Chinese–English Dictionary of Modern Usage* (1972) and the Beijing Foreign Languages Institute's 《汉英词典》 (1978) gave modern Chinese a full treatment; Lu Gusun's (陆谷孙) 《英汉大词典》 (1989–1991) became the largest English–Chinese dictionary compiled by Chinese scholars. Since the 1990s, translators have also checked usage in corpora and, more recently, in the output of machine translation systems, whose suggestions for words always need checking ([[translation/machine-translation]]).
:::

## Where this leads

Idioms and metaphors — words whose meaning is not the sum of their parts — are the subject of [[translation/idioms-culture]], together with names and the choice between domestication and foreignisation. Terminology management in specialised fields is treated in [[translation/specialised-translation]]. The contrasts behind many word-level problems (number, definiteness, nouns and verbs) are in [[translation/english-chinese-compared]], and the word-class shifts that follow from them in [[translation/structural-shifts]]. Common traps between the two languages — false friends and Chinglish — are collected in [[collocation-and-register/false-friends-chinglish]].

::: summary
- Words have several senses; translate the **active sense** in context, with its connotation and register, and check the result in the target language ([[#rule-sense]]).
- Translate **collocations as units**: *strong tea*, *heavy rain*, *make a mistake*, 取得进步 ([[#rule-collocation]]).
- Between **specific and general** words (kinship terms, *borrow*/*lend*), use the general word when the detail does not matter, and find out — do not invent — the detail when the target language requires it ([[#rule-specific]]).
- For **non-equivalence** (上火, *siblings*), choose a strategy — general word, neutral word, cultural substitution, loan word with explanation, paraphrase, omission — by the item's importance and the brief ([[#rule-strategies]]).
- Keep the **evaluative colouring** (褒贬) of words: *ambitious*, *propaganda*, 农民, 宣传 ([[#rule-colouring]]).
- **Terms** have established equivalents; find them in authoritative sources, never paraphrase them, and use them consistently ([[#rule-term]]).
:::

Translate the word *stress* in each sentence. (a) *Exam stress affects many students.* (b) *The bridge was designed to withstand high stress.* (c) *In English, stress falls on the first syllable of "record" as a noun.* (d) *The report stresses the need for action.*
::: solution
(a) 压力 (心理压力); (b) 应力 (engineering term); (c) 重音 (phonetics term, [[connected-speech/word-stress-patterns]]); (d) 强调 (ordinary verb).

::: exercise Translate a menu note {level=3}
Translate into English for the menu of a restaurant abroad, and explain the strategy for each culture-specific item: 本店特色：东北酸菜白肉炖粉条，正宗家乡味，量大实惠。
::: hint
酸菜, 白肉 and 粉条 are ingredients the reader may not know; 家乡味 and 量大实惠 are selling points. A menu is vocative.
:::
::: solution
A model: *House special: Northeastern pork belly braised with pickled cabbage and glass noodles — real home-style cooking, in generous portions.*

Strategies: 东北 → *Northeastern* (a general geographical word; *Dongbei* only if the restaurant uses the name elsewhere). 酸菜 → *pickled cabbage* (paraphrase; *suancai* could be added). 白肉 → *pork belly* (the specific cut that 白肉 refers to in this dish; *white meat* would mean poultry in English). 粉条 → *glass noodles* (an established English food term; *vermicelli* is also used). 正宗家乡味 → *real home-style cooking* (cultural adjustment: an English menu does not mention the chef's home town unless it means something to the reader). 量大实惠 → *in generous portions* (*good value* could be added).
:::

::: exercise A short glossary {level=3}
Read this English paragraph from a fictional product leaflet and list the terms that need a fixed Chinese equivalent. For each, give the equivalent and a source you could check it in. *The water purifier uses reverse osmosis to remove dissolved salts. Replace the pre-filter every six months and the membrane every two years. If the flow rate drops, check the inlet valve.*
::: solution
Terms and usual equivalents: *water purifier* 净水器; *reverse osmosis* 反渗透; *dissolved salts* 溶解性盐类 (in water treatment also 溶解性总固体 for *total dissolved solids*, a different term); *pre-filter* 前置滤芯 / 前置过滤器; *membrane* (反渗透)膜 / 膜元件; *flow rate* 流量; *inlet valve* 进水阀. Sources: the standard terms of the 全国科学技术名词审定委员会 for chemistry and environmental engineering, Chinese national standards for water purifiers, and Chinese manuals written by manufacturers (parallel texts). Record the choices in a glossary and use them in every later leaflet.
