Here is one sentence from a fictional Chinese company's website, and a translation of the kind that machine systems and hurried translators produce:

> 我公司成立于1998年，位于美丽的西湖之畔，经过二十多年的发展，现已成为一家集研发、生产、销售于一体的现代化企业，产品远销欧美等三十多个国家和地区，深受客户好评。
>
> ? *Our company was established in 1998, located beside the beautiful West Lake, after more than twenty years of development, has now become a modern enterprise integrating R&D, production and sales, products are exported to more than thirty countries and regions such as Europe and America, deeply praised by customers.*

The English contains every idea of the Chinese, in the same order. But it is not an English sentence. It has a finite verb (*has now become*) whose subject is ten words and two commas away; the product clause has its own subject and verb and is joined by a comma, a comma splice; the last phrase, *deeply praised by customers*, has no clear attachment; and the whole is one sentence of fifty words in which every clause has the same weight. An English reader stumbles at *located* and gives up at *products are exported*.

A natural English version must decide what the sentence is really about and how its parts are related:

> *Founded in 1998 on the shores of West Lake, the company has grown over two decades into a modern business that handles research, manufacturing and sales. It exports to more than thirty countries, in Europe, North America and elsewhere, and its products are popular with customers.*

The Chinese sentence was a **chain**: each clause added a new fact about 我公司, the topic, in order. The English is a **hierarchy**: one main clause (*the company has grown … into a modern business*), with the founding and the location packed into a participle phrase in front, and a second sentence for the exports. This lesson is about how to build that hierarchy — the hardest skill of Chinese → English translation, and the one most Chinese learners have practised least.

## The run-on sentence of Chinese: 流水句

::: definition 流水句 {#def-liushuiju}
A **流水句** (‘flowing-water sentence’, a term introduced by Lü Shuxiang in 《汉语语法分析问题》, 1979) is a Chinese sentence made of a series of clauses separated by commas, often sharing a topic, often with no subject of their own, and with few or no connectives. The clauses follow one another in the order of events or of thought, and the reader infers their relations.
:::

A 流水句 is not a fault in Chinese. It is the normal shape of narrative, description and much exposition, and it can be elegant. But English grammar cannot reproduce it: every English clause needs a subject and a finite verb, independent clauses cannot be joined by commas alone, and a sentence of more than three or four coordinated clauses is felt as shapeless ([[translation/english-chinese-compared#rule-hypotaxis]]).

::: theorem Translating a 流水句: four steps {#rule-four-steps}
1. **Divide** the Chinese into its clauses and **state the relation** between each clause and its neighbours: time, cause, result, condition, concession, purpose, addition, description, comment.
2. **Choose the main clause or clauses**: the one or two statements that carry the point of the sentence. Choose their **subjects** ([[translation/english-chinese-compared#rule-subject]]).
3. **Attach the other clauses** to the main clause with English means of subordination: conjunctions, relative clauses, participle and infinitive phrases, prepositional phrases, appositives, *with*-phrases. Use coordination (*and*, *but*) only for statements of equal weight.
4. **Decide the sentence boundaries**: one English sentence, two, or more. Then supply tense, number, articles and pronouns, and revise the result as English.
:::

::: proof
The steps follow from the contrast between parataxis and hypotaxis. Chinese leaves the ranking of clauses to the reader; English grammar forces the writer to rank them, because only one clause in a simple sentence can be the main clause, and everything else must be marked as subordinate or explicitly coordinated. Step 1 is the analysis that the Chinese reader performs without noticing; step 2 is the decision that the English writer must make and the Chinese writer did not have to; step 3 is the hypotaxis English needs; step 4 handles the obligatory categories of [[translation/english-chinese-compared#rule-categories]]. Translators who skip step 2 produce English in which every clause is a main clause joined by *and* or by commas — grammatical at best, and monotonous.
:::

::: example Ranking the clauses {#ex-rank}
Translate into English: 小王昨天发烧了，没来上课，老师让我把作业带给他，我放学就去了他家。
::: solution
1. **Clauses and relations**: (i) Xiao Wang had a fever yesterday — cause of (ii); (ii) he didn't come to class; (iii) the teacher asked me to take him the homework — a consequence of (ii); (iv) I went to his home after school — in response to (iii).
2. **Main clauses**: two events carry the story: the teacher's request and my visit. (i) and (ii) are background.
3. **Attach**: (i) + (ii) → a *because*-clause or a participle; (iv) → a second clause joined by *so*.
4. **Sentences and grammar**: past tense throughout, past perfect not needed; *the homework* (definite: the day's homework).

*Xiao Wang was off school yesterday with a fever, so the teacher asked me to take him the homework, and I went round to his house after school.* / *Because Xiao Wang had a fever and missed class yesterday, the teacher asked me to take him his homework. I went to his house as soon as school was over.*

The first version compresses (i) and (ii) into one clause with *with a fever*, a structure Chinese does not have; the second keeps them as a *because*-clause and divides the sentence in two.
:::
:::

## The tools of subordination

English has many ways of attaching one statement to another without making it a main clause. A translator into English needs all of them.

| Chinese chain | English structure | example |
|---|---|---|
| 他推开门，走了进来 | participle phrase | *Pushing open the door, he came in.* |
| 这座寺庙建于唐代，是全市最古老的建筑 | relative clause | *The temple, which dates from the Tang dynasty, is the oldest building in the city.* |
| 她是医生，在北京工作 | appositive or relative | *She is a doctor working in Beijing.* |
| 他低着头，一句话也不说 | *with*-phrase | *He sat with his head down, not saying a word.* |
| 为了赶火车，我们五点就起床了 | infinitive of purpose | *We got up at five to catch the train.* |
| 天很冷，路上没什么人 | absolute or *so*-clause | *It was so cold that there was hardly anyone about.* |
| 价格便宜，质量也好 | coordination | *It is cheap, and the quality is good too.* / *It is both cheap and good.* |
| 他学了三年钢琴，弹得很好 | prepositional phrase | *After three years of lessons, he plays the piano very well.* |

::: theorem Use the full range of subordination {#rule-subordinate}
When attaching a Chinese clause to an English main clause, choose the structure by the **relation** and the **weight** of the clause:

1. **Background or preceding action** with the same subject → participle phrase (*Having finished …*, *Born in …*, *Pushing open …*).
2. **Description of a noun** → relative clause, reduced relative, appositive or adjective phrase.
3. **Accompanying circumstances** → *with*-phrase or participle at the end (*… with her arms folded*, *… waving goodbye*).
4. **Purpose** → *to*-infinitive or *so that*; **cause, condition, concession, time** → *because*, *if*, *although*, *when*, or a prepositional phrase (*because of*, *despite*, *after*).
5. **Equal weight** → *and*, *but*, a semicolon, or a new sentence.

Make sure that a participle phrase has the **same subject** as the main clause ([[complex-sentences/participle-infinitive-clauses]]).
:::

::: proof
These are the means of hypotaxis that English grammar offers ([[translation/english-chinese-compared#def-hypotaxis]]), and each has its own meaning: a participle phrase presents an action as background, a relative clause as description, a *with*-phrase as a circumstance. Choosing among them by relation keeps the logic of the Chinese visible; choosing by weight keeps the English sentence's main point in the main clause. The warning about subjects matters because Chinese topic chains allow the implied subject to shift (他推开门，屋里一片漆黑 — the second clause is about the room), and a translator who turns the first clause into a participle produces a dangling participle: ✗ *Pushing open the door, the room was dark.* ✓ *When he pushed open the door, the room was dark.*
:::

::: warning Dangling participles from topic chains
✗ *Founded in 1998, we have grown into a modern enterprise.* (The company was founded, not *we*.)

✓ *Founded in 1998, the company has grown into a modern business.* ✓ *Since our founding in 1998, we have grown …*

✗ *Walking along the river, the old houses are beautiful.* (for 沿着河边走，老房子很漂亮.)

✓ *As you walk along the river, you pass some beautiful old houses.* ✓ *The walk along the river takes you past some beautiful old houses.*
:::

::: quiz
Which translation of 她打开信，看了一遍，脸色一下子变了 is the best English?
- [ ] *She opened the letter, read it once, her face changed suddenly.*
- [ ] *Opening the letter, reading it once, her face suddenly changed.*
- [x] *She opened the letter and read it through, and her face suddenly changed.*
- [ ] *She opened the letter, she read it once, and then her face had changed suddenly.*
::: solution
The third coordinates the first two actions of *she* and adds the change of expression as a new clause with its own subject, *her face*, joined by *and*. The first is a comma splice; the second makes *her face* the subject of *opening* and *reading* (dangling participles); the fourth uses the past perfect wrongly and repeats *she* without need. Another good version: *She opened the letter and read it, and the colour drained from her face.*
:::
:::

## Supplying what Chinese leaves implicit

::: theorem Decide tense, number, definiteness and pronouns {#rule-supply}
For every English clause and noun phrase, decide:

1. **Tense and aspect**: from time words, from 了, 过, 着, 在, and from the context of the whole text. Keep a consistent time frame — narrative past, present for general facts and descriptions ([[tense-and-aspect/tenses-in-texts]]).
2. **Number**: one or more? 书 may be *the book*, *a book*, *books*, *some books*.
3. **Definiteness**: known to the reader or new? *a* / *the* / zero ([[nouns-and-articles/a-and-the]]).
4. **Pronouns**: who is the subject of a subjectless clause? *he*, *she*, *they*, *we*, *you*, *it*? Is the reference unambiguous in English?
5. **Possession**: English often adds possessives where Chinese has none: 他把手放进口袋里 → *He put his hands in his pockets.*

When the Chinese does not settle a choice and the context does not either, **find out** — ask the author or client, check facts — or choose the reading that is least likely to mislead.
:::

::: proof
English grammar forces these choices ([[translation/english-chinese-compared#rule-categories]]). A wrong choice is not a "small" error: *The company exported a product* and *The company exports products* say different things. Tense is the most frequent difficulty, because Chinese narrative can shift between past and present events without any marking, and because Chinese descriptions of a permanent state (位于西湖之畔) and of a past event (成立于1998年) look the same. Number and definiteness are the most frequent *errors* in learners' English. The rule that the translator should find out rather than guess is a professional obligation: guessing silently transfers the risk to the reader.
:::

::: example Supplying tense and articles {#ex-supply}
Translate into English, and list your decisions: 去年夏天我去了一趟云南。在大理住了一个星期，每天骑车沿着洱海转。那里的天特别蓝，水特别清，我拍了很多照片。
::: solution
*Last summer I went to Yunnan. I spent a week in Dali, cycling round Erhai Lake every day. The sky there was incredibly blue and the water was crystal clear, and I took lots of photos.*

Decisions: **tense** — past simple throughout (去年夏天 sets the frame), including the description of the sky and water, which in the narrative is the sky and water *then* (a travel blog might use the present for a permanent fact: *The sky there is …*); **subject** — the subjectless 在大理住了一个星期 gets *I*; **subordination** — 每天骑车沿着洱海转 becomes a participle phrase (*cycling round …*) attached to *I spent a week*; **articles** — *the sky*, *the water* (unique in the setting), *a week* (indefinite), *Erhai Lake* (a name, no article); **number** — *photos* (很多); **names** — 洱海 is *Erhai Lake* (pinyin + *Lake*, the usual form on maps and in guidebooks; [[translation/idioms-culture]]).
:::
:::

## Sentence boundaries and paragraphs

::: theorem Where an English sentence should end {#rule-boundaries}
1. A Chinese sentence often contains **several English sentences**. Start a new English sentence when the subject changes and the new statement has its own weight, when the time frame changes, or when the sentence would otherwise have more than two main clauses.
2. Conversely, two short Chinese sentences may become **one** English sentence if one is clearly background to the other.
3. A Chinese comma is not an English comma: decide in each case whether English needs a full stop, a semicolon, a conjunction, a dash or a comma.
4. Keep the **paragraph** unit of the Chinese unless the English genre requires otherwise, but remember that English paragraphs often begin with a topic sentence that states the point ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/topic-sentences]]).
:::

::: proof
The Chinese full stop (。) marks the end of a unit of thought, and that unit may contain what English treats as several statements; Chinese writers place full stops at the end of a topic chain rather than after each predication. English readers process sentences of twenty to twenty-five words comfortably, and English style guides advise varying sentence length ([[style-and-rhetoric/sentence-variety]]). Translating sentence for sentence is therefore not a requirement of faithfulness: what must be kept is the information and its organisation, not the punctuation. In legal and some technical texts, sentence-for-sentence correspondence may be part of the brief, so that clauses can be cross-referenced ([[translation/specialised-translation]]).
:::

## Redundancy and parallel phrases

Formal Chinese, especially official and promotional writing, often uses pairs and series of four-character phrases that are close in meaning: 认真学习、刻苦钻研; 团结协作、开拓进取; 积极稳妥地推进. In Chinese they give rhythm and weight. Translated item by item into English, they sound inflated and repetitive.

::: theorem Translate the meaning of a series, not each item {#rule-redundancy}
1. When two or more Chinese phrases **repeat one idea**, translate the idea once, with the most precise English word: 认真学习、刻苦钻研 → *study hard*; 不断发展壮大 → *grow*.
2. When they make **different points**, keep them, in plain English parallel form: 质量可靠，价格合理，服务周到 → *reliable quality, reasonable prices and attentive service*.
3. Remove intensifiers that English does not need: 深受 → *popular with*; 大力 → (often nothing); 非常重要的 → *important* or *essential*.
4. Keep series and repetition in **speeches and literary texts** where they are rhetoric, translating them with English rhetorical forms ([[style-and-rhetoric/figures-of-speech]]).
:::

::: proof
English and Chinese prose traditions value different things. Chinese formal style values balance (对偶), four-character rhythm and accumulation; English expository style, as the style manuals teach, values concision and treats near-synonyms in a row as padding ([[style-and-rhetoric/concision]]). A translator who reproduces every item gives English readers an impression of exaggeration that the Chinese reader does not have — so a formally equivalent translation produces a different effect. This is a case where dynamic equivalence ([[translation/what-translation-is#def-equivalence]]) and faithfulness point the same way. The exception in point 4 is important: in a speech by a public figure, a series may be quoted and analysed, and the brief may require each item to be rendered.
:::

::: example Concision in a promotional text {#ex-concise}
Translate into English for a hotel's website: 本酒店交通便利，环境优雅，设施齐全，服务热情周到，是您商务出行、休闲度假的理想选择。
::: solution
1. **Items**: 交通便利 (convenient location), 环境优雅 (elegant surroundings), 设施齐全 (full facilities), 服务热情周到 (warm and attentive service — two near-synonyms), 理想选择 for 商务出行 and 休闲度假.
2. **Keep the different points, merge the repeated one**: 热情周到 → *friendly* or *attentive*, not both.
3. **English promotional conventions**: second person, concrete words, shorter sentences.

*Conveniently located, with elegant surroundings and a full range of facilities, the hotel offers friendly, attentive service — ideal whether you are travelling on business or on holiday.* / *Our hotel is easy to reach, elegant and well equipped, and our staff will make you welcome — whether you are here for business or for pleasure.*

A word-for-word ? *Our hotel has convenient transportation, elegant environment, complete facilities, warm and thoughtful service, and is your ideal choice for business travel and leisure vacation* has every idea and several collocation errors (*convenient transportation*, *elegant environment*), no articles, and a list that reads like a form.
:::
:::

::: example A descriptive paragraph {#ex-paragraph}
Translate into English for a magazine article: 村子在半山腰上，几十户人家，房子都是石头砌的。村口有棵老槐树，据说有三百多年了，夏天的傍晚，老人们就坐在树下聊天，孩子们在旁边跑来跑去。
::: solution
1. **Topics and clauses**: the village (position, size, houses); the old tree (place, age); summer evenings (old people talk, children play).
2. **Main clauses**: one sentence for the village, one for the tree, one for the scene.
3. **Grammar**: present tense (a description of how things are); *a few dozen households*; *the old people*, *the children* (generic within the village).

*The village sits halfway up the mountain: a few dozen households, in houses built of stone. At its entrance stands an old pagoda tree, said to be more than three hundred years old, and on summer evenings the old people sit under it chatting while the children run around nearby.*

Notes: 几十户人家 becomes a verbless phrase after a colon, an English way of keeping the brevity of the Chinese; 据说 → *said to be* (a reduced passive); 槐树 → *pagoda tree* (the usual English name for *Styphnolobium japonicum*; *locust tree* is also used, less precisely); the last two Chinese clauses are joined with *while*, showing simultaneity.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which English sentence is the best translation of 我们要努力学习，不断提高自己的综合素质 in a student's speech?
- [ ] *We must study hard and constantly improve our comprehensive quality.*
- [x] *We must work hard and keep developing ourselves in every way.*
- [ ] *We must make efforts to study, unceasingly raising our integrated qualities.*
- [ ] *We must study hard to improve.*
::: solution
综合素质 has no direct English equivalent; *comprehensive quality* and *integrated qualities* are calques that English readers cannot interpret. The second version says what the phrase means (all-round development) in natural English. The last one is too thin: it loses the idea of all-round growth.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Revise without the source.** After drafting, read your English as if you had never seen the Chinese. Most comma splices, dangling participles and missing articles are invisible while the Chinese is in front of you, because you read its meaning into your English. The editing method of [[style-and-rhetoric/editing-for-style]] applies to translations as to any other English text.
- **China English and Chinglish.** Some Chinese concepts have established English forms that are not native English but are correct and widely understood — *the Spring Festival*, *the Two Sessions*, *a xiaokang society*, *hukou*. They are part of **China English** (a term introduced by Ge Chuangui, 葛传槼, in 1980), a legitimate variety for expressing Chinese realities ([[world-englishes/english-in-china]]). Chinglish, by contrast, is English shaped by transfer at the level of grammar and collocation, which a translator should remove.
- **Native revision.** Professional Chinese → English translation is often revised by a reviser whose first language is English. If you work with one, give them the source and ask about meaning as well as language: a reviser who does not know what the Chinese says can make the English smooth and wrong.
:::

::: history
For most of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, Chinese texts reached English readers mainly through foreign translators: missionary scholars such as James Legge, whose *Chinese Classics* (1861–1872) translated the Confucian canon, and later sinologists. Translation from Chinese into English by Chinese institutions began on a large scale after 1949. The magazine *Chinese Literature* (中国文学) was launched in 1951, the Foreign Languages Press (外文出版社) was established in Beijing in 1952, and translators such as Yang Xianyi (杨宪益) and Gladys Yang (戴乃迭) worked for both for decades. In 1980 Ge Chuangui proposed the term *China English* for English that expresses things specific to China, distinguishing it from Chinglish; the debate about what such English should look like continues in Chinese translation studies today.
:::

- **Revise the English without the source**, then check it against the source.

## Exercises

::: exercise Relations between clauses {level=1}
State the relation of each clause to the next, then translate. (a) 他太累了，倒头就睡。 (b) 雨下得很大，我们还是去了。 (c) 你先走，我随后就到。
::: solution
(a) Cause → result: *He was so tired that he fell asleep the moment he lay down.* (b) Concession: *It was raining hard, but we went anyway.* / *Despite the heavy rain, we still went.* (c) Sequence: *You go ahead — I'll follow in a minute.*
:::

::: exercise Subjects for subjectless clauses {level=1}
Translate into English, supplying a subject. (a) 下周一开学。 (b) 禁止吸烟。 (c) 到了北京给我打个电话。
::: solution
(a) *School starts next Monday.* / *Term begins next Monday.* (b) *No smoking.* (an English sign has no subject either) / *Smoking is prohibited.* (c) *Give me a call when you get to Beijing.* (imperative with implied *you*).
:::

::: exercise Articles and number {level=1}
Translate, deciding articles and number. (a) 桌子上有本书。 (b) 书在桌子上。 (c) 我喜欢看书。 (d) 这家店卖书。
::: solution
(a) *There's a book on the table.* (new, indefinite) (b) *The book is on the table.* (known, definite) (c) *I like reading* / *I like reading books.* (generic) (d) *This shop sells books.* (plural generic)

::: exercise Participle phrases {level=2}
Translate each sentence using a participle phrase, and make sure the subject is right. (a) 她放下包，坐了下来。 (b) 这本书写于1920年代，至今仍有很多读者。 (c) 走出车站，我一眼就看见了他。
::: solution
(a) *Putting down her bag, she sat down.* (b) *Written in the 1920s, the book still has many readers today.* (c) *Coming out of the station, I saw him at once.* — the subject of *coming out* is *I*, the same as the main clause. ✗ *Coming out of the station, he was the first thing I saw* would dangle.
:::
:::

::: exercise Rank and rebuild {level=2}
Translate into English, with one main clause per sentence: 这家餐馆开了三十多年了，老板换过好几个，菜的味道一直没变，很多老顾客从城外专门开车过来吃。
::: solution
A model: *The restaurant has been open for more than thirty years. It has changed hands several times, but the food tastes just as it always did, and many regular customers drive in from outside the city specially to eat there.* — The first clause is a sentence of its own (background); the change of owners and the unchanged taste are linked by *but* (concession); the customers' trips are the result, added with *and*. Note the present perfect for *has been open* and *has changed hands*.
:::

::: exercise Concision {level=2}
Translate concisely for an English brochure: 我校师资力量雄厚，教学设施先进，校园环境优美，是莘莘学子求学深造的理想场所。
::: solution
*With highly qualified teachers, modern facilities and a beautiful campus, our school is an ideal place to study.* — 师资力量雄厚 → *highly qualified teachers*; 莘莘学子 and 求学深造 merge into *to study*; the four-part parallel structure becomes a *with*-phrase plus one main clause.

Translate the company sentence at the start of this lesson in your own way, without looking at the model, and then compare. Write three sentences explaining your choice of main clause and how you handled 深受客户好评.

Is the sentence about the company's history, its present size, or its products? A website "About us" text often leads with what the company does now.

::: solution
Another acceptable version: *Established in 1998 beside West Lake, we are now a modern company covering research and development, manufacturing and sales, with customers in more than thirty countries across Europe, the Americas and beyond.* Explanation: the main clause states what the company is now (*we are now a modern company …*), the founding and location become a participle phrase, and the exports become a *with*-phrase. 深受客户好评 has been folded into *with customers in …* or could be a separate short sentence (*Our customers rate our products highly*); English websites avoid unsupported claims of praise, so a translator may also discuss with the client whether to keep it. Note that 欧美 is *Europe and North America* (or *the Americas*), not *Europe and America*.

::: exercise A narrative paragraph {level=3}
Translate into English: 那天晚上停电了，屋里一片漆黑。奶奶摸黑找出一根蜡烛点上，我们围着桌子坐着，听她讲她小时候的事。那是我记忆中最温暖的一个晚上。
::: solution
A model: *There was a power cut that evening, and the house was pitch dark. Grandma felt her way to a candle and lit it, and we sat round the table listening to her talk about her childhood. It is the warmest evening I can remember.*

Notes: past tense for the narrative and the present for the narrator's reflection (*It is the warmest evening I can remember*; *was* is also possible); 摸黑找出……点上 → *felt her way to a candle and lit it* (two coordinated verbs, one of them an English phrasal idiom for 摸黑); 坐着, 听她讲 → *sat … listening to her talk* (participle for the accompanying action); 屋里 → *the house* (English chooses the whole home; *the room* is also possible).
