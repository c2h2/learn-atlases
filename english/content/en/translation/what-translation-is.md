A visitor from abroad is staying with a family in Chengdu. At midday the grandmother meets her on the stairs and asks, smiling:

> 吃饭了吗？

Three people are asked to put the question into English. The first writes *Have you eaten rice?* The second writes *Have you eaten yet?* The third writes *Hello! How are you?* Which is the translation?

The first follows every word and gets the meaning wrong: 饭 here means a meal, not rice. The second gets the words right and may still mislead, because an English speaker takes *Have you eaten yet?* as a real question, perhaps an invitation. The third gets the social act right — here the question is a friendly greeting — but throws away what makes the exchange Chinese, including the care for whether the guest has eaten, which is part of its warmth. Each version is right about something and wrong about something else. Which one is best depends on what the translation is *for*: a phrasebook, a novel, subtitles, a linguistics class.

That is the problem this course is about. Translation is not the replacement of words by words, and there is rarely one correct answer; but neither is it a matter of taste. There are reasons why one translation is better than another for a given purpose, and translators and scholars in both China and the West have spent a long time stating them. This lesson sets out the main ideas — 信、达、雅, equivalence, literal and free translation, the purpose of a translation — which the rest of the course turns into techniques.

## What a translation is

::: definition Translation, source and target {#def-translation}
**Translation** is the production of a text in one language — the **target language** — that stands for a text in another language — the **source language** — for readers who need or want it in the target language. The original is the **source text** (原文) and the result the **target text** (译文). Translation in the narrow sense is written; spoken translation is **interpreting** (口译, [[translation/interpreting]]).
:::

The linguist Roman Jakobson, in an essay of 1959 ("On Linguistic Aspects of Translation"), distinguished three kinds of translation: **intralingual** translation, or rewording within one language (explaining a classical Chinese text in modern Chinese, paraphrasing a legal sentence in plain English); **interlingual** translation, or translation proper, between languages; and **intersemiotic** translation, from words into another sign system, such as a film of a novel. This course is about the second kind, but the first is never far away: before you translate a difficult English sentence into Chinese, you often paraphrase it in English ([[academic-reading/paraphrase-and-quotation]]), and before you translate 文言 into English, you understand it through modern Chinese.

A translation is a new text with its own readers, who usually cannot check it against the original and trust that it says what the original says. That trust is the basis of the translator's responsibility.

::: theorem What a translation must do {#rule-two-loyalties}
A translation must be judged on two sides at once:

1. **Accuracy**: it conveys the meaning of the source text — its content, and as far as the purpose allows, its attitude, register and effect — without adding, omitting or distorting anything that matters.
2. **Acceptability**: it reads as a text written in the target language for its readers, following that language's grammar, collocation and conventions of genre.

Neither side is enough alone. A translation that is accurate but unreadable fails its readers; one that is fluent but inaccurate betrays them.
:::

::: proof
The two sides correspond to the two languages the translator stands between. Every major account of translation names them in some form: Yan Fu's 信 and 达 below, Nida's "closest natural equivalent", in which *closest* faces the source and *natural* the target, and the distinction between adequacy and acceptability in descriptive translation studies (Toury, *Descriptive Translation Studies and Beyond*, 1995). What the accounts disagree about is what to do when the two sides conflict — and between English and Chinese they conflict often, because the two languages build sentences so differently ([[translation/english-chinese-compared]]). The rest of this lesson is about the ways of resolving that conflict.
:::

## 信、达、雅

The most famous statement of translation standards in Chinese is three characters long. In 1898 Yan Fu (严复, 1854–1921) published 《天演论》, a translation of T. H. Huxley's lectures *Evolution and Ethics* (1893–1894), which introduced Darwinian ideas to a generation of Chinese readers. In its preface on method, the 译例言, he wrote:

> 译事三难：信、达、雅。求其信，已大难矣！顾信矣不达，虽译犹不译也，则达尚焉。

In our translation: *Translation has three difficulties: faithfulness, comprehensibility and elegance. To be faithful is hard enough already. But a translation that is faithful and not comprehensible is no translation at all — so comprehensibility comes first.* (The text is in the public domain: Yan Fu died in 1921 and the preface was published in 1898.)

::: definition 信、达、雅 {#def-xindaya}
- **信** (*xìn*, ‘faithfulness’): the translation conveys the meaning of the original.
- **达** (*dá*, ‘reaching, getting through’): the translation is comprehensible and fluent to its readers; the meaning arrives.
- **雅** (*yǎ*, ‘elegance’): the translation is written in a refined style. For Yan Fu this meant the prose of the classical writers before the Han; later writers have reinterpreted it as **appropriate style** — a style that matches the original's.
:::

Three points are often forgotten. First, Yan Fu called them *difficulties* (难), not rules: he was describing what made his work hard. Second, his own sentence ranks them: faithfulness is hard, but without comprehensibility a translation is "no translation at all", so 达 takes priority when the two conflict. Third, his practice was free. He summarised, rearranged and added commentaries of his own (marked 复案) to *Evolution and Ethics*, and in the same preface he said that his version conveyed the gist (达旨) rather than being a translation in the strict sense, and that it was not a model for others. His elegant classical style was a deliberate choice of audience: he was writing for scholar-officials who would not take seriously ideas presented in a vulgar style.

::: proof Explanation: why the triad lasted
The triad lasted because it names the two loyalties of [[#rule-two-loyalties]] and adds a third concern — style — that both readers and translators feel strongly about. Its brevity also allowed every later generation to reinterpret it. In the twentieth century, 雅 was criticised as a demand to make every original sound polished (an original that is plain or rough should be translated as plain or rough), and was redefined as fitness of style. Later Chinese formulations kept the same structure while changing the emphasis. Fu Lei (傅雷), the translator of Balzac and Romain Rolland, argued in the preface to his 1951 retranslation of *Le Père Goriot* that a translation should be like a copy of a painting that seeks resemblance in spirit (神似) rather than in form (形似). Qian Zhongshu (钱锺书), in an essay of the early 1960s on Lin Shu's translations (《林纾的翻译》), proposed 化 — transformation — as the highest standard of literary translation: a work moves into another language so completely that it shows no strain of translation and yet keeps its original flavour. 神似 and 化境 both raise the bar above 信: they ask the translator to reproduce what the original *does*, not only what it *says*. ([[translation/literary-translation]] returns to them.)
:::

::: example Judging three translations by 信、达、雅 {#ex-xindaya}
A university notice in English says: *Students who fail to return library books by the due date will be charged a fine.* Judge these three Chinese translations.

(a) 学生们谁未能在到期日之前归还图书馆的书，将被收取一笔罚款。

(b) 逾期未还书者，按规定缴纳罚款。

(c) 同学们，借了书要按时还哦，不然可是要罚钱的！
::: solution
1. **信.** All three keep the core: books not returned on time → a fine. (b) drops *students* and adds 按规定 ‘according to the regulations’, which is implied by the genre of a notice rather than stated. (c) keeps the meaning but changes the speech act from a statement of a rule to friendly advice.
2. **达.** (a) is comprehensible but stiff: 学生们谁 is a word-for-word rendering of *Students who* and is not natural Chinese; 将被收取 copies the English passive; 一笔 copies the article *a*. This is **翻译腔** (translationese). (b) and (c) read naturally.
3. **雅** (as fitness of style). A library notice in Chinese uses the compact formal register of (b): 逾期, 者, 按规定 are exactly what such notices say. (c) is in the register of a chatty social-media post — fine for a poster aimed at first-year students, wrong for the regulations.

Verdict: (b) is the best translation **for a notice**; (c) might be a good *adaptation* for another purpose; (a) is the typical first draft of a learner who translates words in order. Notice that the best version has fewer words than the English: Chinese notices are often more compact than English ones.
:::
:::

::: quiz
In Yan Fu's preface, which of the three is said to take priority when a translation cannot have both?
- [ ] 信 — faithfulness comes before everything else.
- [x] 达 — a faithful translation that is not understood is "no translation at all".
- [ ] 雅 — style is the highest of the three.
- [ ] None: the three are equal and must all be met.
::: solution
The text says 顾信矣不达，虽译犹不译也，则达尚焉: faithfulness without comprehensibility is no translation, "so 达 is to be valued". Many later summaries present the three as equal or put 信 first; Yan Fu's own sentence ranks 达 higher in a conflict.
:::
:::

## Literal and free translation

The oldest debate in translation, in Europe and in China, is between following the words and following the sense. In Latin it goes back to Cicero and Jerome; in China, to the Buddhist translators of the first millennium, who argued about 文 (polished) and 质 (plain) styles. In modern Chinese the debate is between **直译** and **意译**.

::: definition Literal and free translation {#def-literal-free}
**Literal translation** (直译) keeps the grammatical structures and the words of the source as far as the target language allows, changing only what grammar makes necessary. **Word-for-word translation** goes further and keeps the order and the words even where the target grammar does not allow it; it is useful only as a gloss. **Free translation** (意译) reproduces the content without the form of the original, restructuring sentences and replacing images as needed.
:::

The two terms describe a scale, not a choice between two boxes, and both words are used loosely. In Chinese classrooms 直译 often means "a translation close to the original that is still good Chinese", and 意译 "a translation that changes the wording to make the meaning clear" — both of which are good translations. The bad versions at the two ends have their own names: 死译 or 硬译 (dead or rigid translation, keeping foreign structures that the target cannot carry) and 乱译 (translating loosely, adding and dropping at will).

::: theorem As literal as possible, as free as necessary {#rule-literal-first}
A useful working principle, taught in many Chinese and Western textbooks in some form, is: **translate as literally as the target language and the purpose allow, and as freely as they require.** Start from the closest rendering; change structure or wording only when the close rendering is unnatural, unclear or misleading in the target language — and then change exactly as much as is needed.
:::

::: proof
The principle protects both loyalties of [[#rule-two-loyalties]]. Starting close protects accuracy: every change is a decision that has to be justified, and changes made for no reason are where meaning is lost. Changing whenever necessary protects acceptability: the translator does not keep an English structure merely because it is there. The principle also explains why English–Chinese translation is usually much "freer" than translation between, say, French and English: the two grammars differ so much that the closest *natural* rendering is often far from the source form. *He is a good swimmer* becomes 他游泳游得很好, not 他是一个好的游泳者 — not because the translator is being free, but because that is what the sentence means in natural Chinese ([[translation/structural-shifts]]). The principle has limits: in poetry, advertising and jokes, the effect may require a free rendering from the start, and in some legal texts the purpose may require a closeness that sacrifices naturalness ([[translation/specialised-translation]]).
:::

::: warning Mistaking a gloss for a translation
A word-for-word gloss shows the structure of a sentence; it is not a translation.

✗ 他是一个好的游泳者。 (for *He is a good swimmer.*)

✓ 他游泳游得很好。 / 他很会游泳。

✗ *Today weather very good, we go park play.* (for 今天天气很好，我们去公园玩吧。)

✓ *It's a lovely day — let's go to the park.*

In this atlas, when the structure of a Chinese sentence matters, its English gloss is labelled as such: 我们去公园玩吧 (word for word: ‘we go park play 吧’).
:::

## Equivalence

In the 1950s and 1960s, linguists tried to make the study of translation more systematic, and the central idea they used was **equivalence**: a translation is a target text equivalent to the source text. But equivalent in what?

::: definition Formal and dynamic equivalence {#def-equivalence}
Eugene Nida, in *Toward a Science of Translating* (1964), distinguished two orientations:

- **Formal equivalence** focuses on the message itself, in form as well as content: the translation matches the source's sentences, concepts and images as closely as possible, so that the reader can see how the original expressed itself.
- **Dynamic equivalence** aims at the **equivalent effect**: the translation should produce in its readers essentially the same response as the original produced in its readers, in natural target-language expression.

Nida later preferred the term **functional equivalence** (de Waard and Nida, *From One Language to Another*, 1986).
:::

Nida developed these ideas as a Bible translator, translating for readers whose cultures were far from that of the original text, and his own preference was for dynamic equivalence in most cases. With Charles Taber, in *The Theory and Practice of Translation* (1969), he defined translating as reproducing in the receptor language the closest natural equivalent of the source message, first in meaning and then in style.

::: example Formal and dynamic equivalence {#ex-equivalence}
An English children's story says: *She was as busy as a bee all morning, and by lunchtime the kitchen was spotless.* Give a formally equivalent and a dynamically equivalent Chinese translation, and say when each would be chosen.
::: solution
1. **Find the image and its effect.** *As busy as a bee* is a fixed English simile meaning ‘very busy, working steadily and cheerfully’. For an English reader it is a familiar, light expression, not a vivid picture.
2. **Formal equivalence** keeps the image: 她整个上午像蜜蜂一样忙碌，到午饭时厨房已经一尘不染。 Chinese readers understand the bee (bees are proverbially hard-working in Chinese too: 勤劳的小蜜蜂), so the formal version works here — though it sounds a little more vivid, and more "foreign", than the English.
3. **Dynamic equivalence** looks for what a Chinese writer would say: 她忙了一整个上午，忙得团团转，到吃午饭的时候，厨房已经收拾得干干净净。 团团转 gives the same everyday liveliness, and the reduplicated 干干净净 suits a children's story better than the four-character 一尘不染.
4. **Choice.** For a bilingual reader, or a text where the image will come back later (perhaps there is a bee in the story), keep the bee. For a picture book read aloud to Chinese children, the dynamic version sounds like a story told in Chinese. Both are translations; they serve different purposes.
:::
:::

Equivalent effect was criticised from the start: the response of the original readers is hard to know and cannot be measured in the new ones, and "natural" can mean "made to resemble what readers already know" — Venuti's point about domestication ([[translation/idioms-culture]]). Equivalence is best taken as a question: *equivalent in which respect, for which readers, for what purpose?*

## Semantic and communicative translation

Peter Newmark, a translator and teacher, turned the debate into a practical classification. In *Approaches to Translation* (1981) and *A Textbook of Translation* (1988) he distinguished **semantic translation**, which renders as closely as the target language allows the exact contextual meaning of the original, and stays with the author's thought; and **communicative translation**, which renders the contextual meaning in a way readily acceptable and comprehensible to the readership, and stays with the reader. He placed eight methods on a diagram shaped like a V, from those closest to the source language to those closest to the target language.

::: widget graph
title: Newmark's eight translation methods
nodes: emphasis on the source language@1,9; emphasis on the target language@9,9; word for word@1,7.5; literal@2,6; faithful@3,4.5; semantic@4,3; adaptation@9,7.5; free@8,6; idiomatic@7,4.5; communicative@6,3
edges: emphasis on the source language>word for word; word for word>literal; literal>faithful; faithful>semantic; emphasis on the target language>adaptation; adaptation>free; free>idiomatic; idiomatic>communicative
caption: After Newmark (1988), whose diagram is a V. The methods at the top are the extremes: word-for-word glosses on the left, adaptation (rewriting the text for a new culture, as in many stage versions) on the right. Going down the V, the methods come closer together; Newmark regarded only **semantic** and **communicative** translation, at the bottom, as fulfilling the two main aims of translation — accuracy and economy. Drag the nodes to place a translation you know on the diagram.
:::

Newmark tied the choice of method to the **type of text**. Following the psychologist Karl Bühler's functions of language, he distinguished **expressive** texts (literature, authoritative statements, personal writing), where the author's words matter in themselves; **informative** texts (reports, textbooks, technical documents), where the facts matter; and **vocative** texts (advertising, instructions, propaganda, popular fiction), which aim to make the reader act, think or feel. In general, expressive texts call for semantic translation and informative and vocative texts for communicative translation — though most real texts mix the functions.

A related idea, the **skopos theory** of Katharina Reiss and Hans Vermeer (*Grundlegung einer allgemeinen Translationstheorie*, 1984), puts the **purpose** (Greek *skopos*) of the translation first, which may differ from that of the source: a Chinese product brochure translated for foreign customers, for a court case or for a study of marketing calls for three different translations.

::: theorem Choose the method from the purpose and the text {#rule-brief}
Before translating, establish the **brief**:

1. **Who will read the translation**, and what do they already know?
2. **What is it for** — to inform, to persuade, to instruct, to be enjoyed, to serve as a legal or historical record?
3. **What kind of text is the source** — expressive, informative or vocative — and does the translation have the same function?
4. **What constraints apply**: length (subtitles, signs), layout, terminology lists, house style, a deadline?

Then choose where on Newmark's V to work, and keep that choice consistent through the text.
:::

::: proof
The brief turns the quarrel between literal and free into a question with an answer. The same sentence can rightly be translated in different ways, as the 吃饭了吗 example showed, and the brief tells the translator which way. In professional work, the brief comes from the client, often as written specifications agreed before the work begins; in exercises, it should be imagined, because a translation done for no reader is judged by no clear standard. Consistency matters because readers notice a text that changes method half-way — keeping foreign images in one paragraph and replacing them in the next.
:::

::: example Choosing a method by text type {#ex-text-type}
Decide which of Newmark's methods each English text calls for when translated into Chinese, and why. (a) A sonnet by Shakespeare. (b) The safety instructions for a microwave oven. (c) An advertising slogan for a bicycle shop: *Life's a journey. Enjoy the ride.* (d) A clause in a contract.
::: solution
(a) **Expressive.** The words and form matter in themselves, so the translation is semantic: close to the meaning and images, keeping as much of the form (fourteen lines, rhyme) as the translator can. Some translators keep the form and lose detail; others keep the detail in prose. ([[translation/literary-translation]].)

(b) **Informative and vocative** (the reader must act safely). Communicative: clear, natural Chinese in the conventions of Chinese instructions — imperatives, short sentences, standard terms (请勿, 切勿, 注意). Accuracy of content is absolute; the English sentence structure is irrelevant.

(c) **Vocative.** Communicative, near the "free" end: the slogan must work as a slogan. A literal rendering (生活是一段旅程，享受这段骑行) loses the pun on *ride*. A version such as 人生是一场旅行，骑上它，慢慢享受 keeps the idea; a shop might prefer a four-character pair. This is close to adaptation.

(d) **Informative** with legal force. Semantic and faithful: every condition and obligation must be rendered exactly, in the fixed formulas of Chinese contracts, even at some cost to naturalness ([[translation/specialised-translation]]).
:::
:::

::: quiz
A museum asks you to translate its Chinese exhibition labels into English for foreign visitors. Which method fits best?
- [ ] Word-for-word, so that visitors see the Chinese structure.
- [ ] Literal, keeping every four-character phrase as it is.
- [x] Communicative, with names and key terms kept and explained where necessary.
- [ ] Adaptation, replacing Chinese historical references with British ones.
::: solution
Labels are informative: visitors need to understand quickly what they are looking at. Communicative translation gives clear, natural English; names of people, dynasties and objects are kept (in pinyin, with a short gloss if needed), because they are what the visitor came to see. Replacing them with British references would destroy the point of the museum.
:::
:::

## The translator at work

How does a translator actually produce a translation? Nida and Taber (1969) described the process in three stages: **analysis** of the source (its grammar and meaning), **transfer** of the meaning into the target language in the translator's mind, and **restructuring** in the target language. Practising translators usually describe it as a cycle of reading, drafting and revising.

::: theorem A working procedure {#rule-procedure}
1. **Read the whole text** before translating anything. Establish the brief, the text type and the register.
2. **Understand each sentence**: find its main clause, the relations between its parts, the reference of every pronoun, and the meaning of every word in context. If you could not paraphrase the sentence in the source language, you are not ready to translate it.
3. **Draft** sentence by sentence, but think in units of meaning, not words; a source sentence may become two target sentences, or two may become one.
4. **Revise as a target text**: read the draft without the source, as a reader would. Mark anything unnatural, unclear or ambiguous.
5. **Check against the source**: compare sentence by sentence for omissions, additions, wrong meanings, numbers, names and terminology.
6. **Leave time** between drafting and revising if you can: a translator who has just read the source cannot see what the target text says to a reader who has not.
:::

::: proof
Steps 4 and 5 are the two loyalties of [[#rule-two-loyalties]], checked separately, because they cannot be checked together: when the source is in front of you, you read its meaning into your translation and miss the places where the translation does not say it. Step 2 matters most between English and Chinese, where many errors come from misreading the source — a main clause not found, or the implicit links of a Chinese sentence (because? although? if?) guessed wrongly ([[translation/english-chinese-compared]]).
:::

::: example Working through the procedure {#ex-procedure}
Translate into English, for a travel website, this sentence from a Chinese description of a town: 古镇不大，一条小河穿镇而过，两岸都是明清时期的老房子，走累了可以在河边的茶馆坐一坐。
::: solution
1. **Brief and register.** Informative and vocative (it should make the reader want to go); a friendly, clear travel register.
2. **Understand.** Four clauses with no connectives: the town is small; a stream runs through it; both banks are lined with old houses from the Ming and Qing; when (if) you are tired of walking, you can sit in a riverside teahouse. The topic of the first three is the town; the last has an implicit *you*.
3. **Draft.** *The old town is small, with a stream running through the middle. Both banks are lined with houses from the Ming and Qing dynasties (1368–1912), and when your legs need a rest, there are teahouses by the water where you can sit for a while.*
4. **Revise as English.** Two sentences instead of one; the second clause becomes a *with*-phrase, the fourth a *when*-clause; dates are added for readers who do not know the dynasties.
5. **Check.** 古镇 → *the old town* ✓; 穿镇而过 → *running through the middle* ✓; 两岸 ✓; 明清 ✓ (dates added — acceptable for this purpose, and checked); 走累了 → *when your legs need a rest* (a slight change of image, natural in English) ✓; 茶馆 is plural in English because nothing says there is only one — check the source or the facts if possible.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Directions differ.** In many countries professional translators work mainly into their first language. In China, much translation into English is done by Chinese translators, often with a reviser whose first language is English. Learners should practise both directions and know that they need different skills: comprehension of English for English → Chinese, production of English for Chinese → English ([[translation/chinese-into-english]]).
- **Different traditions of naturalness.** Educated Chinese prose values balance and four-character phrases; good English prose values plain words and varied sentences ([[style-and-rhetoric/word-choice]]). Neither tradition's ornament carries mechanically into the other.
- **Translationese in both directions.** 翻译腔 (Chinese that reads like English) and Chinglish (English that reads like Chinese, [[collocation-and-register/false-friends-chinglish]]) are the same phenomenon seen from two sides: the structures of the source pushing through into the target. Both are signs of a translator working at the level of words rather than meaning.
- **For classical Chinese**, see the Chinese Atlas lesson on [translating 文言](/learn/chinese/lesson.php?c=classical-chinese-1&l=translating), and for translation and the reception of world literature in China, its lesson on [translation and reception](/learn/chinese/lesson.php?c=world-literature&l=translation-reception).
:::

::: history
Both great traditions of thinking about translation grew out of religious translation. In China, Buddhist scriptures were translated from Sanskrit and other languages for roughly a thousand years, from the Han to the Song, and Xuanzang (玄奘, 602–664) led one of the largest translation projects in history at the Tang court. In Europe, Jerome translated the Bible into Latin in the late fourth century, and the Reformation brought vernacular Bibles — Luther's German, Tyndale's English New Testament (1526), the King James Version (1611). The late Qing saw a new wave: Yan Fu's translations of Western thought and Lin Shu's classical-Chinese versions of foreign novels. Translation studies as an academic discipline is younger: the scholar James S. Holmes gave the field its name in a paper of 1972, "The Name and Nature of Translation Studies", and the linguistic theories of Vinay and Darbelnet (1958), Nida (1964) and Catford (1965) were followed by functional approaches (skopos theory), descriptive studies of how translations actually behave (Gideon Toury, Itamar Even-Zohar) and, from the 1990s, the "cultural turn" associated with Susan Bassnett, André Lefevere and Lawrence Venuti.
:::

:::

::: exercise Gloss or translation? {level=1}
For each pair, say which is a word-for-word gloss and which is a translation. (a) 我肚子饿了 — *I'm hungry.* / *I belly hungry 了.* (b) *It's raining cats and dogs.* — 正在下猫和狗。/ 外面下着倾盆大雨。
::: solution
(a) The first English version is a translation; the second is a gloss. (b) 正在下猫和狗 is a word-for-word rendering, meaningless in Chinese; 外面下着倾盆大雨 is a translation.

::: exercise Text types {level=1}
Classify each text as mainly expressive, informative or vocative (in Newmark's terms), and say whether you would translate it mainly semantically or communicatively. (a) A user manual for a printer. (b) A poem by Li Bai. (c) A poster urging people to save water. (d) A scientific article on rice genetics. (e) A political leader's famous speech.
::: solution
(a) Informative (with vocative instructions) — communicative. (b) Expressive — semantic. (c) Vocative — communicative, possibly near adaptation. (d) Informative — communicative, with exact terminology. (e) Expressive as well as vocative: an authoritative statement whose exact words are quoted and studied — Newmark would translate it semantically, keeping the speaker's words and images even where they are unusual.

::: exercise Ranking three translations {level=2}
Rank these translations of *Please keep off the grass* for a sign in a Chinese park, and give your reasons using the ideas of this lesson. (a) 请保持离开草地。 (b) 请勿践踏草坪。 (c) 小草也有生命，请您脚下留情。
::: hint
Think of both loyalties, and of the register of signs in Chinese.
:::
::: solution
(b) is the standard notice: accurate, natural, in the compact register of signs (请勿 + verb). (c) is also natural and common on Chinese park signs; it adds a reason and a gentle tone that the English does not have — a communicative, partly adapted version, acceptable if the park wants a friendly tone, but less accurate. (a) is a word-for-word rendering of *keep off*: 保持离开 is not Chinese. Ranking for a standard notice: (b), (c), (a). (Some would put (c) first for a family park; the brief decides.)
:::

::: exercise What did Yan Fu say? {level=2}
A classmate says: "Yan Fu said that a translation must be faithful above all, and that 雅 means using beautiful language." Correct both claims using the text of the 译例言 and what this lesson says about his practice.
::: solution
First, Yan Fu called 信、达、雅 three *difficulties*, and his own sentence ranks 达 above 信 when they conflict: 信矣不达，虽译犹不译也 — a faithful translation that is not comprehensible is no translation at all. Second, for Yan Fu 雅 meant writing in the classical prose of pre-Han writers, chosen to win the respect of his scholar-official readers; it was not a general call for beautiful language, and later writers reinterpreted it as a style appropriate to the original. His own translation of Huxley was free, with summaries and commentaries, and he described it as conveying the gist (达旨).
:::
:::

::: exercise Two purposes, two translations {level=2}
Translate the Chinese sentence 欢迎您下次光临！ (printed on a shop receipt) twice: (a) as a gloss for a student of Chinese grammar; (b) for the English version of the receipt.
::: solution
(a) A gloss: *welcome you next time come-and-honour (us with your presence)!* — 光临 is a polite word for a guest's coming, literally ‘bring light’. (b) For the receipt: *Thank you for shopping with us.* / *We look forward to seeing you again.* / *Please come again.* English receipts usually thank rather than welcome; ✗ *Welcome to come again next time* is a well-known calque.
:::

::: exercise Same text, different briefs {level=3}
This sentence comes from a fictional Chinese restaurant's menu: 本店招牌菜——外婆红烧肉，选用上等五花肉，慢火炖煮三小时，肥而不腻，入口即化。 Translate it (a) for the restaurant's English menu, (b) for a food scientist who wants to know exactly what the dish is and how it is made. Then explain the differences between your two versions.
::: hint
For (a), the menu is vocative: it must make people want to order. For (b), every fact matters; the persuasive phrases can be translated and marked as such, or summarised.
:::
::: solution
Models:

(a) *Our signature dish: Grandma's Red-Braised Pork. Prime pork belly, slow-braised for three hours until rich, never greasy, and melt-in-the-mouth tender.*

(b) *The restaurant's signature dish, "grandmother's red-braised pork" (外婆红烧肉), made from pork belly described as top quality, braised over a low heat for three hours. The menu describes it as fatty but not greasy, and as melting in the mouth.*

Differences: (a) uses the conventions of English menus — a capitalised dish name, verbless descriptions, appealing adjectives (*rich*, *tender*); 肥而不腻 is turned into a positive (*rich, never greasy*), because *fatty* is unappealing in English. (b) keeps the Chinese name, translates 红烧 literally with no commercial gloss, and marks the evaluative claims as the menu's (*described as*), because the reader wants facts, not persuasion. Both are faithful in different senses.
:::

::: exercise Write a brief {level=3}
Choose a short Chinese text you might really need to translate — a notice in your building, a WeChat message to a foreign colleague, a paragraph from a school website — and write a translation brief for it using the four questions of [[#rule-brief]]. Then translate it, and write three sentences explaining one decision that the brief made for you.
::: solution
Answers vary. A good brief names a real reader (e.g. *a new colleague from Kenya who reads English well but no Chinese*), a purpose (*to know when the water will be cut off and what to do*), the text type (*informative and vocative*) and the constraints (*a message, under 80 words*). A good explanation links a decision to the brief: *I left out 给大家带来不便，敬请谅解 as a separate sentence and wrote "Sorry for the inconvenience" at the end, because in an English message the apology is short and comes last; the reader needs the time and the instructions first.*
:::
