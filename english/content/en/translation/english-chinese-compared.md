Here is a sentence of ordinary written Chinese and a natural English translation:

> 天气预报说明天有雨，运动会改到下周三，大家别忘了带运动鞋。
>
> *The weather forecast says it will rain tomorrow, so the sports day has been moved to next Wednesday. Don't forget to bring your trainers.*

The Chinese is one sentence of three clauses separated by commas, with no word that says how they are related. A Chinese reader knows at once that the second clause is the result of the first, and that the third is a reminder addressed to the readers. The English makes those relations visible: *so* marks the result, a full stop separates the statement from the request, *your* says whose trainers. The English also says things the Chinese leaves to context: *it will rain* (a dummy subject *it* and a future form), *has been moved* (a present perfect passive), *the* before *sports day*, the plural *-s* on *trainers*. None of these has a word in the Chinese for it to translate.

Every sentence translated between English and Chinese runs into differences of this kind. They are not random; they come from a small number of systematic contrasts between the two languages, which Chinese contrastive linguists have described in detail — most fully in Lian Shuneng's 《英汉对比研究》 (1993; enlarged edition 2010). This lesson sets out the contrasts that matter most to a translator: how clauses are joined, what a sentence is built around, whether meaning is carried by nouns or by verbs, what kind of thing can be a subject, what must be marked in the grammar, the order of information, and how a text avoids or uses repetition. The next lessons turn each contrast into techniques.

## Hypotaxis and parataxis: 形合 and 意合

::: definition Hypotaxis and parataxis {#def-hypotaxis}
**Hypotaxis** (形合, ‘joining by form’) is the linking of clauses and phrases by explicit grammatical means: subordinating and coordinating conjunctions (*because*, *although*, *when*, *and*), relative words (*which*, *who*), prepositions, and non-finite forms (*having finished*, *to avoid*). **Parataxis** (意合, ‘joining by meaning’) is the placing of clauses side by side without such markers, so that the relations between them are understood from their meaning and order.
:::

::: theorem English tends to hypotaxis, Chinese to parataxis {#rule-hypotaxis}
In most registers, **English marks the relations between clauses explicitly**, and an English sentence usually has one main clause to which the others are grammatically subordinate. **Chinese more often juxtaposes clauses**, especially in speech and in narrative and descriptive writing, and leaves cause, condition, concession, time and purpose to be inferred.

Consequences for translation:

1. **Chinese → English**: decide the relation between each pair of clauses and **add** the connective or structure that expresses it ([[translation/chinese-into-english]]).
2. **English → Chinese**: many English connectives, relative pronouns and prepositions can be **left out**, and subordinate structures turned into a sequence of short clauses ([[translation/english-into-chinese]]).
:::

::: proof
The contrast is a tendency, not an absolute. Chinese has a full set of connectives — 因为……所以, 虽然……但是, 如果……就, 只要……就, 以便 — and formal and academic Chinese uses them freely; English has paratactic sentences too, especially in speech (*I was tired, I went to bed*) and in literary style (*I came, I saw, I conquered*). But the default differs. English grammar requires a finite verb in every main clause and forbids joining two main clauses with a comma alone (the comma splice, [[sentences-and-paragraphs/punctuation]]), so a writer who strings clauses together must choose a connective or a full stop. Chinese grammar has no such requirement: a sentence can run on as a chain of clauses separated by commas — what Lü Shuxiang (吕叔湘) called 流水句, ‘flowing-water sentences’, in 《汉语语法分析问题》 (1979). The Chinese grammarian Wang Li discussed this "joining by meaning" in the 1940s, and 形合 and 意合 are now standard terms of contrastive study (Lian 2010). Translators feel the contrast in both directions: Chinese translated clause by clause gives English with comma splices and no clear structure; English translated connective by connective gives Chinese crowded with 因为, 当……的时候 and 的.
:::

::: example Finding the hidden links {#ex-hidden-links}
State the relation between the clauses in each Chinese sentence, and translate it into English with an appropriate connective. (a) 你不说，我怎么知道？ (b) 有问题，随时找我。 (c) 东西不多，心意是真的。 (d) 他病了，没来上班。
::: solution
(a) **Condition or cause**, as a rhetorical question: *If you don't tell me, how am I supposed to know?* / *How could I know if you didn't tell me?* (The tense depends on context: is this before or after the event?)

(b) **Condition**: *If you have any questions, come and find me any time.* / *Any questions, just ask me* (spoken English can also be paratactic).

(c) **Concession**: *It isn't much, but it comes from the heart.* / *Although it's only a small gift, it's sincerely meant.*

(d) **Cause and effect**: *He was ill, so he didn't come to work.* / *He didn't come to work because he was ill.* / *He was off sick.* Note that English can also use a single clause with an adverbial — *He was off work with flu* — a structure that has no Chinese equivalent.

In every case, the translator has to *decide* the relation; if the context does not settle it, the translator must find out or choose the most neutral connective.
:::
:::

::: warning The comma splice from 意合
A Chinese chain of clauses copied into English with commas produces comma splices:

✗ *The hotel is near the station, it is very convenient, the rooms are clean.*

✓ *The hotel is near the station, which makes it very convenient, and the rooms are clean.*

✓ *The hotel is clean and conveniently close to the station.*

Commas in Chinese separate clauses within a sentence; in English a comma cannot by itself join two independent clauses.
:::

## Subject and topic

::: definition Subject-prominent and topic-prominent languages {#def-topic}
In a **topic–comment** sentence, the first element names what the sentence is about (the **topic**) and the rest says something about it (the **comment**), whether or not the topic is the grammatical subject of the verb: 这本书我看过 ‘this book, I have read’. Charles Li and Sandra Thompson ("Subject and topic: a new typology of language", 1976) classified languages as **subject-prominent**, in which the basic sentence is organised around a grammatical subject (English), or **topic-prominent**, in which it is organised around a topic (Chinese).
:::

Yuen Ren Chao (赵元任), in *A Grammar of Spoken Chinese* (1968), had already argued that the Chinese subject is best understood as a topic. A few Chinese sentences show why:

- 这件事你别管了。 — topic 这件事 ‘this matter’; the subject of 管 is 你. *Don't get involved in this.* / *Leave this to me.*
- 大象鼻子长。 — topic 大象, then a second clause 鼻子长. *Elephants have long trunks.*
- 那场火，幸亏消防队来得快。 — topic 那场火; the comment does not even mention it. *Luckily the fire brigade got to that fire quickly.*
- 今天开会。 — no subject at all; 今天 is a time topic. *There's a meeting today.* / *We have a meeting today.*

English requires a subject in almost every finite clause, and the subject is tightly bound to the verb: it agrees with it (*she goes*), it is usually the doer or the experiencer, and when there is no natural candidate English supplies a **dummy subject** — *it* (*It's raining*, *It's important to …*) or *there* (*There's a meeting*).

::: theorem Choosing the English subject {#rule-subject}
When translating Chinese into English, **do not assume that the first noun is the subject**. For each clause, decide which participant English should make the subject:

1. the **doer** of the main action, if one is named or obvious (那场火，幸亏消防队来得快 → *the fire brigade*);
2. a **possessor** relation turned into *have* (大象鼻子长 → *Elephants have …*);
3. the **topic** as subject of a passive, if the doer is unknown or unimportant (这座桥去年修好了 → *The bridge was repaired last year*);
4. **dummy *it* or *there***, for weather, time, existence and extraposed clauses (今天开会 → *There's a meeting today*);
5. **an implied person** (*you*, *we*, *people*) for general statements (走累了可以休息 → *If you get tired, you can rest*).
:::

::: proof
The rule follows from the grammar of the two languages. In English the subject controls agreement and is usually the starting point of the message and the participant the verb is about; a sentence with the "wrong" subject is grammatical but strange (*The trunk of the elephant is long* is correct English, but no one says it). In Chinese the topic sets the frame, and the verb's arguments need not be expressed if context supplies them; Chinese also drops subjects freely when they can be recovered (zero anaphora), which English does only in diaries, notes and informal speech (*Went to the cinema. Loved it.*). Translating into English is therefore often a matter of re-centring the sentence on a participant. Translating into Chinese, the reverse move — putting a topic first and letting the comment follow — often makes English sentences sound natural in Chinese: *I have never read this book* → 这本书我从来没读过.
:::

::: example Topics into subjects {#ex-topics}
Translate into English, choosing the subject with [[#rule-subject]]. (a) 这个问题我们下次再讨论。 (b) 北京我去过三次。 (c) 屋里很暗，什么也看不见。 (d) 这种药饭后吃。
::: solution
(a) Topic 这个问题, subject 我们. *We'll discuss this question next time.* / *Let's come back to this next time.* (Or a passive: *This question will be discussed at the next meeting* — more formal.)

(b) *I've been to Beijing three times.* — the topic becomes the complement of a preposition.

(c) The second clause has no subject; it means that nobody (or "I") could see anything. *It was so dark in the room that I couldn't see a thing.* / *The room was too dark to see anything.* The paratactic chain becomes a *so … that* or *too … to* structure — hypotaxis.

(d) The topic is the object of 吃 and the doer is the patient: *Take this medicine after meals.* (An imperative, as on English medicine labels.) A passive is also possible: *This medicine should be taken after meals.*
:::
:::

## Static and dynamic: nouns and verbs

::: definition Nominal and verbal style {#def-static}
Lian Shuneng (2010) describes written English as tending to a **static** style (静态), in which actions and processes are often expressed by **nouns** (*the arrival of*, *a decision to*, *the reduction in*), prepositions and adjectives, and Chinese as tending to a **dynamic** style (动态), in which they are expressed by **verbs**, often several verbs in sequence (serial verb constructions: 他开车去机场接朋友 ‘he drove to the airport to meet a friend’).
:::

::: theorem Nouns in English, verbs in Chinese {#rule-static}
1. **English → Chinese**: an English **nominalisation** or an English noun that implies an action (*arrival*, *refusal*, *a good swimmer*, *in the absence of*) is often best translated by a Chinese **verb** or clause: *His arrival surprised everyone* → 他来了，大家都很意外.
2. **Chinese → English**: a sequence of Chinese verbs is often best translated with **one finite verb** and the other actions as nouns, prepositional phrases, participles or infinitives: 他放下电话，穿上外套，出了门 → *Putting down the phone, he pulled on his coat and went out.* / 我们要加强管理，提高效率 → *We need stronger management and greater efficiency.*
:::

::: proof
English allows one finite verb per clause and makes heavy use of nouns derived from verbs, especially in formal and academic writing, where Biber et al. (1999) found nominalisations far more frequent than in conversation ([[academic-reading/dense-sentences]]). Prepositions also carry much of the meaning that Chinese gives to verbs: *a man in a grey coat* (穿灰外套的人), *across the river* (过了河), *for the children* (为了孩子). Chinese, by contrast, has a small set of prepositions, many of which are still verbs (在, 给, 用, 跟, 把), and allows several verbs in sequence with no conjunction. The contrast is again a tendency: modern formal Chinese nominalises too (对……进行研究, 予以批准, 加以改进), partly under the influence of translation from European languages, and good English style often prefers verbs to nouns ([[style-and-rhetoric/clarity]]). A translator should not make Chinese more nominal than the original, nor English more verbal than its genre requires.
:::

::: example Unpacking and packing {#ex-static}
(a) Translate into Chinese: *The rapid growth of the city has led to a shortage of housing.* (b) Translate into English, for a report: 我们调查了二十所学校，发现大多数学生每天睡眠不足八小时。
::: solution
(a) The English has one verb (*has led*) and three nouns of process (*growth*, *shortage*, *housing*). Natural Chinese uses verbs: 城市发展太快，住房不够了。 / 由于城市迅速发展，住房出现短缺。 The first is spoken and plain; the second keeps a little of the formality, with 由于 for the cause. ? 城市的快速增长已导致了住房的短缺 is understandable but is 翻译腔: three 的-phrases and 导致了 copying *has led to*.

(b) The Chinese has three verbs in sequence: 调查, 发现, 睡眠 (as a noun) + 不足. English for a report: *A survey of twenty schools found that most students sleep less than eight hours a night.* — *survey* (a noun) replaces 我们调查了, and the finding becomes the main verb. A version with the researchers as subject is also correct: *We surveyed twenty schools and found that …* — but reports often prefer the nominal style.
:::
:::

## What can be a subject: 物称 and 人称

A related contrast concerns the **kind** of subject. English, especially in writing, often has an inanimate or abstract subject acting on a person: *A sudden fear seized her. The thought never crossed my mind. The news brought tears to his eyes. This year saw a rise in exports.* Lian calls this a preference for **impersonal subjects** (物称) in English and **personal subjects** (人称) in Chinese, where the person is more often the subject and the experience is expressed by the verb.

| English (impersonal subject) | Chinese (personal subject) |
|---|---|
| *A sudden fear seized her.* | 她突然害怕起来。 |
| *It never occurred to me that he was lying.* | 我从来没想到他在说谎。 |
| *The sight of the sea always calms me.* | 我一看到大海，心里就平静下来。 |
| *2025 saw a sharp rise in exports.* | 2025年出口大幅增长。 |
| *Her illness kept her away from school for a month.* | 她病了，一个月没上学。 |

The last two rows show other ways out: a time topic in Chinese (2025年……) and a paratactic chain of cause and effect. Translating into English, the reverse — an abstract subject — is one way to give an English sentence a tighter shape: 我一想起那天的事就后悔 → *The memory of that day still fills me with regret* (literary) or *I still regret what happened that day* (plain). Neither is wrong; the register decides.

::: quiz
Which Chinese translation of *The heavy traffic made us late for the concert* is the most natural?
- [ ] 沉重的交通使得我们对于音乐会迟到了。
- [ ] 繁忙的交通让我们音乐会迟到。
- [x] 路上堵车，我们去听音乐会迟到了。
- [ ] 交通的繁重造成了我们音乐会的迟到。
::: solution
The natural Chinese makes the people the subject and puts the cause first as its own clause: 路上堵车 ‘the roads were jammed’. The other versions keep *traffic* as the subject of a causative (使得, 让, 造成), and two of them translate *heavy* as 沉重 or 繁重, which do not collocate with 交通 in this sense.
:::
:::

## What the grammar must say

English grammar obliges speakers to mark several things that Chinese grammar leaves to context.

::: theorem Obligatory categories {#rule-categories}
| category | English | Chinese |
|---|---|---|
| **tense** | every finite verb is present or past (*works*, *worked*) | no tense inflection; time words (昨天, 将来) and context |
| **aspect** | progressive and perfect forms | 了, 过, 着, 在, often optional |
| **number** | singular or plural on most nouns (*book*, *books*) | no plural for most nouns (书); 们 for some groups of people; numbers and classifiers when needed |
| **definiteness** | *a*, *the* or zero before most nouns | no articles; 这, 那, 一个 when needed, word order |
| **gender** | *he*, *she*, *it* | spoken *tā* for all three (written 他, 她, 它) |
| **agreement** | *he goes*, *they go* | none |

Translating **into English**, the translator must **supply** these categories, deciding from context what the Chinese did not say. Translating **into Chinese**, the translator must decide **which to express** and which to leave implicit: expressing all of them gives 翻译腔.
:::

::: proof
These differences are well described in reference grammars of Chinese (Li and Thompson, *Mandarin Chinese: A Functional Reference Grammar*, 1981) and in the tense lessons of this atlas ([[tense-and-aspect/tense-and-chinese]]). A Chinese sentence such as 我在银行工作 can mean *I work at a bank* or *I worked at a bank*, and 他买了书 can mean *He bought a book*, *He bought the book*, *He bought books* or *He bought some books*; context nearly always decides, and when it does not, Chinese adds a word. English cannot leave these choices open, so a translator into English who does not know the facts must find them out — when was this, how many, which one? Translating into Chinese, the opposite danger is to translate every *a* as 一个, every *the* as 这个 or 那个, every plural as 们 and every past tense as 了: *The students handed in their essays* is 学生们交了作文 or simply 学生交了作文, not 那些学生们交了他们的作文.
:::

::: warning Translating every article and pronoun
✗ 一个男人走进了一家商店，他买了一份报纸。他付了钱，然后他离开了那家商店。 (for *A man walked into a shop and bought a newspaper. He paid and left the shop.*)

✓ 有个男人走进一家商店，买了份报纸，付了钱就走了。

The Chinese leaves the repeated subject and the second *shop* implicit (zero anaphora), uses 有个 to introduce a new person, and joins the actions in one chain.
:::

## The order of information

::: theorem Order in the two languages {#rule-order}
1. **Modifiers**: Chinese puts all modifiers of a noun, however long, **before** it, with 的; English puts single words before the noun and phrases and clauses **after** it ([[complex-sentences/relative-clauses]]).
2. **Adverbials**: Chinese puts time and place adverbials **before** the verb (他昨天在图书馆看书); English usually puts them **after** the verb and object (*He read in the library yesterday*).
3. **Events**: Chinese clauses tend to follow the **order of events** and of logic — cause before effect, condition before result, background before the main point. English often puts the **main clause first** and adds the background after it, or chooses the order for emphasis.
4. **Focus**: both languages tend to put new and important information late in the clause (end-focus); English can move elements to achieve it with the passive, *there*, clefts and inversion ([[complex-sentences/emphasis-inversion]]).
:::

::: proof
Point 3 has been formulated as the **principle of temporal sequence** by James Tai ("Temporal sequence and Chinese word order", 1985): the order of two syntactic units in Chinese corresponds to the order in time of the states they represent. His examples include 他坐公共汽车到这儿 ‘he came here by bus’ (first getting on the bus, then arriving) and 他到这儿坐公共汽车 ‘he came here to take the bus’ (first arriving, then the bus). English expresses the same difference with prepositions and infinitives, in either order. The practical consequence is that a translator into Chinese often reverses the order of English clauses — *I'll call you when I arrive* → 我到了给你打电话 — and a translator into English decides which Chinese clause is the main point and puts it where English emphasis wants it.
:::

::: example Reordering by time and logic {#ex-order}
Translate into Chinese, putting the parts in natural Chinese order. (a) *She went back to her home town after twenty years abroad.* (b) *You can't enter the lab unless you have completed the safety course.* (c) *A conference on climate change was held in Kunming last week, attended by delegates from thirty countries.*
::: solution
(a) Time first, then the event: 她在国外待了二十年之后，回到了家乡。 / 她在国外生活二十年后回到了家乡。

(b) Condition first, then the result: 只有完成安全培训，才能进入实验室。 / 没有完成安全培训的人不得进入实验室。 The English *unless*-clause comes second; the Chinese 只有……才 clause comes first.

(c) Time and place before the verb, and the attendance as a clause after the main event: 上周，一场气候变化会议在昆明召开，来自三十个国家的代表出席了会议。 / 上周在昆明召开了气候变化会议，三十个国家的代表参加。 The English participle *attended by* becomes a new clause with its own subject.
:::
:::

## Substitution and repetition

::: theorem English varies, Chinese repeats {#rule-repetition}
English texts usually avoid repeating a noun or verb in close succession: they use **pronouns** (*it*, *they*, *this*), **substitutes** (*one*, *do so*, *the former*), **synonyms** (*the city … the capital … Beijing*) and **ellipsis** (*She can swim and I can too*). Chinese tolerates — and often prefers — **repetition** of the noun or verb, uses pronouns less, and often leaves out a repeated element entirely (zero anaphora). Translating into Chinese, replace English substitutes with repeated nouns or with nothing where clarity allows; translating into English, replace repeated Chinese nouns with pronouns and substitutes.
:::

::: proof
English cohesion relies heavily on reference and substitution (Halliday and Hasan, *Cohesion in English*, 1976), and English style guides discourage close repetition, though they warn against "elegant variation" that confuses the reader ([[style-and-rhetoric/cohesion-emphasis]]). Chinese, with less use of pronouns for things and no substitutes like *one* or *do so*, relies more on lexical repetition and on zero anaphora; repetition of a key word also carries rhetorical weight in Chinese (排比, 对偶). Lian (2010) treats this as one of his ten contrasts (替换与重复). Examples: *Fresh vegetables are cheaper than frozen ones* → 新鲜蔬菜比冷冻蔬菜便宜; 要发展经济，就要发展教育 → *Economic growth depends on education* (one noun instead of two parallel clauses with 发展).
:::

::: quiz
Which English translation of 我们要学习，学习，再学习 best keeps the force of the original in a speech?
- [ ] *We must study, study, study again.*
- [x] *We must keep learning — and learning, and learning.*
- [ ] *We must study, and do so again and again.*
- [ ] *We must study it repeatedly.*
::: solution
The Chinese repeats the verb for emphasis, a rhetorical figure that English speeches also use, with a different shape. *Keep learning — and learning, and learning* keeps the repetition and makes it idiomatic English (*keep* + *-ing*, polysyndeton). *Study, study, study again* copies the Chinese rhythm but *again* is odd; the other two lose the emphasis.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which contrast explains why 他坐车去上班 and *He goes to work by car* place the means of transport differently?
- [ ] Subject and topic.
- [x] The order of events: Chinese tends to follow temporal sequence, and English expresses the means with a phrase after the verb.
- [ ] Hypotaxis and parataxis.
- [ ] Impersonal and personal subjects.
::: solution
In Chinese, 坐车 ‘sit in a car’ comes first because it happens first, and 去上班 follows (Tai's principle of temporal sequence). English puts the main verb first and expresses the means with the prepositional phrase *by car*, which can only follow it.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **All of these are tendencies.** Formal Chinese uses connectives, nominalisations and even long pre-noun modifiers; spoken English uses parataxis and personal subjects. Compare like with like: academic English with academic Chinese, a chat with a chat.
- **Translation has changed Chinese.** Many features of modern written Chinese — longer 的-phrases, 被 used without an unpleasant meaning, 当……的时候, 进行 + verbal noun — spread in the twentieth century partly through translation from European languages, a process Wang Li described as Europeanised grammar (欧化语法). A translator into Chinese can use these structures, but should not let them multiply.
- **The contrasts explain Chinglish.** Many of the errors described in [[collocation-and-register/false-friends-chinglish]] and in the grammar courses — comma splices, missing articles and plurals, topic-first sentences, ✗ *Although …, but …* — are these contrasts carried over word by word.
:::

::: history
Systematic comparison of Chinese and Western grammar began when Ma Jianzhong (马建忠) published 《马氏文通》 (1898), the first Chinese grammar written on the model of Latin grammar. In the 1940s Lü Shuxiang's 《中国文法要略》 and Wang Li's 《中国现代语法》 and 《中国语法理论》 described Chinese in its own terms, with frequent comparisons with English. In the West, Charles Fries and Robert Lado's contrastive analysis (Lado, *Linguistics across Cultures*, 1957) tried to predict learners' errors by comparing languages; the predictions proved only partly right, but the method survived in translation studies. In China, English–Chinese contrastive linguistics grew into a field after Lü Shuxiang called for it in a lecture of 1977; Lian Shuneng's 《英汉对比研究》 (1993) and the work of scholars such as Liu Miqing (刘宓庆) made it the theoretical basis of translation teaching in Chinese universities.
:::

- **Static and dynamic**: written English favours nouns and prepositions, Chinese favours verbs and verb sequences ([[#rule-static]]); English also uses impersonal subjects where Chinese uses personal ones.
- **Obligatory categories**: English must mark tense, number, definiteness and gender; Chinese marks them only when needed. Supply them into English, and leave many of them out into Chinese ([[#rule-categories]]).
- **Order**: Chinese puts modifiers and adverbials before the head and tends to follow the order of events; English puts phrases and clauses after the head and often leads with the main clause ([[#rule-order]]).
- **Repetition**: English varies and substitutes, Chinese repeats or omits ([[#rule-repetition]]).
- All of these are tendencies of register as well as of language.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Name the contrast {level=1}
Which contrast of this lesson does each pair illustrate? (a) *If you don't go, I won't either.* — 你不去，我也不去。 (b) *There are three people in my family.* — 我家有三口人。 (c) *His refusal surprised us.* — 他拒绝了，我们很意外。 (d) *The book that I bought yesterday* — 我昨天买的书 (e) *A wave of homesickness swept over him.* — 他突然非常想家。
::: solution
(a) Hypotaxis and parataxis (*if* vs no connective). (b) Subject and topic / dummy *there* vs a topic (我家). (c) Static and dynamic (noun *refusal* vs verb 拒绝). (d) The order of modifiers. (e) Impersonal and personal subjects.
:::
:::

::: exercise Supply the categories {level=1}
Translate into English, and list the categories (tense, number, definiteness …) you had to decide. Say what context you assumed. (a) 我在超市买了苹果。 (b) 老师说明天考试。 (c) 他在写信。
::: solution
Models: (a) *I bought some apples at the supermarket.* — past tense; plural and indefinite (*some apples*); definite *the supermarket* (a familiar one). (b) *The teacher said there's a test tomorrow* / *The teacher says we've got an exam tomorrow.* — tense of *say* (past or present: context); *the teacher* (definite); *a test* (indefinite); dummy *there* or *we've got*. (c) *He's writing a letter* / *He was writing letters* — tense, aspect is given by 在 (progressive), number and definiteness of *letter*.
:::
:::

::: exercise Topic or subject? {level=1}
Underline (or name) the topic and the grammatical subject, if there is one, in each Chinese sentence. (a) 这家餐厅我很喜欢。 (b) 水果我只吃苹果。 (c) 明天下雨。
::: solution
(a) Topic 这家餐厅; subject 我. (b) Topic 水果; subject 我 (and 苹果 is the object, a member of the topic's set). (c) Topic 明天 (a time); no subject — English supplies *it*: *It's going to rain tomorrow.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Add the connectives {level=2}
Translate into English, deciding the relation between the clauses and expressing it. (a) 路太远，我们打车吧。 (b) 他嘴上不说，心里很着急。 (c) 早知道这样，我就不来了。 (d) 天一亮，他们就出发了。 (e) 东西放这儿，丢了我可不管。
::: solution
(a) Cause → suggestion: *It's too far to walk — let's get a taxi.* (b) Concession: *He didn't say anything, but he was very worried.* / *Although he said nothing, he was very anxious inside.* (c) Unreal past condition: *If I'd known it would be like this, I wouldn't have come.* (d) Time: *They set off as soon as it was light.* / *They set off at first light.* (e) Condition: *You can leave your things here, but if they go missing, it's not my responsibility.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Personal subjects {level=2}
Translate into natural Chinese, with a personal subject or a chain of clauses where it fits. (a) *Curiosity got the better of me.* (b) *The thought of the exam kept her awake all night.* (c) *A short walk brought us to the lake.* (d) *His speech won him many supporters.*
::: solution
(a) 我实在忍不住好奇心。/ 我终究还是没忍住好奇。 (b) 她一想到考试，整夜都睡不着。 (c) 我们走了没多远，就到了湖边。 (d) 他的演讲为他赢得了很多支持者。 — here an impersonal subject is normal in Chinese too; or 他这次演讲后，支持他的人多了很多.
:::
:::

::: exercise Reduce the 翻译腔 {level=2}
Rewrite each Chinese translation as natural Chinese, and say which contrast caused the problem. (a) 当他回到家的时候，他发现他的钥匙不见了。 (b) 一个对于这个城市的历史的全面的了解是必要的。 (c) 那些学生们把他们的书放在了他们的桌子上。
::: solution
(a) 他回到家，发现钥匙不见了。 — hypotaxis (当……的时候) and repeated pronouns (他, 他的). (b) 必须全面了解这座城市的历史。 — static style: the noun phrase *a thorough understanding of* becomes a verb (了解) with an adverb (全面); the chain of 的 disappears. (c) 学生们把书放在桌子上。 — every article, plural and possessive translated; Chinese leaves them to context.
:::
:::

::: exercise Analyse a sentence pair {level=3}
Analyse every difference between this Chinese sentence and its English translation, naming the contrast behind each: 这次比赛我们输了，主要是准备不够，下次一定要吸取教训。 — *We lost this match mainly because we hadn't prepared enough, and next time we must learn from our mistakes.*
::: hint
Look for: the topic, the subject, the connectives, the tense and aspect forms, the possessives, and the noun 教训.
:::
::: solution
1. **Topic and subject**: 这次比赛 is the topic; English makes 我们 the subject and the match the object (*We lost this match*).
2. **Parataxis → hypotaxis**: 主要是准备不够 has no connective; English adds *because*, and *mainly* attaches to it. The third clause gets *and*.
3. **Subject supplied**: 准备不够 and 下次一定要吸取教训 have no subject; English supplies *we* twice.
4. **Tense and aspect**: 输了 → past *lost*; the earlier lack of preparation → past perfect *hadn't prepared*; 一定要 → *must*.
5. **Noun to clause**: 准备 (verb or noun) becomes a verb with *enough*.
6. **Idiom**: 吸取教训 ‘absorb the lesson’ → *learn from our mistakes* (or *learn our lesson*); English adds the possessive *our*.
:::
:::

::: exercise Translate a short paragraph {level=3}
Translate into English, and write a short note on two decisions you made. 我们小区门口新开了一家面包店。老板是个年轻人，以前在上海学过手艺。店不大，面包种类倒是不少，价格也公道，每天早上门口都排着队。
::: solution
A model: *A new bakery has opened at the entrance to our housing estate. The owner is a young man who learned the trade in Shanghai. The shop is small, but it has a good range of bread at fair prices, and every morning there is a queue outside.*

Possible notes: (1) *The owner is a young man who learned the trade in Shanghai* — two Chinese sentences joined with a relative clause (hypotaxis), and 以前……学过 rendered by a simple past (*learned*), since the time is clear. (2) 店不大，面包种类倒是不少 — the contrast marked by 倒是 becomes *but*; the topic 面包种类 is turned into *it has a good range of bread*, with *the shop* as subject. (3) 排着队 — the Chinese has no subject; English uses existential *there is a queue*. (*Housing estate* is BrE; AmE might say *apartment complex*.)
:::
