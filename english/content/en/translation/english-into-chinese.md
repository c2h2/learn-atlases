Here is a sentence of the kind found in any English report, with two Chinese translations:

> *The council's decision to close the library, which had served the town for over a century, provoked strong protests from residents who depended on it for access to books and the internet.*
>
> (a) 市议会关闭这座已为该镇服务了一个多世纪的图书馆的决定激起了依靠它获取书籍和上网的居民们的强烈抗议。
>
> (b) 这座图书馆为小镇服务了一百多年，市议会却决定把它关掉。许多居民要靠它借书、上网，纷纷提出强烈抗议。

Translation (a) is accurate: every word of the English is there, in an order Chinese grammar allows. It is also very hard to read. Its subject, 决定, comes only after a 的-phrase of twenty-five characters; its object, 抗议, after another of twenty; the reader has to hold the beginning in mind until the very end to find out what the sentence says. Translation (b) says the same things in three short clauses and one sentence more: the history of the library first, then the decision, then the residents and their protest, in the order in which the events happened. Nouns have become verbs (*decision* → 决定把它关掉, *access to* → 借书、上网), the relative clauses have become main clauses, and the long noun phrases have disappeared. This is what Chinese readers recognise as 地道 — natural, idiomatic Chinese.

Translation (a) shows the typical problem of English → Chinese translation, known as **翻译腔**: Chinese that follows English structure. This lesson gives a method for avoiding it: read the long English sentence for its structure and meaning, then rebuild it in Chinese using the four classic procedures for long sentences — keeping the order, reversing it, dividing it, and recasting it — and turning English nouns into Chinese verbs.

## Reading the long sentence first

Most long English sentences are long because of **subordination** and **nominalisation**: one main clause, with relative clauses, participle phrases, prepositional phrases and nouns of action packed around it ([[translation/english-chinese-compared#rule-hypotaxis]]). Before translating, you need to see this structure.

::: theorem Find the trunk, then the branches {#rule-trunk}
1. **Find the main clause** (主干): its subject, its finite verb and its object or complement. Everything else depends on it.
2. **Mark each dependent unit**: relative clauses, adverbial clauses, participle and infinitive phrases, appositives, long prepositional phrases. Note what each one attaches to.
3. **State the relations** between the units in plain words: *time*, *cause*, *condition*, *concession*, *purpose*, *description of X*.
4. **Paraphrase the sentence** to yourself as a sequence of simple statements, in the order of events or of logic.

Only then decide the order and the sentence divisions of the Chinese.
:::

::: proof
The procedure separates **understanding** from **writing**, which the translator of a long sentence otherwise tries to do at once, word by word, and so ends up following the English order. Step 4 is a form of intralingual translation ([[translation/what-translation-is#def-translation]]): restating the meaning in a new structure is the core of translation, and the same skill as paraphrase in academic reading ([[academic-reading/paraphrase-and-quotation]]). The steps also catch misreadings: many errors in English → Chinese translation are not errors of Chinese but of attachment — a relative clause attached to the wrong noun, a participle given the wrong subject ([[reading-skills/long-sentences]], [[academic-reading/dense-sentences]]).
:::

For the library sentence, the analysis is:

- **Trunk**: *the council's decision … provoked strong protests*.
- **Branches**: *to close the library* (content of *decision*); *which had served the town for over a century* (non-defining, about *the library*); *from residents* (source of *protests*); *who depended on it for access to books and the internet* (defining, about *residents*).
- **Paraphrase in order of events**: the library had served the town for over a century; the council decided to close it; residents depended on it for books and the internet; they protested strongly.

Translation (b) follows the paraphrase, not the English.

## Four procedures for long sentences

::: definition Keeping, reversing, dividing and recasting {#def-four-methods}
Chinese translation textbooks, following Zhang Peiji's 《英汉翻译教程》 (1980) and others, describe four procedures for long sentences:

- **顺译** (keeping the order): the English order already matches the natural Chinese order of time and logic; translate in sequence, dividing into clauses where needed.
- **逆译** (reversing the order): the English puts the main point first and background, causes or conditions after it; Chinese puts them before it, so the translation starts from the end.
- **分译** (dividing): a unit of the English sentence — usually a relative clause, a participle phrase, an appositive or a long nominal phrase — is taken out and translated as a separate clause or sentence.
- **综合** (recasting): the sentence is reorganised as a whole, combining the other procedures, in the order of time or logic.
:::

### Keeping the order: 顺译

When the English happens to follow the order of events or of reasoning, the translator keeps it, but still divides it into Chinese clauses.

> *She graduated in 2015, worked for a bank in Hong Kong for three years, and then returned to Chengdu to start her own company.*
>
> 她2015年毕业，在香港一家银行工作了三年，后来回到成都，自己开了公司。

The English has one subject and three coordinated verb phrases; the Chinese has the same order with four verb phrases, because *returned … to start* becomes two actions in sequence (回到成都，自己开了公司). The time adverbial *in 2015* moves before the verb, as Chinese requires.

### Reversing: 逆译

::: theorem Background before the main point {#rule-reverse}
When an English sentence puts the main clause first and its **time**, **cause**, **condition**, **concession** or **purpose** after it, the Chinese translation usually reverses the order:

- *I'll phone you when I get to the station.* → 我到了车站就给你打电话。
- *The match was cancelled because of the storm.* → 由于暴风雨，比赛取消了。 / 下暴风雨，比赛取消了。
- *You can't borrow books unless you have a library card.* → 有借书证才能借书。
- *It is clear that the plan will need more money.* → 这个计划显然需要更多资金。 (an extraposed *it*-clause brought forward)

English also often ends a sentence with a long subject or object moved to the end for weight; Chinese tends to state the background first and the conclusion or evaluation last.
:::

::: proof
The rule follows from the principle of temporal sequence in Chinese ([[translation/english-chinese-compared#rule-order]]) and from the Chinese tendency to put background and conditions before the main assertion — the order in which Chinese subordinate clauses normally appear (因为……所以, 如果……就, 虽然……但是). English allows both orders, and the choice depends on information structure: what the reader already knows comes first, new information last. Reversal is therefore a tendency, not a rule: when an English *because*-clause is the new and important information (*He left the company because he wanted to start his own business*), a Chinese translator may keep it at the end with 是因为: 他离开公司，是因为想自己创业.
:::

::: example Reversing a sentence {#ex-reverse}
Translate into Chinese: *Many young people find it difficult to buy a home in big cities, where prices have risen far faster than incomes over the past decade.*
::: solution
1. **Trunk**: *many young people find it difficult to buy a home* (with extraposed *it*).
2. **Branch**: *where prices have risen far faster than incomes over the past decade* — a non-defining relative clause about *big cities*; logically it is the **cause** of the difficulty.
3. **Order of logic**: cause (prices rose faster than incomes) → result (young people can't buy a home).
4. **Chinese**: 近十年来，大城市房价的涨幅远远超过收入的增长，许多年轻人很难在那里买房。 / 过去十年，大城市的房价比收入涨得快得多，很多年轻人买不起房子。

The relative clause has become the first clause; *where* disappears, and *in big cities* is said once, in the clause where it is needed; the time adverbial *over the past decade* moves to the front. The second version is plainer and turns the nouns *prices* and *incomes* into the subjects of a comparison of verbs (涨得快).
:::
:::

### Dividing: 分译

The most important single technique is the treatment of **relative clauses**. A short defining relative clause usually becomes a 的-phrase before the noun, as the grammar of the two languages suggests ([[complex-sentences/relative-clauses]]). A long one, or a non-defining one, usually cannot.

::: theorem Relative clauses into Chinese {#rule-relative}
1. A **short defining** relative clause → a **的-phrase** before the noun: *the book I bought yesterday* → 我昨天买的书.
2. A **long defining** relative clause → a 的-phrase only if the result stays readable (as a guide, one 的 per noun phrase and not much more than ten characters before the noun); otherwise a **separate clause**, often introduced by a repeated noun or by 有, 这些, 其中: *There are many students who want to study abroad but cannot afford it* → 很多学生想出国留学，但负担不起.
3. A **non-defining** relative clause → usually a **separate clause** or sentence, before or after the main clause by the order of logic: *My grandfather, who was born in 1930, still rides his bicycle every day* → 我爷爷1930年出生，现在还每天骑自行车.
4. A **sentential *which*** (referring to a whole clause) → a new clause with 这, 这样, 因此 or no connective: *He missed the bus, which made him late for the exam* → 他没赶上公共汽车，考试迟到了.
5. A relative clause that is logically **adverbial** (cause, purpose, concession, condition) → an adverbial clause in Chinese: *The doctor, who had worked for twenty hours, could hardly stand* → 医生已经连续工作了二十个小时，几乎站不住了 (cause).
:::

::: proof
A Chinese 的-phrase must come before the noun, so a long relative clause pushes the head noun far to the right, and the reader does not know what is being described until the description is over — exactly the difficulty of translation (a) at the start of this lesson. Chinese prose style tolerates long pre-noun modifiers less than English tolerates long post-noun ones; Chinese writers on style have long criticised piled-up 的 (的的不休) as a symptom of Europeanised Chinese. Separating the clause also recovers its logical relation, which the English relative pronoun hides: a non-defining clause often gives a cause, a background fact or a consequence, and Chinese expresses that relation by the order of clauses and by words such as 却, 因此, 结果. Point 2's guideline is a rule of thumb, not a measured limit; the test is whether a reader can take in the noun phrase in one reading.
:::

::: example 的 or a new clause? {#ex-relative}
Translate the relative clauses in the most natural way, and say which part of [[#rule-relative]] you used. (a) *Children who read a lot usually write well.* (b) *We stayed in a small hotel, which was run by a retired teacher and his wife.* (c) *Visitors who wish to take photographs inside the museum must obtain permission from the front desk, which is open from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m.* (d) *She failed her driving test three times, which surprised everyone.*
::: solution
(a) Point 1 — short defining: 读书多的孩子通常文章写得好。 (Note also 写得好 for *write well*, a verb–complement structure.)

(b) Point 3 — non-defining: 我们住在一家小旅馆里，旅馆是一位退休老师和他太太开的。 / 我们住的是一家小旅馆，由一位退休教师和他妻子经营。 The repeated noun 旅馆 replaces *which*.

(c) Point 2 for the first clause, point 3 for the second, with reversal of the condition: 游客如需在馆内拍照，须到前台申请许可。前台开放时间为上午9点至下午5点。 The defining clause *who wish to …* becomes a condition (如需……); the non-defining clause becomes a separate sentence, as on a Chinese notice.

(d) Point 4 — sentential *which*: 她驾照考了三次都没过，大家都很意外。 The English passive-like evaluation becomes a clause with 大家 as subject (personal subject, [[translation/english-chinese-compared]]).
:::
:::

::: warning The 的-chain
✗ 那个坐在我旁边的穿着一件红色毛衣的正在看一本关于中国历史的书的女孩是我的同学。 (for *The girl sitting next to me, in a red sweater, reading a book about Chinese history, is my classmate.*)

✓ 坐在我旁边的那个女孩是我同学。她穿着红毛衣，正在看一本讲中国历史的书。

Keep at most one or two 的 before a noun; move the rest of the description into its own clause.
:::

### Recasting: 综合

Many sentences need all three procedures at once. The translator then recasts the whole sentence in the order of time or logic, using the paraphrase of [[#rule-trunk]] as the plan.

::: example Recasting an academic sentence {#ex-recast}
Translate into Chinese: *Although the results of the survey, which was carried out in three provinces, must be treated with caution because of the small size of the sample, they suggest that the use of mobile payment among elderly people has increased considerably.*
::: solution
1. **Trunk**: *they suggest that the use of mobile payment … has increased considerably*.
2. **Branches**: *although the results … must be treated with caution* (concession), with *which was carried out in three provinces* (non-defining, about *the survey*) and *because of the small size of the sample* (cause, inside the concession).
3. **Order of logic**: the survey was carried out in three provinces → the sample was small → so the results must be treated with caution → but they suggest that elderly people use mobile payment much more.
4. **Chinese**: 这项调查在三个省份进行，样本量较小，因此结果需要谨慎看待；但调查结果表明，老年人使用移动支付的情况明显增多。

Notes: the non-defining clause becomes the first clause; the noun phrase *the small size of the sample* becomes a short clause with an adjective (样本量较小); *must be treated with caution* (passive) becomes an active verb with the results as topic (结果需要谨慎看待); *the use of mobile payment among elderly people* becomes a clause with the old people as subject (老年人使用移动支付). The concession *although* becomes 但 in the second half. The academic register is kept with 表明, 明显, 较.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which translation of *The hotel, which overlooks the lake, has forty rooms* is the most natural Chinese?
- [ ] 那个俯瞰着湖的酒店有四十个房间。
- [ ] 酒店，它俯瞰着湖，有四十个房间。
- [x] 酒店临湖而建，共有四十间客房。
- [ ] 这家酒店有四十个房间，它俯瞰湖泊。
::: solution
The non-defining clause becomes a clause of its own (临湖而建), and the main clause follows with a natural Chinese collocation (共有……间客房). The first version turns a non-defining clause into a defining 的-phrase (which changes the meaning: as if there were several hotels); the second copies the English punctuation and pronoun; the fourth keeps the English order and an unnecessary 它.
:::
:::

## From nouns to verbs

::: theorem Unpack nominalisations {#rule-unpack}
English nouns of action, state and quality — *arrival*, *refusal*, *existence*, *availability*, *importance* — and nouns that imply a verb (*a good cook*, *a heavy smoker*, *an early riser*) are usually best translated by a **Chinese verb or clause**:

- *the arrival of the train* → 火车到站 (in a clause: 火车到站后……)
- *His refusal to help us was disappointing.* → 他不肯帮我们，真让人失望。
- *the availability of cheap labour* → 有廉价劳动力可用
- *She is a good cook.* → 她很会做饭。
- *In the absence of evidence …* → 由于没有证据……

English prepositions that imply actions are often verbs in Chinese too: *a man with a gun* → 一个拿着枪的人; *tea without sugar* → 不加糖的茶; *the road to the station* → 去车站的路.
:::

::: proof
The rule applies the static–dynamic contrast of [[translation/english-chinese-compared#rule-static]]. An English nominalisation packs a whole clause — who did what — into a noun, and leaves the doer implicit or in an *of*-phrase or possessive. Chinese can nominalise too (他的到来, 拒绝帮助), but a sentence of several such nouns joined by 的 and 对 is heavy and often ambiguous. Unpacking means asking *who does what?* and writing a verb with its subject — the same move that English style manuals recommend for clear English ([[style-and-rhetoric/clarity]], [[academic-reading/dense-sentences#rule-unpack]]). Where the Chinese genre is itself nominal (laws, standards, some academic writing), a noun may be right: *the protection of personal information* in a law is 个人信息保护, not 保护个人的信息.
:::

::: example Unpacking a dense sentence {#ex-unpack}
Translate into Chinese: *The introduction of a four-day working week led to a significant improvement in staff satisfaction and a reduction in sick leave.*
::: solution
1. **Who does what?** Someone (the company) introduced a four-day week; staff became more satisfied; staff took less sick leave.
2. **Nouns → verbs**: *introduction* → 实行; *improvement in satisfaction* → 满意度提高 (or 员工更满意了); *reduction in sick leave* → 请病假的人少了.
3. **Order of events**: introduction → results.
4. **Chinese**: 实行每周四天工作制以后，员工满意度明显提高，请病假的人也少了。 / A more formal version for a report: 推行四天工作制后，员工满意度显著提升，病假率有所下降。

Compare the nominal ? 四天工作周的引入导致了员工满意度的显著提高和病假的减少 — understandable, but 翻译腔: two 的-phrases, 引入 and 导致了 copying *introduction* and *led to*.
:::
:::

## Connectives, pronouns and other words to leave out

::: theorem Leave out what Chinese does not need {#rule-omit}
In English → Chinese translation, **omission** (减译) is regular for words that English grammar requires and Chinese does not:

1. **Articles** and many **possessives**: *He put his hands in his pockets* → 他把手插进口袋里.
2. **Pronouns** that repeat a subject already established: *She opened the door and she saw …* → 她打开门，看见……
3. **Connectives** whose relation is clear from order: *When I got home, I made dinner* → 我回到家就做饭; reserve 当……的时候 for cases where the time relation needs emphasis.
4. **Dummy subjects** *it* and *there*: *It is raining* → 下雨了; *There is a cat on the roof* → 屋顶上有只猫.
5. **Prepositions** that only mark a relation already clear: *In the morning I go running* → 我早上去跑步.

Conversely, **amplification** (增译) supplies what Chinese needs and English leaves implicit: measure words (*three books* → 三本书), words that make an abstract noun concrete (*tension* → 紧张局势, *preparation* → 准备工作), and words that mark tense or aspect when the time is not otherwise clear (已经, 将, 正在).
:::

::: proof
English and Chinese differ in what their grammars make obligatory ([[translation/english-chinese-compared#rule-categories]]). Translating every obligatory English word gives Chinese that is correct and unnatural: 当他回到家的时候，他发现他的钥匙不见了 has five words that a Chinese writer would not use. Zhang Peiji's textbook treats omission and amplification as complementary techniques: nothing of the *meaning* is omitted or added, only words that the target language uses differently. The test of an omission is that the meaning is still recoverable; the test of an amplification is that it adds no meaning the source does not contain.
:::

::: quiz
Which word is it usually best to **leave out** in Chinese when translating *When the meeting ended, we all went to lunch together*?
- [x] *When* — the order of the clauses already shows the time relation: 会开完了，我们一起去吃午饭。
- [ ] *we* — Chinese never needs a subject.
- [ ] *together* — it is redundant in English.
- [ ] *ended* — Chinese has no tense.
::: solution
The time relation is clear from the order of the clauses and from 完了, so 当会议结束的时候 is unnecessary. The subject 我们 is needed here because the first clause has a different subject (the meeting), and 一起 carries meaning; *ended* is rendered by 完了 (a resultative complement plus 了).
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **翻译腔 is not only a beginner's problem.** Much published translation into Chinese shows it, and so does Chinese writing influenced by translation. The writers 思果 (in 《翻译研究》, 1972) and Yu Kwang-chung (余光中, in essays from the 1970s on) collected its symptoms: chains of 的, overuse of 被, 作为, 关于, 进行, 当……的时候 and 一个, abstract nouns as subjects. Checking a draft for these words is a quick way to find unnatural sentences.
- **Do not overcorrect.** Natural Chinese uses 的 and 被 and long sentences too. Academic and legal Chinese tolerate longer modifiers than narrative; a translation of a law that breaks every sentence into short clauses may lose precision ([[translation/specialised-translation]]).
- **Punctuation.** Chinese uses the comma to separate clauses within a sentence and allows long chains of them; when dividing an English sentence, you can often keep it as one Chinese sentence with several commas, or a semicolon, rather than several full stops.
:::

::: history
The debate about how close Chinese translation should stay to European structure is almost a century old. In 1929 Liang Shiqiu (梁实秋) criticised Lu Xun's (鲁迅) "rigid translation" (硬译) as unreadable; Lu Xun replied in 1930, defending close translation as a way of bringing new ways of thinking and new forms of expression into Chinese. The European structures that translation introduced — longer modifiers, more 被, explicit connectives — were described by Wang Li in the 1940s as 欧化语法, and some became a normal part of written Chinese. From the 1970s, 思果 and Yu Kwang-chung argued that translators had gone too far, and their lists of translationese symptoms became standard teaching material. Zhang Peiji's 《英汉翻译教程》 (1980) systematised the techniques of this lesson for university courses, and the 1990s contrastive work of Lian Shuneng supplied their linguistic basis.
:::

- Before translating a long English sentence, **find the trunk and the branches** and paraphrase the meaning as a sequence of simple statements ([[#rule-trunk]]).
- Use the four procedures — **顺译** (keep the order), **逆译** (reverse it), **分译** (divide it) and **综合** (recast it) — choosing the order of time and logic ([[#def-four-methods]]).

::: exercise Nouns into verbs {level=2}
Translate into natural Chinese, unpacking the nouns in bold. (a) *The **failure** of the talks led to the **resumption** of fighting.* (b) *He is a **fast learner**.* (c) ***Prolonged exposure** to loud noise can cause **hearing loss**.*
::: solution
(a) 谈判失败了，双方又打了起来。 / 和谈破裂，战事再起。 (b) 他学东西很快。 (c) 长时间接触噪音会损伤听力。 / 长期处在噪声环境中，听力可能受损。 — *hearing loss* in a medical text can stay a noun: 会导致听力损失.
:::

::: exercise Correct the translationese {level=2}
Each Chinese translation is accurate but unnatural. Rewrite it. (a) 当我们到达山顶的时候，我们看到了一个美丽的日出。 (b) 他对于这个问题进行了一个深入的研究。 (c) 这个由政府资助的、旨在帮助农村儿童的项目已经被证明是成功的。
::: solution
(a) 我们爬到山顶，看到了美丽的日出。 — drop 当……的时候, the second 我们 and 一个. (b) 他深入研究了这个问题。 — 对于……进行了一个……研究 becomes a verb with an adverb. (c) 这个项目由政府资助，旨在帮助农村儿童，事实证明很成功。 — the long pre-noun modifier is divided into clauses; the passive 被证明是 becomes 事实证明.
:::

::: exercise Recast a sentence {level=3}
Translate into Chinese for a general newspaper: *Despite warnings from scientists that the river's fish population had fallen to a critical level, fishing continued unrestricted until last year, when the provincial government finally introduced a ten-year ban.*
::: hint
Find the order of events: the fish declined, scientists warned, fishing continued, the government banned it last year. Then decide where the concession (*despite*) goes.
:::
::: solution
A model: 科学家早就发出警告，这条河的鱼类数量已经降到了危险水平，但捕捞活动一直没有受到任何限制。直到去年，省政府才终于出台了为期十年的禁渔令。

Notes: the concession *despite warnings* becomes a full clause first, with 但 introducing the contrast (逆译 + 分译); *a critical level* → 危险水平 (a newspaper collocation); *unrestricted* (an adjective) → 没有受到任何限制; the non-defining *when*-clause becomes a new sentence with 直到……才, which also carries *finally*.
:::

::: exercise Translate a paragraph {level=3}
Translate into Chinese, and list three techniques from this lesson that you used. *Founded in 1902 by a group of local merchants, the school, which now has more than two thousand students, is known for its strong science programme. Its graduates include several well-known engineers, one of whom designed the bridge that links the old town with the new business district.*
::: solution
A model: 这所学校由当地一批商人于1902年创办，现有学生两千多人，以理科见长。学校培养出了好几位知名工程师，其中一位设计了连接老城区和新商务区的那座大桥。

Techniques: (1) the participle phrase *Founded in 1902 …* is kept first, as a passive with 由 (a natural formal pattern in Chinese for founding), and the non-defining clause becomes a coordinated clause (现有学生两千多人) — 顺译 with division; (2) *is known for its strong science programme* → 以理科见长, a formal four-character expression; (3) *one of whom* → 其中一位, a new clause; (4) *Its graduates include …* is recast with the school as subject (学校培养出了……), a personal–active structure; (5) the short defining clause *that links …* becomes a 的-phrase.
