A delegation of engineers from Ghana is visiting a factory in Suzhou. The production manager, standing by a machine, says:

> 这条生产线是去年引进的，自动化程度比较高，原来要二十个工人，现在三个人就够了，效率提高了差不多五倍，当然，前期投入也不小。

She pauses and looks at the interpreter. The interpreter has heard the sentence once; there is no text to look back at, no dictionary, and twelve people waiting. Within a few seconds she says:

> *This production line was installed last year. It's highly automated: it used to need twenty workers, and now three are enough, so efficiency is up almost fivefold. Of course, the initial investment was considerable.*

Everything that the previous lessons of this course describe has happened in those seconds: the 流水句 has been divided into four English sentences; the topic 这条生产线 has become a subject with a passive (*was installed*); the relations between the clauses — result (*so*), concession (*Of course*) — have been made explicit; tense has been supplied; 不小 has become *considerable* (a positive word for a negative form). And the interpreter has done it in real time, while also listening to whether the manager is about to continue.

**Interpreting** — oral translation — shares its principles with written translation but works under different conditions: the source is heard once, the target is produced at once, and the interpreter cannot revise. This lesson describes the main modes of interpreting, the mental effort they demand, the note-taking that makes consecutive interpreting possible, the strategies of simultaneous interpreting between English and Chinese, and the role of the interpreter.

## Modes of interpreting

::: definition Modes of interpreting {#def-modes}
- **Consecutive interpreting** (交替传译, often 交传): the speaker talks for a segment — a sentence or several minutes — and stops; the interpreter then renders the segment, usually with the help of notes.
- **Simultaneous interpreting** (同声传译, 同传): the interpreter listens and speaks at the same time, usually in a booth with headphones and a microphone, a few seconds behind the speaker.
- **Whispered interpreting** (*chuchotage*, 耳语传译): simultaneous interpreting whispered to one or two listeners, without equipment.
- **Liaison** or **dialogue interpreting**: consecutive interpreting of short turns in a conversation — business meetings, visits, medical and police interviews, community services.
- **Sight translation** (视译): the oral translation of a written text, read on the spot.
- **Remote interpreting**: any of these over a video or audio link, with the interpreter in a different place from the speakers.
:::

The mode is chosen by the situation. Conferences with many languages use simultaneous interpreting, because consecutive would double the length of every speech. Negotiations, press conferences, banquets and visits often use consecutive, which needs no equipment and lets both parties hear each other. Courts and hospitals use consecutive or whispered interpreting for individuals.

## What interpreting demands

::: theorem Interpret meaning, not words {#rule-sense}
An interpreter should **listen for the meaning** of each segment — what the speaker wants to say, and why — and **express that meaning** in natural target-language speech, rather than converting word by word. Words that must be kept exactly — **numbers, names, titles, technical terms, quotations** — are the exception, and are noted or memorised as such.
:::

::: proof
The French interpreter and scholar Danica Seleskovitch, with Marianne Lederer, developed this idea as the **interpretive theory** (*théorie du sens*) from the late 1960s (Seleskovitch, *L'interprète dans les conférences internationales*, 1968): experienced interpreters understand a segment, let go of its words — a stage they called **deverbalisation** — and re-express the sense. There are good reasons for it. Short-term memory cannot hold more than a few seconds of exact wording, but it can hold a meaning; and between English and Chinese, the structures differ so much that word-by-word conversion would produce unacceptable speech ([[translation/english-chinese-compared]]). Numbers and names cannot be deverbalised — they have no "meaning" to fall back on — which is why they are among the commonest errors in interpreting and why interpreters write them down at once.
:::

The interpreter and researcher Daniel Gile described the mental work of interpreting with his **Effort Models** (*Basic Concepts and Models for Interpreter and Translator Training*, 1995; revised edition 2009). In simultaneous interpreting, he proposed, the interpreter divides a limited processing capacity among a **listening and analysis** effort, a **short-term memory** effort, a **speech production** effort and a **coordination** effort that manages the other three. In consecutive interpreting, the first phase (listening) combines listening and analysis, **note-taking**, memory and coordination; the second phase (speaking) combines **remembering**, **reading the notes** and production.

::: widget graph
title: Gile's Effort Model of simultaneous interpreting

caption: After Gile (1995; 2009). All four efforts draw on one limited capacity. When one effort needs more — a dense passage of numbers, an unfamiliar accent, a long Chinese modifier before its noun — less is left for the others, and errors appear elsewhere, often a few seconds later. Gile's **tightrope hypothesis** holds that interpreters work close to saturation much of the time, which is why apparently easy segments can fail. The model is a framework for explaining difficulties, not a measured quantity. Drag the nodes to arrange the efforts differently.
:::

According to Gile's Effort Models, why might an interpreter render a simple sentence wrongly just after a list of figures?

The capacity is shared: noting and producing figures takes extra effort, and the effort borrowed from listening shows up as an error in the following segment. This is why interpreters prepare numbers in advance where they can, and why speakers are asked to read figures slowly.

## Consecutive interpreting and note-taking

Consecutive interpreters do not take down the speech; they note what they need to **reconstruct the meaning** from memory. Jean-François Rozan's *La prise de notes en interprétation consécutive* (1956) set out seven principles that are still the basis of most teaching.

::: theorem Rozan's principles of note-taking {#rule-rozan}
1. **Note the idea, not the word**: one sign for a meaning, whatever words the speaker used.
2. **Abbreviate** long words by a fixed rule (often the first and last letters: *pdt* for *president*, 经 for 经济).
3. **Note the links** between ideas — cause, contrast, result, condition — with clear signs (*bc* or ∵ for *because*, *but* or a line, → or ∴ for *therefore*): they are what memory most often loses.
4. **Mark negation** clearly (a stroke through a sign).
5. **Mark emphasis** (underlining for *very*, *important*; double underlining for *extremely*).
6. **Write vertically**: one idea per line, from top to bottom, so that the structure is visible at a glance.
7. **Use a shift (*décalage*)**: indent each new element (subject, verb, object) further to the right, and place parallel elements under each other.

Add to these a small, personal set of **symbols** — for *country*, *meeting*, *increase*, *problem*, *agree* — learned so well that writing them takes no thought.
:::

::: proof
Rozan's principles follow from the interpretive theory: if the interpreter keeps the meaning in memory, the notes need only to trigger it and to preserve what memory cannot — the structure of the argument, the links, the numbers and names. Word-for-word notes are too slow (people speak at well over a hundred words a minute, and no one writes that fast) and lead to word-for-word rendering. The vertical layout reflects the hierarchy of the speech, so that the interpreter, glancing down, sees the main clause and its dependents — exactly the hierarchy that English needs and Chinese leaves implicit ([[translation/chinese-into-english#rule-four-steps]]). Interpreters disagree about which language to note in: some note in the target language, which starts the conversion early; many use a mixture of symbols, abbreviations and whichever language is quicker for each word — a Chinese character is often faster to write than an English word, and an English abbreviation faster than a complex character.
:::

::: example Notes for a short passage {#ex-notes}
Show the notes an interpreter might take for the factory manager's sentence at the start of this lesson, and how they are turned into English.
::: solution
1. **Notes** (one possible version, written vertically, with indentation shown by spaces):

```
line    in ↑ last y
  auto  ++
was 20 ppl
  now 3 ok
→ eff ↑ ~×5
but $ in  big
```

2. **Reading**: *line* (这条生产线); *in ↑ last y* (引进, last year); *auto ++* (high automation); *was 20 ppl / now 3 ok* (contrast, aligned under each other); *→* (result); *eff ↑ ~×5* (efficiency up about five times); *but* (concession); *$ in big* (investment large).
3. **Rendering**: the links (→ and *but*) tell the interpreter where to put *so* and *Of course*; the vertical contrast *20 / 3* produces the *used to … now* structure. The interpreter speaks from the meaning: *This production line was installed last year. It's highly automated …*

Notice what is not noted: 比较, 差不多 (approximately — kept as ~), 当然 (implied by *but*), 前期 (understood). Memory supplies them.
:::
:::

::: warning Writing too much
✗ Trying to write every word: the interpreter falls behind, misses the next sentence, and reads the notes aloud as a word-for-word translation.

✓ Listening first, writing only the structure, the links, numbers, names and key terms — and looking at the speaker, not only at the pad.
:::

## Numbers

Numbers are the most error-prone element in English–Chinese interpreting, because the two languages group digits differently: English in thousands (*thousand*, *million*, *billion*), Chinese in ten-thousands (万, 亿) ([[core-vocabulary/numbers-dates-money]]).

::: theorem Interpreting large numbers {#rule-numbers}
1. **Write numbers down in digits** as soon as you hear them, with the unit.
2. **Convert by the groups**: 万 = 10 thousand; 十万 = 100 thousand; 百万 = 1 million; 千万 = 10 million; 亿 = 100 million; 十亿 = 1 billion. A quick method is to note the number with a separator for the source system (3|5000万 → 3.5 亿) and convert in writing, not in the head.
3. **Keep approximations approximate**: 近三千万 → *nearly thirty million*; *roughly 1.5 billion* → 约十五亿.
4. **Check the plausibility** of the result: does the city really have 25 billion people?
:::

::: proof
Converting between the two systems requires the interpreter to move the decimal point while keeping the number in memory and continuing to listen — a heavy demand on capacity in Gile's terms. Writing the digits frees memory, and converting on paper is less error-prone than converting mentally. Plausibility checks catch the most common error, a factor of ten (*one hundred million* for 十亿). Interpreters often prepare the key figures of a speech in advance from the speaker's documents.
:::

::: example Converting figures {#ex-numbers}
Interpret into English: 去年全市接待游客一亿两千万人次，旅游收入达一千八百亿元，比前年增长百分之十二点五。
::: solution
1. **Note**: 1.2 亿 人次; 1800 亿 元; +12.5%.
2. **Convert**: 1.2亿 = 120 million; 1800亿 = 180 billion.
3. **Render**: *Last year the city received 120 million tourist visits, and tourism revenue reached 180 billion yuan, up 12.5 per cent on the year before.*

人次 means visits counted per person per trip (one person visiting twice counts twice), so *tourist visits* is more accurate than *tourists*. *Up 12.5 per cent on the year before* is the natural English collocation for 比前年增长.
:::
:::

## Simultaneous interpreting between English and Chinese

In simultaneous interpreting, the interpreter cannot wait for the end of a sentence; she begins speaking when she has enough to start, typically a few seconds behind the speaker (the **ear–voice span**, or *décalage*). Between English and Chinese this creates a special difficulty: the two languages place modifiers, clauses and the main point in different orders ([[translation/english-chinese-compared#rule-order]]).

::: theorem Working in order: segmentation and anticipation {#rule-linearity}
1. **Segment** the source into short units of meaning (sometimes called the "salami technique") and render each unit as it comes, joining them with connectives or short repetitions: this is often called the **principle of syntactic linearity** (顺句驱动 in Chinese interpreting courses).
2. **Restructure only within a segment**, not across the whole sentence.
3. **Use reformulations that allow you to start early**: a long English post-modifier can become a new Chinese clause rather than a pre-noun 的-phrase; a long Chinese pre-noun modifier can be rendered as an English sentence that names the head noun later.
4. **Wait briefly** when a segment cannot start without the next word (a Chinese verb at the end of a long modifier, an English *not* at the end of a clause), but not so long that memory overflows.
5. **Anticipate** from context, speech conventions and the speaker's slides — but be ready to correct.
:::

::: proof
If a Chinese interpreter waited for the end of *The proposal that the committee discussed at its meeting last Tuesday in Geneva after a long debate about funding …* before saying 提案, memory would overflow. Rendering in order — 这项提案，委员会上周二在日内瓦开会时讨论过，之前就资金问题进行了长时间辩论，…… — keeps the load low, at the price of a Chinese that is less elegant than a written translation would be. Interpreting research and teaching describe this trade-off as normal: simultaneous output is judged as speech, where short clauses and repetitions are acceptable, not as written text. Anticipation works because speeches are predictable in structure (thanks, welcomes, the order of agenda items) and often in content; but an interpreter who anticipates wrongly must repair quickly and plainly.
:::

::: example Segmenting a long English sentence {#ex-segment}
Show how a simultaneous interpreter might render this sentence into Chinese segment by segment: *The report, which was published yesterday by an independent panel of experts after two years of research, warns that water shortages could affect a quarter of the world's population by 2050.*
::: solution
1. **Segments as heard**: *The report* / *which was published yesterday* / *by an independent panel of experts* / *after two years of research* / *warns that* / *water shortages could affect* / *a quarter of the world's population* / *by 2050*.
2. **Rendering in order**:

这份报告， / 昨天发布， / 由一个独立专家小组撰写， / 他们研究了两年。 / 报告警告说， / 水资源短缺可能会影响到 / 全世界四分之一的人口， / 时间是到2050年。

3. **Notes**: the relative clause becomes a series of short comments after 这份报告; the passive *published by* is split into 发布 and 由……撰写; *after two years of research* becomes a clause; 报告警告说 repeats the subject to restart the main clause; *by 2050* is added at the end with 时间是 — in a written translation it would come first (到2050年，……). The result is acceptable spoken Chinese; a written translation would reorder.
:::
:::

::: quiz
In simultaneous interpreting from Chinese into English, the speaker says: 我们这次在上海召开的、有来自三十多个国家和地区的代表参加的…… Which start is best?
- [ ] Wait silently until the head noun arrives.
- [x] *We are holding this meeting in Shanghai, and it brings together delegates from more than thirty countries and regions …*
- [ ] *Our this time in Shanghai held …*
- [ ] *The thirty countries meeting in Shanghai …*
::: solution
The head noun (会议 or 大会) has not arrived yet, but it is predictable from context. Starting with a full clause allows the interpreter to render the long modifier in order and attach the head noun when it comes (*… and this conference is …*). Waiting risks overload; the other two options are ungrammatical or wrong.
:::
:::

## The interpreter's role

::: theorem Professional conduct {#rule-conduct}
1. **Accuracy and completeness**: render everything that is said, including what you would not have said — rudeness, mistakes, hedges — without adding, omitting or softening, unless the situation clearly calls for a cultural adjustment and the parties have accepted it.
2. **Impartiality**: do not take sides, advise or argue for either party.
3. **Confidentiality**: do not disclose what you hear in the course of the work.
4. **First person**: interpret in the speaker's person (*I propose …*, 我提议……), not as reported speech (*He says he proposes …*).
5. **Transparency**: if you need a repetition or a clarification, or have made an error, say so openly, in the third person (*The interpreter would like to correct …*).
6. **Preparation**: study the subject, the speakers and the terminology beforehand ([[translation/specialised-translation#rule-termbase]]).
:::

::: proof
These principles appear, in various forms, in the codes of professional associations — for conference interpreters, the code of the International Association of Conference Interpreters (AIIC), founded in 1953 — and in court and medical interpreting standards. They follow from the interpreter's position: the parties depend entirely on the interpreter for each other's words and cannot check them. Softening a rude remark may seem kind, but it deprives the listener of information about the speaker and makes the interpreter a participant. The first person keeps the parties speaking to each other, not to the interpreter. Cultural adjustment is the hardest case: a joke that will not work, a 成语 that will mean nothing, a compliment formula that sounds odd in English. Interpreters usually render the intention plainly (*a Chinese saying that means …*) rather than silently replacing it.
:::

::: example A difficult moment {#ex-role}
At a business dinner, a Chinese host says, smiling: 我们这个小地方，招待不周，请多包涵。 How should a liaison interpreter render it, and why?
::: solution
1. **Meaning**: a conventional modest formula — literally ‘our small place, our hospitality is inadequate, please forgive us’ — which the host does not mean literally; it expresses courtesy.
2. **Risk**: a literal rendering (*Our place is small and our hospitality is poor, please forgive us*) may confuse or embarrass the guests, who may feel they must contradict it.
3. **Rendering**: keep the courtesy and the modesty in an English form that works: *We're a small place, so please forgive us if we haven't looked after you as well as we'd like — we're very glad to have you here.* The last clause makes the intention (welcome) explicit, a small cultural adjustment that the host would accept.
4. **Not**: replacing it with *Enjoy your meal!* (which drops the modesty) or adding a comment (*He doesn't really mean it*), which breaks impartiality and the first person.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Directionality.** Interpreters in China often work both ways (*retour* — into the B language — is normal in Chinese–English booths), while at some international organisations interpreters work mainly into their first language. Into English, the greatest risks are tense, articles and connectives; into Chinese, long English post-modifiers and late negation.
- **Formulas of public speech.** Chinese speeches open and close with fixed formulas (尊敬的各位来宾, 女士们、先生们, 祝大会圆满成功); English speeches use different ones (*Distinguished guests, ladies and gentlemen*; *I wish the conference every success*). Prepare them; they are where the audience first judges the interpreter.
- **Quotations and poetry.** Chinese speakers often quote classical lines (海内存知己，天涯若比邻). The interpreter cannot compose a literary translation in two seconds: give a clear rendering of the meaning, and if possible prepare one beforehand from the speaker's text ([[translation/literary-translation]]).
- **Accents.** Both English and Chinese are spoken with many accents; interpreters train their listening on varieties of English from around the world and on regional Mandarin ([[listening-and-conversation/accents]]).
:::

::: history
Interpreting is as old as contact between languages, but conference interpreting as a profession dates from the twentieth century. At the Paris Peace Conference of 1919, English joined French as a language of diplomacy, and interpreters worked in consecutive, often rendering long speeches from notes. Simultaneous interpreting with headphones was tried in the 1920s and first used on a large scale at the International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg (1945–1946), in English, French, German and Russian. The United Nations adopted it in its early years, and the International Association of Conference Interpreters (AIIC) was founded in Paris in 1953. Jean-François Rozan published his guide to consecutive note-taking in 1956, and Danica Seleskovitch's study of conference interpreters appeared in 1968. Chinese became an official language of the UN at its founding and a working language of the General Assembly in 1973; after the People's Republic took China's UN seat in 1971, the training of Chinese conference interpreters grew, including a UN-supported training programme in Beijing from 1979. Remote and online interpreting spread widely during the COVID-19 pandemic of 2020–2022.
:::

## Where this leads

Machine translation, which now also produces automatic speech translation, is the subject of [[translation/machine-translation]]. The listening skills interpreting depends on are trained in [[listening-and-conversation/how-listening-works]] and [[connected-speech/hearing-fast-speech]]; public speaking, which an interpreter also does, in [[presentations-and-discussion/delivery]]. The restructuring techniques used at speed here are those of [[translation/english-into-chinese]] and [[translation/chinese-into-english]].

::: summary
- The main **modes** are consecutive, simultaneous, whispered, liaison and sight translation, chosen by the situation ([[#def-modes]]).
- **Interpret meaning, not words** — except numbers, names, titles and terms, which must be kept exactly ([[#rule-sense]]).
- Gile's **Effort Models** explain why interpreting fails under load: listening, memory, production (and note-taking) share a limited capacity.
- **Notes** record ideas, links, negation, emphasis, numbers and names, vertically with a shift, following Rozan's principles ([[#rule-rozan]]).
- **Numbers**: write digits at once and convert 万/亿 to thousands, millions and billions on paper; check plausibility ([[#rule-numbers]]).
- In **simultaneous** interpreting between English and Chinese, segment and work in order (顺句驱动), restructuring within segments, waiting briefly and anticipating carefully ([[#rule-linearity]]).
- **Professional conduct**: accuracy and completeness, impartiality, confidentiality, first person, transparency about errors, preparation ([[#rule-conduct]]).
:::

Take notes, using Rozan's principles, while a partner reads this aloud once at normal speed; then interpret it into Chinese. *Thank you, Chair. Our company has worked in this region for fifteen years. We now employ about two thousand local staff, and last year we opened a training centre for young engineers. But we face two problems: high transport costs and a shortage of skilled technicians. We hope that today's meeting will help us find solutions to both.*

Render this sentence into English in segments, as a simultaneous interpreter would, without waiting for the end: 由我国自主研发、历时十年建成、全长五十五公里的这座跨海大桥，今天正式通车。

A possible rendering in order: *Developed independently in this country, / built over ten years, / fifty-five kilometres long, / this sea-crossing bridge / opened to traffic today.* — each Chinese modifier becomes an English participle or adjective phrase placed before the subject, which English allows; the head noun comes when the Chinese gives it. ::: exercise Ethics {level=3}
You are interpreting at a meeting between a Chinese supplier and a British buyer. The buyer says, irritated: *Frankly, your last shipment was a disaster, and if this happens again, we're finished with you.* The supplier's manager, who you know well, looks anxious. What do you say, and why? What would you do if the supplier later asked you privately what the buyer "really thinks"?
::: hint
Think of accuracy, impartiality, confidentiality and the first person.
:::
::: solution
Interpret accurately and in the first person, keeping the force: 坦白说，你们上一批货简直是一场灾难。如果再出现这种情况，我们就不跟你们合作了。 Softening it (e.g. 上一批货有些问题) would deprive the supplier of important information and make you a participant. Tone can be matched without exaggerating. Privately, you must not give your opinion of the buyer's intentions or reveal anything from other conversations: you can say that your role is to interpret what is said, and suggest that the supplier ask the buyer directly, through you, in the meeting.
:::

::: exercise Sight translation {level=3}
Sight-translate this paragraph into English aloud, recording yourself, then listen and list two problems in your delivery and two in your English. 本次活动旨在增进两国青年之间的相互了解与友谊。活动期间，双方代表将参观学校、企业和社区，并就教育、就业等共同关心的问题深入交流。
::: solution
A model rendering: *The aim of this event is to build understanding and friendship between young people from our two countries. During the visit, the delegates will tour schools, businesses and local communities, and will have in-depth discussions on issues of shared concern, such as education and employment.* Typical problems to look for: in delivery, long hesitations before the first verb, restarts, a flat tone; in English, word-for-word renderings such as *mutual understanding and friendship among the youths*, *communities* for 社区 without *local*, or *deeply exchange*. Note that 旨在 becomes *The aim … is to*, and 就……深入交流 becomes *have in-depth discussions on*.
