Every Chinese schoolchild knows Li Bai's 《静夜思》 (‘Quiet Night Thoughts’), four lines of five characters:

> 床前明月光，疑是地上霜。
>
> 举头望明月，低头思故乡。

A translator into English meets a decision in every character. Is 床 a bed, or — as some scholars argue — the frame of a well, or a seat? Is the speaker *I*, or no one in particular? (Chinese needs no subject; English does.) Is the moonlight shining now or then? (Chinese has no tense; English must choose.) Is it *the moon* or *a moon*? Should the English rhyme, as the Chinese does (光, 霜, 乡)? Should it have four lines of five beats, or five syllables, or neither? Here is one translation, made for this lesson:

> *Before my bed, the moonlight, bright —*
>
> *I took it for frost upon the ground.*
>
> *I raise my head to see the moon,*
>
> *and lower it, and think of home.*

It keeps the four lines, the images and the parallel of the last two lines (*raise … lower*, 举头 … 低头). It does not rhyme, and it mixes past (*took*) and present (*raise*), a choice a reader might question. Every translation of the poem — and there are hundreds — makes a different set of choices, and every one loses something.

Literary translation is where the questions of the first lesson of this course ([[translation/what-translation-is]]) are sharpest, because in literature the form is part of the meaning. This lesson looks at what a literary translator tries to keep, at the Chinese ideals of 神似 and 化境, at the translation of fiction — voice, dialogue, style — and of poetry, with older translations in the public domain as case studies, and at how to read and write about literature in translation.

## What the literary translator keeps

::: definition Literary translation {#def-literary}
**Literary translation** is the translation of texts whose language is valued for its own sake — fiction, poetry, drama, literary essays — where the translator is responsible not only for the content but for the **style**, the **voice**, the **imagery**, the **sound and rhythm** and the **form** of the original, and for producing a text that can be read as literature in the target language. In Newmark's terms these are **expressive** texts, which call for semantic translation ([[translation/what-translation-is#rule-brief]]); in practice, literary translators move along the whole scale.
:::

::: widget venn
title: What a translation of a poem can keep
sets: 3
labels: sense; form; sound
expr: sense & form & sound
caption: A poem's **sense** (what it says, its images), its **form** (lines, stanzas, parallelism, length) and its **sound** (rhyme, rhythm, tone, alliteration). A translation that keeps all three (the shaded centre) is rare; most keep sense and part of the form, and recreate sound with the target language's own means. Try *sense - sound* for a prose translation that keeps the meaning and gives up the music, or *sense & sound - form* for a rhymed version that rearranges the lines. The diagram is a way of naming choices, not a scale of value.
:::

::: theorem Priorities in literary translation {#rule-priorities}
1. **Read the whole work first**, and decide what is most important in it: a story, a voice, a pattern of images, a form, a music. That decides what to keep when everything cannot be kept.
2. **Keep the author's images and figures** where they work in the target language; they are the author's creation, not conventional idioms ([[translation/idioms-culture#rule-metaphor]]).
3. **Translate the effect of formal features with target-language means** where the form itself does not carry over: Chinese parallelism (对偶, 排比) by English balance and repetition used sparingly; English rhyme and metre by Chinese rhyme and rhythm.
4. **Keep the register and the voice** of each narrator and character: an uneducated speaker should not become eloquent, a formal one casual.
5. **Do not explain inside the text** what the original leaves implicit; ambiguity and suggestion are part of literary meaning. Put necessary information in notes, a preface or the context, if at all.
6. **Compensate**: an effect lost at one point (a pun, a rhyme) can sometimes be restored at another.
:::

::: proof
A literary text says many things at once, and a translation can rarely reproduce them all; the translator therefore needs an interpretation of the whole that decides priorities. This is why literary translators often say that translation is the closest form of reading. The other points follow from the nature of expressive texts: their images and form are not decoration but meaning, so changing them changes the work. Point 3 is the main exception, because the formal resources of the two languages differ greatly — Chinese has tones, monosyllables and strict parallel structures; English has stress-timed rhythm and a long tradition of rhyme and metre ([[reading-literature/metre-rhythm]]) — and a form that is natural in one may be strained or impossible in the other. Point 5 distinguishes literary translation from informative translation: a technical translator should remove ambiguity; a literary translator should usually keep it.
:::

## 神似 and 化境

The Chinese tradition offers two formulations of what a literary translation should achieve. Fu Lei (傅雷, 1908–1966), the translator of Balzac and Romain Rolland, argued in the preface to his 1951 retranslation of *Le Père Goriot* (《高老头》) that a translation should be like a copy of a painting, seeking **resemblance in spirit** (神似) rather than resemblance in form (形似). Qian Zhongshu (钱锺书, 1910–1998), in his essay on Lin Shu's translations (《林纾的翻译》), proposed **化** — transformation — as the highest standard: a work passes from one language into another so completely that it shows no sign of strain from the difference between the languages, and yet keeps everything of its original flavour. Qian also admitted that complete transformation is an ideal never fully reached, and that some loss or distortion (讹) is unavoidable in practice.

Xu Yuanchong (许渊冲), who translated Chinese poetry into English and French, adapted a formulation from Lu Xun's history of Chinese literature (《汉文学史纲要》) — that writing should be beautiful in meaning, in sound and in form — into the "three beauties" (三美) that a translation of poetry should seek. These are the three circles of the figure above.

::: example 神似 or 形似? {#ex-spirit}
Translate the first sentence of Lu Xun's story 《孔乙己》 (1919), which is in the public domain: 孔乙己是站着喝酒而穿长衫的唯一的人。 Then explain what the sentence does in Chinese and how your translation keeps it.
::: solution
1. **What the sentence does**: in the tavern of the story, customers who drink standing at the counter are poor labourers in short jackets (短衣帮); those in long gowns (长衫, the dress of the educated) sit inside. Kong Yiji is the only one who is both. The sentence defines him by a social contradiction, in a flat, almost statistical style; its slightly awkward construction (……而……的唯一的人) is part of the narrator's voice — a boy who worked in the tavern, recalling it years later.
2. **Form**: one sentence, with the surprise at the end (唯一的人).
3. **A translation**: *Kong Yiji was the only customer who drank standing up and wore a scholar's long gown.*
4. **What it keeps and loses**: it keeps the definition-like form and the end position of the key idea (*the only … who*). It adds *customer* and *scholar's*, because an English reader does not know that a long gown marks a scholar — a small explanation inside the text, which some translators would avoid and put in a note ([[#rule-priorities]], point 5). A version closer in form, *Kong Yiji was the only long-gowned man who drank standing*, is shorter and keeps the strangeness, but leaves the reader to work out the social point from the following paragraphs — which the story in fact explains. A translator seeking 神似 might prefer it.
:::
:::

## Fiction: voice, dialogue and style

Most of the work of translating fiction is not the famous difficulties — puns, dialect, poems inside stories — but the steady recreation of a **narrative voice** sentence by sentence.

::: theorem Translating fiction {#rule-fiction}
1. **Find the narrator's voice** — person, distance, register, rhythm — and keep it consistent ([[reading-literature/narrative]]).
2. **Give each character a way of speaking** that matches the original's distinctions of age, class, region and education, using target-language means; do not translate dialect into a real regional dialect of the target language (a Beijing street seller should not speak Cockney or Shanghainese), but mark it with informal vocabulary, grammar and rhythm.
3. **Recreate free indirect style** with the target language's own signals. English marks it with backshifted tense combined with the character's deictics (*now*, *tomorrow*, *here*) and expressions; Chinese, which has no tense to shift, uses the character's words, sentence-final particles, rhetorical questions and the absence of 他想 or 她觉得.
4. **Keep the rhythm** of sentences: long sentences for a flowing mood, short ones for tension; divide or join only where the target language requires it.
5. **Keep names and cultural details** that belong to the setting (foreignisation of content), while making the language natural (domestication of style), unless the brief is different ([[translation/idioms-culture#rule-domestication]]).
:::

::: proof
The voice of a narrator is the main thing a reader of fiction responds to, and it is built from small choices of word and rhythm that a translator makes in every sentence; that is why point 1 comes first. Dialect is the classic problem: a real target dialect relocates the characters to another country, while ignoring dialect flattens a social distinction the author drew; marking it with non-standard but non-regional features is the usual compromise. Free indirect style depends on grammar that the two languages do not share, so it must be recreated rather than translated; the guide is the effect — the reader hears the character's thoughts in the narrator's sentence — not the form.
:::

::: example Free indirect style into Chinese {#ex-fid}
Translate this passage, written for the atlas, into Chinese, keeping the character's voice in the second and third sentences: *Mei closed the letter and looked out at the rain. So he was coming back after all. Well, she would not be at the station to meet him — not this time.*
::: solution
1. **Find the voices**: the first sentence is the narrator's (*Mei closed the letter*); the second and third are Mei's thoughts in free indirect style — *So … after all*, *Well*, the dash and the emphatic *not this time* are hers, but the tense (*was coming*, *would not be*) and the third person are the narrator's.
2. **Chinese has no tense to backshift**, so the voice must come from words and rhythm: 原来 or 到底 for *after all*, 哼 or 好吧 for *Well*, a short final phrase for *not this time*. Do not add 她想.
3. **A translation**:

小梅合上信，望着窗外的雨。他到底还是要回来了。哼，她才不会去车站接他呢——这回可不去了。

Notes: 到底还是 carries *so … after all*; 才不会……呢 and 可 give Mei's spoken emphasis; the dash is kept. *Mei* becomes 小梅, a natural way for a Chinese narrator to name a young woman; since the passage gives no character for the name, the translator has chosen 梅, a common one.
:::
:::

::: warning Explaining instead of translating
✗ 他到底还是要回来了，她心里想，感到有些生气。 — adds 她心里想 and 感到有些生气, which the English leaves to the reader.

✓ 他到底还是要回来了。

Free indirect style works because the narrator does *not* say whose thought it is; a translation that labels the thought destroys the effect.
:::

## Poetry

::: theorem Choices in translating poetry {#rule-poetry}
Before translating a poem, decide:

1. **Verse or prose?** A prose translation can keep more of the sense; a verse translation keeps the fact that it is a poem.
2. **Rhyme or no rhyme?** Rhyme in English is harder to achieve than in Chinese (Chinese has fewer rhyme classes and a long tradition of shared rhymes), and forced rhymes distort sense; many modern English translators of Chinese poetry do not rhyme, while others keep it.
3. **Line and length**: keep the number of lines; match the compression of the Chinese by short English lines, or accept that English needs more words (articles, prepositions, subjects).
4. **Grammar the poem leaves open**: subject, tense, number. Decide each, or find English structures that keep them open (verbless lines, participles, the present tense).
5. **Allusion**: keep, note or replace? Classical Chinese poems allude constantly to earlier texts; English poems to the Bible and the classics.
:::

::: proof
Every point is a case of the general problem of [[translation/english-chinese-compared#rule-categories]], intensified. Chinese classical poetry is extremely compressed — five or seven syllables a line, few function words, often no subject or time marker — and part of its art is this openness: a line such as 举头望明月 can be read as one person's action, as anyone's, as a moment or a habit. English grammar forces a choice, and each choice narrows the poem. Translators have answered in different ways: some write in fragments and present tense to keep the openness; others accept the choice and make the speaker a person in a moment. The debates about rhyme are old, and both sides have produced great translations; the point is to choose knowingly.
:::

::: quiz
A translator renders 举头望明月 as *Raising my head, I gaze at the bright moon* and another as *Head raised, gazing at the moon*. What does the second version gain?
- [ ] Rhyme with the previous line.
- [ ] A clearer statement of who is looking.
- [x] It leaves the subject and the tense open, as the Chinese does.
- [ ] It is closer to English prose word order.
::: solution
The verbless, participial line has no finite verb, so it states neither *I* nor a tense, keeping the openness of the Chinese ([[#rule-poetry]], point 4). The price is that it reads as a fragment; the first version is clearer and narrower.
:::
:::

::: example Translating English verse into Chinese {#ex-sonnet}
Translate the first two lines of Shakespeare's Sonnet 18 (1609, in the public domain) into Chinese, and explain your choices: *Shall I compare thee to a summer's day? / Thou art more lovely and more temperate.*
::: solution
1. **What must be kept**: the question and the answer; the image of a summer's day, which in England means a mild, beautiful day (not the heat of a Chinese summer); *temperate* ‘mild, moderate’, which the following lines develop (rough winds, the sun too hot).
2. **Form**: iambic pentameter, rhyming *day* with line 3. Chinese can use lines of roughly equal length and a rhyme; *thee* and *thou* are old intimate forms, so a slightly literary register fits.
3. **A translation**:

我可否把你比作夏日？
你却比夏日更可爱，更温和。

4. **Choices**: 夏日 rather than 夏天, for a literary tone; 温和 for *temperate*, which prepares for the next lines' contrast with harsh weather; 却 marks the turn from question to answer. A Chinese reader may still think of a hot summer; the translator relies on the following lines (and perhaps a note) to show that the English summer is mild. This version does not rhyme; a translator keeping the sonnet's rhyme scheme would need to choose the rhyme sound of the whole poem first.
:::
:::

## Older translations as case studies

Translations made long ago are evidence of how translators have solved these problems, and those whose translators died more than seventy years ago and which were published before 1931 can be quoted freely. James Legge (1815–1897) translated the Confucian classics and Daoist texts into English; his versions are scholarly, close to the Chinese and written in the formal English of Victorian scholarship.

The first passage of the *Analects* (《论语·学而》):

> 子曰：学而时习之，不亦说乎？有朋自远方来，不亦乐乎？人不知而不愠，不亦君子乎？

Legge (*The Chinese Classics*, vol. 1, 1861):

> *The Master said, "Is it not pleasant to learn with a constant perseverance and application? Is it not delightful to have friends coming from distant quarters? Is he not a man of complete virtue, who feels no discomposure though men may take no note of him?"*

The opening of the *Daodejing* (《道德经》第一章) — 道可道，非常道；名可名，非常名 — in Legge's version (*The Texts of Taoism*, 1891):

> *The Tao that can be trodden is not the enduring and unchanging Tao. The name that can be named is not the enduring and unchanging name.*

::: example Analysing Legge's choices {#ex-legge}
Compare Legge's *Analects* translation with the Chinese. What has he kept, what has he added, and what would a modern translator do differently?
::: solution
1. **Kept**: the three rhetorical questions with 不亦……乎 rendered each time as *Is it not … ?*, keeping the parallelism — a formal equivalence that also works in English.
2. **Interpreted and added**: 时习之 becomes *with a constant perseverance and application* — Legge reads 时 as ‘constantly’ and 习 as ‘practise, apply’, a reading that modern scholars debate (时 may mean ‘at the right time’, 习 ‘review’ or ‘practise’). 君子 becomes *a man of complete virtue*, a paraphrase with a strongly moral colour; *the gentleman* is the more common modern rendering, and some leave *junzi*. 人不知 becomes *though men may take no note of him*, which adds *him* and fixes the meaning ‘others do not recognise his worth’.
3. **Style**: *discomposure*, *distant quarters*, *take no note* are Victorian formal diction, chosen to give the text the dignity of scripture for English readers of the 1860s.
4. **A modern version**, for comparison (our own): *The Master said: "To learn something and practise it at the proper time — is that not a pleasure? To have friends come from far away — is that not a joy? To go unrecognised by others and feel no resentment — is that not the mark of a gentleman?"* It keeps the three questions, chooses a different reading of 时, and uses plainer modern English. Neither version is "the" translation; each reflects a reading of the text and an idea of its readers.
:::
:::

::: quiz
In Legge's *The Tao that can be trodden is not the enduring and unchanging Tao*, why does he write *trodden*?
- [ ] Because 道 means ‘to walk’ in modern Chinese.
- [x] Because he reads the second 道 as a verb related to 道 ‘way, road’ — a way that can be walked — where many other translators read it as ‘spoken of, told’.
- [ ] Because *Tao* is a kind of path in English.
- [ ] Because he wanted the line to rhyme.
::: solution
道 means both ‘way, path’ and ‘to say’. In 道可道, the second 道 is a verb, and translators divide: ‘the Tao that can be told (spoken of)’ is the more common reading, while Legge chose ‘trodden’, linking it to ‘way’. The choice shows how a single character can force an interpretation on the translator, and why translations of classical texts are also commentaries.
:::
:::

## Reading and writing about literature in translation

::: theorem Reading a translation critically {#rule-reading}
When you study a work in translation, or write about it:

1. **Name the translator and the edition**: *Dream of the Red Chamber* in David Hawkes's translation is not the same text as in Yang Xianyi and Gladys Yang's.
2. **Do not build an argument on a word the translator chose** without checking the original: a metaphor, a repetition or an ambiguity in the English may not be in the Chinese, and the reverse.
3. **Compare translations** where possible: the differences show what the original allows and what each translator decided.
4. **Look for what a translation must lose** when you read in the original: the features of the original's language — rhyme, puns, verse, tense, dialect — that a translation cannot carry are often the features most worth studying ([[reading-literature/writing-about-literature]]).
:::

::: proof
A translation is a reading of the original by one person at one time, and its words are the translator's. Literary criticism that treats a translation as if it were the original attributes the translator's choices to the author. Point 4 is the other side: reading in the original, a learner can notice exactly what translation removes. Shakespeare in Chinese is a clear case: Zhu Shenghao's (朱生豪) translations of the 1930s and 1940s are in prose, as are Liang Shiqiu's, and a prose translation cannot keep blank verse, shared lines or the rhyme of couplets; later translators such as Bian Zhilin (卞之琳) translated in lines of verse to keep more of the form ([[reading-literature/drama-shakespeare]]).
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Figures of speech.** A Chinese 排比 or 对偶 translated word for word often sounds overwrought in English, and an English chiasmus may lose its point in Chinese. Translate the effect, not the form, where the form does not carry over: a Chinese four-part parallel series may become an English tricolon; an English antithesis may become a Chinese 对偶 if one is natural ([[style-and-rhetoric/figures-of-speech]]).
- **Tense in narration.** English novels are mostly in the past tense, and the tense carries distinctions (past perfect for earlier events, backshift in free indirect style) that Chinese marks with time words and 了 or 过, or not at all. Translating into English, choose a consistent narrative tense; translating into Chinese, do not add 了 to every sentence ([[tense-and-aspect/narrative-tenses]]).
- **Titles.** Titles are translated for the market as well as for meaning: 《红楼梦》 has been *The Dream of the Red Chamber*, *A Dream of Red Mansions* and *The Story of the Stone* (the novel's other Chinese title, 《石头记》).
- **For translation and the reception of world literature in China**, see the Chinese Atlas lesson on [translation and reception](/learn/chinese/lesson.php?c=world-literature&l=translation-reception); for reading classical Chinese poetry, its lessons on [imagery and allusion](/learn/chinese/lesson.php?c=poetry-metrics&l=imagery-allusion) and [reading a poem](/learn/chinese/lesson.php?c=poetry-metrics&l=reading-a-poem).
:::

::: history
Literary translation between Chinese and English has a history of about two hundred years. Missionary and diplomatic scholars translated the classics first: Legge's *Chinese Classics* (from 1861) and Herbert Giles's anthologies of Chinese literature (from the 1880s). Into Chinese, Lin Shu's classical-Chinese versions of Western novels, from *La Dame aux camélias* (1899) onward, were enormously popular, though made from collaborators' oral renderings and often abridged; Lu Xun and Zhou Zuoren's closer translations of short fiction (1909) sold poorly. Twentieth-century China produced major translators of Western literature — Zhu Shenghao and Liang Shiqiu of Shakespeare, Fu Lei of Balzac — and English-speaking translators made Chinese literature widely available: Arthur Waley's poetry translations from 1918, and the two complete English translations of 《红楼梦》 by David Hawkes and John Minford (1973–1986) and by Yang Xianyi and Gladys Yang (1978–1980). In 2012 Mo Yan's Nobel Prize drew attention to the role of translators, among them Howard Goldblatt, in bringing contemporary Chinese fiction to world readers.
:::

::: summary
- Literary translation is responsible for **style, voice, images, sound and form** as well as content; decide what matters most in the work before translating ([[#def-literary]], [[#rule-priorities]]).
- Fu Lei's **神似** (resemblance in spirit) and Qian Zhongshu's **化境** (complete transformation, an ideal never fully reached) state the Chinese tradition's highest aims; Xu Yuanchong's **三美** name sense, sound and form.
- In fiction, keep the **narrator's voice**, the characters' ways of speaking, the rhythm, and **free indirect style** — recreated in Chinese with words and particles, not tense ([[#rule-fiction]]).
- In poetry, decide verse or prose, rhyme, line and length, open grammar and allusion knowingly ([[#rule-poetry]]).
- Older translations such as Legge's are evidence of choices: they interpret, add and reflect their readers and their time.
- When writing about literature in translation, **name the translator**, check the original before arguing from a word, and compare versions ([[#rule-reading]]).
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise What must be decided {level=1}
List the grammatical decisions an English translator must make in the line 低头思故乡 that the Chinese does not make.
::: solution
The subject (*I*? *he*? none?); the tense (*lower* / *lowered*); the number and possession of *head* (*my head*); the object of 思 (*home*, *my home town*, *the village where I was born*); whether 低头 and 思 are two actions in sequence (*lower my head and think*) or one with a circumstance (*head bowed, I think*); and the article or possessive before 故乡.
:::
:::

::: exercise Images {level=1}
For each image, say whether it can probably be kept in English or Chinese, and why. (a) 杨柳 as a symbol of parting in a Tang poem. (b) *a summer's day* as an image of beauty in an English poem. (c) 鸿雁 as a bearer of letters. (d) *the rose* as a symbol of love.
::: solution
(a) Kept as an image (*willows*), but the symbolism is not known to English readers; a note or context is needed. (b) Kept, but Chinese readers may associate summer with heat; the context of the poem must make the mildness clear. (c) Kept (*wild geese*), with the same need for a note, unless the poem itself makes the link. (d) Kept: 玫瑰 as a symbol of love is now familiar in Chinese.
:::
:::

::: exercise Name the translator {level=1}
Rewrite this sentence from a student's essay so that it is accurate about translation: *In* Pride and Prejudice*, Austen describes Darcy as 傲慢, which shows that she disliked him.*
::: solution
*In* Pride and Prejudice*, Austen's narrator and characters call Darcy* proud*, translated as 傲慢 in the Chinese version I read (translator X, year).* — the essay should quote the English word, name the translation, and avoid basing an argument about Austen's attitude on a Chinese word chosen by a translator. (Whether Austen "disliked" Darcy is a separate question: the novel's view of him changes.)
:::
:::

::: exercise Dialogue and voice {level=2}
Translate into English, giving the two speakers distinct voices: 老张头儿把烟袋往鞋底上磕了磕，说："这年头儿，谁还种地呀？" 他孙子头也不抬："爷，您又来了。"
::: solution
A model: *Old Zhang knocked his pipe out against the sole of his shoe. "Who farms these days, eh?" His grandson didn't even look up. "Here we go again, Grandpa."* — the old man's voice is marked by the rhetorical question and *eh*; the grandson's by the casual idiom *Here we go again* for 您又来了 (‘you're starting again’). The respectful 您 is lost; *Grandpa* keeps some of the warmth. A rural English dialect is avoided.
:::
:::

::: exercise A passage of Lu Xun {level=2}
Translate the first sentence of Lu Xun's 《故乡》 (1921, in the public domain): 我冒了严寒，回到相隔二千余里，别了二十余年的故乡去。 Then explain how you handled the long modifier before 故乡.
::: solution
A model: *Braving the bitter cold, I travelled more than two thousand li back to the home I had left over twenty years before.* — The two modifiers (相隔二千余里, 别了二十余年) cannot both stand before *home* in English; the distance becomes part of the verb phrase (*travelled more than two thousand li*) and the absence a relative clause (*that I had left …*), with the past perfect for the earlier time. *Li* is kept as a unit of distance (a note could say it is about half a kilometre); *two thousand li* sounds more like the story than *a thousand kilometres*. 冒了严寒 → *braving the bitter cold*, a participle phrase.
:::
:::

::: exercise Parallelism {level=2}
Translate into English for a literary essay, keeping the effect rather than every word: 春有百花秋有月，夏有凉风冬有雪。
::: solution
A model: *Spring has its flowers, autumn its moon; summer its cool breezes, winter its snow.* — the four parallel clauses are kept with English ellipsis (*autumn its moon*), which is a natural English way to balance a series; *百* (‘a hundred’) is dropped, as *flowers* already suggests abundance; the two halves are separated by a semicolon to mirror the couplet. (The lines are from a Song-dynasty Chan Buddhist verse by Wumen Huikai, 无门慧开, and are in the public domain.)
:::
:::

::: exercise Two versions of a poem {level=3}
Write two English translations of 静夜思 — one in prose, keeping as much sense as possible, and one in verse with rhyme — and write a paragraph comparing what each keeps and loses, using the terms *sense*, *form* and *sound*.
::: hint
For the rhymed version, choose the rhyme sound first (*light*, *white*, *night*; or *ground*, *found*). Keep four lines if you can.
:::
::: solution
Models: (a) Prose: *In front of my bed the moonlight is so bright that I wonder whether it is frost on the ground. I lift my head and look at the bright moon; I lower my head and think of home.* (b) Verse: *Before my bed the moon shines white; / I wonder: is it frost tonight? / I raise my eyes to see the moon, / then bow my head, and home comes soon.* Comparison: the prose keeps the sense closely, including 明月 repeated, and the parallel 举头/低头, but loses the form of four short lines and all the sound. The verse keeps four lines and rhyme, but the rhymes force small changes of sense — *white* for 光, *tonight* added, and *home comes soon* for 思故乡, which changes ‘think of home’ into something nearer ‘home comes to mind’ and could even suggest a return. A reader might prefer the prose for study and the verse for reading aloud; neither keeps sense, form and sound together.

::: exercise Review a published translation {level=3}
Choose a short passage (a paragraph or a short poem) from a Chinese or English literary work you know well, and a published translation of it. Without quoting more than a sentence or two of the translation, write 150–200 words analysing three of the translator's choices: one you admire, one you would change, and one that shows a difference between the languages.
::: solution
Answers vary. A good answer names the work, the translator and the edition; quotes only the words under discussion; identifies each choice precisely (a word, an image, a sentence structure, a rhyme); explains it with the concepts of this course (equivalence, domestication and foreignisation, shifts, voice, the three beauties); and gives a reason for any alternative it proposes, saying what that alternative would gain and lose.
