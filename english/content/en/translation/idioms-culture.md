A Chinese student writes in an essay: 这个结尾完全是画蛇添足. How should the sentence be translated? Three possibilities:

- *This ending is like drawing a snake and adding feet.*
- *This ending is like gilding the lily.*
- *This ending is completely unnecessary — it spoils the story.*

The first keeps the Chinese image. An English reader who does not know the fable behind it — a man who won a drinking contest by drawing a snake fastest, then lost by adding feet to it — may find it charming, or puzzling, or both. The second replaces the image with an English idiom of similar meaning; but *gild the lily* means ‘add ornament to what is already beautiful’, and the Chinese means ‘add something unnecessary that spoils what was already finished’ — close, not the same, and *gilding the lily* makes the essay sound as if it were written by an English speaker. The third gives the meaning plainly, and the image is gone.

None of these is wrong in every context. The first might suit a book about Chinese idioms, the second a light piece of journalism, the third an academic translation. Idioms, metaphors, names and references to culture are where the translator's choice between **keeping the foreign** and **making it familiar** is most visible. This lesson sets out the kinds of correspondence between idioms in the two languages, the strategies for translating them, the theory of domestication and foreignisation that describes the choice, and the conventions for names and culture-bound terms.

## Idioms: what corresponds to what

::: definition Idiom {#def-idiom}
An **idiom** is a fixed expression whose meaning cannot be worked out from the meanings of its words: *a piece of cake* ‘something very easy’, *kick the bucket* ‘die’, 吃醋 ‘be jealous’, 炒鱿鱼 ‘dismiss from a job’. Chinese also has a large class of fixed four-character expressions, **成语**, many of them derived from classical texts and stories (画蛇添足, 守株待兔, 对牛弹琴), which educated speakers use in speech and writing ([[collocation-and-register/idioms]]).
:::

When an idiom is translated, two things may or may not carry over: its **image** (the picture the words paint) and its **meaning**. Comparing an idiom with its nearest counterpart in the other language gives four cases.

::: widget venn
title: Idioms in two languages
sets: 2
labels: same image; same meaning
expr: same image & same meaning
caption: Compare a Chinese idiom with its nearest English counterpart. **Both** (shaded): the image and the meaning carry over — 火上浇油 / *pour oil on the fire*; 浑水摸鱼 / *fish in troubled waters*. **Same meaning only**: different images for the same idea — 雨后春笋 ‘like bamboo shoots after rain’ / *spring up like mushrooms*; 对牛弹琴 ‘play the lute to a cow’ / *cast pearls before swine*. **Same image only**: a false friend — 龙 is a symbol of power and good fortune, an English *dragon* is usually a fierce monster; 红 is lucky and rising share prices are shown in red in China, while *in the red* means ‘in debt’. **Neither**: an idiom with no counterpart at all, such as 画蛇添足 or *the elephant in the room*. Shade *same meaning - same image* to see the cases that need a different image.
:::

::: theorem Strategies for translating idioms {#rule-idiom}
Following Baker (*In Other Words*, 2018), the main strategies are:

1. **An idiom of similar meaning and form**, if one exists: 火上浇油 → *add fuel to the fire*.
2. **An idiom of similar meaning but different form**: 雨后春笋 → *spring up like mushrooms*; *a piece of cake* → 小菜一碟.
3. **Borrowing the source idiom**, translated literally, when the image is clear in context or when the text is about the source culture: *draw a snake and add feet to it*; *the elephant in the room* → 房间里的大象 (now often used in Chinese journalism, with or without explanation).
4. **Paraphrase**: giving the meaning without an idiom — the most common strategy when no match exists or when an idiom would not fit the register.
5. **Omission** of the idiom, if its content is not needed or is expressed elsewhere.
6. **Compensation**: losing an idiom at one point and adding an idiomatic expression elsewhere, so that the text as a whole keeps its idiomatic tone.
:::

::: proof
Each strategy trades the image against ease and naturalness. Strategy 1 is ideal and rare. Strategy 2 reads naturally, but brings in a target-culture image that may clash with the setting (*cast pearls before swine* comes from the Gospel of Matthew; in a story set in a Chinese village it may sound out of place) or change the shade of meaning, as *gild the lily* does. Strategy 3 keeps the source culture visible and sometimes enriches the target language — many Chinese expressions have entered English this way (*lose face*, *paper tiger*) and many English ones Chinese (房间里的大象, 黑天鹅, and 灰犀牛, from the title of an English book of 2016) — but it can confuse if the image is opaque. Strategy 4 is accurate and loses the colour. Baker stresses that the choice depends not only on whether a counterpart exists but on **register**, **context** and the **effect** an idiom has in the source text: many Chinese 成语 are part of educated written style and are not felt as vivid images at all, so translating them by a vivid English idiom overstates them.
:::

::: example 画蛇添足 in three contexts {#ex-snake}
Translate 画蛇添足 in each context and name the strategy. (a) A book for foreign readers about the stories behind Chinese idioms. (b) An editor's comment on a report: 最后一段画蛇添足，建议删去。 (c) A novel, in which a grandfather says to his grandson, who has added a long apology to a short letter: 你这是画蛇添足。
::: solution
(a) **Borrowing with explanation**: *"Drawing a snake and adding feet" (画蛇添足) comes from a story in the* Zhanguo Ce *…* — the image is the subject of the book.

(b) **Paraphrase**: *The last paragraph is unnecessary and weakens the report; I suggest deleting it.* In an editor's comment, an image in English would be inappropriate.

(c) In fiction the speaker's voice matters. Options: **borrowing** with enough context to make the image clear — *"You've drawn a snake and given it feet," said Grandpa* — which keeps a Chinese flavour and is understandable after the letter scene; or an **English idiom of similar meaning** with a register that fits a grandfather — *"You've overdone it, lad"* — which loses the Chinese image. A literary translator would probably borrow, possibly adding a few words: *"You've drawn the snake and then given it legs. It was fine as it was."*
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which English idiom has both the same image and the same meaning as 浑水摸鱼?
- [ ] *a fish out of water*
- [x] *fish in troubled waters*
- [ ] *a big fish in a small pond*
- [ ] *there are plenty more fish in the sea*
::: solution
*Fish in troubled waters* means ‘take advantage of a confused situation for one's own gain’, like 浑水摸鱼 ‘grope for fish in muddy water’. The others share the image of fish only: *a fish out of water* is someone in an unfamiliar situation; *a big fish in a small pond* is someone important only in a small world; *plenty more fish in the sea* consoles someone after a break-up.
:::
:::

## 成语 and four-character style

Chinese formal and literary prose uses 成语 and other four-character phrases (四字格) much more densely than English uses idioms. A Chinese essay may contain several in a paragraph without any sense of cliché; English style guides warn against stock phrases ([[style-and-rhetoric/word-choice]]).

::: theorem 成语 into English {#rule-chengyu}
1. **Decide whether the 成语 is functioning as an image or as a plain word.** Many 成语 (一目了然, 迫不及待, 千方百计, 举世闻名) are simply the normal way to say something in formal Chinese; translate them by a plain English word or phrase: *at a glance*, *eagerly*, *by every possible means*, *world-famous*.
2. **Keep the image only when it does work** in the text — when the story behind it is relevant, when the speaker's voice depends on it, or when the text is about Chinese language and culture.
3. **Do not translate every 成语 in a series**: four-character phrases often come in parallel pairs or groups that say one thing ([[translation/chinese-into-english#rule-redundancy]]).
4. **Translating into Chinese**, use 成语 where a Chinese writer would, but do not make the Chinese more ornate than the English.
:::

::: proof
The rule applies the principle of equivalent effect ([[translation/what-translation-is#def-equivalence]]) to style. For a Chinese reader, 一目了然 ‘one look and it is all clear’ is not a picture of an eye; it is the standard formal expression for *clear at a glance*, and a literal rendering (*one eye and all understood*) gives the English reader an image that the Chinese reader does not see. Conversely, 守株待兔 ‘guard the tree stump and wait for a hare’ is still felt as a story, and its image may be worth keeping. Into Chinese, the danger is the opposite: a translator who replaces every plain English phrase with a 成语 makes a plain original sound flowery, which changes its style.
:::

::: example English idioms into Chinese {#ex-english-idioms}
Translate the idioms in context. (a) *Learning to use the new software was a piece of cake.* (b) *Nobody at the meeting mentioned the elephant in the room: the company was running out of money.* (c) *She decided to bite the bullet and tell her boss the truth.* (d) *Don't put all your eggs in one basket.*
::: solution
(a) Similar meaning, different form: 学用这个新软件简直是小菜一碟。 / 太容易了.

(b) Borrowing, now established in Chinese journalism, with the explanation given by the sentence itself: 会上谁也没提那头"房间里的大象"——公司的钱快花光了。 For readers who may not know the phrase, paraphrase: 会上谁也不提那个明摆着的问题：公司快没钱了。

(c) Paraphrase, or a Chinese idiom of similar meaning: 她决定硬着头皮把实情告诉老板。 (硬着头皮 ‘steeling oneself to do something unpleasant’ is a close match in meaning and register.)

(d) Borrowing works, because the image is transparent and now known in Chinese: 不要把所有的鸡蛋放在一个篮子里。 A Chinese idiom of related meaning, 狡兔三窟, is too different in connotation (it suggests cunning).
:::
:::

## Metaphor

Peter Newmark (*A Textbook of Translation*, 1988) distinguished **dead** metaphors (*the foot of the hill*, 山脚), **stock** metaphors (established, but still felt as figurative: *a ray of hope*), and **original** ones created by a writer. Dead metaphors are translated by the normal target word, whatever its image. Stock metaphors are where most translation problems lie. Original metaphors in literature are usually kept, because they are part of what the author created ([[translation/literary-translation]]).

::: theorem Procedures for stock metaphors {#rule-metaphor}
Newmark lists, in order of preference for most texts:

1. **Reproduce the same image** if it has comparable frequency and currency in the target language: *a ray of hope* → 一线希望.
2. **Replace it with a standard target-language image** of the same meaning: *the lion's share* → 大头 / 绝大部分.
3. **Convert it into a simile**, which softens it: *He is a walking dictionary* → 他像一本活字典.
4. **Keep the image and add the sense**, when the reader may not understand it.
5. **Convert it to sense** (paraphrase), especially in informative texts.
6. **Delete** it, if it is redundant.
:::

::: proof
Metaphors work through shared knowledge of the vehicle ([[style-and-rhetoric/figures-of-speech#def-metaphor]]). When the vehicle is understood in the same way in both cultures, the image can be reproduced; when it is not, the translator must choose between keeping the image (risking confusion or a different connotation) and losing it. Many metaphors are shared because they rest on common experience — both languages speak of *warm* and *cold* people, of *heavy* hearts and *high* hopes. Others rest on **conceptual metaphors** that differ: both languages map time onto space, but Chinese also uses the vertical axis (上个月 ‘last month’, 下周 ‘next week’) where English uses only the horizontal (*the week ahead*, *the months behind us*), and Chinese 前 can point to the past (前天, 以前) as well as the future (前途, 向前看). A translator must convert the axis, not the words ([[reading-literature/figurative-language]]).
:::

::: example Stock metaphors in a business report {#ex-metaphor}
Translate the metaphors in this sentence from a fictional English report, choosing a procedure from [[#rule-metaphor]] for each: *The new product line is the jewel in the crown of the company, but rising costs are a ticking time bomb, and the board must grasp the nettle.*
::: solution
1. ***The jewel in the crown*** ‘the most valuable part’: a stock metaphor with a historical British flavour. Replace it with a standard Chinese image or the sense: 公司的王牌产品线 / 最亮眼的业务.
2. ***A ticking time bomb*** ‘a danger that will become serious later’: the image is shared, so reproduce it — 一颗定时炸弹 is current in Chinese.
3. ***Grasp the nettle*** ‘deal firmly with a difficult problem’: the image (a stinging plant that hurts less if seized firmly) is unknown in Chinese; convert to sense or replace — 董事会必须果断应对 / 下决心啃这块硬骨头.

新产品线是公司的王牌业务，但成本不断上升，就像一颗定时炸弹，董事会必须果断应对。 The three English images would be a mixed metaphor in any language; the Chinese keeps one, which also improves the style.
:::
:::

## Domestication and foreignisation

::: definition Domestication and foreignisation {#def-domestication}
**Domestication** (归化) is a translation strategy that brings the text close to the target culture and language: fluent target-language style, familiar images and references, little that reminds the reader that the text is a translation. **Foreignisation** (异化) keeps the foreign in view: source-culture references, names and images are retained, and the style may deliberately keep some features of the source. The terms were made central to translation studies by Lawrence Venuti in *The Translator's Invisibility* (1995).
:::

Venuti built on a lecture given in 1813 by the German philosopher Friedrich Schleiermacher, "On the Different Methods of Translating", which said, in substance, that a translator either leaves the author in peace as far as possible and moves the reader towards the author, or leaves the reader in peace and moves the author towards the reader. Venuti argued that translation into English since the seventeenth century had been dominated by domestication — the demand that translations read "fluently", as if written in English — and that this made translators invisible and erased the cultural difference of foreign texts. He advocated foreignisation as a form of resistance to that dominance.

::: theorem Choosing between domestication and foreignisation {#rule-domestication}
Neither strategy is correct in general. Choose, and be consistent, by asking:

1. **What is the text for?** Practical, informative and persuasive texts (instructions, advertisements, children's books) usually domesticate; literary, historical and cultural texts more often foreignise.
2. **What do the readers know, and what do they want?** A reader of a Chinese novel in English may want to learn about China; a user of a product manual does not.
3. **Which elements matter?** Names, places, food, institutions and social customs that are part of the setting are usually kept; idioms and metaphors that are part of the style are often naturalised.
4. **How much can the reader absorb?** Foreignising every item makes a text opaque; keep what is important, explain where necessary, and naturalise the rest.
:::

::: proof
The two strategies are poles of a scale, and most translations mix them. Venuti's argument concerns translation into English, a dominant language, where domestication can reinforce cultural self-sufficiency; for translation into Chinese, the historical situation is different — Chinese translation of Western texts has often been deliberately foreignising, as in Lu Xun's case for keeping the "foreign flavour" of a text (洋气) in an essay of 1935, and the debates of the 1980s and 1990s in Chinese translation studies about 归化 and 异化 reflected different concerns about how Chinese should change. Point 3 states the practical consensus: a translation can keep 春节 as *the Spring Festival* and 饺子 as *dumplings* or *jiaozi*, and still translate 画蛇添足 into plain English, without inconsistency, because these items do different work in the text.
:::

::: example Domesticating and foreignising a passage {#ex-domestication}
Translate this sentence twice, once domesticating and once foreignising, and say which suits a travel magazine: 春节前夕，家家户户贴春联、包饺子，等着在外打工的儿女回家团圆。
::: solution
**Domesticating**: *In the days before the New Year holiday, every family decorates the house and makes a special meal, waiting for the children who work away from home to come back for the celebrations.* — no foreign item survives; the reader understands everything and learns nothing specific.

**Foreignising**: *On the eve of the Spring Festival, every household pastes up chunlian couplets and wraps jiaozi, waiting for the sons and daughters who work far away to come home for the reunion.* — keeps 春联 and 饺子 as loan words and the image of 团圆.

**For a travel magazine**, a middle course is best: keep what the reader would want to see and taste, and explain it briefly. *In the days before the Chinese New Year, families paste red couplets of good-luck verses on their doors and make dumplings together, waiting for children who work in distant cities to come home for the family reunion that is the heart of the festival.* (*Spring Festival* and *Chinese New Year* are both established; the second is more familiar to general readers outside China. *Lunar New Year* is also widely used, since the festival is celebrated in several countries.)
:::
:::

## Names

::: theorem Chinese names in English {#rule-names}
1. **Mainland Chinese personal names** are written in **Hanyu Pinyin**, family name first, the given name as one word, without tone marks in ordinary texts: 王芳 → *Wang Fang*, 欧阳修 → *Ouyang Xiu*, 张晓明 → *Zhang Xiaoming*. (The national rules are set out in GB/T 28039-2011, 《中国人名汉语拼音字母拼写规则》.) If a person has an established spelling of their own, use it.
2. **Names from Hong Kong, Macau, Taiwan and overseas communities** follow their own romanisations and often an English given name: respect the spelling the person or institution uses.
3. **Historical and established forms** survive in some names: *Confucius* (孔子), *Mencius* (孟子), *Sun Yat-sen* (孙中山), *Peking University*, *Tsinghua University*. Use the established form.
4. **Place names**: transliterate the specific part in pinyin and translate the generic part (*Road*, *Lake*, *Mountain*, *River*) when the English reader needs to know what it is: 中山路 → *Zhongshan Road*; 西湖 → *West Lake* (an established translated name); 泰山 → *Mount Tai* or *Taishan*; 长江 → *the Yangtze River* or *the Chang Jiang*. Be consistent within a text, and follow official forms on maps and signs where they exist.
5. **Foreign names into Chinese** follow established transliterations (莎士比亚, 伦敦) and, for new names, the standard tables used by the Xinhua News Agency; some place names are partly translated (*Oxford* 牛津 ‘ox ford’, *Cambridge* 剑桥 with 桥 ‘bridge’).
:::

::: proof
Hanyu Pinyin was adopted in the People's Republic in 1958; the United Nations conference on the standardisation of geographical names recommended it for Chinese place names in 1977, and the International Organization for Standardization adopted it in ISO 7098 (1982). Older romanisations, chiefly Wade–Giles (Thomas Wade, 1867; Herbert Giles's dictionary, 1892) and the postal system, survive in established names and in names from outside the mainland, which is why *Peking University* and *Beijing* coexist. Family-name-first order is now normal in English for Chinese names, as the examples of this atlas show (*Li Wei*, *Wang Fang*); it is worth telling English readers once, since many will otherwise take the second name as the surname. For place names, the guiding principle is that the reader needs both to find the place (the pinyin name, as on maps) and to understand what it is (the generic term in English); redundancy such as ✗ *Taishan Mountain* (山 twice) is common and better avoided in careful writing, though it appears on some official materials.
:::

::: warning Name order and redundancy
✗ *Fang Wang* (for 王芳, in a Chinese context, unless she chooses that order for international use) ✓ *Wang Fang*

✗ *Xi Hu Lake* ✓ *West Lake* or *Xihu Lake* (the generic repeated in pinyin and English is widely seen but best avoided; follow the official form if one is fixed)

✗ *Zhong Shan Lu* (pinyin for the generic, word-split) ✓ *Zhongshan Road*
:::

How should 欧阳文静 (a mainland Chinese researcher with no established English spelling) be written in an English article?

- [ ] *Ou Yangwenjing*
- [x] *Ouyang Wenjing*
- [ ] *Ouyang Wen Jing*
::: solution

::: note English and Chinese
- **Culture-bound institutions** (户口, 高考, 单位, 居委会) are best handled with the strategies of [[translation/translating-words#rule-strategies]]: an established English form if one exists (*the gaokao*, *household registration*), a loan word with explanation on first use, or a paraphrase.
- **Festivals and foods** have established English names: *the Dragon Boat Festival* (端午节), *the Mid-Autumn Festival* (中秋节), *mooncakes* (月饼), *dumplings* or *jiaozi* (饺子), *tofu* (豆腐). Some loan words have become English words (*tofu*, *kowtow*, *typhoon* — the last of disputed origin, with Chinese 大风 among the proposed sources).
- **Colour and animal symbolism differs**, and these false friends are traps in both directions: a *dragon*, the colour *white* (mourning in traditional Chinese culture, weddings in Western), *red*, the number four (avoided in parts of East Asia because 四 sounds like 死) and thirteen.
- **Don't make Chinese culture exotic.** Foreignisation should keep what is specific, not decorate ordinary things: 吃饭 is *have a meal*, not *eat rice*; 老师好 is *Good morning* in a classroom, not ✗ *Teacher good*.
:::

::: history
Schleiermacher's lecture of 1813, "Über die verschiedenen Methoden des Übersetzens", set out the two directions — moving the reader to the author or the author to the reader — that Venuti's *The Translator's Invisibility* (1995) turned into foreignisation and domestication. In China, the same debate ran under other names. Lin Shu's novels of around 1900 domesticated thoroughly, recasting Western fiction in classical Chinese style; Lu Xun and his brother Zhou Zuoren, in their *Collection of Stories from Abroad* (《域外小说集》, 1909), translated closely and kept foreign features; and in an essay of 1935 Lu Xun argued that a translation should keep the "foreign flavour" of the original. Two famous English translations of 《红楼梦》 in the 1970s show the contrast in their titles alone: David Hawkes's *The Story of the Stone* (1973–1986, the last two of its five volumes by John Minford) and Yang Xianyi and Gladys Yang's *A Dream of Red Mansions* (1978–1980); their different treatment of names and cultural references has been studied ever since.
:::

## Where this leads

Literary translation, where the choice between keeping and replacing images matters most, is the subject of [[translation/literary-translation]]. Public signs, where names and conventions must be consistent, are discussed in [[translation/specialised-translation]]. English idioms themselves are treated in [[collocation-and-register/idioms]], metaphor in [[reading-literature/figurative-language]] and [[style-and-rhetoric/figures-of-speech]], and China English, the variety in which many Chinese cultural terms have their English form, in [[world-englishes/english-in-china]].

::: summary
- An idiom's **image** and **meaning** may both carry over, only one, or neither; false-friend images (龙 and *dragon*, 红 and *in the red*) are traps ([[#def-idiom]]).
- Translate idioms with a stated strategy: a matching idiom, an idiom of different form, borrowing, paraphrase, omission or compensation ([[#rule-idiom]]).
- Many 成语 are plain formal Chinese, not vivid images: translate them by plain English unless the image does work in the text ([[#rule-chengyu]]).
- For stock metaphors, reproduce the image if it works in the target culture; otherwise replace it, convert it to a simile or translate the sense ([[#rule-metaphor]]). Conceptual metaphors such as time as space differ between the languages.
- **Domestication** (归化) brings the text to the reader, **foreignisation** (异化) brings the reader to the text; choose by purpose, readers and the elements involved, and be consistent ([[#def-domestication]], [[#rule-domestication]]).
- Write Chinese names in **pinyin, family name first**, respect established and personal spellings, and translate the generic part of place names ([[#rule-names]]).
:::

(b) 新经理是匹黑马。 — same image: 黑马 is established in Chinese for an unexpected winner; but *dark horse* also means ‘a person who keeps their abilities secret’, and if that is the sense, convert to sense: 新经理深藏不露. ::: exercise Domestication and foreignisation {level=3}
Translate the paragraph twice for two briefs — (a) a children's picture book published in Britain, (b) a collection of Chinese folk tales for adult readers interested in Chinese culture — and explain three differences. 腊月二十三，灶王爷要上天向玉皇大帝汇报这一家人一年的好坏。所以那天晚上，家家都要给灶王爷供糖瓜，好让他嘴甜一点，多说好话。
::: hint
Who are 灶王爷 and 玉皇大帝? What does 糖瓜 do in the story? Which items can a child follow without explanation?
:::
::: solution
Models: (a) *A week before New Year, the Kitchen God goes up to heaven to tell the King of Heaven whether the family has been good or naughty all year. So that night, every family gives him sticky sweets — to make his words sweet, so he'll only say nice things!* (b) *On the twenty-third day of the twelfth lunar month, the Kitchen God (Zaowang) ascends to heaven to report to the Jade Emperor on the family's conduct over the past year. That evening, therefore, every household offers him* tanggua*, sticky malt-sugar sweets, so that his mouth will be sweet and he will speak well of them.*

Differences: (1) the date — a lunar date in (b), a child-friendly *a week before New Year* in (a) (domesticated); (2) 玉皇大帝 — *the Jade Emperor*, the established English name, in (b), *the King of Heaven* in (a); (3) 糖瓜 — a loan word with explanation in (b), *sticky sweets* in (a); (4) the tone — *good or naughty*, *nice things* and an exclamation in (a), the formal *conduct*, *ascends*, *speak well of* in (b). Both keep the Kitchen God, who is the story.
:::

::: exercise A culture-rich paragraph {level=3}
Translate into English for an English-language city guide, and list the strategy for each culture-bound item: 周末的早上，老广们喜欢去茶楼"叹早茶"，一盅两件，看看报纸，一坐就是一上午。
::: solution
A model: *On weekend mornings, many older Cantonese locals like to spend a leisurely morning over* yum cha *in a teahouse — "one pot, two dishes", as the saying goes: a pot of tea and a couple of dim sum — reading the paper and staying until lunchtime.*

Strategies: 老广 → *older Cantonese locals* (paraphrase; 老 here suggests both ‘old-established’ and often older people — the guide may simplify to *locals*); 叹早茶 → *spend a leisurely morning over* yum cha (paraphrase of 叹 ‘enjoy’ in Cantonese + the established loan word *yum cha*); 一盅两件 → literal translation as a quoted saying plus explanation (borrowing with gloss); 茶楼 → *teahouse* (established equivalent); 一坐就是一上午 → *staying until lunchtime* (paraphrase).
