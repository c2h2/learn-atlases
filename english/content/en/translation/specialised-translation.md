A clause from a fictional supply contract, and a Chinese translation that a court might one day have to interpret:

> *The Supplier shall not be liable for any delay in delivery unless such delay is caused by its negligence.*
>
> ? 供应商对任何交货延误不负责任，除非这种延误是由其疏忽造成的。

The translation looks accurate. But in a dispute, each side will read it in its own favour. Is 不负责任 a rule of the contract or a description (in everyday Chinese, 不负责任 also means ‘irresponsible’)? Does the 除非 clause, coming at the end after a comma, limit the whole sentence or add an afterthought? A legal translator would write:

> 除因供应商自身过失造成的交货延误外，供应商对交货延误不承担责任。

The exception comes first, as Chinese legal drafting usually puts it; 不承担责任 is the fixed legal phrase for *shall not be liable*; 过失 is the legal term for *negligence* (疏忽 is the everyday word); and 自身 settles the reference of *its*. The meaning is the same as the English, and there is only one way to read it.

Specialised texts — technical, legal, business, academic, official — are most of the translation done in the world. They are less often discussed than literature, but they have their own demands: exact terminology, fixed formulas, genre conventions that readers expect, and in some cases legal consequences for every word. This lesson describes the conventions of the main types in both languages, the special case of public signs, and the management of terminology.

## What makes a text specialised

::: definition Specialised text and parallel text {#def-specialised}
A **specialised text** is written by and for people in a field — engineering, medicine, law, finance, science, administration — using the field's **terminology**, its **phraseology** (fixed combinations such as *shall be deemed*, 特此通知) and the conventions of its **genres** (a patent, a user manual, a contract, an abstract, a notice). A **parallel text** is a text of the same genre written originally in the target language — a Chinese manual for a similar product, an English abstract in the same field — used by the translator as a model of terminology and convention.
:::

::: theorem Translate into the target genre {#rule-genre}
1. **Identify the genre** of the source and the corresponding genre in the target culture.
2. **Study parallel texts** before translating: how do target-language texts of this genre begin and end, address the reader, state obligations, give warnings, cite sources, use numbers and units?
3. **Keep the content exactly; adapt the conventions** — formulas, layout, register, tense — to the target genre, unless the brief requires the source conventions to be kept (as in some legal translations).
4. **Never improvise terms or formulas**: check them in authoritative sources and parallel texts ([[translation/translating-words#rule-term]]).
:::

::: proof
A specialised reader reads through the conventions of the genre: a Chinese engineer expects a manual to say 注意 before a caution and 警告 before a warning; an English lawyer expects *shall* to impose an obligation. A translation that keeps the source conventions forces the reader to work out what the unfamiliar form means, and in the worst case leads to misreading. Parallel texts are the translator's best evidence of what the target genre requires, better than dictionaries, because they show terms and formulas in use. The exception in point 3 is real: in a translation of a contract submitted as evidence, a court may want to see the source's structure, and in bilingual legislation (as in Hong Kong, where laws are enacted in English and Chinese) both versions must follow the same section structure.
:::

## Technical texts

Technical texts — manuals, specifications, safety instructions, patents — are mostly informative and vocative ([[translation/what-translation-is#rule-brief]]). Their readers want to understand and act, quickly and safely.

::: theorem Technical translation {#rule-technical}
1. **Use the imperative for instructions** in both languages: *Press the power button* → 按下电源键; avoid ✗ 您应该按下电源键 or 电源键被按下.
2. **One term, one concept**: the same English term always gets the same Chinese term, and the reverse ([[#rule-termbase]]).
3. **Keep numbers, units, symbols and references exact**: check every figure; follow target conventions (*1,000.5* in English, 1000.5 or 1 000.5 in Chinese standards; °C, mm).
4. **Translate safety signal words consistently**: the levels *DANGER*, *WARNING*, *CAUTION*, *NOTICE* are usually rendered 危险, 警告, 小心 (or 注意), 注意 (or 提示) — but follow the client's or the industry's established set.
5. **Keep sentences short and in the order of action**: condition first, then action (*If the light flashes, restart the device* → 如果指示灯闪烁，请重启设备).
:::

::: proof
Technical translation is judged by whether the reader can use the product correctly and safely, so accuracy of content and clarity of instruction come before every other consideration. Inconsistent terms are dangerous in this genre: a reader who meets 进水阀 on one page and 入水阀门 on another may look for two valves. Safety signal words have defined meanings in product-safety practice — roughly, *danger* for a hazard that will cause death or serious injury, *warning* for one that could, *caution* for minor injury — and the translation must keep the levels distinct. The order of action follows the Chinese principle of temporal sequence ([[translation/english-chinese-compared#rule-order]]) and is also good English practice for instructions.
:::

::: example Translating instructions {#ex-instructions}
Translate into Chinese for a product manual: *WARNING: Before cleaning the appliance, make sure that it has been unplugged and allowed to cool down. Do not immerse the base in water.*
::: solution
1. **Genre**: a safety warning in a manual — signal word, imperatives, short sentences.
2. **Order**: the English *before*-clause is already first; the passive *has been unplugged and allowed to cool down* becomes active actions in sequence.
3. **Terms**: *appliance* → 本产品 or 本机 (Chinese manuals rarely say 电器 for "this appliance"); *unplug* → 拔下电源插头; *base* → 底座; *immerse* → 浸入.

**警告：**清洁本产品前，请务必拔下电源插头，并待其完全冷却。切勿将底座浸入水中。

Notes: 务必 and 切勿 are the standard intensifiers of Chinese safety instructions; *make sure that* is not translated literally (✗ 确保它已经被拔掉插头).
:::
:::

## Legal texts

Legal language in both languages is conservative, formulaic and designed to be read in only one way. Its translation is one of the most demanding forms of specialised work, and for official purposes often has to be done or certified by authorised translators.

| English legal formula | usual Chinese equivalent | note |
|---|---|---|
| *shall* (obligation) | 应当, 应, 须 | *shall* in contracts and laws imposes a duty; not a future |
| *shall not* | 不得 | prohibition |
| *may* | 可以, 可 | permission or discretion |
| *including but not limited to* | 包括但不限于 | |
| *notwithstanding* (clause X) | 尽管有（第X条）的规定 / 不受……限制 | |
| *subject to* (clause X) | 在符合（第X条）规定的前提下 / 受……约束 | |
| *hereinafter referred to as* | 以下简称 | |
| *in witness whereof* | 双方签字为证 / 兹证明 | closing formula |
| *force majeure* | 不可抗力 | |
| *party* (to a contract) | 当事人, 一方 | *Party A* / *Party B* for 甲方 / 乙方 |

::: theorem Legal translation {#rule-legal}
1. **Translate every condition, exception and qualifier**, and keep their scope exactly: what does *unless*, *provided that*, *except*, *and/or* attach to?
2. **Use the fixed formulas** of the target legal language, not everyday equivalents (不承担责任, not 不负责任).
3. **Be consistent**: a defined term (*the Goods*, *the Effective Date*, 本合同) must be translated in the same way every time it occurs.
4. **Do not resolve ambiguities silently**: if the source is ambiguous, either keep the ambiguity or consult the client; never choose the reading that suits one party.
5. **Follow official translations** where they exist: when a statute has an official English text, quote it rather than retranslate it, and say which version is authoritative.
:::

::: proof
In a legal text, the scope of a condition is the meaning. The opening example shows how a condition placed at the end of a Chinese sentence can seem to attach loosely; Chinese legal drafting usually states exceptions and conditions first (除……外, 未经……不得), which removes the problem. Fixed formulas exist because courts have interpreted them: 不承担责任 and *shall not be liable* have known meanings, while everyday phrases have to be argued about. Point 5 matters because many contracts between Chinese and foreign parties are bilingual, with a clause stating which language prevails in case of conflict, and laws of the People's Republic are officially published in Chinese, so English versions — however carefully made — are aids to understanding unless designated otherwise.
:::

The logic of conditions is where legal translations most often go wrong. *Unless* is a negative condition: *A shall not be liable unless B* means that A is liable **only if** B. A Venn diagram is not the clearest picture of this; a truth table is.

::: widget truthtable
title: *The Supplier shall not be liable unless the delay is caused by its negligence*
formula: ~p -> ~q
compare: q -> p
caption: Let *p* be ‘the delay is caused by the supplier's negligence’ and *q* ‘the supplier is liable’. The clause says: if not *p*, then not *q* (*shall not be liable unless …*). The second formula, *q* → *p*, says: the supplier is liable **only if** the delay is caused by its negligence. The two are equivalent (a contrapositive). Note what neither says: that negligence always makes the supplier liable — the row where *p* is true and *q* false is allowed by both. A translator who writes 如因供应商过失造成延误，供应商应承担责任 has added an obligation that the English does not contain. Change the formulas to test other readings.
:::

::: example A contract clause {#ex-contract}
Translate into English: 未经甲方书面同意，乙方不得将本合同项下的任何权利或义务转让给第三方。
::: solution
1. **Structure**: a condition (未经……书面同意) and a prohibition (不得……转让); the condition is an exception to the prohibition.
2. **Formulas**: 甲方 / 乙方 → *Party A* / *Party B* (keep the defined terms of the contract); 不得 → *shall not*; 本合同项下 → *under this Contract*; 第三方 → *any third party*.
3. **Order**: English legal drafting usually puts the prohibition first and the exception after it.

*Party B shall not assign any of its rights or obligations under this Contract to any third party without the prior written consent of Party A.*

Notes: *prior* is often added in English drafting and is implied by 未经; *assign* (for rights) and *transfer* (for obligations) are sometimes distinguished in English law, and a careful translator may write *assign or transfer*. Check the rest of the contract for the defined terms.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which Chinese rendering of *The Buyer may terminate this Agreement if the Seller fails to deliver within 30 days* is correct?
- [ ] 如果卖方三十天内交货失败，买方将终止本协议。
- [x] 卖方未在三十日内交货的，买方可以终止本协议。
- [ ] 买方可能会终止本协议，如果卖方不在三十天内交货。
- [ ] 卖方若三十天内不能交货，买方必须终止本协议。
::: solution
*May* gives the buyer a right (可以), not a prediction (将, 可能会) or a duty (必须); *fails to deliver* means ‘does not deliver’ (未……交货), and 交货失败 is a calque. The ……的 condition before the main clause and 日 for days are standard in Chinese legal style.
:::
:::

## Business texts

Business correspondence in the two languages has different conventions. Formal Chinese business letters and notices use classical-style formulas — 兹, 特此, 敬请, 为盼, 此致敬礼 — and a respectful, indirect register; modern English business writing prefers plain, direct sentences and a polite but not deferential tone ([[pragmatics/writing-to-people]], [[sentences-and-paragraphs/emails-messages]]).

::: theorem Business letters and emails {#rule-business}
1. **Translate the function of a formula, not its words**: 兹通知如下 → *Please note the following*; 特此函告 → (usually nothing — English letters do not close by announcing that they are letters); 敬请谅解 → *We apologise for any inconvenience*; 为盼 → *We look forward to hearing from you.*
2. **Adjust the order** to the target convention: English emails usually state the purpose in the first sentence; Chinese letters may begin with courtesies and background.
3. **Adjust the register** of promotional language ([[translation/chinese-into-english#rule-redundancy]]), but keep all commitments, prices, dates and conditions exactly.
:::

::: example A business notice {#ex-business}
Translate into an English email: 尊敬的客户：因系统升级，我公司将于2026年5月20日0:00至6:00暂停在线服务。由此给您带来的不便，敬请谅解。特此通知。
::: solution
*Subject: Online services unavailable on 20 May 2026, 00:00–06:00*

*Dear Customer,*

*Our online services will be unavailable from midnight to 6 a.m. on 20 May 2026 while we upgrade our systems. We apologise for any inconvenience.*

Notes: the purpose and the time go into the subject line and the first sentence; 因系统升级 (cause first in Chinese) becomes a *while*-clause after the main point; 由此给您带来的不便，敬请谅解 → the standard English apology formula; 特此通知 is dropped. The time zone should be added if customers are abroad (*Beijing time*).
:::
:::

## Academic texts

Translating academic writing — above all abstracts, which many Chinese journals publish in both languages — combines terminology with the conventions of academic style in each language ([[academic-writing/abstracts-titles]], [[academic-writing/academic-style]]).

- **Self-reference**: 本文 → *This paper* or *This study*; 笔者 → *the author* is acceptable, but English abstracts increasingly use *we* for multi-author papers; follow the journal.
- **Tense**: English abstracts typically use the present for what the paper does and what is known (*This study examines …*; *The results suggest …*) and the past for what was done (*Data were collected from …*). Chinese abstracts mark none of this; the translator must supply it.
- **Hedging**: 表明, 说明, 证明 differ in strength; *show*, *suggest*, *indicate* and *prove* must be chosen to match the claim, not inflated ([[modality/hedging-boosting]]).
- **Nominal style**: academic English accepts more nominalisation than other registers, so some Chinese verbs can become English nouns: 对……进行了分析 → *an analysis of …* is acceptable here.

::: quiz
Which English sentence best translates 本文通过问卷调查，分析了大学生使用手机的情况 in an abstract?
- [ ] *This paper through questionnaire survey analysed the situation of college students using mobile phones.*
- [x] *Using a questionnaire survey, this study examines how university students use their mobile phones.*
- [ ] *Questionnaires were analysed in this paper by the situation of phone use.*
- [ ] *We did a questionnaire to analyse students' situation of phones.*
::: solution
The second puts the method in a participle phrase, uses the present tense for what the paper does, and turns 情况 (‘the situation of …’, often empty in translation) into a *how*-clause. The first copies the Chinese order and the empty noun *situation*; the third distorts the meaning; the fourth has collocation errors (*did a questionnaire*) and a wrong register.
:::
:::

## Public signs

Signs are the most public translations of all: everyone sees them, and errors on them are photographed and shared. Their constraints are severe — very few words, instant comprehension, often a standard format.

::: theorem Translating public signs {#rule-signs}
1. **Use the standard formula** of the target language for the function, not a translation of the words: 小心地滑 → *Caution: Wet Floor* or *Slippery Floor*; 当心碰头 → *Mind Your Head*; 游客止步 → *Staff Only* or *No Entry*; 请勿乱扔垃圾 → *Please Do Not Litter*; 出口 → *Exit*.
2. **Be brief**: signs in English are often noun phrases or imperatives (*No Smoking*, *Keep Right*), without articles.
3. **Name places consistently**, following the conventions of [[translation/idioms-culture#rule-names]] and the forms already used on maps and other signs in the same place.
4. **Avoid tone that English readers will take wrongly**: many Chinese signs give reasons or appeals (小草微微笑，请您绕一绕); a short English formula (*Please Keep Off the Grass*) usually serves better than a translation of the verse.
5. **Check official standards**: in mainland China, a national standard for English in public service areas, 《公共服务领域英文译写规范》 (GB/T 30240, whose first parts were published in 2017), gives recommended translations for many sign types; local standards also exist. Consult the current version rather than relying on memory or on signs already in place.
:::

::: proof
A sign is read in a second or two, often by someone moving, so it must use a form the reader already knows. Both languages have conventional sign formulas, and the translator's job is to match function to formula, like matching a 成语 to a plain English word ([[translation/idioms-culture#rule-chengyu]]). Literal translation of sign wording produces the famous errors — the 小心地滑 sign rendered as a warning about something sliding carefully is the result of a dictionary or machine reading 地 as ‘ground’ and 滑 as an instruction. Consistency of names matters because a visitor follows the names on signs, maps and announcements together; if one sign says *Tian'anmen* and another *Heavenly Peace Gate*, the visitor cannot know they are the same place.
:::

::: example Fixing four signs {#ex-signs}
Correct these English signs, explaining each change. (a) 请勿触摸 → *Please don't touch me.* (b) 残疾人专用 → *Deformed Man Toilet.* (c) 小心台阶 → *Be careful of the stairs, carefully.* (d) 贵重物品请随身携带 → *Please take your valuable things with you all the time.*
::: solution
(a) *Please Do Not Touch.* — the Chinese has no object; *me* was added by a literal reading of a sign on an exhibit.

(b) *Accessible Toilet* (BrE) / *Accessible Restroom* (AmE), or the wheelchair symbol alone. *Deformed* is offensive; *Disabled Toilet* is still seen in Britain, but *accessible* is now generally preferred on signs. This is a change of connotation as well as of wording ([[translation/translating-words#rule-colouring]]).

(c) *Mind the Step* (BrE) / *Watch Your Step* (AmE). One formula, no repetition.

(d) *Please Keep Your Valuables with You.* — *valuables* is the noun for 贵重物品; *all the time* is unnecessary on a sign.
:::
:::

## Managing terminology

::: theorem Build and use a termbase {#rule-termbase}
For any long text or repeated client:

1. **Extract** the terms before translating: names, technical terms, defined terms, product and department names, recurring phrases.
2. **Research** each term in authoritative sources and parallel texts; record the source and the date.
3. **Record** each entry in a **glossary** or **termbase**: source term, target term, definition or context, source of the choice, forbidden alternatives, notes.
4. **Apply and check**: use the termbase during translation and check the final text against it, by search or with the quality-assurance functions of translation software.
5. **Maintain** it: when the client corrects a term, update the entry, so that the correction applies to every later text.
:::

::: proof
Terminology work is about consistency over time and across people. A large project may involve several translators and revisers working for months; without a shared termbase, each makes reasonable choices and the result is inconsistent. **Translation memories** — databases of previously translated sentence pairs, used by computer-assisted translation (CAT) software since the 1990s — serve the same purpose at the sentence level, and also store a client's preferred phraseology. Both raise the quality of specialised translation more than any single translator's skill, and both depend on human judgement for every entry: a termbase that records a wrong term spreads the error.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Chinese legal and official Chinese has its own register**, with classical elements (兹, 之, 予以, 不得, 均) that a translator into Chinese should use where Chinese drafters would. Into English, the equivalents are plain modern legal English, not archaic forms such as *hereinbefore* and *witnesseth*, which modern drafting guides discourage.
- **甲方 and 乙方** are established in Chinese contracts; in English they may be kept as *Party A* and *Party B* (common in contracts with Chinese parties) or replaced by the parties' defined names (*the Supplier*, *the Buyer*). Choose one and be consistent.
- **Units and dates**: Chinese texts may use 万 and 亿 (*ten thousand*, *hundred million*), which must be converted carefully ([[core-vocabulary/numbers-dates-money]]): 3.5亿元 is *350 million yuan*, not ✗ *3.5 billion*. Dates in the order 2026年5月20日 become *20 May 2026* (BrE) or *May 20, 2026* (AmE).
- **For readers outside the field**, the brief may call for a plain-language version; that is a different translation, not a simplification of the specialised one ([[style-and-rhetoric/readers-and-media]]).
:::

::: history
The modern study of terminology began with Eugen Wüster, an Austrian engineer whose doctoral thesis on international standardisation of technical language (1931) founded the "general theory of terminology" behind later ISO terminology standards. In China, the National Committee for Terms in Sciences and Technologies (全国科学技术名词审定委员会) was set up in 1985 to approve standard terms across disciplines. The profession of specialised translation was shaped from the 1990s by computer-assisted translation software with translation memories and termbases, and by international standards for translation services, of which ISO 17100 (2015) is the current general one. In China, a national examination for translators and interpreters, 全国翻译专业资格（水平）考试 (CATTI), was introduced in 2003. Public-sign English became a national concern before the Beijing Olympic Games of 2008, when the city undertook a large-scale revision of its English signs, and national standards for English in public services followed in the 2010s.
:::

## Where this leads

Machine translation, now the first step of much specialised translation, and the post-editing of its output are the subject of [[translation/machine-translation]]. The English genres behind this lesson are taught in [[academic-writing]] (abstracts, methods, citations), [[sentences-and-paragraphs/emails-messages]] and [[pragmatics/writing-to-people]]; plain language for non-specialist readers in [[style-and-rhetoric/readers-and-media]]. The structural shifts used in every example here are collected in [[translation/structural-shifts]].

::: summary
- Specialised texts have **terminology, phraseology and genre conventions**; translate into the corresponding **target genre**, using **parallel texts** as models ([[#def-specialised]], [[#rule-genre]]).
- **Technical**: imperatives, one term per concept, exact numbers and units, consistent safety signal words, condition before action ([[#rule-technical]]).
- **Legal**: every condition and exception with its exact scope, fixed formulas (*shall* → 应当, *shall not* → 不得, 不承担责任), consistent defined terms, no silent resolution of ambiguity; *unless* means ‘only if … not’ ([[#rule-legal]]).
- **Business**: translate the function of formulas, put the purpose first in English, keep every commitment exact ([[#rule-business]]).
- **Academic**: supply tense, match the strength of claims, follow the journal's conventions.
- **Signs**: use the standard formula for the function, be brief, keep names consistent, and check the current official standards ([[#rule-signs]]).
- Keep a **termbase** and use it: extract, research, record, apply, maintain ([[#rule-termbase]]).
:::

::: exercise Revise a translated notice {level=3}
This English notice was translated from Chinese. Rewrite it as a natural English notice, keeping all information, and explain three changes. *Respected tenants: In order to further strengthen the safety management of our building and create a safe and comfortable living environment, the property management office will carry out fire facility inspection work from March 3 to March 5. At that time, staff will enter each household for inspection. Please give your cooperation and support. Hereby notify.*
::: solution
A model:

*Fire safety inspections: 3–5 March*

*Dear Residents,*

*Building management will be inspecting fire safety equipment in every flat from 3 to 5 March. A member of staff will need to come into your flat. Thank you for your cooperation.*

Changes: (1) the purpose sentence (*In order to further strengthen … environment*) is reduced to the essential information, because English notices state the action first and the reasons briefly if at all; (2) *carry out fire facility inspection work* → *inspecting fire safety equipment*, removing the empty noun *work* (工作) and a calque; (3) *Hereby notify* (特此通知) is dropped, and *Respected tenants* (尊敬的住户) becomes *Dear Residents*; *Please give your cooperation and support* becomes the English formula *Thank you for your cooperation*. If residents need to be at home or can arrange a time, the notice should say so — a question for the management office.
