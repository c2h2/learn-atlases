Five English sentences, all in the passive, and their most natural Chinese translations:

- *The bridge was built in 1957.* — 这座桥建于1957年。
- *The meeting has been postponed.* — 会议推迟了。
- *He was hit by a car.* — 他被车撞了。
- *English is spoken all over the world.* — 世界各地都有人说英语。
- *It is said that the house is haunted.* — 据说这房子闹鬼。

Only one of the five uses 被. The others use a formal pattern of dates (建于), a sentence with no passive marker at all (会议推迟了 — the meeting is the topic, and Chinese does not need to say who postponed it), an active sentence with an indefinite subject (有人说), and a fixed phrase (据说). Translated with 被 every time, the five would be understandable and odd: ? 这座桥在1957年被建造, ? 会议已经被推迟了, ? 英语被全世界的人说.

The passive is the best-known example of a larger phenomenon. Translating between English and Chinese constantly requires changes of **structure** — of voice, of word class, of positive and negative, of the unit that carries a meaning. Translation scholars call these changes **shifts**, and this lesson describes the main ones and when to make them.

## What a shift is

::: definition Shift, transposition and modulation {#def-shift}
A **translation shift** is a departure from formal correspondence between source and target: the target expresses the source meaning with a different grammatical structure, word class or unit. The term comes from J. C. Catford's *A Linguistic Theory of Translation* (1965), which distinguished **level shifts** (a meaning expressed by grammar in one language and by vocabulary in the other: English tense ~ Chinese time words) and **category shifts**: of **structure** (word order, active and passive), of **class** (noun to verb), of **unit** (word to phrase, clause to sentence) and **intra-system** shifts (singular in one language, plural in the other).

Vinay and Darbelnet (1958) named two of the most important kinds of oblique translation **transposition** — a change of word class without a change of meaning (*after he arrives* → *after his arrival*) — and **modulation** — a change of point of view (*It is not difficult* → *It is easy*; *Wet paint* → 油漆未干).
:::

::: widget graph
title: Vinay and Darbelnet's procedures
nodes: translation procedures@5,8; direct@2,6; oblique@8,6; borrowing@0,4; calque@2,4; literal translation@4,4; transposition@6,4; modulation@8,4; equivalence@10,4; adaptation@12,4
edges: translation procedures>direct; translation procedures>oblique; direct>borrowing; direct>calque; direct>literal translation; oblique>transposition; oblique>modulation; oblique>equivalence; oblique>adaptation
caption: The seven procedures of Vinay and Darbelnet's *Stylistique comparée du français et de l'anglais* (1958; English translation 1995). **Direct** procedures keep the source form: *borrowing* (*kowtow*, *tofu*; 沙发, 咖啡), *calque* (a loan translation: 热狗 for *hot dog*, *paper tiger* for 纸老虎) and *literal translation*. **Oblique** procedures change it: *transposition* (word class), *modulation* (point of view), *equivalence* (a different fixed expression for the same situation: *Ouch!* / 哎哟) and *adaptation* (replacing a cultural situation). They described French and English, but the classification is widely used for other pairs; English–Chinese translation uses the oblique procedures far more often.
:::

::: theorem When to shift {#rule-when-shift}
Make a shift when the formally corresponding structure in the target language is **ungrammatical**, **unnatural**, **less clear** or **different in meaning or effect**. Do not shift merely to show that you can: a structure that corresponds formally and reads naturally is the best choice ([[translation/what-translation-is#rule-literal-first]]).
:::

::: proof
Shifts are the mechanism of the principle "as literal as possible, as free as necessary". Between English and Chinese they are necessary much more often than between related European languages, because the two grammars differ in exactly the areas where shifts occur: voice, word class, the obligatory categories and the unit of the sentence ([[translation/english-chinese-compared]]). Catford's classification is useful because it shows that shifts are systematic — the same kinds recur in text after text — and therefore teachable. Unnecessary shifts are a fault of the opposite kind: they introduce small changes of emphasis and make the translation harder to check against its source.
:::

## English passives into Chinese

English uses the passive much more often than Chinese, especially in academic, technical, legal and news writing. Corpus comparisons (for example Xiao, McEnery and Qian, "Passive constructions in English and Chinese", 2006) have found passive constructions much less frequent in Chinese texts than in comparable English ones, and the Chinese 被-passive restricted in its uses.

::: theorem Translating the English passive {#rule-passive-zh}
For an English passive, consider these Chinese structures, roughly in order of preference:

1. **Notional passive** (意义被动) — the patient as topic, with no marker: *The problem has been solved* → 问题解决了; *The tickets are sold out* → 票卖完了.
2. **Active with the doer as subject**, if the doer is known or is given in a *by*-phrase: *The decision was made by the board* → 董事会作出了这个决定.
3. **Active with an indefinite or general subject** (有人, 人们, 大家, 我们): *English is spoken in many countries* → 很多国家的人说英语.
4. **Fixed phrases for impersonal passives**: *It is said that* → 据说; *It is reported that* → 据报道; *It is believed that* → 人们认为 / 一般认为; *It must be admitted that* → 必须承认.
5. **Formal patterns**: 由 + doer (这项工作由王教授负责 — official, neutral); 为……所 (literary); 受到, 遭到, 得到, 予以 + verbal noun (受到批评, 得到批准, 予以支持); 是……的 for a past fact (这座寺庙是唐代修建的); 于 for dates and places (建于1957年).
6. **被** (or spoken 叫, 让, 给) — especially when the event is **adverse** for the subject (*He was cheated* → 他被骗了) or when the doer must be expressed and the patient kept as topic.
:::

::: proof
The order reflects Chinese usage. The notional passive is the most common way Chinese expresses a passive meaning, because a topic can be the patient of the verb without any marker (这本书我看完了; 饭做好了) — a direct consequence of topic prominence ([[translation/english-chinese-compared#def-topic]]). The 被-passive has traditionally been used mainly for unpleasant events — Li and Thompson (1981) describe its typical meaning as adversity for the subject — and although modern written Chinese, influenced by translation, uses it more widely (被评为先进, 被选为代表), a 被 on every English passive still sounds like translation. The English passive is often chosen for reasons of information structure — keeping the topic as subject, avoiding a vague doer — which Chinese can satisfy by topicalisation without changing voice. Strategy 5 belongs to formal registers: 予以, 由 and 于 are common in official and legal Chinese and out of place in conversation.
:::

::: example Five passives, five strategies {#ex-passive-zh}
Translate into Chinese, and name the strategy of [[#rule-passive-zh]] you used. (a) *All the seats have been taken.* (b) *The new airport was opened by the mayor last week.* (c) *Smoking is not allowed in the building.* (d) *It is estimated that the project will cost two billion yuan.* (e) *My bike was stolen last night.*
::: solution
(a) Notional passive: 座位都满了。 / 座位都有人了。 (1)

(b) Active with the doer as subject: 新机场上周由市长主持启用。 (5, 由, formal) or 市长上周为新机场揭幕。 (2). In a news report, 新机场上周正式启用，市长出席了启用仪式 is also natural: the passive *was opened by* becomes two active clauses.

(c) Fixed formula of notices: 楼内禁止吸烟。 / 楼内不准吸烟。 — an active verb with no subject.

(d) Fixed impersonal phrase: 据估计，这个项目将耗资二十亿元。 (4)

(e) 被, because the event is adverse: 我的自行车昨晚被偷了。 (6) The notional ? 我的自行车昨晚偷了 is unacceptable because a bicycle cannot be the topic of 偷 without a marker — it would be read as active. Spoken Chinese also allows 我自行车昨晚让人偷了.
:::
:::

::: warning 被 for every passive
✗ 这个问题已经被讨论过很多次了。 ✓ 这个问题已经讨论过很多次了。

✗ 晚饭被做好了。 ✓ 晚饭做好了。

✗ 他被选为班长，被大家祝贺了。 ✓ 他当选为班长，大家都向他表示祝贺。

Use 被 when the event is adverse for the subject, or when the doer must be named; elsewhere try a notional passive or an active sentence first.
:::

## Chinese into English: when to use the passive

The opposite move is just as important. Chinese notional passives and subjectless sentences often need an English passive; and some English texts — scientific reports, official documents — expect passives where Chinese uses active verbs with 我们 or no subject.

::: theorem When English needs a passive {#rule-passive-en}
Use an English passive when translating from Chinese:

1. For a **notional passive** whose doer is unknown or unimportant: 这座桥去年修好了 → *The bridge was repaired last year*; 信已经寄出去了 → *The letter has been sent.*
2. For a **subjectless** statement of what must be done, in formal texts: 要加强安全管理 → *Safety management must be strengthened* (or, less formally, *We need to tighten safety procedures*).
3. In **scientific and technical** writing, for methods: 将样品加热至80度 → *The samples were heated to 80°C.*
4. To **keep the topic as subject** across a sequence of sentences: 这家公司成立于1998年，2010年被一家外国企业收购 → *The company was founded in 1998 and was bought by a foreign group in 2010.*

Do not use the passive when the doer is known and important, or when it makes the sentence vague about responsibility: 我们犯了错误 → *We made mistakes*, not ✗ *Mistakes were made.*
:::

::: proof
English needs a subject in every clause, and when the doer is unknown, unimportant or obvious, the passive allows the patient to be the subject ([[verb-patterns/the-passive]]). Points 3 and 4 follow from register and information structure: English methods sections conventionally use the passive for procedures, and the passive allows a paragraph to keep the same subject, which English cohesion favours. The final warning is a matter of honesty as well as style: the "agentless passive" can hide who did something, and a translation should not hide responsibility that the original states.
:::

::: example Choosing voice in Chinese → English {#ex-passive-en}
Translate into English. (a) 这个问题必须尽快解决。 (b) 会上，大家对这个方案提出了很多意见。 (c) 文物出土后，立即送往省博物馆修复。
::: solution
(a) *This problem must be solved as soon as possible.* — notional passive with an obligation; the doer is not stated. (*We must solve this problem quickly* is also correct if the speaker means "we".)

(b) *At the meeting, many objections were raised to the plan* — or, active, *People at the meeting raised many objections to the plan.* The active is better if the reader should know who objected; the passive if the point is the objections.

(c) *After the artefacts were excavated, they were sent straight to the provincial museum for restoration.* / *Once excavated, the artefacts were taken straight to the provincial museum to be restored.* — Chinese has two subjectless clauses with the artefacts as topic; English keeps them as subject with two passives, the first reduced to a participle (*Once excavated*).
:::
:::

## Changing word class

::: definition Conversion of word class (词类转换) {#def-conversion}
**Conversion of word class** — Vinay and Darbelnet's **transposition**, Catford's **class shift** — translates a word of one class by a word of another class with the same meaning: an English noun by a Chinese verb, an English preposition by a Chinese verb, an English adjective by a Chinese adverb or verb, and the reverse. Zhang Peiji's 《英汉翻译教程》 (1980) treats it as one of the basic techniques of English–Chinese translation (词类转译法).
:::

::: theorem The common conversions {#rule-conversion}
**English → Chinese**

1. **Noun → verb**: *He is a good swimmer* → 他游泳游得很好; *I'm a stranger here* → 我对这儿不熟; *The sight of the sea made her happy* → 她一看到大海就很高兴; *Thank you for your help* → 谢谢你帮我.
2. **Preposition → verb**: *She left without a word* → 她一句话没说就走了; *a house with a garden* → 带花园的房子; *Are you for or against the plan?* → 你赞成还是反对这个计划？
3. **Adjective → verb**: *I'm sure he'll come* → 我相信他会来; *She is fond of music* → 她喜欢音乐; *They are aware of the risks* → 他们知道有风险.
4. **Adjective → adverb**, when the English adjective modifies a noun of action: *We had a brief talk* → 我们简单地谈了谈; *a careful reading of the report* → 仔细阅读报告.
5. **Adverb → adjective or noun**: *He is physically weak but mentally strong* → 他身体虽弱，意志却很坚强.

**Chinese → English**: the reverse moves — Chinese verbs to English nouns, prepositions and adjectives — are the means of hypotaxis described in [[translation/chinese-into-english#rule-subordinate]]: 他很会做饭 → *He is a good cook*; 我们必须了解情况 → *We need an understanding of the situation* (formal) or *We need to understand the situation.*
:::

::: proof
Conversion is the word-level face of the static–dynamic contrast ([[translation/english-chinese-compared#rule-static]]). English nouns of agent (*swimmer*, *cook*, *learner*) and nouns of action carry verbal meaning, and English adjectives such as *sure*, *afraid*, *aware*, *fond*, *capable* correspond to Chinese verbs of mental state. English prepositions are numerous and carry relations that Chinese often expresses with verbs (带, 过, 穿, 拿, 用), many of which are also Chinese prepositions in other uses. The adjective-to-adverb shift in point 4 follows from the first: once a noun of action becomes a verb, its adjective must become an adverb. In word formation the two languages also differ: English conversion within English (*to email*, *a walk*) is a separate phenomenon, described in [[word-formation/conversion]].
:::

::: example Conversions in a sentence {#ex-conversion}
Translate into Chinese, and identify each conversion: *Her rapid recovery after the operation was a great relief to her family, who had been anxious about the outcome.*
::: solution
1. **Trunk**: *Her rapid recovery … was a great relief to her family*.
2. **Nouns of action → verbs**: *recovery* → 恢复; *relief* → 松了一口气 (a verb phrase); *the outcome* → 结果 (a noun is fine here, or 手术能不能成功, a clause).
3. **Adjectives → adverbs or verbs**: *rapid* → 很快地 (adverb); *anxious about* → 担心 (verb).
4. **Order and division**: the non-defining clause *who had been anxious …* is background, so it goes first.

家人原本一直担心手术的结果，见她术后恢复得这么快，都松了一口气。 / 她手术后恢复得很快，一直提心吊胆的家人终于放下心来。

The English has one finite verb (*was*) and two in the relative clause; the Chinese has five verbs (担心, 见, 恢复, 松了一口气, or 放下心来).
:::
:::

## Positive and negative

::: theorem Affirmative and negative shifts (正反表达) {#rule-negation}
A meaning stated positively in one language is often best stated negatively in the other, and the reverse — Vinay and Darbelnet's **modulation**:

- **English positive → Chinese negative**: *Wet paint* → 油漆未干; *Members only* → 非会员免进; *Keep off the grass* → 请勿践踏草坪; *He is the last person to tell a lie* → 他绝不会说谎; *The explanation is far from satisfactory* → 这个解释很不令人满意 (*far from* is affirmative in form).
- **English negative → Chinese positive**: *I couldn't agree more* → 我完全同意; *It won't be long before …* → 很快就会……; *Don't lose heart* → 要有信心; *not until* → 直到……才: *He didn't arrive until midnight* → 他半夜才到.
- **Double negatives and litotes**: *not uncommon* → 很常见 / 并不少见; *not without reason* → 不无道理.

Choose the form that is natural in the target language for the context — on signs, in particular, each language has fixed formulas.
:::

::: proof
Languages differ in which way of saying a thing is conventional, and a modulation keeps the meaning while changing the perspective. Signs and fixed expressions are where the difference is sharpest, because both languages have standard formulas and readers recognise them instantly; ✗ 湿油漆 or ✗ *Paint not dry* is understood, but it is not what a sign says. English also has many expressions that are negative in meaning and positive in form (*fail to*, *far from*, *the last person to*, *too … to*) or the reverse (*cannot … too*: *You cannot be too careful* ‘be as careful as possible’), which learners misread. The translator's first job is to read them correctly; the second is to choose the natural form in the target language.
:::

::: example Reading and translating negatives {#ex-negation}
Translate into Chinese, paying attention to the polarity. (a) *You can't be too careful when you cross this road.* (b) *She failed to notice the sign.* (c) *Nothing could be further from the truth.* (d) *It was not until she got home that she realised she had lost her phone.*
::: solution
(a) *Can't … too* means ‘however careful you are, it is not too much’: 过这条马路，再小心也不为过。 / 过这条马路一定要特别小心。 ✗ 过马路你不能太小心 reverses the meaning.

(b) *Fail to* is negative in meaning: 她没注意到那个标志。

(c) A negative form with a strongly affirmative meaning (‘that is completely untrue’): 这完全不是事实。 / 事实恰恰相反。

(d) *Not until* → 直到……才: 她直到回到家，才发现手机丢了。
:::
:::

::: quiz
What does *He is the last person I'd ask for advice* mean, and how is it best translated?
- [ ] He is the final person in a list I will ask — 他是我最后一个要请教的人。
- [x] I would never ask him for advice — 我才不会去找他出主意呢。
- [ ] He was the most recent person I asked — 他是我最近请教的人。
- [ ] I asked him for advice at the end — 最后我向他请教了。
::: solution
*The last person to …* means ‘the person least likely or least suitable to …’. The Chinese translation must state the negative meaning; a literal 最后一个 would mislead, although in a context where a list is really meant, it would be right.
:::
:::

## Other shifts

Several other structural differences call for regular shifts.

- **Existential *there* and *have***: *There is a cat on the roof* → 屋顶上有只猫 (place first, 有); *This room has two windows* → 这个房间有两扇窗 (same structure).
- **The 把-construction**: an English sentence in which an action changes the state or position of a definite object often corresponds to a Chinese 把-sentence: *Put the book on the shelf* → 把书放到书架上; *She tore the letter to pieces* → 她把信撕得粉碎. Translating into English, 把 disappears and the sentence becomes SVO plus a phrase of result.
- **Resultative verbs**: Chinese verb–result compounds (看完, 听懂, 打破) correspond to English verbs with particles, adverbs or separate verbs: *finish reading*, *understand*, *break*; and English phrasal verbs often to Chinese verb–complement compounds: *eat up* → 吃光, *find out* → 查明.
- **Intra-system shifts of number**: *He has blue eyes* → 他的眼睛是蓝色的 (no plural); *the scissors* (plural in English) → 剪刀 (no number).
- **Unit shifts**: a word becomes a phrase (*bilingual* → 会说两种语言的) or a clause becomes a phrase (*as soon as he arrived* → 他一到). Splitting and combining sentences are unit shifts on a larger scale ([[translation/english-into-chinese]], [[translation/chinese-into-english]]).

::: note English and Chinese
- **The 被 of modern Chinese is changing.** Besides its traditional adverse meaning, 被 is now used in neutral and positive senses in formal written Chinese (被评为, 被授予), and since about 2009 it has also had an ironic internet use (被就业, 被自愿), describing something done to a person who did not choose it. A translator into English must catch the irony; a translator into Chinese should not use 被 in a way readers will hear as ironic.
- **Shifts compensate.** A meaning lost in one place can often be restored in another: if *rapid* cannot be an adjective, it can be an adverb; if a passive cannot keep the doer unexpressed, an indefinite subject can. Vinay and Darbelnet called this **compensation**.
- **Formal correspondence is sometimes required.** Bilingual legal documents, interlinear glosses and linguistic examples may require the translator to avoid shifts and keep the source structure, at a cost to naturalness. The brief decides ([[translation/what-translation-is#rule-brief]]).
:::

::: history
The idea that translation proceeds through regular structural changes was formalised in the 1950s and 1960s. Jean-Paul Vinay and Jean Darbelnet's *Stylistique comparée du français et de l'anglais* (1958) classified the procedures of translation between French and English, with transposition and modulation as the central oblique procedures. J. C. Catford's *A Linguistic Theory of Translation* (1965), based on M. A. K. Halliday's systemic grammar, introduced the term *shift*. In China, the same phenomena had been described in teaching materials as techniques — 词类转译, 正反表达, 被动语态的译法 — and Zhang Peiji's 《英汉翻译教程》 (1980) set them out systematically for university students. The history of the Chinese passive itself is long: 被 originally meant ‘to cover’ or ‘to suffer’, and its use as a passive marker developed in Classical and Middle Chinese, with its association with unpleasant events surviving into the modern language.
:::

## Where this leads

Shifts of structure are the tools of every later lesson: specialised texts, with their passives and nominalisations, in [[translation/specialised-translation]]; interpreting, where shifts must be made at speed, in [[translation/interpreting]]; and machine translation, whose output often fails to make the shifts that natural Chinese or English needs, in [[translation/machine-translation]]. For the English passive itself, see [[verb-patterns/the-passive]]; for English conversion within English, [[word-formation/conversion]].

::: summary
- A **shift** is a change of structure, word class, unit or category between source and target; **transposition** changes word class, **modulation** changes point of view ([[#def-shift]]).
- Shift when the formally corresponding structure is ungrammatical, unnatural, unclear or different in effect — not otherwise ([[#rule-when-shift]]).
- English passives into Chinese: try a **notional passive**, an **active** with the doer or an indefinite subject, a **fixed phrase** (据说, 据报道), or a formal pattern (由, 受到, 于) before **被**, which is typical of adverse events ([[#rule-passive-zh]]).
- Into English, use the passive for notional passives, impersonal obligations, scientific methods and to keep a topic as subject — but not to hide a known doer ([[#rule-passive-en]]).
- **Convert word classes**: English nouns, prepositions and adjectives often become Chinese verbs, and Chinese verbs English nouns and prepositions ([[#rule-conversion]]).
- **Positive and negative** forms often reverse, especially on signs and in fixed expressions; read *can't … too*, *the last person to*, *not until* and *fail to* carefully ([[#rule-negation]]).
:::

::: exercise Misread negatives {level=2}
Each translation misreads the English. Explain the error and correct it. (a) *I couldn't care less.* → 我不能更少地关心了。 (b) *He is too clever not to see the problem.* → 他太聪明了，看不到问题。 (c) *All that glitters is not gold.* → 所有发光的都不是金子。
::: solution
(a) *Couldn't care less* means ‘I don't care at all’: 我才不在乎呢。 (b) *Too … not to* means ‘so clever that he must see it’: 他那么聪明，不会看不出问题。 (c) *All … is not* here means ‘not all’: 闪光的不一定都是金子。 — the English proverb's word order puts *not* after *all*, but the meaning is partial negation.
:::

::: exercise Analyse the shifts {level=3}
List every shift (voice, class, polarity, unit, order) between the English and the Chinese: *Visitors are reminded that photography is not permitted without the written consent of the management.* — 参观者请注意：未经管理方书面同意，不得拍照。
::: hint
Look at what happened to *are reminded*, *photography*, *without* and *consent*.
:::
::: solution
1. **Voice**: *Visitors are reminded* (passive, doer = the management) → 参观者请注意 (an active request addressed to visitors; modulation of point of view).
2. **Class**: *photography* (noun) → 拍照 (verb); *consent* (noun) → 同意 (verb); *written* (adjective) → 书面 (used adverbially).
3. **Polarity and structure**: *is not permitted without* (a negative plus a negative preposition) → 未经……不得 (two negatives, a fixed formal pattern of Chinese regulations); the passive *is not permitted* becomes the modal 不得.
4. **Order**: the condition *without … consent* comes before the prohibition in Chinese (background first).
5. **Unit**: the *that*-clause becomes a separate statement after a colon.
:::

::: exercise Translate with shifts {level=3}
Translate into Chinese, then into English again from your Chinese without looking at the original, and compare. Which shifts were reversed, and which were not? *The proposal was rejected by a large majority, which came as no surprise to those who had followed the debate.*
::: solution
A Chinese model: 这项提案以压倒性多数被否决了，一直关注这场辩论的人对此并不感到意外。 (or 提案遭到绝大多数人反对，未获通过……) A back-translation: *The proposal was voted down by an overwhelming majority, and people who had been following the debate were not surprised.* Shifts: the passive was kept (被否决, because rejection is adverse for the proposal) or turned into 遭到 + noun; *which came as no surprise to* (a sentential relative with an impersonal subject) became a clause with a personal subject (……的人对此并不感到意外) and was back-translated as a coordinated clause with *people* as subject — that shift was not reversed. *No surprise* (negative noun phrase) → 并不感到意外 (negative verb) → *were not surprised* (negative adjective).
