The student council of a university has asked its secretary, Nadia Rahman, to report on a meeting. Her first draft begins:

> *A decision was taken by the committee to postpone the implementation of the new timetable owing to the existence of concerns regarding the adequacy of the consultation with students.*

Nothing is wrong with this sentence: the grammar is impeccable and the register suitably formal. Yet most readers have to read it twice, and many will still be unsure who was worried and about what. Her second draft:

> *The committee decided to postpone the new timetable, because its members were worried that they had not consulted students enough.*

The second sentence says everything the first did, and the reader takes it in at once. The difference is not the vocabulary but the build. In the first draft, the people who acted are hidden in prepositional phrases or left out, and what they did has been turned into nouns: *a decision*, *the implementation*, *concerns*, *the consultation*; the only verb, *was taken*, means almost nothing. In the second, the people are the subjects and the verbs name what they did.

This lesson teaches the principle behind that revision, which Joseph M. Williams made the centre of his book *Style* (first edition 1981; twelfth edition, with Joseph Bizup, 2017): **readers find prose clear when the main characters of the story it tells are the subjects of its sentences, and their important actions are its verbs.** The principle gives a writer a way to diagnose a heavy sentence and a method for revising it, and it explains when nominalisations and the passive help a reader — questions that simple handbook rules answer badly. Reading dense nominal prose is the subject of [[academic-reading/dense-sentences]], and nominalisation in research writing of [[academic-writing/academic-style]].

## Stories, characters and actions

Almost every sentence of expository prose can be read as a small story: someone or something does something, or is in some state. The story has **characters** and **actions**; the grammar has **subjects** and **verbs**. Clear prose lines the two up.

::: definition Characters and actions {#def-characters-actions}
- The **characters** of a sentence are the people, groups, things or ideas that the sentence is about and that do or undergo the actions: *the committee*, *students*, *the river*, *inflation*, *this policy*.
- The **actions** are what the characters do, or the states they are in, expressed by meaning rather than grammar: *decide*, *consult*, *worry*, *rise*, *fail*, *be uncertain*.

Characters and actions belong to the story, subjects and verbs to the grammar. A sentence can put an action in a noun (*a decision*) or an adjective (*uncertain*), and a character in a prepositional phrase (*by the committee*) or nowhere at all.
:::

Compare three sentences that tell the same story:

- *There was a failure on the part of the city to respond to complaints from residents about the noise.*
- *The city's failure to respond to residents' complaints about the noise was noted.*
- *The city did not respond when residents complained about the noise.*

In the first, the subject is the empty *There* and the verb is *was*; the characters appear only after *on the part of* and *from*, and the actions are the nouns *failure* and *complaints*. In the second, the subject is a long noun phrase built round *failure*, and *was noted* adds a new action — noted by whom? In the third, each character is the subject of a clause and each action is a verb. It is the only one a reader understands without translating it back into a story.

::: theorem Characters as subjects, actions as verbs {#rule-characters-subjects}
In most expository prose, readers find a sentence clearer when:

1. its **main characters are the subjects** of its main clauses and of any subordinate clauses; and
2. the **important actions of those characters are verbs**, not nouns or adjectives.

A sentence that breaks both, held together by an empty verb such as *be*, *occur*, *take place* or *make*, is usually harder to read than it needs to be. This is advice about how readers respond, not a rule of grammar: every such sentence is grammatical, and some are the best choice (see [[#rule-useful-nominalisations]] and [[#rule-passive]]).
:::

::: proof
Williams and Bizup (*Style*, Lessons 3 and 4) base the principle on what readers do when they read: they look for a story in the sentence and expect the grammar to tell it in the order *who did what*. When the subject names the character and the verb names the action, the grammar does that work; when it does not, the reader must rebuild the story from the nouns, deciding for each one who acted and what was done. In *the implementation of the new timetable*, the reader has to supply someone who will implement it; in *concerns regarding the adequacy of the consultation*, the reader has to supply who was concerned, who consulted whom, and what "adequate" consultation would be. Each step is small, but they add up, and a reader who supplies the wrong character misunderstands. Steven Pinker makes a related point in *The Sense of Style* (2014, chapter 2): good expository writing directs the reader's attention to something happening in the world, and people doing things are easier to picture than abstractions related by *of*. The principle is a tendency, not a law — some main characters are abstract, and sometimes a writer has good reason to leave a character out — but in the drafts of most writers, native speakers as well as learners, the sentences readers find hard break it.
:::

## Diagnosing a sentence

Williams's principle turns a vague impression — *this sentence feels heavy* — into something a writer can check. Writers reread their own prose with its meaning already in mind; a mechanical diagnosis gets round that.

::: theorem A diagnostic for clarity {#rule-diagnosis}
To check a sentence or a paragraph:

1. **Underline the first seven or eight words** of each sentence. If you have to read more than that to reach the main verb, or if the underlined words are mostly nouns joined by *of*, *for*, *in* and *on*, look more closely.
2. **Find the subject of each verb.** Is it a character in the story — a person, a group, or the main concept the passage is about? Or is it an abstraction that is really an action (*the implementation*, *a reduction*), or an empty word (*there*, *it*)?
3. **Find the actions.** Look for nouns ending in *-tion*, *-ment*, *-ance*, *-ence*, *-ity*, *-al*, *-ure* and *-ing*, and for nouns with the same form as a verb (*a change*, *a need*, *a review*). Ask whether each names something a character does.
4. **Find the verbs.** Are they empty (*is*, *occur*, *exist*, *take place*, *make*, *give*, *have*), or do they carry the main action?

If the subjects are not characters and the actions are not verbs, revise ([[#rule-revision]]).
:::

::: proof
The procedure is adapted from the diagnostic tests in Williams and Bizup's *Style* (Lessons 3 and 4). It works because it looks at positions a writer can find without judging meaning: the subject comes early and the main verb soon after it. A sentence whose first eight words contain no verb usually has a long, noun-heavy subject, and that is usually a nominalisation with its characters attached by prepositions. The suffixes in step 3 ([[word-formation/suffixes]]) are only a clue: *nation* and *government* end in *-tion* and *-ment* and are characters, not actions. The real question is about meaning — *does this noun name something that someone does?* — and the suffixes tell you where to ask it.
:::

::: example Diagnosing a paragraph {#ex-diagnose}
Apply the diagnostic to this paragraph from a fictional report on a hospital's appointment system.

*The introduction of online booking by the hospital in 2024 was followed by a significant reduction in missed appointments. An explanation for this improvement may be the ability of patients to choose times that suit them. There is also a greater likelihood of cancellation in advance when a change of plans occurs.*
::: solution
1. **First seven or eight words.** *The introduction of online booking by the hospital in 2024* — ten words before the verb. *An explanation for this improvement may be* — the verb is *be*. *There is also a greater likelihood of* — an empty subject.
2. **Subjects.** *The introduction …*, *An explanation …*, *There*. None is a character. The characters of the story are *the hospital* and *patients*, and both are hidden in phrases (*by the hospital*, *of patients*).
3. **Actions.** *introduction* (introduce), *reduction* (reduce, or *fall*), *missed* (miss), *explanation* (explain), *ability* (be able, can), *choose* (already a verb), *likelihood* (be likely), *cancellation* (cancel), *change* (change).
4. **Verbs.** *was followed*, *may be*, *is*, *occurs* — all empty or nearly so, except *choose*.

**Revision**, with the characters as subjects and the actions as verbs:

> *After the hospital introduced online booking in 2024, patients missed far fewer appointments. One reason may be that patients can now choose times that suit them. They are also more likely to cancel in advance when their plans change.*

The revision is shorter, but that is a side effect: its gain is that every sentence starts with a character and moves at once to what the character did. The hedge *may* stays, because the report does not know why the improvement happened; *significant* became *far fewer*, because in a report it may be read as a claim about statistics.
:::
:::

## Nominalisations

::: definition Nominalisation {#def-nominalisation}
A **nominalisation** is a noun that expresses an action or a quality — something that could be said with a verb or an adjective: *decide* → *decision*, *fail* → *failure*, *move* → *movement*, *careful* → *care*, *likely* → *likelihood*, *able* → *ability*. Some have the same form as the verb (*a change*, *a review*, *a need*); some are *-ing* forms used as nouns (*the testing of the samples*). The grammar of nominalisation, and how to read it, is set out in [[academic-reading/dense-sentences#def-nominalisation]].
:::

Nominalisations are not mistakes, and some are indispensable. But they have a cost: a nominalisation does not need a character. *The decision to close the library* does not say who decided; *a reduction in staff* does not say who reduced it, or how. When the character matters and is missing, the reader has to guess; when it is present, it is usually pushed into a prepositional phrase (*by the council*), where it is harder to see as the one who acts.

Four patterns account for most of the nominalisations that make prose heavy:

| pattern | draft | revision |
|---|---|---|
| a nominalisation as subject, with an empty verb | *The cancellation of the concert took place on Friday.* | *The orchestra cancelled the concert on Friday.* |
| *there is* / *there was* + nominalisation | *There was a discussion of the plan.* | *The staff discussed the plan.* |
| a weak verb + nominalisation | *The team made a decision to carry out a review of the rules.* | *The team decided to review the rules.* |
| two or more nominalisations linked by prepositions | *the evaluation of the effectiveness of the reorganisation of the department* | *how well the department works now that it has been reorganised* |

The third pattern is the commonest in student writing and is covered in [[academic-writing/academic-style#rule-nominalise]]. The fourth is the hardest to read: the reader must work out how each pair of nouns is related, which a clause would have stated.

::: theorem Revising nominalisations {#rule-revision}
To revise a sentence whose actions are nominalisations:

1. **Find the characters** of the story — including those the sentence leaves out — and **the actions**, wherever they are.
2. **Make the main characters subjects** of clauses.
3. **Make their actions verbs** in those clauses.
4. **Connect the clauses** with words that state the logic the nominalisations left implicit: *because*, *when*, *if*, *although*, *after*, *how*, *whether*. A preposition such as *due to*, *following* or *in the event of* usually becomes a conjunction: *due to the failure of the system* → *because the system failed*.
5. **Read the result** to check that nothing has been lost or added. If a character was deliberately left out, decide whether it should stay out ([[#rule-passive]]).
:::

::: proof
Turning a clause into a noun phrase removes its subject (step 2 puts it back), its tense and finite verb (step 3), and the conjunction that linked it to the next clause (step 4). Step 4 is the one writers most often miss: they restore characters and verbs but join the clauses with *and*, losing the logic. Step 5 matters because *a reduction in costs* may be vague on purpose — the writer may not know who will reduce them — and a revision must not invent a character the writer cannot name.
:::

::: example Revising a sentence step by step {#ex-revise}
Revise: *Due to the unavailability of the lead researcher, a postponement of the presentation of the findings to the board was necessary.*
::: solution
1. **Characters and actions.** Characters: *the lead researcher*, *the board*, and someone who presents the findings and someone who postpones — probably the research team. Actions: *be unavailable*, *postpone*, *present*.
2. **Characters as subjects.** *the lead researcher*; *the team* (the writer must check that this is true; if the board postponed it, the board is the subject).
3. **Actions as verbs.** *was unavailable*; *postponed*; *present*.
4. **Connect.** *Due to* becomes *because*; *necessary* becomes *had to*.

> *Because the lead researcher was unavailable, the team had to postpone presenting its findings to the board.*

Or, with the main clause first and the reason as new information at the end:

> *The team had to postpone presenting its findings to the board, because the lead researcher was unavailable.*

The context decides the order: after a sentence about the researcher, the first; after one about the board meeting, the second ([[style-and-rhetoric/cohesion-emphasis]]). *Presenting* is a lighter kind of nominalisation than *presentation*: it takes an object without *of*.
:::
:::

### Nominalisations worth keeping

::: theorem Useful nominalisations {#rule-useful-nominalisations}
Keep a nominalisation, or create one, when:

1. **it refers back** to the previous sentence as a single idea, so that the new sentence can begin with it: *Prices rose by twelve per cent. **This rise** surprised economists.*
2. **it names a concept** that is a character of the passage in its own right — a technical term or the topic of the text: *Inflation reduces the value of savings.* *Evaporation cools the surface.*
3. **it is lighter than the alternative**, such as a *the fact that* clause: *the fact that she resigned surprised us* → *her resignation surprised us*.
4. **the character is unknown, obvious, or deliberately general**: *Education improves health* (by whom? by anyone).
:::

::: proof
Williams and Bizup list nominalisations of these kinds as ones to keep (*Style*, Lesson 3). The principle of this lesson is not *avoid nominalisations* but *make the main characters subjects*, and when a concept *is* the main character, the noun that names it should be the subject. Points 1 and 2 are also how arguments move forward: a finding stated as a clause becomes a noun that the next claim can build on ([[academic-writing/academic-style#rule-nominalise]]).
:::

::: warning Unpacking everything
✗ *When people inflate things in an economy, the money that people have saved becomes worth less.*
✓ *Inflation reduces the value of savings.*

A writer who has just learned to dislike nominalisations may unpack every one. *Inflation* is not a hidden action of identifiable characters; it is the character. If the reader needs to know who acted, make the doer the subject; if the concept is what the passage discusses, keep the noun.
:::

::: quiz
Which nominalisation is best **kept**?
- [ ] *The committee made a recommendation that the fees be lowered.*
- [ ] *There was an increase in the number of complaints after the change.*
- [x] *Air pollution shortens lives. This effect is largest in cities.*
- [ ] *The performance of an analysis of the data was carried out by the team.*
::: solution
*This effect* refers back to the previous sentence and makes it the subject of the next ([[#rule-useful-nominalisations]]). The others are better unpacked: *The committee recommended lowering the fees*; *More people complained after the change*; *The team analysed the data*.
:::
:::

## Abstract characters

Not every main character is a person. In a paragraph about a policy or a disease, the main character may be the policy or the disease, and the principle still applies.

::: theorem Choose the character the passage is about {#rule-abstract-characters}
1. Decide **who or what each passage is about**, and make that the subject of most of its sentences, whether it is a person or an abstraction.
2. When an abstraction is the main character, give it **verbs of action** where they are accurate: *The policy reduced waiting times* rather than *A reduction in waiting times occurred as a result of the policy*.
3. **Do not switch** the main character from sentence to sentence without a reason. A paragraph whose subjects are *the policy*, *waiting times*, *doctors*, *the reduction* and *there* is harder to follow than one whose subjects are mostly *the policy* and *doctors* ([[style-and-rhetoric/cohesion-emphasis#rule-topic-strings]]).
:::

::: proof
People are not better subjects than ideas; readers want the subject to be what the sentence is about. Treating an abstraction as a character that acts is a mild metaphor accepted in every register, from news (*Prices soared*) to science (*The enzyme breaks down the protein*). Williams calls the sequence of subjects in a paragraph its **topic string**; a consistent one tells the reader what the paragraph is about without any signpost.
:::

::: example An abstract character {#ex-abstract}
Revise this paragraph so that the main character is the subject of most of its sentences. *Water scarcity is a growing problem in many regions. Pressure on rivers and aquifers is caused by the expansion of irrigated farming. In addition, there is a contribution to scarcity from the growth of cities. Wastage of water through leaking pipes is also common.*
::: solution
1. **What is the paragraph about?** The causes of water scarcity: farming, cities and leaks. Each original sentence has a different subject (*Water scarcity*, *Pressure*, *there*, *Wastage*), and every cause is a nominalisation.
2. **Choose the characters.** After announcing the causes, make each cause the subject of its own clause, with a verb of action.

> *Water scarcity is a growing problem in many regions, and it has several causes. Irrigated farming has expanded, putting pressure on rivers and aquifers; cities have grown and need more water; and in many places leaking pipes waste much of what is supplied.*

The three parallel clauses, separated by semicolons, read as the list of *several causes* that the first sentence announced.
:::
:::

## The passive

The passive moves the doer of an action out of subject position, or leaves it out: *The council closed the library* → *The library was closed (by the council)*. Handbooks have long been hostile to it. *The Elements of Style* tells writers to *Use the active voice* (rule 10 in William Strunk's privately printed edition of 1918; rule 14 in E. B. White's later editions), and George Orwell's essay "Politics and the English Language" (1946) gave as its fourth rule that a writer should never use the passive where the active would do. Stated as a rule, such advice is too simple.

::: definition Passive {#def-passive}
A **passive** clause has a form of *be* (or *get*) followed by a past participle, and its subject corresponds to the object of the active verb: *The samples **were tested**.* *The bridge **is being repaired**.* *He **got arrested**.* The doer, if it appears at all, follows *by*. *There were many problems* and *It is important to …* are not passives. The grammar of the passive is taught in [[verb-patterns/the-passive]].
:::

::: theorem Choosing active or passive {#rule-passive}
Choose the voice that makes the **right character the subject**:

1. Use the **active** when the doer is a main character the reader needs to know about: ✓ *The landlord raised the rent* rather than ? *The rent was raised*, if the landlord's responsibility matters.
2. Use the **passive** when the doer is **unknown, obvious or unimportant**: *The museum was built in 1880.* *The suspect was arrested last night.*
3. Use the **passive** to keep the **same subject** across sentences, so that the paragraph keeps its topic: *The bridge opened in 1932. It was designed by …; it was repaired in 1990 …*
4. Use the **passive** to put **new or long information at the end** of the sentence, where readers expect it: *The prize was won by a student who had entered the competition only a week before.*
5. Do **not** use the passive to hide a responsible doer the reader is entitled to know: ✗ *Mistakes were made* when the writer made them.
:::

::: proof
Corpus grammars show that the passive is normal English, far more frequent in academic prose than in conversation (Biber et al., *Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English*, 1999), for the reasons in points 2–4: in science the doer is usually the researcher, and the topic is the material studied. Points 3 and 4 tie the passive to the order of information ([[style-and-rhetoric/cohesion-emphasis]]). The handbook rule treats voice as a matter of vigour rather than of what the sentence is about, and it is hard to apply, because many writers cannot tell a passive from other sentences. In "50 Years of Stupid Grammar Advice" (*The Chronicle of Higher Education*, 2009), the linguist Geoffrey K. Pullum pointed out that most of the examples offered as passives in the relevant section of *The Elements of Style* are not passive at all — they contain *there were*, *was sorry* or *became impaired*, but no passive verb — and that the book's own prose uses passives where they suit it. Point 5 is the real objection, and it is moral as much as stylistic: *Mistakes were made* can be grammar used to avoid saying who is responsible, which Williams and Bizup treat under the ethics of style.
:::

::: quiz
Which of these sentences is a **passive**?
- [ ] *There were several broken windows in the building.*
- [ ] *The results became clear after the second test.*
- [x] *The windows were broken during the storm.*
- [ ] *It is important to check the results twice.*
::: solution
Only *were broken* is *be* + past participle with a subject that would be the object of an active verb (*The storm broke the windows*). The first has *there were* and *broken* as an adjective; the second *became* + adjective; the fourth an anticipatory *it*.
:::
:::

::: example Active or passive in a paragraph {#ex-voice}
A fictional local history leaflet reads: *The old market hall stands at the centre of the town. Local merchants built it in 1764. The town council restored it in 2005, and today a weekly farmers' market fills it.* Which sentences would be better in the passive, and why?
::: solution
1. **What is the paragraph about?** The market hall, so it should be the subject wherever possible.
2. **Sentence 2.** *Local merchants built it* switches the subject to people who appear nowhere else. The passive keeps the hall as subject and puts the builders, new information, at the end.
3. **Sentence 3.** The same for *restored*; the council may not matter to the reader and can go. For the last clause, neither *a weekly farmers' market fills it* nor its passive is natural; an active verb with the hall as subject is best: *it holds*.

> *The old market hall stands at the centre of the town. It was built in 1764 by local merchants and restored in 2005, and today it holds a weekly farmers' market.*

The passives make the paragraph clearer because its main character is not the one who acted.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **进行 and the weak-verb pattern.** Chinese often combines a light verb with a verbal noun: 进行研究, 进行讨论, 加以分析, 作出决定. Translated word for word, these give *carry out research on*, *conduct a discussion of*, *make a decision* — grammatical, but heavier than *research*, *discuss*, *decide*. After *carry out*, *conduct*, *make* or *perform* + nominalisation, try the plain verb first.
- **"English prefers nouns."** Comparative studies contrast a "static" English, rich in nouns, with a "dynamic" Chinese, rich in verbs (连淑能《英汉对比研究》). The contrast is real for formal written English, but it does not mean that the more nouns a sentence has, the better its English. Skilled writers use nominalisations where they help ([[#rule-useful-nominalisations]]) and verbs elsewhere — closer to Chinese habits than learners often expect.
- **Topic and subject.** A Chinese topic need not be the doer: 这件事我们已经讨论过了. English makes the doer the subject (*We have already discussed this*) or, to put the topic first, uses the passive (*This has already been discussed*). Choose by asking which character the paragraph is about ([[#rule-abstract-characters]]).
- **被 and the English passive.** The Chinese 被 passive has traditionally been used mainly for events that are unfortunate for the subject (他被批评了), while English uses the passive for any event (Li & Thompson, *Mandarin Chinese*, 1981). The English criterion is not whether the event is unfortunate but which character the sentence is about.
:::

::: history
The advice is old; its explanation is recent. Strunk's *The Elements of Style* (1918) told students to *Use the active voice* and to *Use definite, specific, concrete language*, and Orwell (1946) and Sir Ernest Gowers's *Plain Words* (1948) gave similar advice to journalists and civil servants, without explaining why some sentences are hard. The explanation came from linguistics and composition research. M. A. K. Halliday described nominalisation as **grammatical metaphor** — a process expressed as if it were a thing — and traced its growth with the language of science (Halliday and Martin, *Writing Science*, 1993). Joseph M. Williams, at the University of Chicago, turned the idea round for writers: *Style: Ten Lessons in Clarity and Grace* (1981) explained clarity as the match between the characters and actions of a story and the subjects and verbs of a sentence, and gave diagnostic tests in place of rules. Later editions, with Gregory G. Colomb and, after Williams's death in 2008, with Joseph Bizup, are still widely taught. Helen Sword has since called heavy nominalisations "zombie nouns" (in an article in *The New York Times*, 2012), and Steven Pinker's *The Sense of Style* (2014) has explained the same preferences in terms of how readers process sentences.
:::

## Where this leads

[[style-and-rhetoric/concision]] cuts what these revisions leave behind: empty phrases, redundant pairs and unnecessary hedges. [[style-and-rhetoric/cohesion-emphasis]] develops topic strings and the use of the passive and nominalisations to put old information first. Nominalisation in research writing is treated in [[academic-writing/academic-style]], and this lesson's diagnostic is the first pass of [[style-and-rhetoric/editing-for-style]].

::: summary
- A sentence tells a story of characters and actions; readers find it clear when the main characters are subjects and their actions are verbs ([[#def-characters-actions]], [[#rule-characters-subjects]]).
- Diagnose a sentence by its first seven or eight words, its subjects, actions and verbs; noun-heavy openings and empty subjects and verbs are warning signs ([[#rule-diagnosis]]).
- Revise a nominalisation by finding the characters, making them subjects, making their actions verbs and joining the clauses with conjunctions that state the logic (*because*, *if*, *although*) ([[#rule-revision]]).
- Keep nominalisations that refer back, name the concept a passage is about, replace *the fact that*, or stand for an unknown or general doer ([[#rule-useful-nominalisations]]).
- An abstraction can be the main character; make it the subject and keep it there ([[#rule-abstract-characters]]).
- Choose active or passive by which character should be the subject; the passive is right when the doer is unknown or unimportant, to keep the topic, or to put new information last — but not to hide responsibility ([[#rule-passive]]).
:::

::: exercise Passive or not? {level=1}
Which of these sentences are passive? (a) *The doors were locked at ten.* (b) *There were no tickets left.* (c) *The town has changed a great deal.* (d) *Several homes were damaged by the flood.* (e) *She seemed tired after the journey.* (f) *The report is being written now.*
::: solution
Passive: (a), (d), (f) — each has *be* + past participle with a subject that would be the object of an active verb (*Someone locked the doors*; *The flood damaged several homes*; *Someone is writing the report*). Not passive: (b) *there were* + noun; (c) active present perfect of an intransitive verb; (e) *seemed* + adjective.
:::
:::

::: exercise Unpack the nominalisations {level=2}
Revise each sentence so that the characters are subjects and the actions are verbs. (a) *The committee's recommendation was for an extension of the deadline.* (b) *Our expectation is that there will be an improvement in sales following the launch of the new website.* (c) *The occurrence of errors was due to the inexperience of the staff with the new software.* (d) *A failure to provide an explanation of the delay resulted in dissatisfaction among customers.*
::: solution
Possible answers:

(a) *The committee recommended extending the deadline.*

(b) *We expect sales to improve after we launch the new website.* (or *… once the new website is launched*, if the launch is not the writers' own action)

(c) *The staff made errors because they had little experience with the new software.* (or *… because they were new to the software*)

(d) *Customers were dissatisfied because no one explained the delay* — or, if the writer knows who should have, *… because the company did not explain the delay*. The revision must choose a subject, and should choose the true one.
:::
:::

::: exercise Keep or unpack? {level=2}
In each pair of sentences, decide whether the underlined nominalisation should be kept or unpacked, and give your reason. (a) *Tuition fees doubled in five years. **This increase** has deterred many applicants.* (b) ***The provision of feedback** to students by tutors should take place within two weeks.* (c) ***Photosynthesis** converts light energy into chemical energy.* (d) *There was **a refusal** by the landlord **to carry out** repairs.*
::: solution
(a) **Keep**: *This increase* refers back to the previous sentence and makes it the subject of this one.

(b) **Unpack**: the characters (*tutors*, *students*) are hidden and the verb *take place* is empty. ✓ *Tutors should give students feedback within two weeks.*

(c) **Keep**: *photosynthesis* is the technical term for the concept the sentence is about; it is the main character.

(d) **Unpack**: *There was* is empty, and the landlord, the main character, is hidden. ✓ *The landlord refused to carry out repairs* (or *… refused to repair the flat*).
:::

::: exercise Choose the voice {level=2}
Rewrite this paragraph so that its main character, the vaccine, is the subject of each sentence, using the passive where needed. *Researchers at a university developed the vaccine in 2019. Regulators approved it two years later. Since then, clinics have given it to over a million people, and it has prevented thousands of hospital admissions.*
::: hint
Which sentences already have the vaccine as subject? For the others, which doers matter to the reader?
:::
::: solution
A model:

> *The vaccine was developed in 2019 by researchers at a university and approved by regulators two years later. Since then it has been given to over a million people, and it has prevented thousands of hospital admissions.*

The passives keep the vaccine as subject, move the researchers and regulators to the end as new information, and drop the obvious *clinics*.

::: exercise Revise a paragraph {level=3}
Revise this paragraph (from a fictional email to staff) for clarity. Use the diagnostic of [[#rule-diagnosis]], and explain your main changes.

*With regard to the matter of the relocation of the office, a decision has been reached by senior management that implementation will be deferred until the completion of the refurbishment of the new building. Notification of staff regarding the revised timetable will take place once there is confirmation from the contractors of a completion date. The provision of parking for staff at the new site is still under consideration.*
::: hint
The characters are senior management, the contractors and the staff (and the reader is one of the staff). What does each of them do?
:::
::: solution
A model:

> *Senior management has decided not to move the office until the new building has been refurbished. We will tell you the new timetable as soon as the contractors confirm when they will finish. We are still considering whether we can provide staff parking at the new site.*

Changes:

1. *With regard to the matter of the relocation of the office* — eleven words before anything happens — is cut.
2. Characters as subjects (*Senior management*, *We*, *the contractors*); actions as verbs (*decided*, *move*, *tell*, *confirm*, *finish*, *considering*, *provide*). *has been refurbished* is a passive because who refurbishes does not matter.
3. Prepositions became conjunctions: *until the completion of* → *until*; *once there is confirmation* → *as soon as … confirm*.
4. An email to staff can address its readers as *you*.
:::

::: exercise Write two versions {level=3}
Write a short paragraph (60–90 words) explaining a change at your school, university or workplace — a new rule, a new building, a new system — in a style full of nominalisations and empty verbs. Then revise it according to the principles of this lesson. Finally, write one or two sentences explaining which nominalisations you kept in the revision and why.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

**Draft.** *The introduction of a new system for the borrowing of laptops by students took place in September. The aim of the change was a reduction in the loss of equipment. Registration of each loan is now required, and there is an obligation for the return of laptops within three days. Initial results show a decrease in losses.*

**Revision.** *In September the library introduced a new system for lending laptops to students. It wanted to lose fewer of them. Students now register each loan and must return the laptop within three days. Since the change, fewer laptops have gone missing.*

**Kept.** *the change* refers back to the whole new system; *a new system* names the concept the paragraph is about. Check your own revision: does each sentence start with a character, and is each important action a verb?
