Here are two versions of a paragraph from a fictional town newsletter. They contain the same information, in sentences of about the same length.

> **A.** *Volunteers cleaned the River Lea last spring. Over two tonnes of rubbish were collected by them in three weekends. Shopping trolleys, tyres and a sofa were among the rubbish. Fish have returned to the stretch below the old mill since the river was cleaned.*

> **B.** *Last spring, volunteers cleaned the River Lea. In three weekends they collected over two tonnes of rubbish, including shopping trolleys, tyres and a sofa. Since the clean-up, fish have returned to the stretch below the old mill.*

Most readers find B easier to follow, and the reason is not in the words — B uses almost the same ones — but in their order. In A, the second sentence starts with *Over two tonnes of rubbish*, which the reader has not heard of, and ends with *by them*, which the reader knows already; the third starts with a list of new objects and ends with *the rubbish*, which is old; the fourth starts with new *fish* and ends with the clean-up, which is old again. Each sentence makes the reader hold something unfamiliar in mind until the end explains where it fits. In B, each sentence begins with something the reader has just met (*volunteers*, *they*, *the clean-up*) and ends with something new — and the most important new fact of the paragraph, that the fish came back, comes at the very end.

This lesson is about these two principles: put **familiar information first**, and put **new and important information last**. They are the foundation of what Joseph Williams calls cohesion and emphasis (*Style*, Lessons 5 and 6), and they explain many choices that look like matters of taste — when to use the passive, when *there is* is right, why a sentence that ends with *in many cases* feels weak. The basic idea of given and new information is introduced in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]] and its grammar in [[complex-sentences/emphasis-inversion#def-focus]]; the structures that move information about (clefts, fronting, inversion) are taught in [[complex-sentences/emphasis-inversion]]. Here the unit is the sentence in its paragraph, and the question is how a writer chooses.

## Old before new

::: definition Old and new information {#def-old-new}
**Old** (or **given**) information is what the reader already knows, or can easily connect to what they know: something mentioned in the previous sentence or two, something referred to by a pronoun or *this* + noun, something the context makes obvious. **New** information is what the sentence adds. Most sentences contain both, and English tends to put the old before the new.
:::

::: theorem Begin with the familiar {#rule-old-new}
1. **Begin a sentence with information the reader already has** — usually something from the end of the previous sentence, or the topic of the paragraph.
2. **End it with information the reader does not have.**
3. When a sentence has to introduce something entirely new, **prepare for it**: introduce it at the end of the previous sentence, or use a construction that puts it at the end (*There is …*, the passive, a cleft).
:::

::: proof
Williams and Bizup present this as the principle of **cohesion**: readers judge a sequence of sentences to "flow" when each one begins with something that connects it to what came before (*Style*, Lesson 5). Linguists of the Prague School described the same tendency in many languages in the 1920s and 1930s, as the movement in a sentence from *theme* to *rheme*, and Halliday (1967) gave the analysis of given and new information that most English grammars now follow. The reason is in how reading works. A reader who meets a familiar phrase at the start of a sentence can attach the sentence at once to what they already understand; a reader who meets an unfamiliar one must hold it in memory, without knowing where it fits, until the rest of the sentence explains. That is a small cost for one sentence, but a paragraph in which every sentence imposes it feels disjointed even when every sentence is clear on its own. Notice that this principle concerns the reader's knowledge, not the writer's: what is old to a writer who knows the subject well may be new to the reader.
:::

::: example Reordering for cohesion {#ex-reorder}
Revise the second and third sentences so that each begins with old information. *Many cities are now planting trees along their streets. Lower temperatures in summer are one benefit of street trees. Several degrees of cooling were measured on tree-lined streets compared with bare ones in a study in one fictional city.*
::: solution
1. **What is old at the start of sentence 2?** *street trees* (from sentence 1). The sentence begins instead with the new *Lower temperatures in summer*. Move *street trees* forward: *One benefit of street trees is lower temperatures in summer.* Now the sentence ends with the new information, which sentence 3 takes up.
2. **Sentence 3** begins with *Several degrees of cooling* — new and specific — and ends with the study, also new. Begin with what connects to *lower temperatures*: the study that measured them. *In one fictional city, a study found that tree-lined streets were several degrees cooler than bare ones.* (Or begin with *Tree-lined streets*, which connects to *street trees*.)

> *Many cities are now planting trees along their streets. One benefit of street trees is lower temperatures in summer: in one city, a study found that tree-lined streets were several degrees cooler than bare ones.*

The revision runs as a chain: *trees along their streets* → *street trees … lower temperatures* → *a study found* → *several degrees cooler*. (The study is fictional; a real text would cite one.)
:::
:::

## Topics and topic strings

The beginning of a sentence has a second job besides linking. It tells the reader what the sentence is about.

::: definition Topic and topic string {#def-topic}
The **topic** of a sentence is what the sentence is about — in English usually the subject of the main clause, and almost always somewhere in its first few words. A **topic string** is the sequence of topics through a paragraph. Linguists use the term **theme** for the starting point of a clause (Halliday) and describe the ways a text moves from one theme to the next as **thematic progression** (Daneš 1974).
:::

Two patterns of topic string are common in clear prose:

- **constant topic**: the paragraph keeps the same topic, referred to by a pronoun, a repeated noun or a synonym: *The bridge opened in 1932. It was designed by … It carries …*
- **linear progression**: the new information at the end of one sentence becomes the topic of the next: *The city built a new **metro line**. **The line** connects the airport with the **main station**. **The station** …*

Most good paragraphs mix the two: they have a main topic that recurs, with short excursions along a chain.

::: theorem Keep a consistent topic string {#rule-topic-strings}
1. **Make the topics of a paragraph's sentences a short, consistent set** — the main characters and concepts of the paragraph — rather than a new topic in every sentence.
2. **Make the topic the subject** wherever possible, so that the reader finds it at the start of the sentence ([[style-and-rhetoric/clarity#rule-characters-subjects]]).
3. **Signal a change of topic** clearly, often with an introductory phrase that links it to what came before: *Of the three proposals, …*, *In rural areas, by contrast, …*.
4. **State the paragraph's main topics early** — in its first sentence or two — so that the reader knows what to expect.
:::

::: proof
Williams and Bizup give a test for this (*Style*, Lesson 5): underline the first seven or eight words of each sentence in a paragraph and read them as a list. If the list names a few related characters or concepts, the paragraph will feel focused; if it names a new thing each time, the paragraph will feel scattered, even if every sentence is clear. The test works because readers use the topics to build an idea of what the paragraph is about. Daneš's analysis of thematic progression (1974) showed that well-formed texts follow a small number of such patterns. This is also why the advice to vary vocabulary can harm cohesion: if the topic is *the bridge*, calling it *the structure*, *the crossing* and *the span* in successive sentences makes the reader wonder whether the topic has changed ([[essay-writing/signposting#rule-reference-chains]]).
:::

::: example Revising a topic string {#ex-topic-string}
Underline the topics of this fictional paragraph, then revise it so that the topic string is consistent. *Electric buses are being introduced in many cities. Lower running costs are their main advantage over diesel buses. However, a high price must be paid for each vehicle. Charging points at depots also have to be built by operators. Over the life of a bus, savings on fuel can outweigh these costs.*
::: solution
1. **Topics**: *Electric buses* — *Lower running costs* — *a high price* — *Charging points* — *savings on fuel*. Five topics in five sentences.
2. **What is the paragraph about?** Electric buses, and their costs and savings. Make *electric buses* (or *they*) the topic where possible, and let costs and savings come later in the sentence.

> *Electric buses are being introduced in many cities. Their main advantage over diesel buses is that they are cheaper to run. They are, however, expensive to buy, and operators must also build charging points at their depots. Over the life of a bus, though, the savings on fuel can outweigh these costs.*

Topics now: *Electric buses* — *Their main advantage* — *They* — (*operators*, in a second clause) — *Over the life of a bus … the savings*. The last sentence changes topic, but its introductory phrase links it to the buses and *these costs* points back to the previous sentence. The passive *a high price must be paid* has gone, because the buses, not the price, are the topic.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which second sentence continues the topic string best? *The Grand Canal of China is the longest artificial waterway in the world.*
- [ ] *Construction of its oldest sections began in the fifth century BC.*
- [x] *It was begun in the fifth century BC and extended over many centuries.*
- [ ] *Many centuries of work by different dynasties produced it.*
- [ ] *In the fifth century BC, workers began to dig its oldest sections.*
::: solution
*It* keeps the canal as the topic and subject, and the sentence ends with new information. The first makes *Construction* the topic; the third begins with new information and ends with the old *it*; the fourth makes *workers* the subject — grammatical, and acceptable in a passage about the builders, but not in one about the canal. (The traditional dating of the earliest sections is the fifth century BC; the canal was greatly extended under the Sui dynasty.)
:::
:::

## Emphasis: the stress position

The end of a sentence has a job too. In speech, the main stress of a clause usually falls on its last important word ([[connected-speech/sentence-stress]]), and readers seem to give the end of a written sentence the same weight.

::: definition Stress position {#def-stress-position}
The **stress position** is the end of a sentence (or of a clause ending in a semicolon or colon), where the reader naturally places emphasis. The terms **topic position** and **stress position** were made popular among writers by George Gopen and Judith Swan's article "The Science of Scientific Writing" (*American Scientist*, 1990); Williams and Bizup use the same idea (*Style*, Lesson 6).
:::

William Strunk Jr. had made the point in 1918, in the last rule of *The Elements of Style* (rule 18; rule 22 in White's editions), *Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end*:

> *The proper place in the sentence for the word, or group of words, which the writer desires to make most prominent is usually the end.*

::: theorem End with what matters {#rule-stress}
1. **Put the most important new information in the stress position**: the word or phrase the reader should remember, or the one the next sentence will develop.
2. **Trim the end of the sentence**: do not let it trail off into hedges, afterthoughts, references or metadiscourse (*… in many cases*, *… according to the report*, *… as mentioned above*, *… in the modern world*). Move them earlier or cut them.
3. **Put long and complex elements at the end** (end-weight): a long subject or a long list in the middle of a sentence is harder to process than the same material at its end ([[complex-sentences/clause-structure]]).
4. **Make the last sentence of a paragraph the stress position of the paragraph**: end it with the point you want the reader to take into the next.
:::

::: proof
Gopen and Swan's argument was addressed to scientists: a reader, they said, will treat whatever stands at the end of a sentence as what the writer wants emphasised, so a writer who puts the important finding in the middle and a citation at the end has told the reader that the citation matters most. The same expectation explains why ending on a hedge weakens a sentence. *Fees should be lowered, at least in some cases* leaves the reader with *some cases*; *In some cases, at least, fees should be lowered* makes the same qualification and ends on the claim. Point 3 is the written counterpart of a tendency of spoken English: long elements come last because the listener can process them once the structure of the clause is clear. Williams and Bizup add that the principles of topic and stress work together: the topic position links a sentence to what came before, the stress position prepares for what comes next, and a paragraph whose sentences use both runs as a chain.
:::

::: example Revising the end of sentences {#ex-stress}
Revise each sentence so that it ends with its most important information. (a) *The company lost forty per cent of its customers in a single year, according to its annual report.* (b) *Doctors should ask about sleep, which is as important to health as diet and exercise, at every routine check-up.* (c) *Students who read for pleasure write better, it seems, in most of the studies we have reviewed.*
::: solution
(a) The source has the stress position. Move it forward: *According to its annual report, the company lost forty per cent of its customers in a single year.* The sentence now ends on *in a single year*, the striking part.

(b) Two candidates for the end: *as important as diet and exercise* (the reason) and *at every routine check-up* (the recommendation). Decide which the paragraph is about. For a paragraph that argues for asking: *At every routine check-up, doctors should ask about sleep, which is as important to health as diet and exercise.* For one about how often to ask: *Doctors should ask about sleep — which is as important to health as diet and exercise — at every routine check-up.*

(c) The sentence trails off into a hedge and a reference. ✓ *In most of the studies we reviewed, students who read for pleasure wrote better.* The hedge survives (*In most of the studies*) but no longer weakens the end.
:::
:::

::: warning Ending on the weak part
✗ *The new drug reduced symptoms by half, which is a significant finding in our opinion.*
✓ *In our view, the most significant finding is that the new drug halved symptoms.*

Sentences that end with an evaluation, a source or a hedge (*in our opinion*, *as shown above*, *to some extent*) put the least interesting element in the strongest position. Move these elements to the start, or into the middle, or cut them.
:::

## Tools for putting information in place

English has several structures that let a writer put old information first and new information last without changing the meaning. Most are taught as grammar elsewhere; here is when a writer reaches for them.

::: theorem Choosing a structure for order {#rule-tools}
| to … | use … | example |
|---|---|---|
| put the doer (new) at the end and the affected (old) first | the **passive** | *The bridge was designed by an engineer who had never built one before.* |
| introduce something new without beginning with it | ***there* + *be*** | *There is one exception to this rule.* |
| move a long clause subject to the end | ***it* + *be* … *that*** (extraposition) | *It is surprising that so few people applied.* |
| give one element special focus | a **cleft** ([[complex-sentences/emphasis-inversion#def-it-cleft]]) | *What the survey shows is that most residents want the lanes.* |
| link to the previous sentence before a new subject | an **introductory phrase** | *Of these causes, the most important is cost.* |
| comment on a whole clause at its end | a **summative modifier** (noun + relative clause) | *Fares rose by a third, **an increase that** drove many passengers away.* |
| add a second point about the last word | a **resumptive modifier** (repeat a key word) | *The plan depends on volunteers, **volunteers who** may not stay.* |
| reduce the load of one sentence | **two sentences** | — |

Use each where it puts the right information first and the right information last; do not use them for their own sake.
:::

::: proof
The first three structures exist largely to manage the order of information. Biber et al. (*Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English*, 1999) show from corpus data that the *by*-phrase is left out of most passives, so that the passive mostly serves to keep the affected participant as subject (topic); when a *by*-phrase is used, the doer is usually new information, often a long phrase, placed at the end. *There* + *be* lets a writer avoid beginning with an indefinite new noun (*A problem exists* sounds odd; *There is a problem* does not). Summative and resumptive modifiers are Williams's terms (*Style*, Lesson 10); they let a sentence that would otherwise end weakly gather what it has said into a noun and extend it, which keeps both the topic and the stress positions full. Clefts are powerful but marked: a text with a cleft in every paragraph sounds insistent, which is why the grammar lesson treats them as structures of contrast.
:::

::: example Using the tools {#ex-tools}
Join or revise each pair so that old information comes first and the important new information last, choosing a structure from [[#rule-tools]]. (a) *The council cut the bus service by half. This cut left villages without transport.* (b) *A man who had never written a novel before won the prize.* (In a paragraph about the prize.) (c) *That the plan has so little support is surprising.*
::: solution
(a) Summative modifier: *The council cut the bus service by half, a decision that left many villages without transport.* The second clause now continues from the first without a new sentence, and ends on the result.

(b) Passive, to keep the prize as topic and put the long new phrase last: *The prize was won by a man who had never written a novel before.*

(c) Extraposition: *It is surprising that the plan has so little support.* The heavy clause moves to the end, and the sentence ends on the point.
:::
:::

::: quiz
In a paragraph about a museum, which sentence puts old information first and new information last?
- [ ] *A local merchant who had made his fortune in tea founded the museum in 1880.*
- [x] *The museum was founded in 1880 by a local merchant who had made his fortune in tea.*
- [ ] *In 1880, by a local merchant who had made his fortune in tea, the museum was founded.*
- [ ] *It was a local merchant who had made his fortune in tea that founded the museum in 1880.*
::: solution
The passive keeps *the museum* (the topic) first and puts the long new phrase about the merchant last. The first begins with new information; the third is ungrammatical in ordinary prose; the fourth is a cleft that contrasts the merchant with other possible founders, which nothing in the context requires.
:::
:::

### When principles conflict

Sometimes old-before-new conflicts with the principle of [[style-and-rhetoric/clarity]] that characters should be subjects: the old information is the thing affected, and the character who acted is new. Williams and Bizup advise that in such cases cohesion should usually win, which is the main reason the passive exists (*Style*, Lesson 5). In a paragraph about a painting, *The painting was bought in 1920 by a collector in Shanghai* is better than *A collector in Shanghai bought the painting in 1920*, even though the collector is the doer.

::: note English and Chinese
- **Old before new in Chinese.** Chinese also tends to put known information before new, and in some cases the position of a noun is what tells the hearer whether it is known. Li and Thompson (*Mandarin Chinese*, 1981) note that a noun before the verb tends to be understood as definite and one after it as indefinite: 客人来了 ‘the guests have come’ (guests we were expecting) but 来客人了 ‘some guests have come’. English marks the same difference with articles and with *there*: *The guests have arrived* / *There are some guests here*.
- **Topic first.** Chinese is often described as topic-prominent: the topic comes first even when it is not the subject (这本书我看过; [[complex-sentences/emphasis-inversion]]). English cannot put an object first so freely, and uses the passive, *as for*, or a change of subject instead. The aim is the same; the means differ.
- **有 and *there is*.** Chinese introduces new referents with 有 (有一个人在门口等你), and English with *there* + *be* (*There's someone at the door for you*). Learners who avoid *there* write ✗ *A man is waiting for you at the door* in contexts where it sounds abrupt, or translate 有 as *have* (✗ *Have a man at the door*).
- **把 and the end of the sentence.** The 把 construction moves a known object before the verb, leaving the verb and its result at the end (我把作业做完了): Chinese, too, uses word order to give the end of the clause its weight.
:::

::: history
The idea that a sentence moves from known to new is old in rhetoric — Strunk's rule about putting emphatic words last (1918) has many predecessors — but its systematic study began in Prague. In the 1920s and 1930s Vilém Mathesius described the "actual division" of the sentence into its starting point and what is said about it; his successors, notably Jan Firbas and František Daneš, developed this into the theory of **functional sentence perspective**, and Daneš's paper on thematic progression (1974) described how texts chain their themes. M. A. K. Halliday's "Notes on transitivity and theme in English" (1967–68) separated the *theme* (the starting point of the clause) from *given* information (what the listener knows), two notions that usually coincide but need not. For writers, the decisive translations came from American composition teaching: Joseph Williams's *Style* (1981) and George Gopen and Judith Swan's "The Science of Scientific Writing" (1990) turned these ideas into advice about topic and stress positions that is now taught in many writing courses.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Old or new? {level=1}
In each second sentence, mark which part is old information and which is new. (a) *We visited a small museum. The museum had a collection of old clocks.* (b) *The project needed funding. A local charity provided it.* (c) *Rents rose sharply last year. This rise forced many families to move.*
::: solution
(a) Old: *The museum*; new: *had a collection of old clocks*. (b) Old: *it* (the funding); new: *A local charity provided* — the new information comes first, so a better order is *It was provided by a local charity.* (c) Old: *This rise*; new: *forced many families to move*.
:::

::: exercise Choose the second sentence {level=1}
Which second sentence follows better, and why? *The school has opened a new library.* (a) *Ten thousand books and thirty computers are in it.* (b) *It has ten thousand books and thirty computers.*
::: solution
(b): it begins with *It* (the library, old) and ends with the new information. (a) begins with new information and ends with the old *in it*.
:::

::: exercise Trim the end {level=1}
Move or cut the weak ending of each sentence. (a) *The policy has reduced crime, according to the police.* (b) *Most students passed the exam, as can be seen in Table 3.* (c) *Water use has fallen by a fifth, which is very interesting.*
::: solution
(a) *According to the police, the policy has reduced crime.* (b) *Most students passed the exam (Table 3).* or *As Table 3 shows, most students passed the exam.* (c) *Water use has fallen by a fifth.* — if the fall is interesting, the next sentence should say why.
:::

::: exercise Topic strings {level=2}
List the topics of this paragraph, then revise it so that it has a consistent topic string. *The bicycle was invented in the nineteenth century. Pedals were added to the front wheel in the 1860s. In the 1880s, a chain drive to the rear wheel and wheels of equal size were introduced, producing the "safety bicycle". Today's bicycles are still based on this design.*
::: solution
Topics: *The bicycle* — *Pedals* — (*In the 1880s*) *a chain drive … and wheels* — *Today's bicycles*. A revision with *the bicycle* as constant topic:

> *The bicycle was invented in the nineteenth century. In the 1860s it was given pedals on the front wheel, and in the 1880s it acquired a chain drive to the rear wheel and wheels of equal size — the "safety bicycle", on whose design today's bicycles are still based.*

The time phrases (*In the 1860s*, *in the 1880s*) are allowed before the topic because they link the sentences in a chronological sequence.
:::

::: exercise Use a summative modifier {level=2}
Join each pair with a summative modifier. (a) *The library extended its opening hours to midnight. This change was popular with students.* (b) *Prices fell for six months in a row. This had not happened for twenty years.*
::: solution
(a) *The library extended its opening hours to midnight, a change that was popular with students.* (b) *Prices fell for six months in a row, a decline not seen for twenty years.* (or *… something that had not happened for twenty years*)
:::

::: exercise The right structure {level=2}
Rewrite each sentence so that its end carries the new information, using the passive, *there* + *be* or extraposition. (a) *A serious problem with the plan exists.* (b) *That the results were so different from last year's is not surprising.* (c) In a paragraph about the station: *Architects from three countries designed the station.*
::: solution
(a) *There is a serious problem with the plan.* (b) *It is not surprising that the results were so different from last year's.* (c) *The station was designed by architects from three countries.*
:::

::: exercise Revise for flow {level=3}
Revise this fictional paragraph so that each sentence begins with old information and ends with new, the topic string is consistent, and the paragraph ends on its main point. Explain two of your changes.

*Night trains are returning to Europe. Concern about the carbon emissions of flights explains this, according to operators. Passengers can sleep while travelling and arrive in a city centre, which are advantages. However, sleeping cars cost more to run than ordinary carriages, and lower prices are offered by airlines on many routes. Whether night trains can compete without public support is therefore uncertain, in the view of many analysts.*
::: hint
What is the paragraph about? Which sentences end with a source or a judgement?
:::
::: solution
A model:

> *Night trains are returning to Europe. Operators say that the main reason is concern about the carbon emissions of flights. Night trains also let passengers sleep while they travel and arrive in a city centre. They are, however, expensive to run: sleeping cars cost more than ordinary carriages, and on many routes airlines charge less. Many analysts therefore doubt that night trains can compete without public support.*

Changes: (1) *according to operators* and *in the view of many analysts* were in the stress position; they now begin their sentences, and the sentences end on the reason and on *without public support*, the main point. (2) Sentence 3 began with *Passengers* and ended with *which are advantages*; it now has *Night trains* as topic and ends with the advantages themselves. (3) *lower prices are offered by airlines* was a passive that put the old *airlines* last; the active *airlines charge less* ends on the new comparison.
:::

::: exercise Write a chained paragraph {level=3}
Write a paragraph (80–120 words) explaining a process you know well — how tea is processed, how a bill becomes law, how a vaccine is tested — so that each sentence begins with something from the previous one. Then underline the first seven or eight words of each sentence and check the topic string.
::: solution
Answers will vary. Check that (1) each sentence after the first begins with a word or idea from the one before (a pronoun, a repeated noun, *this* + noun, or an introductory phrase that links); (2) the topics form a short, related set; (3) each sentence ends with new information, and the last sentence ends with the result or the main point; (4) nothing is ended with a hedge or a source. A model opening: *Green tea is made from leaves that are picked young. The leaves are heated soon after picking, a step that stops them from oxidising. This heating — by steaming in Japan, by pan-firing in much of China — gives each tea its character …*
:::
:::
