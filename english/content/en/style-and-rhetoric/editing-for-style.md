Here is a paragraph from the first draft of a fictional student report on a university's plan to replace printed exam papers with exams on laptops:

> *In relation to the issue of the introduction of computer-based examinations, it is important to note that there are a number of various advantages and disadvantages. The marking of scripts can be completed more quickly by examiners, and the legibility of answers is no longer a problem. However, concerns have been raised by students regarding the possibility of technical failures occurring during the exam. Moreover, it was found in a survey that was conducted by the students' union that a significant proportion of students, 41%, feel that their writing speed is slower when typing. It is therefore clear that the university should definitely carry out a pilot scheme before full implementation takes place.*

The paragraph is correct English, and its content is good: two advantages, two concerns with a source, and a sensible recommendation. But it is hard to read, for reasons that every lesson of this course has named. Its characters (examiners, students, the university) are hidden in phrases or passives; it opens with empty words; its subjects change in every sentence; it ends on *takes place*; it hedges where it should not and boosts where it should not (*definitely*). Here is the same paragraph after editing:

> *Computer-based exams would have clear advantages. Examiners could mark scripts faster, and no one would have to decipher bad handwriting. But students worry about two things. Many fear that a laptop might fail during an exam, and in a survey by the students' union, 41% said that they type more slowly than they write. The university should therefore try the new system in a pilot scheme before introducing it everywhere.*

This lesson is about how to get from the first version to the second — not by inspiration, but by a sequence of passes, each looking for one kind of problem. It brings together the principles of [[style-and-rhetoric/clarity]], [[style-and-rhetoric/concision]], [[style-and-rhetoric/cohesion-emphasis]], [[style-and-rhetoric/sentence-variety]], [[style-and-rhetoric/word-choice]] and [[style-and-rhetoric/readers-and-media]]. It assumes that the draft's structure and argument are already settled: revising an essay for argument and editing it for correctness are taught in [[essay-writing/revising-editing]] and [[sentences-and-paragraphs/editing]].

## Editing in passes

::: definition Editing for style {#def-editing-style}
**Editing for style** is the revision of a draft's sentences and words — not its argument or its grammar — so that readers can understand it easily and find it pleasant to read: characters as subjects and actions as verbs, old information before new, important information at the ends of sentences, no needless words, plain and precise vocabulary, and varied rhythm. It comes after revision for structure and before proofreading.
:::

::: theorem The passes of a style edit {#rule-passes}
Edit in this order, one concern at a time:

1. **Reader and purpose.** Remind yourself who the reader is, what they need to do and how they will read ([[style-and-rhetoric/readers-and-media#rule-reader]]).
2. **Topics.** Underline the first seven or eight words of each sentence in a paragraph; check that the topics form a short, consistent string ([[style-and-rhetoric/cohesion-emphasis#rule-topic-strings]]).
3. **Characters and actions.** Make the main characters subjects and their actions verbs; unpack nominalisations that hide them; choose active or passive by the topic ([[style-and-rhetoric/clarity#rule-revision]], [[style-and-rhetoric/clarity#rule-passive]]).
4. **Old and new, and the stress position.** Begin sentences with what the reader knows; end them with what matters ([[style-and-rhetoric/cohesion-emphasis#rule-stress]]).
5. **Concision.** Cut the six kinds of wordiness, stacked hedges and empty intensifiers ([[style-and-rhetoric/concision#rule-six-kinds]], [[style-and-rhetoric/concision#rule-hedges]]).
6. **Words.** Replace inflated words, vague nouns, unexplained jargon and clichés ([[style-and-rhetoric/word-choice]]).
7. **Rhythm.** Read aloud; vary the length and shape of sentences; check parallel structures ([[style-and-rhetoric/sentence-variety]]).
8. **Proofread** last, for slips that the editing itself may have introduced.
:::

::: proof
The order runs from the changes that affect most to those that affect least, for the reason that governs all revision: a later pass should not undo the work of an earlier one ([[essay-writing/revising-editing#rule-revise-first]]). Topics come before sentences because the topic string decides what the subject of each sentence should be. Characters and actions come before concision because restoring them removes many wordy patterns automatically — *the marking of scripts can be completed by examiners* becomes *examiners could mark scripts* — and what is left is easier to see. Word choice comes after concision because there is no point in choosing the best word for a phrase that will be cut. Rhythm comes near the end because it depends on the final shape of the sentences. Separate passes work because each needs a different kind of attention; a writer who reads for everything at once sees little ([[essay-writing/revising-editing#rule-edit-passes]]). The order is a default: experienced editors often fix small things as they go, but they still make the large passes in this order.
:::

## A worked edit

::: example Editing the exam paragraph, pass by pass {#ex-full-edit}
Edit the draft paragraph at the start of the lesson, following [[#rule-passes]].
::: solution
**Pass 1 — reader and purpose.** The readers are university staff who must decide whether to adopt computer-based exams. They need the advantages, the concerns and a recommendation, in a formal register.

**Pass 2 — topics.** The first words of each sentence: *In relation to the issue of the introduction* — *The marking of scripts* — *However, concerns* — *Moreover, it was found* — *It is therefore clear*. Five topics, none of them the main characters. The paragraph is about computer-based exams and the people they affect: examiners, students, the university.

**Pass 3 — characters and actions.**
- *The marking of scripts can be completed more quickly by examiners* → *Examiners could mark scripts faster.*
- *the legibility of answers is no longer a problem* → *no one would have to decipher bad handwriting* (the characters are the examiners; *legibility* hides them).
- *concerns have been raised by students regarding the possibility of technical failures occurring* → *students worry … a laptop might fail*.
- *it was found in a survey that was conducted by the students' union* → *in a survey by the students' union*.
- *carry out a pilot scheme before full implementation takes place* → *try the new system in a pilot scheme before introducing it everywhere*.

**Pass 4 — old and new, stress position.** The first sentence should announce the topic; *Computer-based exams would have clear advantages* does, and ends on *advantages*, which the next sentence develops. *But students worry about two things* links back to *advantages* by contrast and ends on *two things*, which the next sentence lists. The last sentence ends on *before introducing it everywhere* — the condition that is the point of the recommendation.

**Pass 5 — concision.** Cut *In relation to the issue of*, *it is important to note that*, *a number of various* (doubled), *It is therefore clear that*. Remove *definitely* (a boost the evidence does not support) and decide on hedges: *41%* is a fact from the survey, so *a significant proportion of students, 41%,* becomes *41%*; *significant* might be read as a statistical claim.

**Pass 6 — words.** *Legibility is no longer a problem* → *decipher bad handwriting* (concrete); *implementation* → *introducing it*. *Moreover* is wrong: the survey result is not an additional point but the second of the two concerns, joined by *and*.

**Pass 7 — rhythm.** Read aloud: the sentence lengths are now 6, 14, 6, 28 and 16 words — varied, with the short *But students worry about two things* marking the turn.

**Pass 8 — proofread.** Check *41%* (a figure stated as a percentage throughout), the apostrophe in *students' union*, and that the tense (*would*, *could*) is consistent for a proposal that has not yet been adopted.

The result is the edited paragraph above: 70 words against the draft's 113, but more important, a paragraph whose subjects are its characters and whose sentences run from what the reader knows to what the reader needs.
:::
:::

In which pass would you change *The decision was made by the board to close the branch* to *The board decided to close the branch*?
- [ ] topics
- [x] characters and actions
- [ ] rhythm
- [ ] proofreading
::: solution
The change makes the character (*the board*) the subject and the action (*decide*) the verb: pass 3. It also makes the sentence more concise, but concision is a side effect here.

::: example Why the order matters {#ex-order}
A writer edits this fictional sentence for concision first and gets: *Implementation of the scheme by the council led to a fall in complaints.* Then she applies pass 3. Original: *The implementation of the new parking scheme by the council in March has led to a noticeable decrease in the number of complaints received.* Show what pass 3 does, and explain why doing it first would have saved work.
::: solution
1. **Pass 3 on the original**: characters *the council* and *residents* (who complain); actions *introduce*, *complain*. ✓ *Since the council introduced the new parking scheme in March, residents have complained less.*
2. **What happened to the concision edit**: almost every word she cut or kept (*Implementation of*, *led to a fall in*) has been replaced anyway. Her first pass was wasted.
3. **Why**: unpacking a nominalisation removes most of the wordy phrases built around it — *the number of complaints received* becomes *complained*. Doing pass 3 first leaves less for pass 5 to do, and nothing to undo.
:::
:::

Which edit is a **zombie-rule** correction that a careful editor would not make?
- [ ] *There was an increase in costs* → *Costs increased*
- [x] *the problem we talked about* → *the problem about which we talked*
- [ ] *each and every applicant* → *every applicant*
- [ ] *at this point in time* → *now*
::: solution
Moving a final preposition forward is not a correction: *the problem we talked about* is standard and more natural ([[#rule-zombies]]). The other three are genuine improvements.

## Editing someone else's writing

Editing a colleague's text, a classmate's essay or a translation raises a question that editing your own does not: whose voice is it?

::: theorem Editing another writer's text {#rule-others}
1. **Edit for the reader, not for your taste**: change what makes the text harder to read or less accurate, not what is merely different from how you would write it.
2. **Keep the writer's meaning, register and voice**; if you are not sure what a sentence means, ask instead of guessing.
3. **Explain your main changes** — or, better, show a pattern with one example and let the writer revise the rest ([[essay-writing/revising-editing#rule-giving-feedback]]).
4. **Do not enforce zombie rules** ([[#rule-zombies]]).
5. **Respect the conventions the text must follow**: a house style, a journal's rules, a legal form of words.
:::

::: proof
An editor's authority comes from the reader: the editor reads the text as its readers will and changes what would make them stumble. Changes made for the editor's taste — a preferred synonym, a favourite sentence pattern — cost the writer effort and confidence and gain the reader nothing. Asking instead of guessing matters because an editor who misunderstands a sentence may "clarify" it into something false. Point 3 is how editing teaches: a writer who is shown why one nominalisation was unpacked can unpack the next ten, and a writer who sees only a heavily corrected text learns little. Publishers' house styles (point 5) exist so that matters of choice — *-ise* or *-ize*, serial comma or not — are settled once and applied consistently.
:::

### Zombie rules

::: theorem Do not "correct" these {#rule-zombies}
The following are not errors in Standard English, and an editor should not change them for that reason:

1. **a split infinitive**: *to fully understand*, *to more than double* — keep it when the alternative is awkward or ambiguous;
2. **a preposition at the end of a sentence**: *the person I spoke to*, *What is it for?*;
3. **a sentence beginning with *And* or *But***;
4. **singular *they*** for a person whose gender is unknown or not relevant: *Each student should bring their own laptop*;
5. ***which*** in a defining relative clause in British English (*the book which I bought*): American usage guides often prefer *that*, but both are standard;
6. **the passive**, where it puts the right character in subject position ([[style-and-rhetoric/clarity#rule-passive]]).

Each may be changed for a reason of style — rhythm, emphasis, a house style — but not as a correction.
:::

::: proof
Each of these "rules" has been traced to individual writers or teachers rather than to the usage of careful writers: the objection to final prepositions to John Dryden (1672), the split-infinitive objection to the nineteenth century, and so on ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/editing]]). Usage guides based on evidence — Fowler's *Modern English Usage* (1926) already called the preposition rule a superstition, and modern guides such as Swan's *Practical English Usage* and the *Merriam-Webster Dictionary of English Usage* (1989) document the facts — show that good writers in every period have done all six. Pullum's critique of *The Elements of Style* (2009) argued that handbooks which teach such rules make writers anxious about non-problems and distract them from real ones. Singular *they* has been used since the fourteenth century and is accepted by major style guides; APA style recommends it. An editor who "corrects" these features makes the text no better and may make it worse.
:::

::: warning Hypercorrection by editors
✗ *The figures to which she referred and upon which the report relied were those with which the committee had been provided.*
✓ *The report relied on the figures she referred to, which the committee had been given.*

An editor who moves every final preposition forward can produce sentences that are stiff and hard to read. The same is true of splitting no infinitive (✗ *to understand fully the risks* where *to fully understand the risks* is natural) and avoiding *they* at the cost of *he or she* in every sentence.
:::

::: example Editing a peer's paragraph {#ex-peer}
A classmate asks you to edit this paragraph from her fictional essay on public transport. Mark what you would change and why, and what you would leave alone. *And yet the problem is not the buses themselves. It is that nobody knows when they will come. A passenger who checks their phone sees a time which turns out to be wrong. What the city needs is not more buses but information people can rely on.*
::: solution
1. **Leave alone**: *And yet* at the start (a natural, emphatic link to the previous paragraph); *their* (singular *they*); *which* in a defining relative clause (standard in BrE); *rely on* at the end (a final preposition, natural here). None is an error.
2. **Consider**: the cleft *What the city needs is …* gives deliberate focus to the conclusion, and the antithesis *not more buses but information …* is effective — leave both.
3. **Possible change**: *A passenger who checks their phone sees a time which turns out to be wrong* is clear; a more concrete version might name the time (*sees "3 minutes" and waits twenty*), but that is a suggestion, not a correction.

The paragraph is well written. The most useful edit is to tell the writer so, and why: its topic string (*the problem*, *It*, *A passenger*, *What the city needs*) is consistent, and its last sentence ends on the point.
:::
:::

## Tools and their limits

::: theorem Using editing tools {#rule-tools}
1. **Spelling and grammar checkers** catch many slips; check every suggestion, because some are wrong, and do not expect them to catch a correctly spelled wrong word (*form* for *from*).
2. **Readability formulas** (Flesch Reading Ease, Flesch–Kincaid, the Fog Index) measure sentence length and word length, not clarity; use them, if at all, as a rough warning, never as a target.
3. **Style checkers and AI writing tools** can suggest revisions; judge each against the principles of this course, and follow the rules of your school, university or employer on what help is allowed (as of 2026 these rules vary and change). Never present text you did not write as your own ([[academic-writing/academic-integrity]]).
4. **The final judge is a reader**: read the text aloud, or give it to someone in its intended audience.
:::

::: proof
Tools are useful because they are tireless and consistent, and limited because they do not understand what the text is for. A readability formula will give *The cat sat on the mat* a better score than a precise sentence about a legal right, and it can be "improved" by cutting sentences in half, which may make the text harder to follow. Rudolf Flesch himself designed his 1948 formula as an aid for writers, not as a standard, and its scores were calibrated on school texts for native speakers. Style checkers flag passives, long sentences and "complex words" mechanically, which is the zombie-rule problem in software form. AI tools can propose fluent rewrites, but fluency is not accuracy: they may change the meaning, invent facts or remove a hedge the evidence requires. None of this makes tools useless; it makes the writer responsible for every change.
:::

::: example Judging a suggestion {#ex-suggestion}
A style checker flags three sentences in a fictional lab report and suggests changes. Accept or reject each, with your reason. (a) *The samples were heated to 80 °C.* — suggestion: *We heated the samples to 80 °C* ("avoid the passive"). (b) *In order to ensure that the results were accurate, each test was repeated.* — suggestion: *To ensure accuracy, each test was repeated.* (c) *The data suggest a link.* — suggestion: *The data prove a link* ("use stronger verbs").
::: solution
(a) **Either is acceptable**: many fields now accept *we*; others prefer the passive in methods, where the samples are the topic ([[academic-writing/academic-style#rule-first-person]]). Follow the field's convention, not the tool.

(b) **Accept**: *In order to ensure that the results were accurate* is wordy; the suggestion is more concise and loses nothing.

(c) **Reject**: *suggest* is an accurate hedge; *prove* claims more than the data show ([[style-and-rhetoric/concision#rule-hedges]]).
:::
:::

## When to stop

Editing has diminishing returns. The first pass through a draft removes the worst problems; later passes improve less and less, and at some point an editor begins to change things for the sake of change. A text is ready when its intended reader can find what they need, understand it at the first reading and act on it — when nothing in it makes the reader stop or reread without reason. That is a test of the reader's experience, not of the writer's satisfaction, and it is why the last step of an important edit is to give the text to a reader.

::: note English and Chinese
- **Editing English drafted in Chinese.** Writers who plan or draft in Chinese often find the same patterns in their English drafts: topics that change in every sentence (Chinese allows a topic that is not the subject; [[style-and-rhetoric/clarity]]), long *of*-chains translating 的-chains (*the improvement of the efficiency of the management of the department* for 部门管理效率的提高), doubled words from 四字格 (*serious and responsible*), category nouns (*the problem of*, *the work of*) and connectives at the start of most sentences (*Moreover*, *Furthermore* for 此外, 而且). Pass 2 and pass 5 of [[#rule-passes]] catch most of these.
- **润色 is not the whole edit.** Chinese 润色 ‘polishing’ suggests improving the language of a text whose content and structure are fixed — which is what this lesson does. But polishing cannot fix a paragraph whose argument is unclear; revise first ([[essay-writing/revising-editing]]).
- **"Advanced" English.** Some writers edit in the wrong direction, replacing plain words with rarer ones to make the text sound more advanced. Edit for the reader: the plain word is usually the right one ([[style-and-rhetoric/word-choice#rule-plain]]).
- **Machine translation in editing.** Translating a sentence into Chinese to check that it means what you intended can be a useful test of meaning; it is not a test of style, because a stiff English sentence may translate into natural Chinese.
:::

::: history
Editing as a profession grew with printing. Printers' houses developed rules for spelling, capitals and punctuation, and some were published: Horace Hart's *Rules for Compositors and Readers* at Oxford (first printed in 1893), and *The Chicago Manual of Style*, first published by the University of Chicago Press in 1906. These were rules of consistency rather than of clarity. Advice on clarity came from usage guides and style books — Henry Fowler's *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage* (1926), Strunk's *The Elements of Style* (1918), Gowers's *Plain Words* (1948) — and from the readability research of the 1940s and 1950s. The idea of editing for style as a method, with diagnostic tests in a fixed order, owes most to Joseph Williams's *Style* (1981) and to the composition research that it drew on, and later to style guides grounded in linguistics, such as Steven Pinker's *The Sense of Style* (2014), which set out to explain the reasons behind the advice and to separate it from the zombie rules.
:::

## Where this leads

This is the last lesson of the course. Its method applies to every kind of writing in the atlas: essays ([[essay-writing/revising-editing]]), research papers ([[academic-writing/academic-style]]), emails and letters ([[pragmatics/writing-to-people]]) and translations ([[translation/chinese-into-english]]). Reading literary prose closely — the work of writers who break these principles deliberately and well — is the subject of [[reading-literature/close-reading]].

::: summary
- Editing for style revises sentences and words for the reader, after the structure is settled and before proofreading ([[#def-editing-style]]).
- Edit in passes: reader and purpose, topics, characters and actions, old–new and stress, concision, words, rhythm, proofreading ([[#rule-passes]]).
- Each pass applies one principle of the course; the order prevents later work from being undone.
- When editing another writer, edit for the reader, keep their meaning and voice, explain patterns, and ask rather than guess ([[#rule-others]]).
- Do not "correct" split infinitives, final prepositions, sentences beginning with *And* or *But*, singular *they*, *which* in defining clauses, or well-chosen passives ([[#rule-zombies]]).
- Checkers, readability formulas and AI tools can help, but they do not understand purpose; the writer is responsible for every change, and the final judge is a reader ([[#rule-tools]]).
:::

List the topics (first few words) of each sentence and say whether the string is consistent. *The library opened in 1905. Its first librarian was a woman named Ada Price. Thousands of books were donated by local families. The building was extended in 1960.*

Topics: *The library* — *Its first librarian* — *Thousands of books* — *The building*. Mostly consistent (the library, its librarian, its building), but *Thousands of books* breaks the string; ✓ *Local families donated …* would be worse; better: *It received thousands of books from local families.*

Edit each sentence in two passes — characters and actions, then concision — and show both stages. (a) *There was a failure on the part of the contractor to provide notification to residents of the commencement of the works.* (b) *It is the recommendation of the committee that consideration be given to the possibility of an extension of the deadline.*

(a) Pass 3: *The contractor failed to notify residents that the works would begin.* Pass 5/6: *The contractor did not tell residents when the works would begin.*

(b) Pass 3: *The committee recommends that we consider extending the deadline.* Pass 5: *The committee recommends extending the deadline* — if the committee is recommending the extension and not merely its consideration; otherwise keep *considering*.

A tool suggests these changes to an essay. Accept or reject each, with your reason. (a) *the book which I read* → *the book that I read* (b) *It may possibly be true* → *It may be true* (c) *Climate change is a serious problem* → *Climate change is a very serious problem* (d) *to really understand* → *to understand really*

::: exercise Edit for the reader {level=2}
Edit this sentence from a fictional notice to tenants for its readers. *Tenants are reminded that, in accordance with the provisions of the tenancy agreement, the responsibility for the disposal of refuse in the designated receptacles rests with the occupier.*
::: solution
> *Please remember that, under your tenancy agreement, you must put your rubbish in the bins provided.*

Readers: tenants. Characters: the tenant (*you*); action: put rubbish in bins. *Refuse*, *designated receptacles* and *occupier* replaced with plain words; *in accordance with the provisions of* → *under*.

::: exercise A full edit {level=3}
Edit this fictional paragraph from a report to a company's directors, using all the passes of [[#rule-passes]]. Show your result and explain three changes.

*With reference to the matter of remote working, it should be noted that the implementation of a hybrid model has resulted in a number of positive outcomes. A reduction in office costs of approximately 20% has been achieved. Staff satisfaction, according to the annual survey, has also increased. However, there have been some difficulties in terms of communication between teams, which have been reported by managers. It is therefore recommended that consideration be given to the introduction of one fixed office day per week for each team.*
::: hint
Who are the characters? What is the main point, and where should it go?
:::
::: solution
A model:

> *Since the company introduced hybrid working, it has cut office costs by about 20%, and the annual survey shows that staff are more satisfied. Managers report, however, that teams find it harder to communicate with each other. We recommend that each team spend one fixed day a week in the office.*

Changes: (1) the empty opening (*With reference to the matter of …, it should be noted that*) cut; (2) nominalisations unpacked with characters as subjects — *the company … has cut*, *staff are more satisfied*, *teams find it harder*, *We recommend*; (3) *according to the annual survey* and *which have been reported by managers* moved from the stress position to the start of their clauses; (4) *a number of positive outcomes* (vague) replaced by the outcomes themselves. From 87 words to 51 — a side effect of the other changes.

::: exercise Edit your own writing {level=3}
Take a paragraph of 150–250 words that you have written recently in English — an essay, a report, an email. Edit it using the eight passes, keeping a note of what you changed in each pass. Then write two or three sentences on which pass changed most, and what that suggests about your writing habits.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A useful note lists, for each pass, one or two changes with before and after. Common findings: pass 2 or 3 changes most (topics that change in every sentence, nominalisations with weak verbs); pass 5 removes doubled words and empty openings; pass 7 shows runs of sentences of the same length. Keep the note: the passes that changed most are the ones to make first next time, and the patterns in them are the ones to add to your error log ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/editing]]).
