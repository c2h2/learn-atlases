In "Politics and the English Language" (1946), George Orwell quoted a verse from the King James Bible (Ecclesiastes 9:11):

> *I returned, and saw under the sun, that the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong, neither yet bread to the wise, nor yet riches to men of understanding, nor yet favour to men of skill; but time and chance happeneth to them all.*

He then rewrote it, as a parody, in what he called "modern English of the worst sort": a single sentence about "objective considerations of contemporary phenomena" and whether success is "commensurate with innate capacity". Orwell counted the words. The verse has 49 words and only 60 syllables, and every word is one of everyday life. His parody has 38 words but 90 syllables, and eighteen of its words come from Latin roots. More important than the count is what happens to the meaning: the verse names things a reader can see — a race, a battle, bread, riches — and the parody names only abstractions. The verse makes its point through five concrete cases; the parody states the general conclusion and leaves the reader nothing to picture.

This lesson is about choosing words: plain words rather than inflated ones; concrete and specific words rather than abstract and general ones; technical terms for readers who know them and plain explanations for readers who do not; and the euphemisms and clichés that let a writer avoid saying something precisely. Connotation, near-synonyms and the basic idea of euphemism are taught in [[collocation-and-register/connotation]], and formal and informal vocabulary in [[collocation-and-register/register]]; here the question is how a writer chooses among words that a dictionary says mean the same thing.

## Plain words

::: definition Plain language {#def-plain}
**Plain language** is language that its intended readers can understand and use at the first reading. The International Organization for Standardization's standard ISO 24495-1 (*Plain language — Part 1: Governing principles*, 2023) defines it by its effect on readers: a text is in plain language if its wording, structure and design let them easily find what they need, understand what they find, and use that information. Plain language is not a simplified language or a register of its own; a plain text can be formal, technical and precise.
:::

The commonest obstacle to plain English is a habit, found in learners and native speakers alike, of choosing the longer and more formal of two words because it sounds more serious.

| inflated | plain |
|---|---|
| *commence*, *initiate* | *begin*, *start* |
| *terminate* | *end* |
| *endeavour* (verb) | *try* |
| *utilise* | *use* |
| *facilitate* | *help*, *make easier* |
| *ascertain* | *find out*, *learn* |
| *purchase* (verb) | *buy* |
| *sufficient* | *enough* |
| *numerous* | *many* |
| *in excess of* | *more than* |
| *reside* | *live* |
| *demonstrate* | *show* |
| *assist* | *help* |
| *prior to* | *before* |

::: theorem Prefer the plain word {#rule-plain}
1. **Where two words mean the same thing in the context, prefer the shorter and more familiar one**: *use*, not *utilise*; *begin*, not *commence*.
2. **Keep the longer word when it means something different**: *demonstrate* in the sense ‘prove by reasoning’, *purchase* as a noun in accounting, *terminate* of a contract or a process in computing. Plain does not mean imprecise.
3. **Keep the register consistent**: in formal writing, plain words are still formal words — *many*, *show*, *before* are neutral, not informal ([[collocation-and-register/register#rule-formality]]). Plain English does not require *a lot of*, *get* or *kids*.
4. **Use the term your readers expect** in a specialised field, even if it is long ([[#rule-jargon]]).
:::

::: proof
The inflated word costs the reader a little more time and, if it is rare, may not be understood at all; it gains nothing when the plain word means the same. Many inflated words are Latinate (*utilise*, *commence*, *terminate*) and their plain equivalents Germanic or older borrowings (*use*, *begin*, *end*): English formal style has been built on Latin and French vocabulary since the Middle Ages ([[history-of-english/norman-french]]), and writers who want to sound formal reach for it. Sir Ernest Gowers's *Plain Words* (1948), written for British civil servants, argued that this reach usually makes official prose harder to read and no more exact; Orwell's second rule (1946) was never to use a long word where a short one will do. Point 2 is the qualification that the rules leave out: many long words have meanings that short ones do not, and the aim is the right word, not the shortest. Point 3 matters especially for learners, who sometimes hear "plain" as "informal". Plain English in a report is formal English without the inflation.
:::

::: example Deflating a notice {#ex-deflate}
Rewrite this fictional notice from a library in plain English, keeping it polite and formal. *Patrons are advised that, in order to facilitate the commencement of essential maintenance works, the facility will be rendered temporarily inaccessible for a period of time not exceeding three working days. Patrons who are in possession of borrowed items may utilise the external return facility.*
::: solution
1. **Characters and actions** ([[style-and-rhetoric/clarity]]): the library is closing; readers can return books.
2. **Plain words**: *Patrons* → *readers* or *you*; *facilitate the commencement of* → cut (the library is closing *for* maintenance); *rendered temporarily inaccessible* → *closed*; *for a period of time not exceeding three working days* → *for up to three working days*; *in possession of borrowed items* → *with books to return*; *utilise the external return facility* → *use the returns box outside*.

> *The library will be closed for up to three working days for essential maintenance. If you have books to return, please use the returns box outside the main entrance.*

The notice is still formal — no contractions, no slang — and it adds one fact the reader needs (*outside the main entrance*), because *external return facility* did not say where it was.
:::
:::

::: application Plain language in law and government
Plain language is now required by law for some documents in several jurisdictions (as of 2026; laws change, so check the current text before relying on any of them):

- The European Union's directive on unfair terms in consumer contracts (93/13/EEC, 1993) requires written terms offered to consumers to be "drafted in plain, intelligible language".
- In the United Kingdom, the Consumer Rights Act 2015 (section 68) requires written terms and consumer notices to be transparent, that is, "expressed in plain and intelligible language" and legible.
- In the United States, the Securities and Exchange Commission adopted plain-English rules for parts of securities prospectuses in 1998 and published *A Plain English Handbook* the same year, and the Plain Writing Act of 2010 requires federal agencies to use clear language in documents for the public.
- ISO 24495-1 (2023) gives an international, voluntary standard for plain language, built on the principles that readers can find, understand and use what they need.

These laws define plain language by its effect on the intended reader, not by word counts or banned words.
:::

## Concrete and specific words

::: definition Concrete, abstract, general and specific {#def-concrete}
- A **concrete** word names something that can be perceived — seen, heard, touched: *bread*, *a bicycle*, *rain*, *shouting*. An **abstract** word names a quality, idea or relation: *justice*, *efficiency*, *growth*, *the situation*.
- A **general** word names a large class; a **specific** word names a smaller class or a single member: *vehicle* → *car* → *a ten-year-old red hatchback*; *food* → *bread* → *a loaf of rye bread*.

The two scales are independent: *furniture* is concrete and general; *a kitchen chair* is concrete and specific; *justice* is abstract and general; *the court's decision on Tuesday* is abstract but specific.
:::

The American semanticist S. I. Hayakawa called the movement from specific to general a **ladder of abstraction** (*Language in Action*, 1941; revised as *Language in Thought and Action*): a particular cow, then *cow*, *livestock*, *farm assets*, *assets*, *wealth*. Every rung is useful. Good prose moves up and down the ladder: it states the general idea and then grounds it in a specific case, or gives the case and then draws the general point.

::: theorem Prefer the concrete and specific {#rule-concrete}
1. **Where a concrete or specific word is accurate, prefer it** to an abstract or general one: *The roof leaks* rather than *There are issues with the building*.
2. **Support abstract claims with specific cases**: *The scheme helps families — a parent with two children saves about £30 a week* (a fictional figure).
3. **Watch for vague nouns** — *issue*, *aspect*, *factor*, *situation*, *thing*, *area*, *problem*, *element* — and replace them with the specific noun they stand for, or cut them.
4. **Use the general word when you mean the class**: *Vehicles must not park here* includes cars, vans and motorbikes, and *cars* would not.
:::

::: proof
William Strunk Jr. made this rule 12 of *The Elements of Style* (1918): *Use definite, specific, concrete language*, explaining that the writer should "prefer the specific to the general, the definite to the vague, the concrete to the abstract". The reason is that a reader can picture a concrete word and check a specific one. *There are issues with the building* could mean a leaking roof, a broken lift or a legal dispute; the reader cannot act on it, and may suspect that the writer is avoiding the facts. A claim grounded in a case is both clearer and more persuasive, because the reader can see what it means and judge whether it is true ([[style-and-rhetoric/rhetorical-appeals]]). Point 4 is the limit: a general word is precise when the general class is what is meant, and a law, a rule or a definition usually needs one.
:::

Mark Twain put the point about precision memorably in a letter of 1888 to George Bainton, who published it in *The Art of Authorship* (1890): *The difference between the almost right word and the right word is really a large matter — 'tis the difference between the lightning-bug and the lightning.*

::: example Making a paragraph concrete {#ex-concrete}
Revise this fictional paragraph from a student's report on a volunteering project, making it concrete and specific. You may invent plausible details, as the student would know them. *The project addressed various issues in the local area. Several aspects of the situation were improved, and there was a positive impact on the community. Participants gained valuable experience in a number of areas.*
::: solution
1. **Find the vague nouns**: *various issues*, *the local area*, *several aspects*, *the situation*, *a positive impact*, *the community*, *valuable experience*, *a number of areas*. None tells the reader what happened.
2. **Ask what each stands for** — the student knows: a park full of rubbish, a broken playground, children with nowhere to play; the volunteers learned to plan work and to talk to the council.
3. **Replace each with the specific noun, and keep one general sentence** to frame the details.

> *The project set out to make Riverside Park usable again. Over six weekends, twenty volunteers cleared rubbish from the paths, repaired two benches and persuaded the council to replace the broken swings, so that families on the estate now have somewhere for children to play. The volunteers learned to plan a job, to estimate costs and to make a case to officials.*

Every abstract noun has become a specific action or object. (All details here are invented for the exercise; in a real report they must be true.)
:::
:::

## Jargon

::: definition Jargon {#def-jargon}
**Jargon** has two meanings. In the neutral sense, it is the technical vocabulary of a field — *morpheme* in linguistics, *liability* in accounting, *latency* in computing — which lets specialists speak precisely to each other. In the critical sense, it is specialised or fashionable vocabulary used where it is not needed or not understood: *leverage our synergies*, *going forward*, *a paradigm shift in stakeholder engagement*. Fashionable terms of the second kind are often called **buzzwords**.
:::

::: theorem Use technical terms for the reader {#rule-jargon}
1. **With specialist readers, use the field's terms**: they are precise and expected, and replacing them with plain paraphrases can make the text less exact.
2. **With general readers, define a technical term the first time** you use it, or replace it with a plain explanation if you need it only once: *Latency — the delay before a response begins — …*.
3. **Avoid buzzwords** in every kind of writing: they claim importance without saying anything specific. Ask what the phrase means and write that.
4. **Do not invent terms** for things that have plain names, and do not use a term in a sense different from its field's.
:::

::: proof
Technical terms exist because specialists need words for distinctions the everyday vocabulary does not make; a linguist who wrote *word part* instead of *morpheme* would be less precise, not plainer. The difficulty is knowing who the reader is. Steven Pinker calls the main cause of bad writing **the curse of knowledge** — a term from economics (Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber, 1989) for the difficulty of imagining what it is like not to know something one knows (*The Sense of Style*, chapter 3). Experts forget that other people do not know their abbreviations and terms, and so write for themselves. Buzzwords are a different problem: they are not technical at all, but borrow the prestige of technical language. *Leverage our synergies* sounds precise and means only *work together*, if that.
:::

::: example Writing for two readers {#ex-jargon}
A fictional hospital report to specialists says: *Post-operative mobilisation within 24 hours reduced the incidence of deep vein thrombosis.* Rewrite it for a leaflet for patients.
::: solution
1. **Find the terms a patient may not know**: *post-operative mobilisation* (getting out of bed and moving after an operation), *incidence* (how often something happens), *deep vein thrombosis* (a blood clot in a deep vein, usually in the leg).
2. **Decide which term the patient needs.** *Deep vein thrombosis* appears on hospital forms and patients will hear doctors use it, so keep it with a definition; the others can be replaced.
3. **Make the patient the character** ([[style-and-rhetoric/clarity]]).

> *If you get out of bed and start moving within a day of your operation, you are less likely to develop a blood clot in your leg (a deep vein thrombosis, or DVT).*

The specialist sentence was right for its readers; the leaflet sentence is right for its own.
:::
:::

::: warning Learners' "advanced vocabulary"
✗ *In contemporary society, the utilisation of digital devices has become ubiquitous, and its ramifications for the cognitive faculties of adolescents necessitate scrutiny.*
✓ *Teenagers now use phones and tablets for hours a day, and we need to know how this affects their concentration and memory.*

Students preparing for exams are often told to use "advanced vocabulary" to show their range. A sentence full of rare words does show range, but it is harder to read and often less exact: *the cognitive faculties of adolescents* does not say which faculties. Examiners and other readers reward the precise word, which may be short. Use a rare word when it is the right word.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence uses a technical term appropriately?
- [ ] In a leaflet for patients: *Take this tablet post-prandially.*
- [x] In a report for engineers: *The beam's deflection under load exceeded the design limit.*
- [ ] In an email to staff: *Going forward, let's leverage our learnings to drive engagement.*
- [ ] In a notice for tenants: *Remediation of the damp ingress will be actioned shortly.*
::: solution
*Deflection* and *design limit* are the precise terms that engineers expect. The patients' leaflet should say *after meals*; the email uses buzzwords that say nothing specific; the notice should say *We will repair the damp soon*.
:::

## Euphemism and cliché

Euphemism — a mild or indirect word used in place of one that might be unpleasant — is defined and illustrated in [[collocation-and-register/connotation#def-euphemism]]. For a writer, the question is when it is kind and when it misleads.

::: theorem Use euphemism for tact, not to hide facts {#rule-euphemism}
1. **Use euphemism for tact** where the reader's feelings matter and the facts are clear: *passed away* in a letter of condolence; *let go* in a conversation with the person concerned.
2. **Do not use euphemism to hide facts the reader needs**: *We are rightsizing the team* to staff who will lose their jobs; *negative growth* for a fall in sales; *a pre-existing issue with the brakes* for a fault.
3. **In public, technical and legal writing, name things plainly**: a report on job losses says how many jobs were lost.
:::

::: proof
Euphemism has a proper place in every language, because some subjects — death, illness, the body, money troubles — are painful, and speakers spare each other ([[pragmatics/politeness-face]]). It becomes a problem of style, and sometimes of ethics, when it is used not to spare the reader's feelings but to stop the reader from understanding. Orwell's essay attacked this second kind in the political writing of his time, which he said used abstract and euphemistic phrases so that terrible things could be named "without calling up mental pictures of them"; his own examples included *pacification* and *transfer of population* for acts of violence. Writers on corporate and official language since then have given many more (*downsizing*, *collateral damage*). The test is whether the reader, once they knew the plain fact, would feel misled.
:::

::: quiz
In which context is the euphemism most clearly **misleading**?
- [ ] *She passed away peacefully* in a family's announcement of a death
- [ ] *Where is the restroom?* asked in an American restaurant
- [x] *The product has been the subject of a voluntary recall* in a notice that does not say the product can catch fire
- [ ] *He is between jobs* said by a friend at a party
::: solution
The recall notice hides a fact the reader needs for their safety. The others spare feelings or follow social convention, and nobody is left without information they need.
:::
:::

::: definition Cliché {#def-cliche}
A **cliché** is a phrase, often originally a vivid metaphor or comparison, that has been used so often that it no longer makes the reader see anything: *at the end of the day*, *a double-edged sword*, *the tip of the iceberg*, *think outside the box*, *in today's fast-paced world*. A **dead metaphor** is one that has become an ordinary word (*the leg of a table*, *to grasp an idea*) and is no longer a cliché, because nobody hears it as a metaphor at all.
:::

::: theorem Avoid stock phrases in writing {#rule-cliche}
1. **Avoid clichés** in formal and persuasive writing: say the plain thing, or find a fresh comparison if the point needs one.
2. **Avoid stock openings and closings**: *Since the dawn of time*, *In today's world*, *With the rapid development of society*, *Every coin has two sides*, *Only time will tell*.
3. **Do not mix metaphors**: ✗ *We must grasp the nettle before the ship sinks.*
4. **Idioms are not clichés**: *make up your mind*, *take part*, *on the other hand* are ordinary English, and fixed expressions are part of fluent writing ([[collocation-and-register/idioms]]).
:::

::: proof
Orwell's first rule was never to use a metaphor, simile or other figure of speech that one is used to seeing in print. The reason is that a cliché looks like a thought without being one: *a double-edged sword* tells the reader that something has advantages and disadvantages, but not which. Readers recognise clichés at once, and a text full of them sounds as if its writer was not paying attention. Mixed metaphors are what happens when a writer uses metaphors without seeing them: anyone who pictured the nettle and the ship would not have put them together. Point 4 prevents an overreaction: much of English is built from fixed phrases, and a writer who avoided all of them would sound stilted. The difference is that an idiom is the normal way of saying something, while a cliché is a figure used in place of saying it.
:::

::: example Replacing clichés {#ex-cliche}
Revise this fictional opening of an essay on online learning. *In today's fast-paced world, online learning has become a hot topic. Like a double-edged sword, it has both advantages and disadvantages. At the end of the day, only time will tell whether it is the wave of the future.*
::: solution
1. **Find the clichés**: *In today's fast-paced world*, *a hot topic*, *a double-edged sword*, *At the end of the day*, *only time will tell*, *the wave of the future* — six in three sentences.
2. **Ask what each was standing in for**: *now*; *widely discussed*; *has advantages and disadvantages* (already said in the same sentence); nothing; *we do not know yet*; *will replace classroom teaching*.
3. **Write the plain point, specifically.** An essay opening should lead to a position ([[essay-writing/thesis-introduction]]), so name the advantages and disadvantages instead of announcing them.

> *Since 2020, many universities have taught at least some courses online. Online courses let students study at their own pace and from anywhere, but they make it harder to ask questions and to stay motivated. Whether they will replace classroom teaching depends on whether these weaknesses can be overcome.*
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **成语 and 四字格 are not clichés in Chinese.** A well-chosen 成语 is a mark of good style in Chinese. Translated literally into English, it often becomes either a cliché or an obscure image (*draw a snake and add feet* for 画蛇添足). In English, say the meaning plainly (*overdo it*, *spoil it by adding something unnecessary*), or use an English idiom with the same meaning only if one exists and fits the register ([[translation/idioms-culture]]).
- **Stock phrases in essays.** Chinese students preparing for English exams have often learned set phrases for essays — *With the rapid development of society*, *Every coin has two sides*, *As the saying goes*, *Last but not least*. They are understandable translations of common Chinese essay formulas (随着社会的发展, 凡事都有两面性), and they are used so widely that examiners and teachers recognise them as memorised. A specific first sentence is more effective.
- **Hu Shi's eight points.** Chinese has its own history of campaigns against inflated writing. In 1917 Hu Shi's 《文学改良刍议》 called for writing in the vernacular and set out eight points, among them 务去烂调套语 ‘get rid of worn-out phrases’, 不用典 ‘do not use allusions’ and 不讲对仗 ‘do not insist on parallel couplets’ — advice remarkably close to Orwell's rules thirty years later, though its aim was a new literary language rather than clear political prose.
- **大词 ‘big words’.** Chinese teachers also warn against 大词 and 空话 in writing; the English advice about plain, specific words will be familiar. What differs is which words count as inflated: in English, the Latinate formal word (*utilise*, *commence*) is often the problem, and the short Germanic word is formal enough.
:::

::: history
English has argued about plain and ornate vocabulary since the Renaissance. Thomas Wilson's *The Arte of Rhetorique* (1553) mocked "inkhorn terms", showy borrowings from Latin, and urged writers to use words the common people understood. Jonathan Swift's *A Proposal for Correcting, Improving and Ascertaining the English Tongue* (1712) complained of fashionable abbreviations and slang from the opposite side. In the twentieth century the argument became one about public writing. Arthur Quiller-Couch's lectures *On the Art of Writing* (1916) attacked the vague abstractions he called "jargon"; Orwell's "Politics and the English Language" (1946) tied stale and inflated language to political dishonesty; and Sir Ernest Gowers's *Plain Words* (1948) and *The Complete Plain Words* (1954) gave civil servants a practical guide. The plain-language movement that followed — the Plain English Campaign in Britain (1979), plain-language rules for contracts and official documents in many countries, the US Plain Writing Act (2010) and the ISO standard of 2023 — shifted the test from the writer's taste to the reader's ability to understand and act.
:::

::: summary
- Plain language is language its readers can find, understand and use; it can be formal and technical ([[#def-plain]]).
- Prefer the shorter, familiar word when it means the same; keep the longer word when its meaning is different, and keep the register consistent ([[#rule-plain]]).
- Prefer concrete and specific words, support abstract claims with cases, and replace vague nouns such as *issue* and *aspect* ([[#def-concrete]], [[#rule-concrete]]).
- Use technical terms with specialists and define them for others; avoid buzzwords; remember the curse of knowledge ([[#def-jargon]], [[#rule-jargon]]).
- Use euphemism for tact, never to hide facts the reader needs ([[#rule-euphemism]]).
- Avoid clichés, stock essay phrases and mixed metaphors, but do not mistake normal idioms for clichés ([[#def-cliche]], [[#rule-cliche]]).
:::

A model (the details are invented, as the company would know them):

The original hid two losses — in-person help and an old service — behind abstractions (*streamlined service model*, *legacy functionalities*, *a degree of change*) and buzzwords (*going forward*, *sunsetted*, *delivering value*). ::: exercise Write up and down the ladder {level=3}
Write a paragraph (100–150 words) arguing that something in your town or school should change. Begin with a general claim, move down the ladder of abstraction to one specific case described concretely, and end by moving back up to the general point. Underline one abstract word and one concrete word, and check that every vague noun has been replaced.
::: solution
Answers will vary. Check that (1) the first sentence states a general claim (*The town's buses do not serve the people who need them most*); (2) the middle gives one specific case with concrete details — a named route, a time, a person in a typical situation (*A nurse finishing a night shift at 6 a.m. waits forty minutes for the first bus home*); (3) the last sentence returns to the claim, now supported (*Timetables built around office hours leave out everyone who works outside them*); (4) there are no clichés, buzzwords or vague nouns such as *issue*, *factor* or *aspect*.
:::
