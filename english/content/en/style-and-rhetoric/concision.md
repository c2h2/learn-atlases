Kofi Mensah manages a small team, and he has written this to them about a training day:

> *I just wanted to let you all know that, basically, at this point in time we are actually in the process of making some changes and modifications to the arrangements for the training day. In the event that you have any questions or queries, please do not hesitate to contact me. It is important to note that the final outcome will in all probability be that the session will take place in the afternoon rather than in the morning.*

The message has 79 words. Here is what it says:

> *We are changing the arrangements for the training day: the session will probably be in the afternoon, not the morning. If you have any questions, please ask me.*

That is 28 words, and nothing has been lost — not the news, not the uncertainty (*probably*), not the friendly offer of help. The other 51 words did no work. Some said the same thing twice (*changes and modifications*, *questions or queries*, *final outcome*); some said nothing at all (*basically*, *actually*, *just*); some took a phrase to say what one word says (*at this point in time*, *in the event that*, *in all probability*); and some talked about the message instead of delivering it (*I just wanted to let you all know that*, *It is important to note that*).

This lesson is about finding and cutting words like these. It follows [[style-and-rhetoric/clarity]], because most wordiness disappears when characters become subjects and actions verbs; what remains is the subject here. It also says when *not* to cut: concision is not the same as brevity, and some apparently unnecessary words — politeness, a useful hedge, the redundancy a listener needs — do real work.

## What concision is

::: definition Concision {#def-concision}
Prose is **concise** when every word does some work for the reader: it carries meaning, marks a relation, signals the writer's attitude or the reader's relation to the writer, or helps the rhythm. **Wordiness** is the use of words that do none of these. Concision is not the same as **brevity**: a long text can be concise, and a short one wordy.
:::

The classic statement of the principle is in William Strunk Jr.'s *The Elements of Style*, privately printed at Cornell in 1918 (and therefore in the public domain), where it is rule 13 (rule 17 in E. B. White's later editions):

> *Omit needless words. Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts. This requires not that the writer make all his sentences short, or that he avoid all detail and treat his subjects only in outline, but that every word tell.*

The famous part is the first three words. The important part is the last sentence, which is often forgotten: Strunk does not ask for short sentences or for less detail, but for words that *tell*. A writer who cuts detail, examples or qualifications to make a text shorter has not made it more concise; he or she has made it say less.

::: theorem Six kinds of wordiness {#rule-six-kinds}
In revising for concision, look for six kinds of word that do no work:

1. **Meaningless words**: *basically*, *actually*, *really*, *just*, *kind of*, *sort of*, *particular*, *certain*, *various*, *given*, *specific*, *in terms of* — when they add nothing to the meaning.
2. **Doubled words**: pairs of near-synonyms joined by *and* or *or*: *each and every*, *full and complete*, *first and foremost*, *changes and modifications*, *questions or queries*, *hopes and desires*.
3. **What the reader can infer**: redundant modifiers (*past history*, *future plans*, *completely finish*, *final outcome*, *advance warning*, *free gift*) and redundant categories (*large in size*, *red in colour*, *a period of time*, *at an early hour*, *of an unusual nature*).
4. **Phrases where a word will do**: *due to the fact that* → *because*; *in the event that* → *if*; *at this point in time* → *now*; *in spite of the fact that* → *although*; *has the ability to* → *can*; *a large number of* → *many*.
5. **Negatives that could be affirmatives**: *not many* → *few*; *not the same* → *different*; *did not remember* → *forgot*; *not unlike* → *like*; *not insignificant* → *significant* (unless the understatement is intended).
6. **Metadiscourse that says nothing**: *It is important to note that*, *I would like to point out that*, *It should be mentioned that*, *What I am trying to say is that*.
:::

::: proof
The six categories follow the list in Williams and Bizup's lesson on concision (*Style*, Lesson 9), with examples of our own. They are useful because each is a pattern a writer can search for, rather than a judgement to be made about every word. Each kind of wordiness costs the reader something different. Meaningless words dilute: the reader processes them and finds nothing. Doubled words make the reader ask whether two things are meant — are *changes* different from *modifications*? Redundant modifiers and categories tell the reader what the head word already says: a history is past and an outcome is final. Long phrases delay the relation they express; *due to the fact that* takes five words to say *because*. Negatives are harder to process than affirmatives, because the reader must first understand the positive and then reverse it, and two negatives together (*not uncommon*, *not unlikely*) require two reversals. Empty metadiscourse puts the writer between the reader and the content: *It is important to note that X* is a long way of saying *X*, and if X is important the sentence should show it.
:::

### Phrases and their one-word equivalents

| wordy | concise |
|---|---|
| *due to the fact that*, *owing to the fact that*, *in view of the fact that* | *because*, *since* |
| *despite the fact that*, *in spite of the fact that* | *although* |
| *in the event that*, *on condition that* (where nothing more formal is meant) | *if* |
| *at this point in time*, *at the present time*, *in this day and age* | *now*, *today* |
| *prior to*, *in advance of* | *before* |
| *subsequent to*, *following on from* | *after* |
| *in order to* | *to* (but see [[#rule-keep]]) |
| *with regard to*, *with reference to*, *in relation to*, *in connection with* | *about*, *on* |
| *is able to*, *has the ability to*, *is in a position to* | *can* |
| *it is possible that*, *there is a chance that* | *may*, *might* |
| *a large number of*, *a great many of* | *many* |
| *the majority of* | *most* |
| *in the near future* | *soon* |
| *for the purpose of* | *to*, *for* |
| *the question as to whether* | *whether* |

The left-hand phrases are not wrong, and some have their uses: *prior to* is normal in legal and technical writing, and *in order to* can make a sentence easier to parse when another *to* is nearby. But a writer who uses them out of habit adds three or four words to every sentence.

::: example Cutting a paragraph {#ex-cut}
Revise this paragraph from a fictional student report for concision, naming the kind of wordiness each cut removes.

*In this report I would like to basically look at the various different reasons why a large number of students do not attend the lectures that are held early in the morning. First and foremost, it is important to note that, due to the fact that many students have part-time jobs in the evening, they are not able to get up at an early hour. In addition to this, the lectures are recorded and put online, and so it is not uncommon for students to watch them at a later point in time.*
::: solution
1. *I would like to* (metadiscourse) — cut; *basically* (meaningless) — cut; *various different* (doubled; *various* already means *different*) → *the*; *a large number of* → *many*; *the lectures that are held early in the morning* → *early-morning lectures*.
2. *First and foremost* (doubled) → *First*; *it is important to note that* (metadiscourse) — cut; *due to the fact that* → *because*; *are not able to* → *cannot*; *at an early hour* (redundant category) → *early*.
3. *In addition to this* → *also*; *it is not uncommon* (double negative) → *often*; *at a later point in time* → *later*.

> *This report looks at why many students miss early-morning lectures. First, many have evening jobs and cannot get up early. Second, the lectures are recorded and put online, so students often watch them later.*

The revision has 34 words against 93. The content is the same; *Second* has been added, because the original had *First and foremost* but no second item marked, and the reader needs to know that the recordings are a second reason.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which revision of *The reason why the experiment failed was because the equipment was faulty* is correct and concise?
- [ ] *The reason why the experiment failed was that the equipment was faulty.*
- [x] *The experiment failed because the equipment was faulty.*
- [ ] *The reason was because the equipment was faulty.*
- [ ] *Due to the fact that the equipment was faulty, the experiment failed.*
::: solution
*The reason why … was because* says the same thing three times (*reason*, *why*, *because*). The first option corrects the grammar but keeps the repetition; the third keeps the redundant *reason … because*; the fourth replaces it with another wordy phrase. The second uses one word, *because*, for the relation.
:::

## Hedges and intensifiers

Two kinds of word are easy to overuse because each seems to make a sentence better: **hedges**, which make a claim more cautious (*may*, *perhaps*, *tends to*, *somewhat*), and **intensifiers**, which make it stronger (*very*, *extremely*, *really*, *totally*, *absolutely*). Both have a proper use, and both are often wordiness in disguise.

::: theorem Hedge accurately, intensify rarely {#rule-hedges}
1. **Keep a hedge that is true.** If the evidence supports only *probably*, write *probably*; cutting it would make the sentence wrong, not concise ([[modality/hedging-boosting#rule-what-to-hedge]]).
2. **Use one hedge per claim.** Stacked hedges — *it may perhaps be possible to suggest that this could* — make the reader wonder whether the writer believes anything. Choose the one that states the degree of certainty: *this may …*.
3. **Cut intensifiers that add no information**: *very*, *really*, *extremely*, *totally*, *completely*, *absolutely*, *quite* (in AmE ‘very’, in BrE often ‘fairly’ — another reason to avoid it). Often a more exact word does the job: *very big* → *huge* or a number; *very important* → *essential* or a reason why.
4. **Never intensify absolute words**: ✗ *very unique*, ✗ *completely essential*, ✗ *more perfect* (outside fixed phrases). In careful writing *unique* means ‘the only one’, and *essential* cannot be more essential.
:::

::: proof
Hedges are a necessary part of honest writing, especially in academic and professional prose, where a writer who claims too much loses the reader's trust ([[modality/hedging-boosting]]). The problem is not hedging but **compulsive** hedging — the phrase is Steven Pinker's, in *The Sense of Style* (2014, chapter 2), where he argues that writers often pile up qualifications not because the evidence is uncertain but to protect themselves from criticism. One accurate hedge states the writer's confidence; several hedges state the writer's anxiety. Intensifiers fail for a different reason: they are so common that they no longer intensify. In a text where everything is *very important*, nothing is, and the reader learns to skip the word. A specific word or a fact (*Fees rose by forty per cent*) persuades where *very* only asserts. Some usage guides accept *very unique* as informal; in formal writing it is widely criticised, and avoiding it costs nothing.
:::

::: example Hedges and intensifiers {#ex-hedges}
Revise each sentence so that its hedges and intensifiers say exactly what the writer means. (a) *It could perhaps possibly be argued that the new policy may have had some effect on attendance.* (The writer has attendance figures showing a clear rise after the policy.) (b) *This is a really very important point that is absolutely essential to understand.* (c) *The results seem to suggest that there might be a link between sleep and memory.* (The writer is summarising one small study.)
::: solution
(a) Four hedges (*could*, *perhaps*, *possibly*, *may*) and a vague *some effect*, for a claim the evidence supports. The figures show a rise, but not by themselves that the policy caused it; one hedge is honest: *Attendance rose after the new policy was introduced, and the policy is the likeliest explanation.* Or, if the writer has ruled out other causes: *The new policy increased attendance.*

(b) Three intensifiers and a doubled claim (*important*, *essential*). Replace with the reason the point matters: *This point matters because the rest of the argument depends on it.*

(c) *seem to suggest* and *might* are two hedges for one small study; one is enough: *The results suggest a link between sleep and memory.* (*Suggest* is itself a hedge: it claims less than *show*.)
:::
:::

## Metadiscourse: when to keep it

Not every sentence about the text is empty. Metadiscourse — language about the writing itself — includes the signposts that show a reader the structure of an argument (*This section compares …*, *A second objection is …*), and these are a large part of what makes a long text easy to follow ([[essay-writing/signposting#def-signposting]]).

::: theorem Keep metadiscourse that guides {#rule-metadiscourse}
1. **Keep** metadiscourse that tells the reader something they need: the structure of a long text, the relation of a paragraph to the argument, a definition (*By "access" I mean …*), or the status of a claim (*This is speculation, but …*).
2. **Cut** metadiscourse about the writer's thinking or intentions that the text itself shows: *I think that*, *In my opinion*, *I would like to say that*, *It is interesting to note that*, *As we can see*.
3. **Cut** announcements that delay the point: *In this paragraph I will discuss the causes of …* → *Three causes explain …*.
:::

::: proof
Williams and Bizup distinguish metadiscourse that refers to the writer's thinking (*I believe*, *I will argue*), to the reader's actions (*Consider now …*), and to the text's own structure (*first*, *in conclusion*), and they treat it as wordy only when it does not help the reader (*Style*, Lesson 9). The difference is whether the reader could infer it. In an essay, every unhedged claim is the writer's opinion, so *In my opinion* says what the reader already knows — unless the writer is contrasting their view with others', in which case it is information. *It is interesting to note that* tells the reader to be interested instead of making the point interesting. A signpost in a ten-page report, by contrast, tells the reader something they could not easily see for themselves.
:::

::: example Which metadiscourse to keep {#ex-metadiscourse}
A fictional twelve-page report contains these sentences. Which metadiscourse would you keep? (a) *Section 3 compares the two proposals; section 4 recommends one.* (b) *I would like to point out that the second proposal is cheaper.* (c) *As we can clearly see from the table, costs rose.* (d) *By "users" this report means everyone who borrowed a book in 2025, not only registered members.*
::: solution
1. **(a) Keep.** In a twelve-page report, a map of the sections helps the reader find their way.
2. **(b) Cut.** *I would like to point out that* is about the writer's intention: ✓ *The second proposal is cheaper.*
3. **(c) Cut most of it.** *As we can clearly see* tells the reader what they see; say what the table shows: ✓ *Costs rose by 15% (Table 2).*
4. **(d) Keep.** A definition of a key term is information the reader cannot infer.
:::
:::

## When not to cut

::: theorem Words that do work {#rule-keep}
Do not cut:

1. **politeness** that the relationship needs: *Could you possibly …*, *Thank you for …*, *I'm afraid …* — these are not needless words but part of the message ([[pragmatics/requests-refusals]]);
2. **accurate hedges** ([[#rule-hedges]]);
3. **redundancy that a listener or a hurried reader needs**: in speech, instructions and safety notices, repeating the key point, or saying *Turn left — that is, away from the river*, prevents errors;
4. **terms of art** in law, science and administration whose exact form matters, even when they look wordy;
5. **articles, pronouns and conjunctions** that the grammar requires: concision is not telegraphic style;
6. **words that help the reader parse a sentence**, such as *that* after verbs like *say* and *believe* when a noun phrase could be misread as the object: *She noticed that the error had been corrected* is easier to read than *She noticed the error had been corrected*.
:::

::: proof
The definition of concision ([[#def-concision]]) counts as work anything that helps the reader, and these six kinds of word all do. Politeness carries the writer's relation to the reader; a request with every courtesy cut can read as an order, which is a change of meaning, not of length. Redundancy is useful whenever the reader cannot easily go back: a listener cannot reread, so speakers repeat and preview (Chaudron and Richards's lecture study is discussed in [[presentations-and-discussion/signposting-a-talk]]). Point 6 concerns what psycholinguists call garden-path sentences, in which the reader first takes a noun phrase as the object of the verb and then has to reanalyse; Pinker discusses keeping the optional *that* to prevent this (*The Sense of Style*, chapter 4). The point of concision is to save the reader effort; a cut that costs the reader effort is not concision.
:::

::: warning Telegraphic style
✗ *Received your file. Will check, send comments asap. Meeting moved Thursday, room TBC.*
✓ *I've received your file and will send my comments as soon as I can. The meeting has moved to Thursday; I'll confirm the room.*

Cutting articles, subjects and auxiliaries produces notes, not prose. Telegraphic style is acceptable in text messages between colleagues who know each other well, in headlines and in lists; in an email to someone you do not know well, it reads as abrupt, and it can be ambiguous (*Meeting moved Thursday* — from Thursday or to Thursday?).
:::

::: quiz
Which phrase is **not** wordy in its context?
- [ ] *each and every student* in a report on exam results
- [ ] *in the event that* in an email to a friend
- [x] *Could you possibly send me the file by Friday?* in a request to a senior colleague
- [ ] *It is important to note that* at the start of every paragraph of an essay
::: solution
*Could you possibly* does work: it makes the request polite, which matters when writing to someone senior ([[#rule-keep]]). The others are doubled words, a long phrase for *if*, and empty metadiscourse.
:::
:::

## Cutting a whole text

Wordiness is not only a matter of words and phrases. Whole sentences can be needless — a sentence that repeats the previous one in other words, an introduction that announces what the text will say, a conclusion that repeats it all again.

::: theorem Cut from the top down {#rule-top-down}
1. **Cut whole sentences** that repeat what another sentence says, or that the reader does not need for the purpose of the text.
2. **Cut empty openings and closings**: introductions that only announce the topic, and conclusions that only summarise a short text.
3. **Then revise for clarity** ([[style-and-rhetoric/clarity]]): unpack nominalisations and empty verbs, which often removes a third of the words at once.
4. **Finally, search for the six kinds** of [[#rule-six-kinds]], one at a time.
:::

::: proof
The order follows the principle of revising before editing ([[essay-writing/revising-editing#rule-revise-first]]): there is no point polishing the phrases of a sentence that will be cut. Step 3 comes before step 4 because clarity revisions remove many of the wordy patterns automatically — *there is a need for the provision of* becomes *we must provide* — and what is left is easier to see. Blaise Pascal apologised at the end of the sixteenth of his *Lettres provinciales* (1656) that he had made the letter longer than usual only because he had not had the time to make it shorter: concision is the product of revision, not of drafting, and a writer who tries to draft concisely usually drafts slowly and still has to revise.
:::

::: example Cutting sentences {#ex-sentences}
Shorten this fictional product description by cutting whole sentences and then revising the rest. *Our new water bottle is a bottle for carrying water. It keeps drinks cold for up to 24 hours. This means that if you put a cold drink in it, it will still be cold up to 24 hours later. It is made of steel. Steel is a strong material. The bottle holds 750 ml. In conclusion, our bottle is the ideal bottle for anyone who needs to carry a drink.*
::: solution
1. **Cut sentences that repeat or say nothing**: sentence 1 (a water bottle carries water), sentence 3 (repeats sentence 2), and the conclusion (says nothing a reader of a five-line text needs). Sentence 5 says something general about steel; keep the useful idea (*strong*) and attach it to the bottle.
2. **Combine what remains** so that each sentence gives the reader one or two facts they want.

> *Our new steel water bottle holds 750 ml and keeps drinks cold for up to 24 hours. It is strong enough for everyday use.*

The revision keeps all four facts — steel, 750 ml, cold for 24 hours, strong — in 24 words instead of 71. (Whether *strong enough for everyday use* is true is a question for the manufacturer; a product description must not claim more than the product can do.)
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Doubled words and 四字格.** Chinese prose, especially formal and official prose, often pairs near-synonyms or builds four-character phrases from two related words: 认真负责, 积极主动, 团结协作, 全面系统. These give Chinese its rhythm and are a sign of a good style. Translated word for word, they give English doubled words — *serious and responsible*, *active and proactive*, *comprehensive and systematic* — which an English reader hears as redundancy. Often one English word is enough (*conscientious*, *proactive*, *thorough*).
- **Category words.** Chinese uses general nouns such as 问题, 情况, 工作, 方面 and 措施 to name a category (这方面的问题, 做好安全工作, 采取措施). In English they become wordy: *the problem of unemployment* → *unemployment*; *carry out safety work* → *keep people safe*; *in the aspect of teaching* → *in teaching*. Contrastive studies call these 范畴词 and note that English usually leaves the category unstated (连淑能《英汉对比研究》).
- **进一步, 有效, 大力.** Adverbs of degree and manner that are common in Chinese official prose (进一步加强, 有效提高, 大力发展) become English intensifiers (*further strengthen*, *effectively improve*, *vigorously develop*). Keep them only where they carry information: *further* is right when something has already been strengthened.
- **Politeness in emails.** Chinese and English emails both use politeness formulas, but different ones. English rarely needs the long openings of formal Chinese letters, but it does need *please*, *thank you* and indirect requests, which learners sometimes cut in the name of concision ([[#rule-keep]], [[pragmatics/writing-to-people]]).
:::

::: history
Complaints about wordiness are as old as the teaching of style. Pascal's apology for a long letter dates from 1656. In 1913–14, Arthur Quiller-Couch lectured at Cambridge on "jargon", by which he meant the habit of circumlocution — *In the case of …*, *with regard to …* — and published the lectures as *On the Art of Writing* (1916), which also contains his advice to "murder your darlings", to cut the passages a writer is most proud of. Strunk's *Omit needless words* (1918) became the best-known version in America after E. B. White's revision of 1959. In the 1940s, Rudolf Flesch's *The Art of Plain Talk* (1946) and Ernest Gowers's *Plain Words* (1948) applied the idea to public writing, and George Orwell's "Politics and the English Language" (1946) made "If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out" one of its six rules. Joseph Williams's *Style* (1981) systematised the kinds of wordiness, and later critics, among them Geoffrey Pullum (2009), pointed out that *Omit needless words* is useful only to a writer who can already tell which words are needless.
:::

The next lesson, [[style-and-rhetoric/cohesion-emphasis]], turns from cutting words to ordering them. Plain words, jargon and cliché are the subject of [[style-and-rhetoric/word-choice]], and hedges in academic writing of [[modality/hedging-boosting]]. ::: summary
- Concise prose is prose in which every word does work for the reader; concision is not brevity ([[#def-concision]]).
- Strunk's *Omit needless words* asks "not that the writer make all his sentences short … but that every word tell".
- Search for six kinds of wordiness: meaningless words, doubled words, what the reader can infer, phrases for single words, unnecessary negatives and empty metadiscourse ([[#rule-six-kinds]]).
- Keep accurate hedges, one per claim; cut stacked hedges and intensifiers that add nothing, and never intensify absolutes ([[#rule-hedges]]).
- Keep metadiscourse that guides the reader through a long text; cut metadiscourse about the writer's thinking ([[#rule-metadiscourse]]).
- Do not cut politeness, accurate hedges, useful redundancy, terms of art or grammar words ([[#rule-keep]]).
- Cut from the top down: whole sentences first, then clarity revisions, then the six kinds ([[#rule-top-down]]).
:::

In this email from a student to a lecturer she has not met, which words would you keep, and which cut? Explain. *Dear Dr Ahmed, I hope you are well. I am writing to ask whether it might be possible for me to attend your seminar on Tuesday, as I am very interested in the topic. I would be really very grateful if you could let me know. Thank you very much in advance for your time and help. Best wishes, Liu Yang*
::: solution
Keep *I hope you are well* (a normal, short courtesy), *I am writing to ask whether* (it states the purpose of an email to a stranger), *it might be possible for me to* (polite indirectness to someone senior — though *I could* is also fine), *as I am interested in the topic* (a reason), and the thanks. Cut *very* in *very interested* (optional), *really very* in the third sentence, and either the third sentence or the fourth, which say almost the same thing.

> *Dear Dr Ahmed, I hope you are well. I am writing to ask whether I might attend your seminar on Tuesday, as I am interested in the topic. Thank you for your time. Best wishes, Liu Yang*

The revision is shorter but not curt: concision in a request keeps the politeness ([[#rule-keep]]).
:::

::: exercise Revise a paragraph {level=3}
Revise this paragraph from a fictional council leaflet for concision. Aim to cut at least a third of the words without losing any information, and list the kinds of wordiness you removed.

*At this point in time, the council is currently in the process of carrying out a review of the arrangements for the collection of household waste. It is important to note that the final decision has not yet been made. In the event that residents have any comments or opinions that they would like to share, they are kindly requested to submit them in writing prior to the end of the month of March. Each and every comment will be read and given careful consideration.*
::: hint
Find the characters (the council, residents) and make them subjects; then search for the six kinds.
:::
::: solution
A model:

> *The council is reviewing how it collects household waste and has not yet decided what to change. If you would like to comment, please write to us before the end of March. We will read and consider every comment.*

Removed: *At this point in time* and *currently* (both redundant with the present progressive); *in the process of carrying out a review of* (phrase for a verb: *reviewing*); *It is important to note that* (empty metadiscourse); *final* (inferable); *In the event that* (→ *If*); *comments or opinions* (doubled); *they are kindly requested to submit them in writing* → *please write to us* (a leaflet can address residents directly, and *please* keeps the politeness); *prior to* → *before*; *the month of* (redundant category); *Each and every* (doubled); *given careful consideration* → *consider*. From 85 words to 39.
:::
:::

::: exercise Concise and complete {level=3}
Write instructions (80–120 words) telling a new colleague how to book a meeting room in your school or workplace — real or imagined. Then revise them for concision, and finally check that you have not cut anything the reader needs ([[#rule-keep]]). Write one sentence about a word or phrase you decided to keep although it could be cut.
::: solution
Answers will vary. Check that:

1. each step is one sentence, in order, starting with a verb (*Open*, *Choose*, *Click*);
2. there are no doubled words, empty openings (*In order to book a room, what you need to do is …*) or meaningless words;
3. nothing the reader needs has gone: the address of the booking system, what to do if the room is taken, how far ahead to book;
4. any redundancy left is deliberate — for example, repeating *Thursday* in *The system closes on Thursday at 5 p.m., so book by Thursday afternoon*, because a missed deadline costs the reader more than two words.
:::
:::
