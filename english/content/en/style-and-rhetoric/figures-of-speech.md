Charles Dickens's *A Tale of Two Cities* (1859) begins:

> *It was the best of times, it was the worst of times, it was the age of wisdom, it was the age of foolishness, it was the epoch of belief, it was the epoch of incredulity, it was the season of Light, it was the season of Darkness, it was the spring of hope, it was the winter of despair …*

Almost every English speaker knows the first two clauses, and many could not say what the novel is about. What makes the sentence memorable is its form. Every clause begins with the same words, *it was the*; the clauses come in pairs, and each pair sets opposites against each other — *best* and *worst*, *wisdom* and *foolishness*, *Light* and *Darkness*; and the pairs pile up, ten clauses in all, until the reader feels the contradictions of the age rather than being told about them. These patterns have names: the repetition at the start of each clause is **anaphora**, the pairing of opposites in parallel form is **antithesis**, and the whole is built on the **parallelism** of [[style-and-rhetoric/sentence-variety#def-parallelism]].

Figures of speech are often taught as features of poetry and novels, and their literary uses are the subject of [[reading-literature/figurative-language]]. But they began as tools of persuasion — the classical rhetoricians catalogued them for orators — and they are still used every day in speeches, essays, journalism and advertising. This lesson describes the figures a writer of prose is most likely to use or meet, shows how they work, and gives advice on when they help and when they get in the way.

## Tropes and schemes

::: definition Figure of speech, trope and scheme {#def-figure}
A **figure of speech** is a use of language that departs from the plain, literal or ordinary pattern for an effect. Rhetoricians since Quintilian have divided figures into two groups:

- **Tropes** (Greek ‘turns’) change the meaning of words: a word is used for something other than what it literally names. Metaphor, simile, metonymy, hyperbole, understatement and irony are tropes.
- **Schemes** change the arrangement of words, while the words keep their ordinary meaning: repetition, balance, inversion, omission. Anaphora, antithesis, chiasmus and the tricolon are schemes.
:::

## Tropes

### Metaphor and simile

::: definition Metaphor and simile {#def-metaphor}
A **metaphor** describes one thing in terms of another, without a word of comparison: *The city is a machine for living in.* *Her argument has a hole in it.* A **simile** makes the comparison explicit, with *like*, *as* or *as if*: *The rumour spread like a fire through dry grass.* In both, the thing described is the **tenor** and the thing it is compared to the **vehicle** (terms introduced by the critic I. A. Richards in 1936).
:::

Aristotle thought metaphor the most important of all the resources of style. In the *Poetics* (chapter 22) he wrote, in Ingram Bywater's translation (1909), that *the greatest thing by far is to be a master of metaphor*, because a good metaphor shows a perception of likeness in things that are unlike. The linguist George Lakoff and the philosopher Mark Johnson went further in *Metaphors We Live By* (1980): much of ordinary language, they argued, is built on **conceptual metaphors** that speakers do not notice. English speakers talk about argument as war (*She attacked my position*; *His claims are indefensible*; *I won the argument*) and about time as money (*spend time*, *save time*, *waste an hour*).

::: theorem Using metaphor in prose {#rule-metaphor}
1. **Use a metaphor to explain or to make the reader see**, not to decorate: a good expository metaphor carries an idea from a familiar domain to an unfamiliar one (*The immune system has a memory*).
2. **Make it fresh**: a metaphor the reader has met a thousand times is a cliché ([[style-and-rhetoric/word-choice#def-cliche]]).
3. **Make it apt**: the vehicle should share the features that matter with the tenor, and not suggest features that mislead.
4. **Keep it consistent**: do not mix two vehicles in one sentence (✗ *The government must grasp the nettle before the ship sinks*), and if you extend a metaphor across several sentences, stop before it strains.
5. **Signal a metaphor in formal writing** if the reader might take it literally, and do not let it do the work of an argument: an analogy illustrates a claim, it does not prove it.
:::

::: proof
A metaphor asks the reader to map one domain onto another: what is true of the vehicle is, the metaphor suggests, true of the tenor. That is why it can explain so quickly — *the immune system has a memory* tells the reader in five words that the body responds faster to an infection it has met before — and also why it can mislead: the reader may carry over features the writer did not intend (does the immune system forget?). Mixed metaphors are a sign that the writer was not picturing either vehicle; a reader who pictures them sees the absurdity at once. Point 5 matters in argument: *A country is like a household and must balance its budget* is an analogy, and whether a country is like a household in the relevant ways is the question at issue, which the metaphor can hide.
:::

::: example Evaluating metaphors {#ex-metaphor}
Evaluate each metaphor or simile from fictional texts and improve it if needed. (a) In a science article: *DNA is the blueprint of life.* (b) In a report: *The new manager hit the ground running and turned the department into a well-oiled machine, steering it through stormy waters.* (c) In an essay: *Learning a language is like building a house: you must lay the foundations before you can put on the roof.*
::: solution
(a) Familiar and partly misleading. A blueprint specifies a structure exactly; DNA does not determine an organism in that way, since development depends on environment and on how genes are regulated. A writer for a general audience might keep the metaphor and qualify it (*a set of instructions, read differently in different cells*), or choose a better one.

(b) Three clichés, mixed: running, a machine, a ship. ✓ *The new manager reorganised the department within three months and kept it running smoothly through a difficult year.*

(c) An apt simile for its point — basic grammar and vocabulary come before advanced use — but limited: unlike a house, language is learned in many areas at once. Fine as an illustration, provided the essay does not treat it as an argument.
:::
:::

### Other tropes

- **Metonymy** names something by a thing closely associated with it: *The White House said …* (the US president's office), *the press* (journalists), *the crown* (the monarchy). **Synecdoche**, a kind of metonymy, names a whole by a part or a part by the whole: *all hands on deck*, *new faces in the office*.
- **Hyperbole** is deliberate exaggeration: *I've told you a thousand times.* It is normal in speech, but in writing that must be believed it costs credibility ([[style-and-rhetoric/rhetorical-appeals#rule-fair]]).
- **Understatement** says less than is meant, and **litotes** is understatement by denying the opposite: *not bad* (‘good’), *no small achievement* (‘a great achievement’). British English is often said to favour understatement; its uses and misunderstandings are discussed in [[pragmatics/irony-understatement]].
- **Irony** says one thing and means another, usually the opposite, and expects the reader to see the difference. Jane Austen's first sentence of *Pride and Prejudice* is ironic ([[style-and-rhetoric/sentence-variety]]); so is Mark Antony's *Brutus is an honourable man* ([[style-and-rhetoric/rhetorical-appeals]]).

::: quiz
Which sentence contains a **metonymy**?
- [ ] *The news hit him like a train.*
- [x] *Downing Street refused to comment.*
- [ ] *It was not the worst meal I have eaten.*
- [ ] *The classroom was a zoo.*
::: solution
*Downing Street* stands for the British prime minister's office, by association — metonymy. The first is a simile, the third litotes (understatement), the fourth a metaphor.
:::
:::

## Schemes of repetition

::: definition Anaphora, epistrophe and other repetitions {#def-repetition}
- **Anaphora** repeats a word or phrase at the beginning of successive clauses or sentences: *it was the … , it was the …*
- **Epistrophe** repeats a word or phrase at the end of successive clauses: *… of the people, by the people, for the people.*
- **Polysyndeton** repeats a conjunction (*and … and … and*); **asyndeton** omits conjunctions altogether (*I came, I saw, I conquered*).
:::

Mark Twain opened *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* (1884) with a mock warning that uses anaphora and a climax:

> *Persons attempting to find a motive in this narrative will be prosecuted; persons attempting to find a moral in it will be banished; persons attempting to find a plot in it will be shot.*

Each clause begins with the same words, so the reader's attention goes to what changes: *motive*, *moral*, *plot*, and the rising punishments. The comedy comes from the last one.

::: theorem Using repetition {#rule-repetition}
1. **Use anaphora or epistrophe to mark a series of parallel points** that the reader should hear as one argument or one feeling: the repeated words make the structure audible.
2. **Let what changes carry the meaning**: in *we cannot …, we cannot …, we cannot …*, the verbs are what the reader attends to, so they should be the words that matter.
3. **Use polysyndeton to slow down and accumulate** (*and the rain and the wind and the dark*), **asyndeton to speed up** (*fast, cheap, reliable*).
4. **Use these figures at points of emphasis** — an opening, a turn, an ending — and rarely in expository prose; repeated in every paragraph, they sound like a speech.
:::

::: proof
Repetition is the simplest scheme and the easiest to hear. In a spoken text it helps the listener, who cannot look back: a repeated opening tells them that a new item in a series has begun. In a written text it works the same way, but the reader does not need it to follow the structure, so it is felt as emphasis. The principle in point 2 is that of all parallelism: when form repeats, the reader looks for difference ([[style-and-rhetoric/sentence-variety#rule-parallel]]). Point 3 follows from rhythm: each *and* adds a beat, while asyndeton removes the beats between items. Point 4 is a matter of register; repetition is expected in speeches and sermons, accepted in essays at climactic moments, and out of place in reports.
:::

::: example Anaphora in a speech {#ex-anaphora}
A student is giving a two-minute speech asking classmates to join a tree-planting day. Her draft ending is: *The trees will give shade. They will also clean the air, and birds can live in them. Our children may enjoy them in the future.* Revise it with anaphora, and explain the choices.
::: solution
1. **Find the parallel points**: shade, clean air, homes for birds, enjoyment in the future — four benefits, the last the most moving.
2. **Choose the repeated frame**: *The trees we plant on Saturday will …* is too long to repeat; *They will …* is short, and the reader attends to the verbs.
3. **Order by force**, ending on the future ([[style-and-rhetoric/sentence-variety#rule-order]]).

> *The trees we plant on Saturday will give shade. They will clean the air. They will give birds a home. And they will still be growing when we are old.*

The repeated *They will* makes the four benefits audible as one list; the *And* before the last item and its longer, different form mark the climax. In a written report the same content would be one sentence with no repetition.
:::
:::

## Schemes of balance

::: definition Antithesis, chiasmus and the tricolon {#def-balance}
- **Antithesis** sets contrasting ideas side by side in parallel form: *It was the best of times, it was the worst of times.*
- **Chiasmus** reverses the order of parallel elements in the second half (A B, B A): *Fair is foul, and foul is fair* (Shakespeare, *Macbeth*, 1.1). When the same words return in reverse order, the figure is also called **antimetabole**: *The sabbath was made for man, and not man for the sabbath* (Mark 2:27, King James Version).
- A **tricolon** is a series of three parallel elements: *I came, I saw, I conquered* (the Latin *veni, vidi, vici*, a saying that Plutarch and Suetonius attribute to Julius Caesar). When the elements grow in length or force, it is a **tricolon crescens** or **climax**.
:::

Abraham Lincoln's Second Inaugural Address (1865), given near the end of the American Civil War, describes how the war began in two balanced clauses and a short sentence that breaks the balance:

> *Both parties deprecated war; but one of them would make war rather than let the nation survive; and the other would accept war rather than let it perish. And the war came.*

The two long clauses are parallel (*one of them would … rather than let …*; *the other would … rather than let …*) and contrast the two sides' choices; *And the war came* is four words, with no subject choosing anything. The balance shows the reader two positions held in opposition; the short sentence shows the result that neither side, in Lincoln's account, wished for. (The analysis concerns the form; the historical judgements in the speech are Lincoln's.)

::: theorem Using balance {#rule-balance}
1. **Use antithesis for a contrast you want remembered**: the two halves should be parallel in form, so that the contrast lies in the words that differ.
2. **Use chiasmus rarely**, for a reversal of perspective: it is memorable but conspicuous, and a forced chiasmus sounds clever rather than wise.
3. **Use a tricolon for completeness or climax**, with the longest or strongest element last ([[style-and-rhetoric/sentence-variety#rule-order]]). Three items feel complete; two feel like a contrast; four or more feel like a list.
4. **Make the balance true**: an antithesis that oversimplifies (*Either we cut taxes or the economy dies*) is a false dilemma dressed as a figure.
:::

::: proof
Balanced figures work because the reader compares what is presented in the same form. In an antithesis, the parallel structure isolates the contrasting words and so makes the contrast precise; in a chiasmus, the reversal makes the reader look again at the relation between two terms. The tricolon is the commonest figure in public speaking, and its power seems to come from the sense of a pattern completed: two items establish a pattern, the third confirms it, and the third position is the stress position of the series. Point 4 is the ethical warning: the neatness of an antithesis can make a false choice sound inevitable, and a careful reader asks whether the two halves really exhaust the options ([[presentations-and-discussion/debate-persuasion#def-fallacies]]).
:::

::: example Analysing a passage {#ex-gettysburg}
Analyse the figures in this sentence from Lincoln's Gettysburg Address (1863), in the text of the copy Lincoln wrote out for Colonel Alexander Bliss: *But, in a larger sense, we can not dedicate—we can not consecrate—we can not hallow—this ground.*
::: solution
1. **Anaphora**: *we can not* repeated at the start of three clauses.
2. **Tricolon with climax**: three verbs of rising solemnity — *dedicate* (set apart for a purpose), *consecrate* (make sacred), *hallow* (make holy, the oldest and most religious word of the three).
3. **Asyndeton**: no *and* between the items; the dashes slow the rhythm and give each verb its own beat.
4. **Periodic suspense**: the object, *this ground*, is held back to the end, the stress position.
5. **Effect**: the sentence denies the speakers' own power to do what the occasion was for — the dedication of a cemetery — and prepares for the next sentence, which gives that power to the soldiers who died there.
:::
:::

::: example Writing balanced sentences {#ex-write-balance}
Rewrite each sentence as an antithesis, a chiasmus or a tricolon, as indicated, and say whether the figure suits a formal essay. (a) Antithesis: *The scheme costs a lot at the beginning, but it saves money over time.* (b) Tricolon: *The new timetable is good for students because they sleep more, and they also learn better, and attendance improves.* (c) Chiasmus: *We should adapt our methods to the learners rather than expecting learners to fit our methods.*
::: solution
(a) *The scheme is expensive to start and cheap to run.* Parallel adjectives and infinitives isolate the contrast. Suitable in an essay.

(b) *A later start would let students sleep longer, learn better and attend more often.* Three parallel verb phrases, the longest last. Suitable, once in an essay.

(c) *We should fit our methods to our learners, not our learners to our methods.* A chiasmus (antimetabole); memorable but conspicuous. Suitable as the last sentence of an essay or a speech, not in the middle of a paragraph.
:::
:::

## The rhetorical question

::: definition Rhetorical question {#def-rhetorical-question}
A **rhetorical question** is a question asked not to get an answer but to make a point, because the answer is obvious or because the question itself is the claim: *Who would choose to be ill?* A related figure, **hypophora**, asks a question and then answers it: *Why does this matter? Because …*
:::

Samuel Johnson's letter of 1755 to Lord Chesterfield, who had offered praise of Johnson's *Dictionary* only when it was about to be published, after years of neglect, contains one of the most famous rhetorical questions in English:

> *Is not a Patron, my Lord, one who looks with unconcern on a man struggling for life in the water, and when he has reached ground, encumbers him with help?*

The question is a definition and an accusation at once; to answer it, Chesterfield would have to agree. Shylock's speech in *The Merchant of Venice* (3.1) uses a series of rhetorical questions with anaphora and a climax: *If you prick us, do we not bleed? if you tickle us, do we not laugh? if you poison us, do we not die? and if you wrong us, shall we not revenge?* The first three questions have answers no listener can deny; the fourth uses their momentum to claim something far more disputable.

::: theorem Using rhetorical questions {#rule-questions}
1. **Use a rhetorical question when the answer is genuinely obvious to the audience**, or to frame the question that a section will answer (hypophora).
2. **Do not use one in place of a claim** in academic and formal writing: ✗ *So is homework useful?* as the end of a paragraph leaves the reader to supply the writer's view ([[academic-writing/academic-style#rule-formality]]).
3. **Beware of the question that smuggles in a disputable answer**: if the audience might answer differently, state the claim and argue for it.
:::

::: proof
A rhetorical question involves the reader: they answer it in their head, and an answer they have given themselves feels more convincing than one they were told. That is its power and its danger. When the answer is obvious, the question makes a shared assumption vivid. When it is not, the question pretends that it is, and a reader who answers differently feels manipulated. Academic writing avoids rhetorical questions because its readers expect claims to be stated and supported; speeches, opinion journalism and advertising use them freely ([[presentations-and-discussion/debate-persuasion]]). Shylock's series shows the technique of building on undeniable answers to carry a deniable one.
:::

::: warning Figures as decoration
✗ *Education is the key that unlocks the door to the future, the light that guides us through the darkness, and the bridge that connects the generations. Is there anything more important? Education, education, education!*

Three metaphors, a rhetorical question and a repetition in three sentences, and no claim the reader can test. Figures concentrate attention on a point; without a point, they draw attention to themselves. Use one figure where the idea deserves emphasis, and give the reader an argument first.
:::

::: quiz
Which figure does *The council should serve the town, not the town the council* mainly use?
- [ ] anaphora
- [ ] hyperbole
- [x] chiasmus (antimetabole)
- [ ] metonymy
::: solution
The words *the council* and *the town* return in reverse order in the second half (A B, B A), with the verb *serve* understood: *the council … the town* / *the town … the council*.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **修辞格.** Chinese rhetoric classifies figures as 修辞格; 陈望道's 《修辞学发凡》 (1932), which set out thirty-eight of them, is the basis of the classification taught in Chinese schools. Many correspond closely to English figures: 比喻 (with 明喻 ‘simile’ and 暗喻 ‘metaphor’), 借代 ‘metonymy’, 夸张 ‘hyperbole’, 反语 ‘irony’, 反问 ‘rhetorical question’, 设问 ‘hypophora’, 层递 ‘climax’.
- **对偶 and antithesis.** 对偶 pairs two phrases or lines of equal length and matching structure. The pair may be similar in meaning (正对), opposed (反对) or continuous (串对); only 反对 corresponds closely to English antithesis. English antithesis also requires parallel form, but not equal length or word-for-word matching ([[style-and-rhetoric/sentence-variety]]).
- **排比 and anaphora.** 排比 — three or more parallel units, often with a repeated word — combines what English calls parallelism, anaphora and the tricolon. Chinese school essays and speeches often use 排比 at length; in English expository writing, a three-part parallel once in a paragraph is usually enough.
- **回环 and chiasmus.** The Chinese figure 回环 (or 回文 in the broader sense) repeats words in reverse order, as in 人人为我，我为人人 ‘everyone for me, me for everyone’ — the same pattern as English antimetabole.
- **顶真 and anadiplosis.** 顶真 (also 联珠) begins each clause with the last word of the previous one; English rhetoric calls this anadiplosis, but English prose uses it far less than Chinese, mostly for comic or solemn effect.
- **Translating figures.** A Chinese 排比 or 对偶 translated word for word into English often sounds overwrought, and an English chiasmus may lose its point in Chinese. Translate the effect, not the form, where the form does not carry over ([[translation/literary-translation]]).
:::

::: history
The classical rhetoricians catalogued figures as tools for orators. The anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium* (usually dated to the 80s BC) listed dozens of "figures of diction" and "figures of thought" with examples, and Quintilian's *Institutio oratoria* (about AD 95), book 8–9, distinguished tropes from figures and became the authority for later writers. Renaissance English schoolboys learned the figures from Latin textbooks, and English handbooks soon followed: Henry Peacham's *The Garden of Eloquence* (1577) described and illustrated a great many, and *The Arte of English Poesie* (1589), usually attributed to George Puttenham, gave many of them English names. Shakespeare's plays, written for audiences trained in this tradition, are full of figures, often used for characterisation and sometimes mocked. The modern study of metaphor was transformed by I. A. Richards's *The Philosophy of Rhetoric* (1936) and by George Lakoff and Mark Johnson's *Metaphors We Live By* (1980), which treated metaphor as a structure of thought, not only of style. In China, 陈望道's 《修辞学发凡》 (1932) founded the modern classification of 修辞格.
:::

## Where this leads

The structure of a whole persuasive text, and how figures are used within it, is the subject of [[style-and-rhetoric/persuasive-writing]]. Figures in poetry and fiction are studied in [[reading-literature/figurative-language]], irony and understatement in conversation in [[pragmatics/irony-understatement]], idioms (many of them dead metaphors) in [[collocation-and-register/idioms]], and translating figures in [[translation/literary-translation]].

::: summary
- Figures of speech are departures from plain usage for effect; tropes change meaning, schemes change arrangement ([[#def-figure]]).
- A metaphor describes one thing in terms of another, a simile makes the comparison explicit; in prose, use fresh, apt, consistent metaphors that explain, and never let an analogy stand in for an argument ([[#def-metaphor]], [[#rule-metaphor]]).
- Anaphora and epistrophe repeat words at the start or end of clauses; polysyndeton slows and asyndeton speeds; use them at points of emphasis ([[#def-repetition]], [[#rule-repetition]]).
- Antithesis contrasts in parallel form, chiasmus reverses, the tricolon completes a series of three; balance must be true to the facts ([[#def-balance]], [[#rule-balance]]).
- Rhetorical questions make a point when the answer is obvious; they do not replace claims in formal writing ([[#def-rhetorical-question]], [[#rule-questions]]).
- Chinese 修辞格 such as 比喻, 对偶, 排比 and 反问 have English counterparts, but English expository prose uses schemes more sparingly and less symmetrically.
:::

::: exercise Analyse an antithesis {level=2}
Analyse the form of Brutus's line from *Julius Caesar*: *Not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more.* Which words are parallel, and which contrast?
::: solution
The two halves are parallel: *that I loved* + object + comparative adverb. The contrasting words are the objects (*Caesar* / *Rome*) and the comparatives (*less* / *more*). The figure is antithesis; it presents Brutus's choice as a matter of degree between two loves rather than a betrayal. Its neatness is part of its persuasion: it makes a political killing sound like a reasonable preference.
:::
:::

::: exercise Write a tricolon {level=2}
Write a tricolon for each purpose, putting the strongest element last. (a) The benefits of a city park. (b) The costs of a long commute. (c) What a good teacher does.
::: solution
Possible answers: (a) *A park gives the city shade in summer, space for children to play, and a place where neighbours who would never otherwise meet sit side by side.* (b) *A long commute costs money, time and, for many, their health.* (c) *A good teacher explains, encourages and, when it matters most, believes in a student before the student does.* Check that the three elements are parallel in form and that the last is the longest or strongest.
:::
:::

::: exercise Rhetorical questions {level=2}
For each rhetorical question, decide whether the answer is obvious to most readers, and rewrite it as a statement if not. (a) In a speech at a school: *Who here does not want a safer route to school?* (b) In an essay: *Is it not clear that social media has ruined a generation?* (c) In an article: *Why do so many people still drive to work? Because the buses are unreliable.*
::: solution
(a) Obvious to the audience: acceptable in a speech. (b) Not obvious, and the claim is disputable: ✓ *Some researchers argue that heavy use of social media harms teenagers' mental health, though the evidence is debated.* (c) Hypophora: the question frames the answer, which is stated. Acceptable in an article; in an academic essay, a statement is usually better: *Many people still drive to work because the buses are unreliable.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Analyse a passage {level=3}
Analyse the figures of speech in Shylock's lines from *The Merchant of Venice* (3.1) quoted in this lesson: *If you prick us, do we not bleed? if you tickle us, do we not laugh? if you poison us, do we not die? and if you wrong us, shall we not revenge?* Name at least four figures and explain how the series persuades.
::: hint
Look at the beginnings of the clauses, the kind of question, the number of items and the change in the last one.
:::
::: solution
1. **Rhetorical questions**: each expects the answer *yes*.
2. **Anaphora**: *if you … us* begins every clause; the repeated frame makes the verbs (*prick*, *tickle*, *poison*, *wrong*) and the answers (*bleed*, *laugh*, *die*, *revenge*) stand out.
3. **Parallelism**: each question has the same conditional structure.
4. **Climax**: the actions rise from trivial (*prick*, *tickle*) to fatal (*poison*) to moral (*wrong*).
5. **Polysyndeton** at the turn: *and if you wrong us* — the *and* marks the last item as the culmination.

**Persuasion.** The first three questions concern physical facts no listener can deny: Jews and Christians share a common humanity. The fourth moves from the body to morality and uses the momentum of three undeniable answers to claim a right of revenge. The figures make the step feel inevitable; logically, it does not follow from the first three. The speech is often quoted for its plea for common humanity, and it is also a study in how a series of figures can carry an audience from agreement to a conclusion they might otherwise reject.
:::

::: exercise Revise for restraint {level=3}
Revise this fictional conclusion of an essay on public libraries so that it keeps one or two figures that serve the argument and removes the rest. Explain your choices.

*Libraries are the beating heart of the community, the lighthouse in the storm, the treasure chest of knowledge. Can we really let them die? Without libraries, where will children read? Where will the lonely find company? Where will the curious find answers? We must act now, before it is too late, before the last page is turned, before the lights go out forever!*
::: solution
A model:

> *Libraries give children books their families cannot afford, give lonely people a warm place to be among others, and give anyone with a question the means to answer it. Closing them would save the council a small part of its budget and cost the town far more.*

Kept: a tricolon (*give children …, give lonely people …, give anyone …*), which turns the three rhetorical questions of the original into claims a reader can test; and an antithesis (*save … a small part / cost … far more*), which states the essay's conclusion. Removed: three clichéd metaphors (*beating heart*, *lighthouse*, *treasure chest*), the rhetorical question *Can we really let them die?*, which assumes the answer, and the closing tricolon of clichés (*before it is too late …*), which adds feeling but no argument.
