Three students want their school to install drinking-water fountains in the playground. Each writes a short letter to the head teacher.

> **Ananya:** *I have been the school's health representative for two years, and I have read the council's guidance on water in schools. I know that the budget is tight, and I have tried to suggest a plan that the school could afford.*

> **Tomás:** *Last July, on the hottest day of the year, a Year 7 boy fainted on the playing field. He had finished his water at break, and the nearest tap was in a locked building.*

> **Mei:** *Pupils who drink enough water concentrate better. Two fountains would cost less than a set of new textbooks, and they would last for years. The school would also save on the plastic bottles it now buys for sports days.*

Each letter tries to persuade, and each does it differently. Ananya asks the head teacher to trust *her*: she shows that she knows the subject, that she has been fair and that she has the school's interests at heart. Tomás makes the reader *feel* the problem, through one specific case. Mei gives *reasons*: a benefit, a comparison of costs, a saving. The best letter would probably use all three.

These are the three means of persuasion that Aristotle identified in his *Rhetoric* in the fourth century BC: **ethos**, the character of the speaker; **pathos**, the feelings of the audience; and **logos**, the argument itself. This lesson explains each, shows how writers use them in essays, letters and speeches, and asks the question that matters most for a careful writer and reader: when is an appeal fair, and when does it manipulate? The structure of an argument — claim, reasons, evidence, warrant — is taught in [[essay-writing/arguing-a-point]] and [[presentations-and-discussion/debate-persuasion#def-toulmin]], and common faults of reasoning in [[presentations-and-discussion/debate-persuasion#def-fallacies]]; this lesson builds on both.

## Rhetoric and its three appeals

::: definition Rhetoric {#def-rhetoric}
**Rhetoric** is the art of using language to persuade or influence an audience. Aristotle defined it as a capacity: in W. Rhys Roberts's translation (1924), *Rhetoric may be defined as the faculty of observing in any given case the available means of persuasion* (*Rhetoric* I.2). In modern use, *rhetoric* also has a negative sense, ‘language that sounds impressive but is insincere or empty’ (*mere rhetoric*); this lesson uses the older, neutral sense.
:::

Aristotle went on to name the three means, in the same translation:

> *Of the modes of persuasion furnished by the spoken word there are three kinds. The first kind depends on the personal character of the speaker; the second on putting the audience into a certain frame of mind; the third on the proof, or apparent proof, provided by the words of the speech itself.*

::: definition Ethos, pathos and logos {#def-appeals}
- **Ethos** (Greek ‘character’) is persuasion through the character of the speaker or writer as it appears in the text: the audience believes the argument partly because it trusts the person making it.
- **Pathos** (‘experience, emotion’) is persuasion through the feelings of the audience: the audience is moved to accept or act because it feels pity, anger, fear, hope or pride.
- **Logos** (‘word, reason’) is persuasion through the argument itself: reasons, evidence, examples and the inferences that connect them.

The three are called the **rhetorical appeals** or the **modes of persuasion**. Aristotle's word for them, *pisteis*, is usually translated ‘proofs’ or ‘means of persuasion’.
:::

::: theorem Use all three, in proportion {#rule-proportion}
1. **Most effective persuasion uses all three appeals**: an argument (logos) made by someone the audience trusts (ethos) about something the audience cares about (pathos).
2. **Fit the proportion to the audience, the purpose and the genre**: an academic paper relies mostly on logos, with ethos built through accuracy and citation; a charity appeal uses more pathos; a letter of complaint needs ethos (reasonableness) and logos (facts) with a little pathos.
3. **Make each appeal serve the argument**: feeling and character should support a conclusion that the reasons also support, not replace the reasons.
:::

::: proof
Aristotle presented the three as working together, and he held that the argument was the most important. But he also observed that an audience does not judge an argument in isolation: it judges whether the speaker seems to know the subject and to mean well, and its judgement is affected by its state of mind: "Our judgements when we are pleased and friendly are not the same as when we are pained and hostile", in Rhys Roberts's translation (*Rhetoric* I.2). Modern research on persuasion has broadly confirmed that the perceived credibility of a source and the emotional framing of a message affect how people respond, alongside the strength of the arguments. Point 2 follows from Aristotle's own division of speeches by occasion (see [[#rule-occasions]]): different situations call for different mixes. Point 3 is the ethical limit, discussed at the end of the lesson ([[#rule-fair]]).
:::

::: quiz
*As a nurse who has worked night shifts for fifteen years, I can tell you that the new rota is unsafe.* Which appeal does the first part of this sentence mainly use?
- [x] ethos
- [ ] pathos
- [ ] logos
- [ ] none of them
::: solution
*As a nurse who has worked night shifts for fifteen years* establishes the writer's experience and therefore her authority to judge — ethos. The sentence does not yet give a reason (logos) or appeal to feelings (pathos); the reasons should follow.
:::
:::

## Ethos: character in the text

For Aristotle, ethos was not the speaker's reputation before speaking but the character that the speech itself displays: "persuasion is achieved by the speaker's personal character when the speech is so spoken as to make us think him credible" (*Rhetoric* I.2). In book II he named its three components: good sense (*phronēsis*), good moral character (*aretē*) and goodwill towards the audience (*eunoia*).

::: theorem Building ethos in writing {#rule-ethos}
A writer shows:

1. **good sense (competence)** by accurate detail, correct facts and figures, sources the reader can check, and the right technical terms ([[style-and-rhetoric/word-choice#rule-jargon]]);
2. **good character (fairness)** by stating opposing views accurately and answering them ([[essay-writing/counter-arguments]]), admitting what is uncertain, and not exaggerating ([[modality/hedging-boosting]]);
3. **goodwill** by addressing the reader's interests and concerns, not only the writer's, and by a tone that respects the reader ([[pragmatics/politeness-face]]);
4. **care** through the style itself: a text with errors, inconsistent register or careless formatting tells the reader that the writer is careless, and the reader may infer that the argument is careless too.
:::

::: proof
A reader cannot check most claims for themselves, so they decide how far to trust a text partly on signs of the writer's reliability. Each point gives such a sign. Accurate detail shows knowledge, and a single detectable error damages trust in everything else: a reader who catches the writer misquoting one figure will doubt the others. Fairness to the other side shows that the writer has considered it and is not simply arguing a brief; O'Keefe's review of persuasion studies, discussed in [[presentations-and-discussion/debate-persuasion#rule-persuade]], found that two-sided messages that refute the other side tend to persuade better than one-sided ones. Goodwill shows that the writer's conclusion is not merely in their own interest. Point 4 is why style matters to persuasion: Williams and Bizup end *Style* with a lesson on the ethics of style, arguing that a writer's choices about clarity are also choices about how they treat the reader.
:::

::: example Strengthening ethos {#ex-ethos}
Revise this fictional opening of a letter to a local newspaper so that it builds ethos rather than damaging it. *Everyone knows the council's new parking charges are a complete disaster. Only an idiot would think that charging £5 an hour will help the high street. The council never listens to anybody.*
::: solution
1. **Competence.** *Everyone knows* and *a complete disaster* assert without evidence. Replace them with a fact the writer can check: the writer runs a shop and has counted customers, say.
2. **Fairness.** *Only an idiot* attacks the people who disagree instead of their argument (an *ad hominem*: [[presentations-and-discussion/debate-persuasion#def-fallacies]]). State the council's reason fairly.
3. **Goodwill.** *The council never listens to anybody* is an overgeneralisation that tells the council there is no point in replying. Address the reader — the council and other residents — as people who can be persuaded.

> *I have run a bookshop on the high street for twelve years, and since the new parking charges began in March, the number of customers on weekday mornings has fallen by about a third. I understand the council's aim — to reduce traffic in the centre — but for small shops like mine, the charges are working against the high street they are meant to protect. I hope the council will look again at the rate for short stays.*

(The figures are invented for the exercise; a real letter should give the writer's own.)
:::
:::

::: warning Claiming ethos instead of showing it
✗ *Trust me, I am an expert, and I can assure you that this is true.*
✓ *In a survey of 400 households that I carried out for the council in 2024, …*

Telling the reader to trust you is weak ethos, and often has the opposite effect. Show the basis of your authority — your experience, your data, your sources — and let the reader draw the conclusion.
:::

## Pathos: the audience's feelings

::: theorem Using pathos {#rule-pathos}
1. **Move through specific cases**: one person, one place, one moment described concretely is more moving than a statistic ([[style-and-rhetoric/word-choice#rule-concrete]]). Pair it with the general evidence, so that the reader knows the case is typical.
2. **Appeal to values the audience already holds** — fairness, safety, the welfare of children, pride in a place — and show how the argument serves them.
3. **Choose words for their connotation** with care ([[collocation-and-register/connotation]]): *home* and *house*, *gamble* and *invest* move the reader in different directions.
4. **Keep pathos proportional** to the facts and to the genre: a feeling the reader thinks is exaggerated turns into distrust.
:::

::: proof
Aristotle devoted much of book II of the *Rhetoric* to the emotions — anger, calm, fear, confidence, pity and others — and to the circumstances in which audiences feel them, because he thought a speaker needed to understand them to persuade at all. Point 1 is the most important for writers. Readers respond more strongly to an individual case than to numbers, a tendency psychologists have studied as the "identifiable victim effect"; that is why Tomás's letter about one boy who fainted is more moving than a statement that dehydration is common. But an individual case proves nothing about how common something is, so a fair writer joins pathos to logos: the case makes the reader care, the figures show that the case is not unusual. Point 4 marks the difference between appeal and manipulation: the feeling should fit the facts.
:::

::: example Pathos in proportion {#ex-pathos}
A fictional student writes to the university asking for a later library closing time: *Students are literally dying of stress because the library shuts at nine! It is a tragedy that nobody cares about our futures!!* Revise it so that the feeling is proportional and joined to evidence.
::: solution
1. **Proportion.** *literally dying* and *a tragedy* are exaggerations that a reader will discount; the double exclamation mark signals anger rather than a reason. Name a real, specific difficulty instead.
2. **A concrete case.** One student's evening shows the problem: a part-time job, a group project, nowhere quiet to work after nine.
3. **Logos beside it.** Show that the case is typical — for example, a count of how many students are still in the library at closing time.

> *Many students work part-time in the evenings, and for them the library's nine o'clock closing leaves little time to study. One second-year student in my group finishes her shift at eight and has one hour to work before she must leave. Last week, library staff counted over two hundred students still at their desks at closing time.*

(The count is invented for the exercise; a real letter would give a real one.) The revised letter still appeals to feeling — sympathy for a student with too little time — but the feeling now fits the facts.
:::
:::

Shakespeare's *Julius Caesar* (first printed in the First Folio, 1623) gives a famous demonstration of pathos in Mark Antony's speech over Caesar's body (act 3, scene 2). Antony has promised the conspirators not to blame them, and he keeps the promise in words while breaking it in effect. He reminds the crowd of Caesar's generosity, then holds up Caesar's cloak:

> *If you have tears, prepare to shed them now.*
> *You all do know this mantle: I remember*
> *The first time ever Caesar put it on;*
> *'Twas on a summer's evening, in his tent,*
> *That day he overcame the Nervii.*

The cloak is a concrete object the crowd can see; the memory makes it personal; the date ties it to a victory the crowd is proud of. Only then does Antony show them the holes the daggers made. By the end of the speech the crowd, which had cheered Brutus minutes before, is ready to riot. The speech is a model of pathos — and, because Antony uses it to stir a mob, a warning about it.

## Logos: the argument

::: definition Enthymeme and example {#def-enthymeme}
Aristotle named two forms of reasoning in rhetoric:

- An **enthymeme** is an argument in which one premise is left unstated because the audience can be expected to supply it: *We should install fountains, because pupils who drink enough water concentrate better* leaves unstated the premise *The school should do what helps pupils concentrate*.
- An **example** (*paradeigma*) argues from one or more particular cases to a general conclusion or to another case: *A neighbouring school installed fountains and its pupils now drink more water, so ours would too.*
:::

::: theorem Making the logos visible {#rule-logos}
1. **State the claim, the reasons and the evidence**, in an order the reader can follow ([[essay-writing/arguing-a-point#rule-paragraph-shape]]).
2. **Find the unstated premise** of each enthymeme and ask whether the audience accepts it. If they might not, state it and support it.
3. **Check that the reasoning is valid**: that the conclusion follows from the premises. A common invalid form is **affirming the consequent**: *If the policy works, crime will fall. Crime has fallen. So the policy works* — crime may have fallen for another reason.
4. **Use examples that are typical**, and say how far one case can be generalised.
:::

::: proof
The enthymeme is the characteristic argument of public persuasion because speakers rarely have time, or need, to state every premise; the audience supplies what it already believes. That is efficient when the audience shares the premise and dangerous when it does not, because the speaker's argument then seems to the audience to have a gap. Toulmin's later model makes the same point with the term *warrant* — the principle that connects the evidence to the claim, often unstated ([[presentations-and-discussion/debate-persuasion#def-toulmin]]). Point 3 belongs to logic: a valid argument is one in which the conclusion must be true if the premises are. The figure below shows the difference between a valid form (*If p then q; p; so q*) and the invalid one in point 3 (*If p then q; q; so p*). Many persuasive texts use the invalid form, often without the writer noticing, because it sounds like the valid one.
:::

::: widget truthtable
title: A valid and an invalid argument
formula: ((p -> q) & p) -> q
compare: ((p -> q) & q) -> p
caption: Let *p* = *the policy works* and *q* = *crime falls*. The first formula says: if *if p then q* and *p* are both true, then *q* is true. Its final column is true in every row: the form (called *modus ponens*) is valid. The second formula — *if p then q*, and *q*, therefore *p* — is false in the row where *p* is false and *q* is true: the policy did not work, but crime fell anyway, for some other reason. That row is why affirming the consequent is invalid.
:::

::: example Finding the unstated premise {#ex-enthymeme}
Find the unstated premise in each argument, and say whether a sceptical reader would accept it. (a) *The city should ban cars from the centre, because cars cause most of its air pollution.* (b) *This book must be good: it won a major prize.* (c) *Homework should be abolished, because Finland gives little homework and its pupils do well.*
::: solution
(a) Unstated: *The city should ban whatever causes most of its air pollution* — or, more charitably, *Reducing air pollution justifies a ban on cars*. A sceptic might accept that pollution should be reduced but not that a ban is the right way; the writer must argue that it is.

(b) Unstated: *Books that win major prizes are good.* Often true, but prize juries disagree with each other and with readers; a sceptic would want reasons from the book itself.

(c) An argument from example. Unstated: *What works in Finland would work here*, and *Finnish pupils do well because of the small amount of homework*. A sceptic would question both: education systems differ in many ways, and one case does not show cause.
:::
:::

### Appeals and occasions

::: theorem The three occasions of rhetoric {#rule-occasions}
Aristotle divided speeches into three kinds by their audience and their time (*Rhetoric* I.3):

1. **Deliberative** rhetoric addresses an audience that must decide what to do — an assembly, a committee, voters — and concerns the **future**: it argues that something is advantageous or harmful.
2. **Forensic** (judicial) rhetoric addresses an audience that must judge what was done — a court — and concerns the **past**: it accuses or defends.
3. **Epideictic** (ceremonial) rhetoric addresses an audience at a ceremony — a funeral, a celebration, a prize-giving — and concerns the **present**: it praises or blames, and affirms shared values.

The mix of appeals changes with the occasion: deliberative texts lean on logos (consequences), forensic on logos and ethos (evidence and credibility), epideictic on pathos and shared values.
:::

The division still describes modern genres well: a policy proposal or a letter to a council is deliberative; a complaint, an appeal against a decision or a review of what went wrong is forensic; a eulogy, a speech at a graduation or an anniversary tribute is epideictic.

## Fair and unfair appeals

::: theorem When an appeal is fair {#rule-fair}
An appeal is fair when it is:

1. **true** — the credentials, the case, the facts are real and accurately stated;
2. **relevant** — the character, feeling or evidence bears on the question at issue;
3. **proportional** — the strength of the feeling or the confidence of the claim fits the evidence.

Appeals that fail these tests — attacking a person instead of the argument, fear out of proportion to the risk, loaded words in place of reasons, an unrepresentative case presented as typical, an authority speaking outside their field — manipulate rather than persuade.
:::

::: proof
The appeals are tools, and every one can be misused. Ethos becomes manipulation when the authority is invented or irrelevant (a famous actor's opinion on medicine), or when the writer attacks the opponent's character instead of answering the argument. Pathos becomes manipulation when the feeling is out of proportion to the facts, or when a moving case is not typical. Logos can be misused too, by invalid reasoning that looks valid, or by numbers chosen to mislead. Aristotle himself defended rhetoric against the charge that it teaches deception by arguing that a speaker must know the tricks in order to answer them (*Rhetoric* I.1). The same holds for readers today: recognising how an appeal works is the best defence against being manipulated by it.
:::

::: quiz
Which appeal is **unfair**?
- [ ] A doctor citing her clinical experience in an article about a disease she treats
- [ ] A charity describing one real family it has helped, alongside figures on how many families it helps each year
- [x] An advertisement for a car insurer showing a burning house to persuade drivers to buy its car insurance
- [ ] An essay pointing out that a proposal has failed in two other cities, with sources
::: solution
The burning house is an appeal to fear that is irrelevant to the product (car insurance does not cover houses) and out of proportion — it fails tests 2 and 3 of [[#rule-fair]]. The others use credentials in the right field, a case set beside typical figures, and examples with sources.
:::
:::

::: example Analysing the appeals of a speech {#ex-brutus}
In *Julius Caesar* (act 3, scene 2), Brutus speaks to the crowd before Antony. He begins (in prose):

> *Romans, countrymen, and lovers! hear me for my cause, and be silent, that you may hear: believe me for mine honour, and have respect to mine honour, that you may believe: censure me in your wisdom, and awake your senses, that you may the better judge.*

Later he gives his reason for killing Caesar: *Not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more.* Which appeals does Brutus use, and how do they compare with Antony's?
::: solution
1. **Ethos.** Brutus asks the crowd to believe him *for mine honour* — an explicit appeal to his own character, which is his main asset: the Romans know him as honourable.
2. **Logos.** He asks them to *judge* with *wisdom* and *senses*: he presents himself as reasoning with them. His central argument is a balanced contrast (*not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more*): the good of the state outweighs loyalty to one man.
3. **Pathos.** Brutus uses little; his prose is balanced, controlled and abstract (*honour*, *Rome*).
4. **Comparison.** Antony answers in verse, with concrete objects (the cloak, the wounds, the will) and repeated irony (*Brutus is an honourable man*), which turns Brutus's ethos against him. Brutus appeals to principle, Antony to feeling and to the crowd's self-interest. In the play, Antony wins — which is one reason the scene is used to teach rhetoric: it shows that an abstract appeal to honour can lose to a concrete appeal to feeling, whatever the merits of the case.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **以理服人, 以情动人.** Chinese has its own common formulation of two of the appeals: 以理服人 ‘win people over by reason’ and 以情动人 ‘move people by feeling’, often joined in advice on speaking and writing (晓之以理，动之以情). Ethos has a classical counterpart too: the phrase 修辞立其诚 from the 《周易·文言》, usually read as ‘in cultivating one's words, establish one's sincerity’, ties good speech to the speaker's integrity. The Greek and Chinese traditions developed separately, and their terms are not exact equivalents, but they notice similar things.
- **Authority and quotation.** In Chinese essays, quoting a classical author or a famous saying can build ethos and show learning. In English argument, a quotation from an authority counts as evidence only if the authority is relevant and the quotation is explained; an English reader asks *why* the authority is right ([[presentations-and-discussion/debate-persuasion]]). Use quotations as support for reasoning, not instead of it.
- **Modesty and ethos.** Chinese writing conventions often value modesty (拙见, 浅谈), and learners sometimes carry this into English as excessive hedging or apology (*I am only a student, so my opinion may be wrong, but …*). In English, ethos comes from competence and fairness; a writer who belittles their own view gives the reader a reason to ignore it. Hedge claims according to the evidence, not according to the writer's status ([[modality/hedging-boosting]]).
- **Emotional appeals in exams.** English exam essays and academic writing generally reward logos far more than pathos. A paragraph that ends with an exclamation or a call to the reader's heart (*Let us all work together for a better tomorrow!*) can seem out of place, however natural its Chinese counterpart would be in a speech.
:::

::: history
Aristotle's *Rhetoric*, written in the fourth century BC from lectures he gave in Athens, set out the three modes of persuasion and the three kinds of speech, and devoted its third book to style and arrangement. Roman rhetoric built on it: the anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium* (usually dated to the 80s BC) and Cicero's works divided the art into five parts — invention, arrangement, style, memory and delivery — and Cicero described the orator's duties as to teach, to delight and to move (*docere*, *delectare*, *movere*, in *De optimo genere oratorum*), roughly logos, ethos and pathos. Quintilian's *Institutio oratoria* (about AD 95) became the standard Roman textbook. In English, George Campbell's *The Philosophy of Rhetoric* (1776) rebuilt the subject on the psychology of his day, describing persuasion as addressed to the understanding, the imagination, the passions and the will. In the twentieth century, Chaïm Perelman and Lucie Olbrechts-Tyteca's *La nouvelle rhétorique* (1958) and Stephen Toulmin's *The Uses of Argument* (1958) returned to Aristotle's view that everyday argument must be judged by its audience and its field, not by the standards of formal logic alone.
:::

## Where this leads

The figures of speech that give appeals their force — metaphor, antithesis, the tricolon, the rhetorical question — are in [[style-and-rhetoric/figures-of-speech]], and the structure of a whole persuasive text or speech in [[style-and-rhetoric/persuasive-writing]]. Argument in essays is taught in [[essay-writing/arguing-a-point]] and [[essay-writing/counter-arguments]], debate and the analysis of reasoning in [[presentations-and-discussion/debate-persuasion]], and evaluating claims as a reader in [[academic-reading/claims-and-evidence]].

::: summary
- Rhetoric, in Aristotle's sense, is the capacity to see the available means of persuasion in a case; it is not "mere rhetoric" ([[#def-rhetoric]]).
- The three appeals are ethos (the character of the writer as shown in the text), pathos (the feelings of the audience) and logos (the argument) ([[#def-appeals]]); most effective persuasion uses all three, in proportion ([[#rule-proportion]]).
- Writers build ethos by competence, fairness, goodwill and care; showing authority works better than claiming it ([[#rule-ethos]]).
- Pathos works best through specific cases tied to shared values, joined to evidence that the case is typical ([[#rule-pathos]]).
- Logos is reasoning; rhetorical arguments are often enthymemes with unstated premises, which a writer must check, and invalid forms such as affirming the consequent must be avoided ([[#def-enthymeme]], [[#rule-logos]]).
- Deliberative, forensic and epideictic occasions call for different mixes of appeals ([[#rule-occasions]]).
- An appeal is fair when it is true, relevant and proportional ([[#rule-fair]]).
:::

Is each argument valid? (a) *If it rained, the match was cancelled. It rained. So the match was cancelled.* (b) *If it rained, the match was cancelled. The match was cancelled. So it rained.* (c) *All the students who passed attended the revision class. Lin passed. So Lin attended the revision class.*

(a) Valid (*modus ponens*). (b) Invalid (affirming the consequent): the match could have been cancelled for another reason. (c) Valid, if the first premise is true.

::: solution
(a) *If two laptops both do what we need, we should buy the cheaper one.* (Most readers would accept it, unless quality or durability matters.) (b) *Young teachers are not good teachers* — an overgeneralisation a sceptic would reject. (c) *Things paid for by taxes should be free to use.* Many would accept it; others would point to roads with tolls or to the cost of upkeep.
:::

Possible additions — logos: *According to the city council's 2025 survey, one child in eight lives in a household that sometimes cannot afford enough food* (in a real letter, the figure and source must be real). Say whether each appeal is fair, and why. (a) A dentist in an advertisement for toothpaste. (b) A famous footballer in an advertisement for a pension fund. ::: exercise Analyse the appeals {level=3}
Analyse the appeals in Mark Antony's lines quoted in this lesson and in the following lines from the same speech: *He was my friend, faithful and just to me: / But Brutus says he was ambitious; / And Brutus is an honourable man.* What does the repetition of *Brutus is an honourable man* do to Brutus's ethos?
::: hint
Antony never says that Brutus is dishonourable. What does the crowd infer, and why?
:::
::: solution
Antony sets his personal knowledge of Caesar (*my friend, faithful and just to me* — Antony's own ethos as a witness, and pathos) against Brutus's bare claim (*Brutus says*). The phrase *Brutus is an honourable man*, repeated after each piece of evidence that Caesar was not ambitious (the captives' ransoms, his weeping for the poor, his refusal of the crown), comes to mean the opposite of what it says: if Caesar was not ambitious, the honourable Brutus was wrong — or lied. The repetition is irony, and it destroys the ethos on which Brutus's whole speech depended, while Antony keeps his promise not to blame the conspirators. A full answer notes that Antony also uses logos (the counter-evidence) and pathos (the cloak, the wounds), and that the scene shows how an appeal can be fair in each sentence and manipulative in its overall effect.

::: exercise Write three versions {level=3}
Choose a change you would like in your school, workplace or town. Write three short paragraphs (50–80 words each) arguing for it: one that relies mainly on ethos, one on pathos and one on logos. Then write a fourth paragraph (100–150 words) that combines all three fairly.
::: solution
Answers will vary. Check that (1) the ethos paragraph shows competence, fairness and goodwill rather than claiming them; (2) the pathos paragraph uses one specific, concrete case and appeals to shared values; (3) the logos paragraph states a claim, reasons and evidence and has no hidden premise a sceptic would reject; (4) the combined paragraph uses each appeal for the argument, and every appeal passes the tests of [[#rule-fair]].
