A fictional water company is replacing the main pipe that supplies the town of Eastbridge. The same facts have to reach three kinds of reader.

The company's engineers read a report: *Section 4.2. Replacement of the 450 mm trunk main between Hill Road reservoir and the Eastbridge distribution network is scheduled for 14–18 April. During the works, supply will be maintained from the Mill Lane borehole at reduced pressure (est. 1.5 bar at the network extremities).*

Residents read the company's web page:

> **Low water pressure in Eastbridge, 14–18 April**
> *We are replacing the main water pipe into Eastbridge. From Monday 14 to Friday 18 April your water will still be safe to drink, but the pressure may be low, especially upstairs and in the evening. If you rely on water for medical equipment, call us on the number below.*

And readers of the local paper see a news story: *Thousands of homes in Eastbridge will have low water pressure for five days next month while the town's main supply pipe is replaced, the water company said yesterday.*

None of these texts is better than the others. Each is right for its readers: the engineers need the pipe's size, the alternative source and the predicted pressure; residents need to know what will happen in their kitchens and what to do; newspaper readers need to know first that something affects thousands of people, and who says so. This lesson is about adapting style to readers and media: how to find out what a reader needs, and the conventions of four common kinds of text — reports, web pages, news stories and instructions. The general principles of the earlier lessons apply to all of them; what changes is what the reader knows, what the reader is trying to do, and how the reader reads.

## Know the reader

::: definition Audience, purpose and medium {#def-audience}
- The **audience** is the people who will read a text: what they already know, what they want, what they believe and how they relate to the writer.
- The **purpose** is what the text must do for them: inform, instruct, persuade, record, or help them decide.
- The **medium** is how the text reaches them and how they read it: on paper or on a screen, at a desk or on a phone, from start to finish or by searching and scanning.
:::

::: theorem Four questions before writing {#rule-reader}
Before writing for a new reader, answer:

1. **Who are they, and what do they already know?** Specialists, the general public, a mixture? Decide which terms need defining ([[style-and-rhetoric/word-choice#rule-jargon]]).
2. **What do they need to do with the text?** Decide, act, follow steps, check a fact, keep a record? Put what they need most where they will find it first.
3. **How will they read it?** Most readers of reports, web pages and news do not read every word; they read the beginning, scan the headings and stop when they have what they need. Write for that.
4. **What is the relationship?** A customer, a manager, a colleague, the public — choose the register and the degree of politeness accordingly ([[collocation-and-register/register]], [[pragmatics/writing-to-people]]).
:::

::: proof
Every principle of this course depends on the reader. *Old information first* depends on what the reader knows; *plain words* depends on which words the reader understands; *concision* depends on which words do work for this reader. A writer who has not decided who the reader is cannot apply any of them. Point 3 is the one writers most often overlook: they imagine a reader who starts at the beginning and reads to the end, as they themselves did while writing. Studies of how people read on screens have repeatedly found that they scan: in eye-tracking studies published by Jakob Nielsen in 2006, web users often read across the top of a page, then a shorter line further down, and then scanned the left-hand side — the "F-shaped" pattern. The ISO plain-language standard (ISO 24495-1, 2023) builds the same idea into its definition: a text succeeds if readers can *find* what they need, not only understand it ([[style-and-rhetoric/word-choice#def-plain]]).
:::

::: example Analysing the reader {#ex-reader}
Answer the four questions of [[#rule-reader]] for the resident reading the Eastbridge water company's web page at the start of this lesson, and say how each answer shaped the text.
::: solution
1. **Who and what they know**: a resident, not an engineer; knows the town, not the pipe network. So no *trunk main*, *borehole* or *bar*: *the main water pipe into Eastbridge*, *the pressure may be low*.
2. **What they need to do**: plan for five days and, for a few, take action. So the dates are in the heading, the safety question (*still safe to drink*) is answered at once, and the one action — calling if water is needed for medical equipment — is stated plainly.
3. **How they read**: they will see the heading and perhaps one paragraph. So the heading carries the place, the problem and the dates.
4. **The relationship**: a company to its customers. So *we* and *your*, neutral and polite, without the company's internal terms.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A hospital is writing a leaflet about a new appointment system for patients aged over seventy, many of whom do not use the internet. What should come first?
- [ ] the history of the old system and why it was replaced
- [x] what patients should do to make an appointment, including a phone number
- [ ] the technical name of the new booking software
- [ ] the results of the hospital's consultation on the change
::: solution
The readers need to act, so the action — how to book, by a method they can use — comes first. The history, the software and the consultation may interest some readers, but they can come later or be left out.
:::
:::

## Reports

::: theorem Writing a report {#rule-report}
1. **Put the main findings or recommendations first**, in a summary (often called an *executive summary*) that a busy reader can read alone.
2. **Use headings and numbered sections** so that readers can find and refer to parts: *Section 4.2*, *Recommendation 3*.
3. **Separate findings, conclusions and recommendations**: what you found, what it means, and what should be done.
4. **Put detail where the reader who needs it can find it**: in tables, figures and appendices, not in the main text.
5. **Use the readers' terms and keep them consistent**; state numbers with their units and sources.
:::

::: proof
Reports are read by people who must decide or act, and who often do not have time to read all of them. A summary at the start serves the reader who will read only that; headings serve the reader who wants one section; tables and appendices serve the specialist who wants to check. This is the opposite of the order of discovery — background, method, results, conclusion — in which writers often draft, and in which many school reports are taught. Separating findings from conclusions and recommendations (point 3) lets the reader see where the facts end and the writer's judgement begins, which is part of the writer's ethos ([[style-and-rhetoric/rhetorical-appeals#rule-ethos]]). Research reports in academic fields have their own, related conventions ([[academic-writing/methods-results]], [[academic-writing/abstracts-titles]]).
:::

::: example Writing a summary {#ex-summary}
A fictional report for a school's governors finds that (1) the school's canteen serves 420 lunches a day, (2) a survey of 300 pupils found that 62% would eat there more often if queues were shorter, (3) the queue is longest from 12.30 to 12.50, and (4) two extra serving points would cost £8,000. The report recommends adding the serving points. Write a summary of three or four sentences.
::: solution
1. **Lead with the recommendation and the main reason**, because governors must decide.
2. **Give the key facts** that support it, with numbers.
3. **Give the cost.**

> *We recommend adding two serving points to the canteen, at a cost of about £8,000. Queues are longest between 12.30 and 12.50, and in a survey of 300 pupils, 62% said they would eat in the canteen more often if the queues were shorter. The canteen now serves about 420 lunches a day.*

The summary can be read alone. The survey method, the full figures and the alternatives considered belong in the body of the report.
:::
:::

## Web pages

::: theorem Writing for the web {#rule-web}
1. **Front-load**: put the most important information in the first sentence of the page, of each section and of each paragraph, and the most important words at the start of headings and links.
2. **Use descriptive headings**, often as statements or questions the reader might ask: *How to apply*, *What happens if I miss the deadline?*
3. **Keep paragraphs short** and use lists for steps and options.
4. **Make link text say where it goes**: ✓ *Read the full timetable*; ✗ *Click here*. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG, from the World Wide Web Consortium) require that the purpose of a link can be determined from its text or its context, because people using screen readers often move from link to link.
5. **Address the reader as *you*** and use the active voice where it makes clear who does what: *You must apply by 30 June.*
:::

::: proof
These conventions follow from how people use web pages: they arrive from a search, look for the answer to one question, and leave. A page that makes them read three paragraphs of background before the answer loses many of them. Front-loading works with the scanning pattern described above: the first words of a line and the start of each paragraph are the parts most often read. Descriptive headings let the reader skip to the right section, and question headings match the questions readers bring. The accessibility requirement in point 4 is a matter of law for many public bodies, and of courtesy for everyone else: a link that says *Click here* tells a blind user, who hears a list of links read aloud, nothing at all. Point 5 is what plain-language guidance for government websites generally recommends; in the earlier example, *your water will still be safe to drink* speaks to the resident in a way that *supply will be maintained* does not.
:::

::: warning Burying the answer
✗ *Our council is committed to providing high-quality waste services to all residents. Following a review in 2025, and in line with national guidance, we have made a number of changes to our collection arrangements, which are set out below. From 1 June, recycling will be collected every two weeks on Tuesdays.*
✓ ***From 1 June, recycling will be collected every two weeks, on Tuesdays.** Check your collection dates.*

Readers came to the page to find out when their bins will be emptied. Put that first; the review and the council's commitment can follow, or be cut.
:::

## News

::: definition Inverted pyramid and lead {#def-inverted-pyramid}
A news story is usually written as an **inverted pyramid**: the most important information first, then the next most important, and so on down to background and detail, so that a reader can stop at any point and an editor can cut from the end. Its first sentence, the **lead** (BrE also *intro*), normally says who did what, and often when and where; the following sentences add why and how, quotations and reactions.
:::

::: theorem Writing news {#rule-news}
1. **Put the news in the first sentence**: what happened that the reader did not know, and to whom it matters.
2. **Attribute** claims and figures: *the water company said*, *according to police*. In news, *said* is the neutral reporting verb; *claimed*, *admitted* and *insisted* carry judgements ([[collocation-and-register/connotation]]).
3. **Order the rest by importance**, not by time: background goes near the end.
4. **Keep sentences and paragraphs short**: in many newspapers, one or two sentences per paragraph is normal.
5. **Headlines** follow their own grammar: articles and auxiliary *be* are often dropped; the present simple reports past events (*Council approves market*); the infinitive reports future events (*Minister to visit flood towns*); the past participle alone is passive (*Three hurt in crash*).
:::

::: proof
The inverted pyramid serves readers who stop early and editors who must cut a story to fit: either way, the most important facts survive. The form became standard in American and British newspapers in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries; its origins are debated by historians, who have linked it to the telegraph, to the wire services and to changing ideas of objective reporting. Attribution (point 2) is part of the ethics of news: a reader must know who is the source of each claim, and the choice of reporting verb can take sides. Headline grammar developed to save space, and it can confuse learners because it breaks the ordinary rules of tense: *Council approves market* almost always means the council *has approved* it.
:::

::: example Writing a lead {#ex-lead}
Write a lead for a local news story from these fictional facts, in order of their arrival in a press release: the town council met on Tuesday evening; it discussed the station car park; the car park has been underused since 2020; councillors voted 9–3 to turn it into a covered market; the market will open next spring; some residents had objected to the noise.
::: solution
1. **What is the news?** The vote, and its result: a market.
2. **Who, what, when, where**: councillors / voted to turn the car park into a covered market / on Tuesday / at the station.
3. **What comes next?** When it opens; the vote count; the objections; the background of underuse.

> *The station car park in Eastbridge is to become a covered market after councillors voted on Tuesday to approve the plan, which had been opposed by some residents.*

Then: *The market is due to open next spring. Councillors voted 9–3 in favour …* and, near the end, the history of the car park. A possible headline: *Station car park to become market.*
:::
:::

::: quiz
What does the headline *Council approves market* normally mean?
- [ ] The council approves of markets in general.
- [x] The council has approved (or has just approved) a market.
- [ ] The council will approve a market.
- [ ] The market was approved by the council long ago.
::: solution
Headlines use the present simple for recent past events. A future event would be *Council to approve market*.
:::
:::

## Instructions

::: theorem Writing instructions {#rule-instructions}
1. **Number the steps** and put them in the order in which the reader does them.
2. **Begin each step with an imperative verb**, and give one action per step: *Press the green button.*
3. **Put conditions and warnings before the action they apply to**: *If the light is red, unplug the machine.* ✗ *Unplug the machine if the light is red.* — the reader may already have acted.
4. **Use the words the reader sees**: the labels on the screen or the machine, in the same form, with the same capitals.
5. **Say what should happen**, so that readers know a step has worked: *The screen shows "Ready".*
6. **Test the instructions** on someone who has not done the task.
:::

::: proof
A person following instructions reads a step, does it, and reads the next; they do not read ahead. That is why order matters more than in any other kind of text, and why a condition or warning that comes after an action comes too late (point 3, an application of reading order: [[style-and-rhetoric/cohesion-emphasis]]). The imperative is the clearest form for an action because it begins with the verb; *The green button should be pressed* hides the action and the person who does it ([[style-and-rhetoric/clarity]]). Point 4 matters because readers match the words of the instructions against what they see; a manual that says *Settings* when the screen says *Preferences* leaves them searching. Point 6 is the only reliable check, because the writer, who knows the task, cannot see where a newcomer will go wrong — the curse of knowledge again.
:::

::: example Revising instructions {#ex-instructions}
Revise these fictional instructions for a library's self-service machine. *The machine can be used to borrow books. Your library card should be placed on the reader after books have been put on the shelf, but make sure there are not more than five books, as the machine will not work otherwise. Your receipt will be printed, unless you have selected the email option on the screen before confirming.*
::: solution
1. **Find the actions and their order**: choose receipt type; put books on the shelf (no more than five); place the card; confirm.
2. **Move conditions before actions**: the five-book limit and the receipt choice must come before the reader acts.
3. **One imperative per step.**

> *To borrow books:*
> 1. *Put up to five books on the shelf. (The machine cannot read more than five at a time.)*
> 2. *Place your library card on the reader.*
> 3. *Choose **Print receipt** or **Email receipt** on the screen.*
> 4. *Press **Confirm**. The screen shows the books you have borrowed and their return date.*

(The labels in bold should match those on the real screen.)
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **News leads.** Chinese news writing also puts the news first, in the 导语, and Chinese journalism teaching uses the same idea of the inverted pyramid (倒金字塔). Chinese official news reports, however, often begin with the occasion and the officials present; in English, that information usually comes later, after what was decided.
- **Official documents.** Chinese administrative writing (公文) has fixed formats and formulas. English official writing in many countries has moved towards plain language, addressing readers as *you* and using short words ([[style-and-rhetoric/word-choice]]); a notice translated from formal Chinese into equally formal English can sound remote.
- **Dates and names.** *03/11/2026* means 3 November in British usage and March 11 in American; write the month as a word (*3 November 2026*, *November 3, 2026*) for international readers. Chinese writes 2026年11月3日, from large to small, as ISO 8601 does (2026-11-03). Similarly, Chinese names are written family name first (*Li Wei*); for international readers, it helps to make the family name clear, for example by capitals in forms (*LI Wei*) where that is the convention.
- **Instructions.** Chinese instructions use imperatives too (请按绿色按钮), often with 请. English instructions do not need *please* in every step; it is normal in a request to a person, but in a numbered procedure the bare imperative is polite enough.
:::

::: history
Writing for specific readers became a subject of study with the growth of mass literacy and of large organisations. In the 1940s and 1950s, readability formulas tried to measure how hard a text was: Rudolf Flesch's "A New Readability Yardstick" (1948) gave the Reading Ease score, and Robert Gunning's *The Technique of Clear Writing* (1952) the Fog Index, both used by newspapers and businesses to check their prose. They count sentence and word length, not meaning, and were made for native-speaker readers, so they are at best a rough guide. Technical writing developed as a profession in the same years. On the web, the World Wide Web Consortium published the first Web Content Accessibility Guidelines in 1999, and Jakob Nielsen's usability studies from the late 1990s onwards argued that web readers scan rather than read. More recently, plain-language laws and the ISO standard of 2023 have defined good public writing by whether its readers can find, understand and use it.
:::

::: summary
- Before writing, decide the audience, purpose and medium: who reads, what they must do, and how they read ([[#def-audience]], [[#rule-reader]]).
- Most readers of practical texts scan; put what they need most where they will find it first.
- Reports put findings and recommendations first in a summary, use numbered headings, and separate findings, conclusions and recommendations ([[#rule-report]]).
- Web pages front-load information, use descriptive headings and short paragraphs, and give links text that says where they go ([[#rule-web]]).
- News follows the inverted pyramid: the news in the lead, attribution with neutral *said*, background last; headlines have their own grammar ([[#def-inverted-pyramid]], [[#rule-news]]).
- Instructions number one imperative action per step, in order, with conditions and warnings before the action ([[#rule-instructions]]).
:::

Write each headline as a full sentence. (a) *Mayor to open new bridge* (b) *Schools closed as snow hits north* (c) *Firm cuts 200 jobs*
::: solution
(a) *The mayor will open (is going to open) the new bridge.* (b) *Schools have been closed because snow has hit the north.* (c) *A firm has cut (is cutting) 200 jobs.*

Improve each link text. (a) *To see the timetable, click here.* (b) *More information is available here.* (c) *Read this.*
::: solution
Possible answers: (a) *See the timetable.* (b) *Read more about fees and funding.* (c) *Read the safety guidance for parents.* — each says where the link goes.

::: exercise Condition first {level=1}
Rewrite each instruction so that the condition or warning comes first. (a) *Press Start, but only after closing the lid.* (b) *Remove the battery if the device gets hot.* (c) *Pour the liquid slowly, wearing gloves.*
::: solution
(a) *Close the lid. Then press Start.* (b) *If the device gets hot, remove the battery.* (c) *Put on gloves. Then pour the liquid slowly.*
:::

::: exercise Same facts, three readers {level=2}
A fictional museum will close its east wing from 1 to 30 September to repair the roof; the café and the shop stay open; school groups already booked will be moved to the west wing. Write (a) one sentence for a news story, (b) a heading and two sentences for the museum's web page, and (c) one sentence for a letter to teachers with bookings.
::: solution
Possible answers: (a) *The museum's east wing will close throughout September for roof repairs, the museum said.* (b) ***East wing closed 1–30 September.** The east wing is closed while we repair the roof. The rest of the museum, the café and the shop are open as usual.* (c) *Your class's visit will go ahead as booked, but it will take place in the west wing, because the east wing will be closed for roof repairs throughout September.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Reporting verbs {level=2}
Explain how each reporting verb changes the reader's impression, and choose the neutral one. *The minister ___ that the figures were accurate.* (a) *said* (b) *claimed* (c) *admitted* (d) *insisted*
::: solution
(a) *said* is neutral. (b) *claimed* suggests doubt about the truth. (c) *admitted* suggests that the accuracy was against the minister's interest or had been denied. (d) *insisted* suggests that others disagreed and the minister repeated the point. In a neutral news report, *said* is the default.
:::
:::

::: exercise Front-load a page {level=2}
Rewrite this opening of a fictional web page so that the answer comes first. *Welcome to the student services page. Our team is here to support you throughout your studies. We know that money can be a worry, and we offer a range of support. If you are experiencing financial difficulty, you may be eligible for a hardship grant of up to £1,500, for which you can apply online before 31 March.*
::: solution
> ***Hardship grants of up to £1,500: apply by 31 March.** If you are having money problems, you may be able to get a grant. Apply online.*

The welcome and the description of the team can follow, or be cut. The heading now contains the amount and the deadline — the two facts readers look for.
:::
:::

::: exercise Write instructions {level=3}
Write instructions for a task you know well — setting up a new phone, making a pot of tea, registering for a library card — for a reader who has never done it. Follow [[#rule-instructions]]. Then ask someone to follow them, or imagine doing so step by step, and revise.
::: solution
Answers will vary. Check that (1) the steps are numbered and in the order of doing; (2) each begins with an imperative and has one action; (3) every condition and warning comes before its action; (4) labels match what the reader will see; (5) at least one step says what should happen next; (6) nothing assumes knowledge the reader does not have.
:::
:::

::: exercise Rewrite a report for the public {level=3}
Rewrite this fictional extract from an engineers' report as a short web notice for residents (80–120 words), following [[#rule-reader]] and [[#rule-web]].

*Section 3.1. Following structural assessment, the footbridge over the Lea at Mill Street has been classified as unsafe for pedestrian loading. Closure is effective immediately. Remedial works are programmed for completion by the end of Q2. An alternative pedestrian route via the Station Road crossing (approx. 400 m additional distance) has been signed.*
::: solution
A model:

> ***Mill Street footbridge closed***
> *The footbridge over the River Lea at Mill Street is closed from today because it is not safe to walk on. We plan to repair it and reopen it by the end of June.*
> *To cross the river, please use the Station Road crossing, about 400 metres to the south. The route is signposted.*
> *If you have difficulty walking and need help with this route, call us on the number below.*

Changes: the news (closed, from today) first; *classified as unsafe for pedestrian loading* → *not safe to walk on*; *end of Q2* → *end of June*; the alternative route explained in the reader's terms; a direction (*to the south*) and help for readers who need it added. (The direction is invented; a real notice would check it.)
:::
