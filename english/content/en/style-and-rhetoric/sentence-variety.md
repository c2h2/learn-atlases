Read this fictional paragraph aloud:

> *The village lies at the end of a narrow valley. The road to it is steep and winding. Most of the houses are built of local stone. The school closed in 2009 for lack of pupils. Many young people have moved to the city for work. The population has fallen by half in thirty years. A few new families have arrived recently. They work from home with fast internet connections.*

Every sentence is clear, correct and in a sensible order. But by the fourth sentence most readers hear a drone: eight sentences of eight to eleven words, each a single main clause beginning with its subject. Nothing stands out, because everything is given the same weight, and the reader cannot tell which facts matter. Here is a revision:

> *The village lies at the end of a narrow valley, up a steep and winding road, and most of its houses are built of local stone. For decades it lost its young people to the city. The school closed in 2009 for lack of pupils, and in thirty years the population fell by half. Recently, though, something has changed. A few new families have arrived — people who can work from anywhere with a fast internet connection, and who have chosen to work from here.*

The facts are the same. The difference is in the shapes and lengths of the sentences: a long descriptive sentence, a short summary, a sentence of two balanced clauses, a very short turn (*Recently, though, something has changed*), and a long final sentence whose ending — *chosen to work from here* — carries the point. The reader now hears where the paragraph is going.

This lesson is about the length, shape and rhythm of sentences: how to vary them, how to build parallel and balanced structures correctly, and how to use the ear as a test. It builds on [[style-and-rhetoric/cohesion-emphasis]], since rhythm and the order of information work together; the figures of speech that exploit parallelism — antithesis, anaphora, the tricolon — are the subject of [[style-and-rhetoric/figures-of-speech]].

## Length

::: theorem Vary the length of sentences {#rule-length}
1. **Mix long and short sentences.** A run of sentences of the same length, especially short ones, sounds monotonous; a run of long ones tires the reader.
2. **Use a short sentence for emphasis**, especially after one or more long ones: it stands out because it breaks the pattern.
3. **Use a long sentence for material that belongs together** — a description, a sequence, a qualification with its claim — provided it is easy to follow: its main clause near the start, or a clear structure the reader can predict.
4. **There is no ideal length.** What matters is that length follows meaning: a sentence should be as long as its idea and no longer.
:::

::: proof
Readers use the length of a sentence as a signal. A short sentence after long ones is read as a conclusion, a turn or a point to remember, because its brevity sets it off; that is why the revision above slows down at *Recently, though, something has changed*. Monotony of length removes the signal: when every sentence is short, nothing is emphasised, and the prose sounds like a list; when every sentence is long, the reader has no place to rest. Advice to keep sentences short is common in guides to plain English, and it has a basis — a long sentence with its main verb far from its subject is hard to process ([[style-and-rhetoric/clarity]]) — but Williams and Bizup point out that a long sentence can be perfectly clear if it is well shaped (*Style*, Lesson 10), and Steven Pinker argues that the difficulty of a sentence depends less on its length than on how much the reader must hold in mind before it resolves (*The Sense of Style*, chapter 4). Average sentence length also varies with register: it is usually longer in academic prose and shorter in news, conversation and instructions.
:::

::: example Varying length {#ex-length}
Revise this fictional paragraph so that its most important point stands out, by changing the lengths of its sentences. *The experiment was repeated three times over six months. Each time the samples were prepared in the same way. Each time the results were within one per cent of each other. The original result was confirmed. It had been criticised by other researchers as an error of measurement.*
::: solution
1. **What is the point?** That a result once dismissed as an error was confirmed. That should be short and come at the end.
2. **Group the material that belongs together**: the three repetitions, the method, the close results — one long sentence.
3. **Move the criticism** before the confirmation, so the confirmation answers it.

> *Other researchers had dismissed the original result as an error of measurement. Over six months, the experiment was repeated three times, with the samples prepared in the same way each time, and on every occasion the results were within one per cent of each other. The result was real.*

The long middle sentence carries the evidence; the short final sentence carries the conclusion, and its brevity gives it weight. (Whether a scientific report should end so emphatically is a matter of register; a more cautious version: *The original result was confirmed.*)
:::
:::

## The shapes of sentences

Sentences differ not only in length but in where their main clause sits and how the rest is attached to it. Rhetoricians have named three shapes since antiquity — Aristotle contrasted a "strung-on" style with a "periodic" one in the *Rhetoric* (book III, chapter 9) — and a fourth, the cumulative sentence, was described by the American teacher Francis Christensen in 1963.

::: definition Loose, periodic, cumulative and balanced sentences {#def-shapes}
- A **loose** sentence states its main clause first and then adds further material: *The meeting ended late, after a long argument about the budget.*
- A **periodic** sentence delays its main clause, or the completion of its main clause, until the end: *After a long argument about the budget, which had already been revised twice and still satisfied no one, the meeting ended.*
- A **cumulative** sentence is a loose sentence built up from a short base clause by a series of modifying phrases, each adding detail to the base or to the phrase before: *The ferry left the harbour, slowly, its horn sounding twice, gulls wheeling behind it in the grey morning light.*
- A **balanced** sentence has two parts of similar length and structure, often contrasting: *The plan is cheap to start but expensive to keep.*
:::

Each shape has its use.

::: theorem Choose the shape for the effect {#rule-shapes}
1. **Loose sentences** are the default in modern prose: the reader gets the main point at once, and the additions can be followed easily. Use them for most sentences of exposition.
2. **Periodic sentences** create suspense and put the main point in the stress position. Use them sparingly, for a point that deserves the build-up; keep what comes before the main clause easy to follow.
3. **Cumulative sentences** are a natural shape for description and narrative: the base clause gives the event, and the modifiers zoom in on its details. Keep the base clause short and the modifiers parallel in form where you can.
4. **Balanced sentences** set two ideas side by side for comparison or contrast. Use them for a contrast you want the reader to remember.
5. **Do not use any one shape repeatedly**: a succession of loose sentences sounds like a list of afterthoughts — Strunk's rule 14 in 1918 was *Avoid a succession of loose sentences* — and a succession of periodic ones sounds contrived.
:::

::: proof
The effects follow from the principles of [[style-and-rhetoric/cohesion-emphasis]]. A loose sentence puts its topic and its main verb first, which is easy to process, and then adds information that the reader can attach to a clause they already understand. A periodic sentence puts the main verb or its completion last, so the reader holds the introductory material without knowing where it fits — a cost, but one that buys emphasis, because the sentence resolves in the stress position. Christensen argued, in "A Generative Rhetoric of the Sentence" (*College Composition and Communication*, 1963), that the cumulative sentence is the typical sentence of good modern prose: its additions move the reader's attention from the general statement of the base clause to particular details, so that the sentence grows as the writer looks more closely. Balanced sentences exploit the reader's tendency to compare things that are presented in the same form.
:::

Jane Austen's *Pride and Prejudice* (1813) begins with one of the best-known periodic sentences in English:

> *It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife.*

The sentence makes the reader wait: *It is a truth universally acknowledged* promises a grand general law, and the law turns out, in the stress position, to be about the marriage market. The irony depends on the shape. A loose version — *Everyone agrees that rich single men want wives* — says the same thing and has no joke at all. (The commas after *acknowledged* and *fortune* follow the punctuation of the period, which was freer than today's; a modern editor would remove the second.)

Charles Dickens's *Bleak House* (1853) begins quite differently, with sentences that have no main verb at all:

> *London. Michaelmas Term lately over, and the Lord Chancellor sitting in Lincoln's Inn Hall. Implacable November weather.*

These are fragments, and Dickens uses them deliberately: the lack of finite verbs freezes the scene, like a series of stage directions. Fragments are a resource of literary and journalistic prose, and of advertising; in academic and formal writing they read as errors ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/complete-sentences]]).

::: example Building a cumulative sentence {#ex-cumulative}
Combine these notes into one cumulative sentence with a short base clause: *The market opened at dawn. Stallholders were shouting prices. Their breath showed in the cold air. Crates of oranges were stacked high. The oranges glowed under the lamps.*
::: solution
1. **Choose the base clause**: the event that the rest describes — *The market opened at dawn.*
2. **Turn the other notes into modifiers** that can follow it: absolute phrases (noun + participle or adjective) are the commonest — *stallholders shouting prices*, *their breath white in the cold air*, *crates of oranges stacked high*.
3. **Attach each modifier to what it describes**: the breath belongs to the stallholders (level 2), the glow to the oranges (level 2).

> *The market opened at dawn, stallholders shouting prices, their breath white in the cold air, crates of oranges stacked high and glowing under the lamps.*

The reader gets the event in five words and then the scene, detail by detail, without losing the main clause.
:::
:::

## Parallelism

::: definition Parallelism {#def-parallelism}
**Parallelism** is the use of the same grammatical form for elements that have the same function in a sentence: two or more nouns, verbs, phrases or clauses joined by *and*, *or*, *but*, or a pair of correlatives (*both … and*, *either … or*, *neither … nor*, *not only … but also*, *whether … or*). *She likes **swimming**, **cycling** and **running**.* *The plan would **save money**, **reduce traffic** and **improve the air**.*
:::

::: theorem Keep coordinate elements parallel {#rule-parallel}
1. **Give elements joined by a coordinator the same form**: all nouns, all *-ing* forms, all infinitives, all clauses. ✗ *She likes swimming, to cycle and runs.* ✓ *She likes swimming, cycling and running.*
2. **With correlatives, put the same kind of element after each half**: ✗ *She not only speaks French but also German.* ✓ *She speaks not only French but also German.* ✓ *She not only speaks French but also writes it.*
3. **Repeat a word that marks the parallel** — a preposition, an article, *to*, *that* — when it makes the structure clearer: *We need to decide what to cut, **to** whom we should explain it, and **when**.* *He said **that** the plan was ready and **that** it would cost less.*
4. **In a list, keep the items of the same kind**: ✗ *The course covers grammar, vocabulary and it is taught online.* ✓ *The course covers grammar and vocabulary, and it is taught online.*
:::

::: proof
Strunk's rule 15 in 1918 was *Express co-ordinate ideas in similar form*; the reason is that the reader uses the form of the first element to predict the rest. After *She likes swimming*, the reader expects more *-ing* forms; *to cycle* breaks the expectation and makes the reader re-parse. With correlatives, the position of *not only* tells the reader what is being contrasted: *not only speaks French* leads the reader to expect another verb (*but also writes it*), not another language. Point 3 is a matter of choice: repeating *to* or *that* is not required by grammar, but in a long sentence it shows where each parallel element begins, and in the second example of point 3 it prevents the reader from taking *it would cost less* as something other than a second thing he said. Faulty parallelism is not always a grammatical error — *She likes swimming and to cycle* is heard in speech — but in writing it is one of the clearest signs of an unrevised sentence.
:::

::: warning Faulty parallelism with correlatives
✗ *The report was either written in a hurry or the writer did not check it.*
✓ *Either the report was written in a hurry or the writer did not check it.*
✓ *The report was either written in a hurry or not checked.*

*Either*, *both*, *neither* and *not only* must stand immediately before the first of the two parallel elements. In the first sentence, *either* stands before a verb phrase (*written in a hurry*) but *or* is followed by a clause (*the writer did not check it*).
:::

::: example Correcting parallelism {#ex-parallel}
Correct the parallelism in each sentence. (a) *The job requires accuracy, being patient and that you can work in a team.* (b) *Students can apply online, by post or they can come to the office.* (c) *The new policy will both reduce costs and waiting times.* (d) *He was asked to check the figures, correct the errors and for a summary of the results.*
::: solution
(a) Three requirements in three forms (noun, *-ing*, clause). Make them all nouns: *The job requires accuracy, patience and the ability to work in a team.*

(b) Two prepositional/adverbial phrases and a clause. ✓ *Students can apply online, by post or in person at the office.*

(c) *both* stands before the verb *reduce*, but *and* is followed by a noun (*waiting times*). ✓ *The new policy will reduce both costs and waiting times.*

(d) Two infinitives and a prepositional phrase. ✓ *He was asked to check the figures, correct the errors and write a summary of the results.*
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correctly parallel?
- [ ] *The course teaches students how to plan an essay, writing a draft and revision.*
- [ ] *Not only did the plan fail but also cost money.*
- [x] *The council can either raise taxes or cut services.*
- [ ] *She was tired, hungry and wanted to go home.*
::: solution
In the third, *either* and *or* are each followed by a verb phrase (*raise taxes*, *cut services*). The first mixes an infinitive phrase, an *-ing* form and a noun; the second needs a clause after *but* (*… but it also cost money*); the fourth mixes two adjectives with a verb phrase (✓ *She was tired and hungry and wanted to go home*).
:::
:::

### Parallelism for rhythm and emphasis

Parallel structures are not only correct; they are memorable, because the repetition of a form sets up a rhythm the reader can hear. The King James Bible (1611), translating a Greek text that is itself built on parallels, gives a famous example in 1 Corinthians 13:11:

> *When I was a child, I spake as a child, I understood as a child, I thought as a child: but when I became a man, I put away childish things.*

Three short parallel clauses, each ending with *as a child*, then a turn (*but*) and a longer clause that breaks the pattern. The effect depends on both the parallel and its interruption: the reader expects a fourth *as a child*, and gets the change instead.

::: theorem Order parallel elements by length and weight {#rule-order}
1. **In a series, put shorter elements before longer ones** where the meaning allows: *cheap, quick and easy to maintain* rather than *easy to maintain, cheap and quick*.
2. **Put the strongest or most important element last** (climax): *The flood damaged roads, destroyed crops and cost three lives.*
3. **Use a series of three** for a sense of completeness; two suggests a contrast, four or more a list ([[style-and-rhetoric/figures-of-speech]]).
:::

::: proof
The first point is a tendency of English and many other languages: the German linguist Otto Behaghel described a "law of increasing members" (1909), by which, of two or more coordinated elements, the longer tends to come last, and studies of fixed pairs in English, such as Yakov Malkiel's on "irreversible binomials" (1959), found similar patterns in phrases like *bread and butter* and *safe and sound*. It agrees with end-weight ([[style-and-rhetoric/cohesion-emphasis#rule-stress]]): the longest element is easiest to process last. Point 2 applies the stress position to the series: the last item is the one the reader remembers, so it should be the one that matters most. A series that ends with its weakest item (*cost three lives and damaged some roads*) sounds as if it is running down.
:::

## Rhythm and the ear

::: theorem Read your prose aloud {#rule-aloud}
1. **Read each paragraph aloud**, or at least under your breath, at the speed of speech.
2. **Listen for**: runs of sentences with the same length or the same opening; sentences you cannot say in one breath without losing the structure; unintended rhymes and repeated sounds (*The station's location causes frustration*); and endings that fade out on unstressed words.
3. **Revise** what you stumble over: the place where you lose breath or emphasis is usually where a reader loses the thread.
:::

::: proof
Reading aloud works because it slows the writer down to the pace of a reader and forces them to choose a stress for every phrase. A sentence whose structure is unclear will be hard to say with natural intonation, because the speaker does not know where the main stress should fall; a paragraph of identical rhythms will sound monotonous to the speaker before it does on the page. The advice is old — writers from the classical orators onwards composed for the ear, since most texts were read aloud — and it survives in modern guides. For learners, it has a second benefit: it trains the sense of English stress and intonation that makes written rhythm audible ([[connected-speech/rhythm]]).
:::

::: example Revising by ear {#ex-ear}
Read this fictional paragraph aloud and revise what sounds wrong. *The implementation of the regulation's modification necessitated consultation. Consultation took time. Time was short. The committee decided to delay the decision to the next session of the committee, which was in the autumn, as a result of this.*
::: solution
1. **Sentence 1** is a chain of nominalisations with a sound pattern (*implementation*, *modification*, *consultation*) that is hard to say and sounds like a rhyme. Unpack: *Before the rule could be changed, the committee had to consult.*
2. **Sentences 2–3** repeat a chain pattern (*Consultation took time. Time was short.*) that sounds like a deliberate figure; in a report it is distracting. Combine: *Consultation takes time, and there was little of it.*
3. **Sentence 4** repeats *committee*, and ends on the weak *as a result of this*. Move the link to the front and end on the new information: *So the committee postponed its decision until the autumn session.*

> *Before the rule could be changed, the committee had to consult, and there was little time to do it. It therefore postponed its decision until the autumn session.*
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **排比 and parallelism.** Chinese rhetoric names 排比 as a figure: three or more phrases or clauses of the same structure and related meaning, often with a repeated word (陈望道《修辞学发凡》 and Chinese school textbooks). English parallelism is the same device, but expository English uses it more sparingly than Chinese school essays and speeches often do. One three-part parallel in a paragraph is emphatic; three in a row sound like a speech.
- **对偶 and balance.** 对偶 pairs two lines of equal length that match word for word in grammatical class and often in meaning or tone (as in 对联). English balance is looser: the halves need similar structure, not equal numbers of syllables or matching parts of speech. A Chinese writer who looks for exact symmetry in English may produce sentences that sound forced; aim for similar, not identical.
- **四字格 and rhythm.** Four-character expressions give Chinese prose a steady beat. English rhythm comes from the alternation of stressed and unstressed syllables and from varied sentence lengths, not from equal units, so a sequence of equal-length phrases in English (*fast, cheap, safe, clean, green*) sounds like a list or a slogan.
- **Long chains of clauses.** Chinese can join many short clauses with commas into one long sentence whose relations are left to the reader — what grammarians call 流水句 ‘flowing sentences’. English needs sentence boundaries and usually makes the relations explicit with subordination ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/subordination]]). Translating such a sentence, decide which clause is the main one and attach the rest to it; the result will often be one long sentence and one short one, not one very long one.
:::

::: history
Classical rhetoric treated the rhythm of prose as part of style. Aristotle's *Rhetoric* (book III, chapter 9) distinguished a "strung-on" style, in which clauses follow one another without a planned end, from the **periodic** style, in which a sentence has a beginning and an end in itself and a length that can be taken in at a glance, and later rhetoricians, Cicero and Quintilian among them, discussed the prose rhythms of sentence endings in detail. In English, Hugh Blair's *Lectures on Rhetoric and Belles Lettres* (1783) devoted several lectures to the structure of sentences, and nineteenth-century school rhetorics taught the loose, periodic and balanced sentence as types. William Strunk Jr.'s *The Elements of Style* (1918) condensed this tradition into rules about loose sentences, parallel form and emphatic endings. Francis Christensen's "A Generative Rhetoric of the Sentence" (1963) argued that the cumulative sentence, not the periodic, was the characteristic sentence of modern prose, and his method of analysing sentences by levels of modification was widely taught in American writing classes in the 1960s and 1970s.
:::

- Vary the length of sentences; a short sentence after long ones gives emphasis, and length should follow meaning ([[#rule-length]]).

Analyse the sentence lengths and shapes of the opening of Dickens's *Bleak House* quoted above (*London. Michaelmas Term lately over, and the Lord Chancellor sitting in Lincoln's Inn Hall. ::: solution
A model:

Choices: (1) the opening sentence combines four facts (new, path, river, May) that belong together; (2) the cost and the opposition form one sentence, with the cost as an introductory phrase that links to the path; (3) the final sentence is the point, and the dash before *and most of them used to drive* sets off the most important fact in the stress position.
