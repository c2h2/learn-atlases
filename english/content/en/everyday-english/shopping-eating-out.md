Two customers in a busy café in Toronto order the same thing.

**First customer:** *Give me a latte.*

**Second customer:** *Hi! Could I have a latte, please?*

Both get their coffee. Both sentences are grammatical, and the first is even shorter. But the barista smiles at the second customer and not at the first. In English, *Give me a latte* sounds like an order given to a servant; *Could I have a latte, please?* sounds like a request from one person to another. The difference is not the word *please* alone — it is the whole shape of the sentence.

Shops, cafés and restaurants are where learners use English most often, and where small differences of form are noticed most. This lesson teaches the exchanges of shopping, paying, ordering in a café and eating in a restaurant, the polite request forms that make them friendly, and the British and American words that differ (*bill* and *check*, *chips* and *fries*). Requests in general are the subject of [[everyday-english/requests-offers]]; here we use them in shops and restaurants.

## In a shop

When you enter a shop, an assistant may greet you and offer help.

**Assistant:** *Hi there. Can I help you?* / *Are you looking for anything in particular?*

**Customer:** *I'm just looking, thanks.* (= 'I do not need help now')

or **Customer:** *Yes, I'm looking for a winter jacket.*

To ask for something, the commonest forms are:

- *Excuse me, do you have any umbrellas?* / *Have you got any…?* (BrE)
- *I'm looking for a present for my mother.*
- *Where can I find the batteries?* / *Where are the batteries?*
- *Do you sell phone chargers?*

::: definition Request {#def-request}
A **request** is something we say to ask another person to give us something or to do something for us. A request can be **direct** (*Give me a bag.*) or **indirect**, made with a question or a softer form (*Could I have a bag, please?*, *Do you have a bag?*). In English, everyday requests to people who are serving us are usually indirect.
:::

### Sizes, colours and trying on

**Assistant:** *What size are you?*

**Customer:** *Medium, I think.* / *I'm a size 12.* / *I take a 42 in shoes.*

- *Do you have this in a large?* / *in blue?* / *in a size 40?*
- *Can I try it on?* / *Where are the changing rooms?* (BrE) / *the fitting rooms?* (AmE)
- *It's too small. Do you have it in a bigger size?*
- *It's a bit tight.* / *It's too long.*
- *It fits perfectly.*

::: theorem *Fit* and *suit* {#rule-fit-suit}
Clothes **fit** you when they are the right size: *These shoes don't fit — they're too small.* Clothes or colours **suit** you when they look good on you: *That colour really suits you.* Both are verbs with a direct object (not ✗ *fit for you*, ✗ *suit to you*).
:::

::: proof
*Fit* is about size and shape, *suit* about appearance, so a jacket can fit but not suit (the right size, but the colour is wrong for you) or suit but not fit (it looks lovely, but you cannot close it). Chinese 合适 covers both meanings, which is why the two verbs are often confused. A third verb, *match* or *go with*, is used for two things that look good together: *Does this scarf go with my coat?*
:::

### Deciding

- *I'll take it.* / *I'll take this one, please.* — 'I will buy it'
- *How much is it?* / *How much are these?* / *How much does this cost?*
- *Is this on sale?* (AmE, also BrE: 'at a reduced price') / *Is this in the sale?* (BrE)
- *I'll leave it, thanks.* / *I'll think about it.* — polite ways of not buying

Notice *I'll* (*I will*) for a decision made at the moment of speaking: *I'll take it.* This use of *will* is explained in [[core-grammar/talking-about-the-future]].

Here is a complete shopping exchange.

**Assistant:** *Hello. Are you looking for anything in particular?*

**Ana:** *Yes — do you have this sweater in a medium?*

**Assistant:** *Let me check… Yes, here you are. In grey or in dark blue?*

**Ana:** *Dark blue, please. Can I try it on?*

**Assistant:** *Of course. The changing rooms are just over there.*

**Ana:** (later) *It fits really well. How much is it?*

**Assistant:** *It's thirty-five pounds, but it's twenty per cent off this week, so twenty-eight.*

**Ana:** *Great, I'll take it.*

::: quiz
A friend tries on a red jacket. It is the right size, but you think the colour does not look good on her. What do you say?
- [ ] *It doesn't fit you.*
- [x] *It fits, but I'm not sure the colour suits you.*
- [ ] *It suits, but it doesn't fit for you.*
- [ ] *It's not fit.*
::: solution
The jacket is the right size, so it **fits**; the colour does not look good, so it does not **suit** her. *Fit* and *suit* take a direct object with no preposition. (*I'm not sure…* makes the criticism softer.)
:::
:::

## Paying

At the till (BrE) or register (AmE), also called the checkout in both:

**Assistant:** *That's twenty-eight pounds, please. Are you paying by card?*

**Ana:** *Yes, contactless, please.* (BrE) / *Card, please.*

**Assistant:** *Would you like a bag?* — *No, thanks, I've got one.*

**Assistant:** *Do you want the receipt?* — *Yes, please.*

Useful phrases:

- *Can I pay by card?* / *Do you take cards?* / *Can I pay in cash?*
- *Do you have change for a fifty?* — 'smaller notes or coins for a 50 note'
- *Keep the change.* — said when you do not want the coins back (for example to a taxi driver)
- *Could I have a receipt, please?*

If there is a problem later, take the item back with the receipt:

- *I'd like to return this, please. It doesn't work.*
- *Could I exchange this for a smaller size?*
- *Could I get a refund?* — 'my money back'

::: example Returning an item {#ex-return}
Kwame bought headphones yesterday, and one side does not work. Write what he says at the shop, step by step, and explain each choice.
::: solution
Step 1 — get attention politely and say what he wants: *Excuse me. I bought these headphones here yesterday, and I'd like to return them.* (*I'd like to…* is polite and clear.)

Step 2 — explain the problem briefly: *The left side doesn't work.*

Step 3 — show the evidence: *Here's the receipt.*

Step 4 — say what he wants: *Could I have a refund, please?* or *Could I exchange them for a new pair?*

**Assistant:** *I'm sorry about that. Would you like a refund or an exchange?*

**Kwame:** *An exchange, please — I like them otherwise.*

The tone is calm and factual. English speakers usually start even a complaint with *Excuse me* or *Sorry*, and state the problem as a fact (*It doesn't work*) rather than as an accusation (*You sold me broken headphones*).
:::
:::

## In a café

At a counter, the person serving often speaks first.

**Barista:** *Hi, what can I get you?* / *Who's next?* / *What would you like?*

**Customer:** *Could I have a cappuccino, please?*

**Barista:** *Small, medium or large?*

**Customer:** *Medium, please.*

**Barista:** *Anything else?*

**Customer:** *And a croissant, please.*

**Barista:** *Eat in or take away?* (BrE) / *For here or to go?* (AmE)

**Customer:** *Take away, please.* / *To go, please.*

**Barista:** *That's five sixty.*

::: theorem Polite ways to order {#rule-order-forms}
To order food and drink, use one of these forms, from the most neutral to the most informal:
1. *Could I have…, please?* — polite everywhere
2. *I'd like…, please.* — polite and clear (*I'd* = *I would*)
3. *Can I have…, please?* — slightly less formal; very common
4. *I'll have…, please.* — common in restaurants, when the waiter has asked *What would you like?*
5. *Can I get…?* — common in American English; increasingly used in Britain, though some British speakers find it too direct
6. *A…, please.* — the short form, natural at a busy counter, but only with *please*

Avoid the bare imperative *Give me…* to people who are serving you.
:::

::: proof
All six forms ask for something, but forms 1–5 do it indirectly: they ask about possibility (*Could I…?*, *Can I…?*) or state a wish (*I'd like*) or a decision (*I'll have*), instead of telling the other person what to do. English politeness to people who serve us relies on this indirectness; *please* adds to it but cannot replace it. The short form *A cappuccino, please* is fine because it contains no command at all — it names what you want. The difference between *can* and *could* is explained in [[everyday-english/requests-offers]] and [[core-grammar/first-modals]].
:::

::: warning *Give me…* sounds rude
✗ *Give me a coffee.* ✗ *I want a coffee.* (said to a barista, without *please*)

✓ *Could I have a coffee, please?* ✓ *I'd like a coffee, please.* ✓ *A coffee, please.*

*Give me* is an imperative: it tells the person what to do. *I want* states your need and is common with children (*I want an ice cream!*), but from an adult to a stranger it sounds demanding. These are among the most frequent politeness errors of learners. Chinese 给我一杯咖啡 or 我要一杯咖啡 are normal and polite enough in Chinese, so the direct English translation feels natural to Chinese speakers — but it does not sound the same to English speakers. Note also *a coffee*: in a café, *coffee* is counted in cups, so *a coffee*, *two teas* are normal ([[nouns-and-articles/countable-uncountable]]).
:::

::: example Understanding the barista {#ex-barista}
Wang Lin is ordering in a café. She understands every word, but not what the barista wants to know. Explain what each question means and give a short answer.

1. *Is that everything?*
2. *Do you want room for milk?*
3. *Have you got a loyalty card?*
4. *What name is it?*
::: solution
1. *Is that everything?* means 'Do you want to order anything else?'. Answer: *Yes, that's everything, thanks.* or *Actually, could I have a muffin as well?*
2. *Do you want room for milk?* (mainly American) means 'Shall I leave some space at the top of the cup so you can add milk yourself?'. Answer: *Yes, please.* or *No, thanks — I take it black.*
3. *Have you got a loyalty card?* asks whether she has the café's card that collects points for free drinks. Answer: *No, I haven't.* or *No, thanks* (if the barista offers one).
4. *What name is it?* — in some cafés, the barista writes your name on the cup and calls it when the drink is ready. Answer with a name that is easy to say and hear: *Lin.* Many people choose a short form of their name for this.

Questions like these are routine, so they are said fast and in a reduced form (*That everything?*, *Room for milk?*). Knowing what they are likely to be makes them easier to understand.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A barista asks you *Eat in or take away?* You want to drink your coffee while walking to work. What do you answer?
- [ ] *Eat in, please.*
- [x] *Take away, please.*
- [ ] *Yes, please.*
- [ ] *I take it.*
::: solution
*Take away* (AmE *to go*) means you will take the drink out of the café. *Eat in* (AmE *for here*) means you will stay. The question offers two choices, so *Yes, please* does not answer it.

## In a restaurant

A restaurant meal follows a predictable order, and each step has its phrases.

**Arriving**

- *A table for two, please.* / *Do you have a table for four?*
- *I have a booking for eight o'clock, under the name Okafor.* (BrE) / *I have a reservation for eight, under Okafor.* (AmE, also BrE)
- *Could we sit by the window?*

**Ordering**

**Waiter:** *Are you ready to order?* / *What can I get you?*

- *Could we have a few more minutes?* — when you are not ready
- *What would you recommend?* / *What's the soup of the day?*
- *I'll have the chicken curry, please.* / *I'd like the soup to start, and then the fish.*
- *Could I have it without onions?* / *Could I have the sauce on the side?*
- *Does this have nuts in it?* / *I'm allergic to nuts.* / *I'm vegetarian.* / *I don't eat pork.*
- *Just tap water, please.* / *A bottle of sparkling water, please.*

With steak, the waiter may ask *How would you like it?* — *Rare / medium / well done, please.*

**During the meal**

**Waiter:** *Is everything all right with your meal?* — *Yes, it's lovely, thank you.*

To get the waiter's attention, make eye contact or raise your hand slightly and say *Excuse me*. Calling out *Waiter!* is considered rude in most English-speaking restaurants today.

**Paying**

- *Could we have the bill, please?* (BrE) / *Could we get the check, please?* (AmE)
- *Can we pay separately?* / *Shall we split the bill?* / *This is on me.* (= 'I will pay for everyone')
- *Is service included?*

Customs about tipping differ between countries and change over time. In the United States a tip is normally expected in restaurants and is calculated as a percentage of the bill; in Britain many restaurants add a service charge to the bill, and where they do not, many customers leave a tip. If you are not sure, ask a local friend, or ask *Is service included?*

::: example Improving an order {#ex-order}
Li Ming orders in a restaurant like this: *I want the beef noodles. No onions. Give me water.* Every part is understandable, but the whole sounds abrupt. Rewrite it politely, and explain the changes.
::: solution
**Waiter:** *Are you ready to order?*

**Li Ming:** *Yes. I'll have the beef noodles, please.* — *I'll have* replaces *I want*: it is the usual way to order once the waiter has asked.

*Could I have them without onions?* — the request is a question with *could*, and *them* refers to the noodles.

*And could I have some water, please? Just tap water is fine.* — *could I have* replaces *give me*; *Just tap water is fine* tells the waiter that free water is enough.

**Waiter:** *Of course.*

**Li Ming:** *Thank you.*

The content is the same; only the form has changed. Three things made the difference: questions instead of commands, *please*, and *thank you* at the end.
:::
:::

::: example British and American menus {#ex-menu}
An American visitor in London reads the menu: *Starters — soup, prawn cocktail. Mains — fish and chips, vegetable pie. Puddings — apple crumble.* She asks *Do the fish and chips come with fries?* What does she misunderstand, and how would the menu be written in the United States?
::: solution
In British English *chips* are what Americans call *fries* (long pieces of fried potato), so *fish and chips* already includes them; American *chips* are British *crisps* (thin slices, sold in packets). The headings would also change: *Starters* → *Appetizers*; *Mains* → *Entrées* or *Main courses* (in the US *entrée* means the main course); *Puddings* → *Desserts* (*pudding* is an informal British word for any dessert, and in American English means a soft, sweet, creamy food). So the answer to her question is *Yes — the chips **are** the fries.*
:::

In an American diner, you have finished eating and want to pay. What do you say to the server?
- [ ] *Could we have the bill, please?*

::: note English and Chinese
- **请 and *please*.** 请给我一杯水 is polite in Chinese, but *Please give me a glass of water* is still an imperative in English, and sounds more like an instruction than a request. Use a question: *Could I have a glass of water, please?*
- **我要 and *I want*.** 我要一个汉堡 is a normal way to order in Chinese; *I want a burger* is not, for adults. Use *I'll have* or *I'd like*.
- **Calling the staff.** 服务员！ is a normal way to call staff in many Chinese restaurants. In English-speaking restaurants, catch the waiter's eye and say *Excuse me*.
- **买单 and *the bill*.** 买单 and 埋单 are said *Could we have the bill?* (BrE) or *the check* (AmE), not ✗ *Buy the bill*.
- **Counting drinks.** 一杯咖啡 is *a coffee* (or *a cup of coffee*): in cafés, drinks are counted by the cup without a word for 杯.
:::

::: history
Until 1971, British money was not decimal. A pound was divided into 20 shillings, and a shilling into 12 pence, so a pound had 240 pence, and prices were written like *£1 2s 6d* ('one pound, two shillings and sixpence'). On 15 February 1971, known as Decimal Day, the United Kingdom changed to the present system of 100 new pence to the pound. Older British books and films are full of the old words — *shilling*, *half a crown*, *a tanner* — which no longer refer to any coin.
:::

## Where this leads

Requests, offers and polite refusals are the subject of [[everyday-english/requests-offers]], which explains *can*, *could*, *would* and *shall* in more detail. Prices and paying use the numbers of [[everyday-english/numbers-in-conversation]]. *Coffee* and *a coffee*, *some* and *any* are taught in [[nouns-and-articles/countable-uncountable]] and [[nouns-and-articles/quantifiers]]; the vocabulary of food, clothes and shops in [[core-vocabulary/everyday-topics]]. Why indirect requests sound more polite in English is explained in [[pragmatics/requests-refusals]].

::: summary
- In shops: *Can I help you?* — *I'm just looking, thanks* or *I'm looking for…*; *Do you have this in a medium?*; *Can I try it on?*; *I'll take it.*
- *Fit* is about size, *suit* about appearance: *It fits, but the colour doesn't suit me* ([[#rule-fit-suit]]).
- Order with *Could I have…?*, *I'd like…*, *I'll have…* or *A…, please*; avoid *Give me…* and *I want…* ([[#rule-order-forms]]).
- A request is usually indirect in English; *please* helps but does not turn a command into a request ([[#def-request]]).
- Restaurants: *A table for two*, *What would you recommend?*, *Could I have it without…?*, *Excuse me* to get attention, *the bill* (BrE) / *the check* (AmE).
- Food and shop words differ between BrE and AmE: *chips*/*fries*, *crisps*/*chips*, *takeaway*/*takeout*, *queue*/*line*.
- To return something: *I'd like to return this. Here's the receipt. Could I have a refund?*
:::

::: solution
A model answer:

**You:** *Excuse me. I bought this kettle here last Saturday, and it's stopped working. It doesn't heat the water at all. Here's the receipt. Could I have a replacement, or a refund?*

**Assistant:** *I'm sorry about that. Let me have a look… Yes, I'm afraid it's faulty. Would you prefer a new one or your money back?*

**You:** *A new one, please. Thank you for your help.*

The complaint begins with *Excuse me*, gives the facts (when, what is wrong), shows the receipt, and asks for a solution with *Could I have…?*. *I'm afraid* is the assistant's polite way of saying something negative.
