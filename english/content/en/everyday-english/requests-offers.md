Chen Yu is staying with a family in Bristol. After dinner, her host, Mrs Clarke, says:

**Mrs Clarke:** *Would you like some more cake?*

**Chen Yu:** *Oh, no, thank you.*

Chen Yu would actually love more cake. In the family she grew up in, a guest says no the first time to be modest, and the host offers again — two or three times — before the guest accepts. But Mrs Clarke smiles, says *OK!*, and puts the cake away. In her family, an offer is made once, and *No, thank you* means no.

Neither family is impolite: they follow different conventions. This lesson is about the conventions of English for three everyday kinds of speech: **requests** (asking someone to do or give something), **offers** (proposing to do or give something) and **invitations** (asking someone to join you). It teaches the forms — *Can I…?*, *Could you…?*, *Would you mind…?*, *Shall I…?*, *Would you like…?* — and, as important, how to answer them, how to say no without offending, and why a bare imperative can sound rude in English when the same sentence in Chinese does not.

The modal verbs *can*, *could* and *would* themselves are taught in [[core-grammar/first-modals]]; here we use them.

## Making requests

A request either asks for **permission** — for you to do something — or asks **another person** to do something.

| asking for permission (I do it) | asking someone to do something (you do it) |
|---|---|
| *Can I open the window?* | *Can you open the window?* |
| *Could I open the window?* | *Could you open the window?* |
| *May I open the window?* (formal) | *Would you open the window?* |
| *Do you mind if I open the window?* | *Would you mind opening the window?* |
| *Is it OK if I open the window?* (informal) | *Could you possibly open the window?* |

Requests can be more direct or less direct. Here is the same request — to close a window — on a scale from most direct to least direct:

1. *Close the window.* — an imperative: possible to a child or a close family member, or as an instruction
2. *Close the window, would you?* — informal, to someone you know well
3. *Can you close the window?* — informal to neutral
4. *Could you close the window, please?* — neutral and polite: the safest choice
5. *Would you mind closing the window?* — polite
6. *Could you possibly close the window?* / *Do you think you could close the window?* — very polite
7. *I was wondering if you could close the window.* — very polite, rather formal
8. *It's a bit cold in here, isn't it?* — a hint: the request is not said at all

::: definition Softener {#def-softener}
A **softener** is a word or phrase that makes a request less direct and so more polite, without changing what is asked for: *please*, *possibly*, *just* (*Could I just ask a question?*), *a bit* / *a moment* (*Could you wait a moment?*), *do you think…*, *I was wondering if…*, and the choice of *could* or *would* instead of *can* or *will*.
:::

::: theorem Choosing a request form {#rule-request-forms}
Choose a more indirect form, with more softeners, when (1) the other person is not close to you (a stranger, a new colleague), (2) the other person has more power or status (a manager, a teacher, an official), or (3) the request is big (a lot of time, effort or money). Choose a more direct form with family, close friends and for very small requests. When in doubt, *Could you…, please?* and *Could I…?* are polite in almost every situation.
:::

::: proof
A request asks the other person to give up some of their freedom. Indirect forms give some of it back: *Could you…?* asks whether they are **able** to help, so it lets them say no without refusing (*Sorry, I'm afraid I can't right now*). *Could* and *would* are past forms used for the present, and English often uses the past to create distance — in time, in reality, or between people ([[modality/modality-politeness]]). These three factors — distance, power and the size of the request — come from Brown and Levinson's theory of politeness (see the historical note). Too much indirectness is also possible: *I was wondering if you could possibly pass the salt* sounds strange or ironic at a family dinner, where *Could you pass the salt?* is right.
:::

Answering a request:

- Yes: *Sure.* / *Of course.* / *No problem.* / *Certainly.* (formal, often from staff) / *Go ahead.* (to a request for permission: 'do it')
- No: *Sorry, I'm afraid I can't — I'm using it this afternoon.* / *I'd rather you didn't, actually.* (to a request for permission)

A refusal is almost always softened (*sorry*, *I'm afraid*) and followed by a reason.

::: theorem Answering *Would you mind…?* {#rule-would-you-mind}
*Would you mind…?* and *Do you mind if…?* literally ask whether the request bothers you. So to say **yes, I'll do it** or **yes, you can**, answer in the negative: *No, not at all.* / *Of course not.* / *No, go ahead.* To refuse, use a softened explanation: *Well, actually, I'm a bit cold — could we leave it closed?*
:::

::: proof
*Would you mind closing the window?* means 'Would closing the window be a problem for you?'. The answer *No* ('it is no problem') agrees to the request. Answering *Yes* would mean 'Yes, I do mind' — a refusal, and a rude one. Many speakers avoid the confusion by answering with an action: *Sure — there you go.* Note also the form: *Would you mind* + *-ing* (*closing*), and *Do you mind if I* + present simple (*open*). See [[verb-patterns/ing-or-infinitive]].
:::

::: quiz
A colleague asks you: *Would you mind lending me your stapler for a minute?* You are happy to lend it. What do you say?
- [ ] *Yes, I would.*
- [ ] *Yes, I mind.*
- [x] *No, not at all — here you are.*
- [ ] *No, I wouldn't lend it.*
::: solution
*Would you mind…?* asks whether lending it would bother you. *No, not at all* means 'It does not bother me at all', so it agrees; *here you are* is what you say when you hand something to someone. *Yes, I would* or *Yes, I mind* would be refusals. The last option refuses rudely.
:::
:::

### *Lend* and *borrow*

::: theorem *Lend* and *borrow* {#rule-lend-borrow}
You **borrow** something **from** someone: you take it and give it back later. You **lend** something **to** someone: you give it, and they give it back later. *Could I borrow your pen?* = *Could you lend me your pen?* Both mean the same request, seen from different sides.
:::

::: proof
Chinese 借 covers both directions (借给我 and 跟你借), so it is easy to use the wrong English verb: ✗ *Could you borrow me your pen?* The test is the direction: if the thing goes **away from** the subject, the verb is *lend*; if it comes **to** the subject, the verb is *borrow*. Money is often *lent* and *borrowed* too; for things that are not returned, such as a sheet of paper, say *Could I have…?*
:::

::: warning *Please* + imperative is still an order
✗ *Please lend me your charger.* ✗ *Please send me the file.* (to a colleague)

✓ *Could I borrow your charger?* ✓ *Could you send me the file, please?*

In Chinese, 请 + verb is a polite request. In English, *please* + imperative is understood, but it remains an instruction: it suits a notice (*Please close the door*), a teacher speaking to a class, or a form (*Please write in capitals*), not a favour asked of a colleague or a stranger. Put the request into a question with *could* or *can*, and keep *please*.
:::

::: example Choosing the right request {#ex-request}
Write a suitable request for each situation, and explain the choice.

1. You want your younger brother to pass you the remote control.
2. You need a colleague you do not know well to check a report for you before 5 p.m. today.
3. You need your manager to sign a form.
4. You want to use the empty seat next to a stranger on a train.
::: solution
1. *Pass me the remote, will you?* or *Can you pass me the remote?* — family, a very small request: a direct form is fine. (Even here, many families add *please*.)
2. *Sorry to bother you — I was wondering if you could have a quick look at this report before five? I know it's short notice.* — little closeness and a big request (time, today): an indirect form, a softener (*a quick look*), and an apology for the short time.
3. *Could you sign this form for me when you have a moment, please?* — more power, a small request: *Could you…, please?* with a softener (*when you have a moment*).
4. *Excuse me, is this seat free?* — a question about the seat rather than a request for permission is the usual form; the answer *Yes, go ahead* gives permission. (*Do you mind if I sit here?* is also correct.)

The same need — someone's help — is expressed in four different ways, because the relationship and the size of the request differ.
:::
:::

## Offers

An **offer** proposes to give something or do something for the other person.

| form | example | notes |
|---|---|---|
| *Would you like…?* | *Would you like a cup of tea?* / *Would you like to sit down?* | polite, everywhere; + noun or *to* + verb |
| *Can I get you…?* | *Can I get you something to drink?* | very common, friendly |
| *Do you want…?* | *Do you want a lift to the station?* | informal |
| *Shall I…?* | *Shall I open the window?* | mainly British; American English prefers *Should I…?* or *Do you want me to…?* |
| *I'll…* | *I'll carry that for you.* / *I'll help you with that, if you like.* | a decision made now ([[core-grammar/talking-about-the-future]]) |
| *Let me…* | *Let me get the door for you.* | friendly, often while already acting |
| imperative | *Have a seat.* / *Help yourself.* / *Have another biscuit.* | a host's offer: warm, not rude |

Notice the last row. An imperative that offers something to the listener is polite, for the same reason that directions are ([[everyday-english/directions-travel]]): the action is for the listener's benefit. *Help yourself* ('take what you want, without asking') is one of the most hospitable things an English-speaking host can say.

Answering an offer:

- Accept: *Yes, please.* / *That would be great, thanks.* / *Thanks, that's very kind of you.* / *Oh, lovely — thank you.*
- Refuse: *No, thank you.* / *No, thanks, I'm fine.* / *That's kind of you, but I'm OK, thanks.*

::: theorem *Yes, please* and *No, thank you* {#rule-yes-please}
Accept an offer with *Yes, please* and refuse it with *No, thank you* (or *No, thanks*). *Thank you* alone can be unclear, because it is used both to accept and to refuse. And take the speaker's offer seriously: an English-speaking host usually makes an offer once, or twice at most, and accepts a refusal as meant.
:::

::: proof
English pairs *please* with accepting (you are asking to be given something) and *thank you* with both accepting and refusing (you are grateful for the offer either way). This is why *Yes, please* — not ✗ *Yes, thank you* in reply to *Would you like…?* — is the usual acceptance, although *Yes, thanks* is also common. On repeating offers: in English-speaking cultures, insisting after a clear *No, thank you* can seem pushy, as if the host does not believe the guest; so hosts usually stop. Practices vary between families and regions in every country, of course — some English-speaking hosts do offer again — but a guest who wants something is safer saying *Yes, please* the first time.
:::

::: example The second piece of cake {#ex-offer}
Go back to Chen Yu at the start of the lesson. What could she say (a) if she wants more cake, (b) if she really does not want more, and (c) if she said *No, thank you* but then changes her mind?
::: solution
(a) *Yes, please — it's delicious!* Accept the first time, and add a compliment, which hosts like to hear.

(b) *No, thank you — it was lovely, but I'm really full.* A refusal with *thank you*, a compliment, and a reason, so that it does not sound as if she disliked the cake.

(c) *Actually, I think I will have another small piece, if that's OK!* *Actually* signals a change of mind; *if that's OK* is a softener. In English this is perfectly acceptable and often makes the host laugh.

The lesson for guests: in English, say what you mean the first time, and soften the refusal with thanks and a reason rather than by refusing several times.
:::
:::

::: quiz
At a colleague's house, the host asks: *Can I get you a drink?* You would like some water. Which answer is best?
- [ ] *Give me water.*
- [x] *Yes, please — could I just have some water?*
- [ ] *No, thank you.* (and wait to be asked again)
- [ ] *I want water.*
::: solution
*Yes, please* accepts, and *Could I just have some water?* names what you want politely (*just* is a softener meaning 'only something simple'). Refusing and waiting may leave you without a drink, because the host will probably take the refusal at face value. The other two are too direct.
:::
:::

## Invitations

An **invitation** asks someone to do something with you or to come to an event.

- *Would you like to come to dinner on Saturday?* — polite, everywhere
- *Do you want to get a coffee after class?* — informal
- *Are you free on Friday evening?* — often asked first, before the invitation itself
- *How about going to the cinema?* / *What about a walk?* — suggestions (+ *-ing* or a noun)
- *Why don't we try the new Thai place?* — a suggestion
- *Let's have lunch together sometime.* — a suggestion; *sometime* often makes it vague, not a real plan
- *Do you fancy a game of tennis?* — British, informal

Accepting:

- *I'd love to!* / *That sounds great.* / *Yes, that would be lovely.* / *Sure, why not?* (informal)
- Then often a detail: *What time?* / *Shall I bring anything?*

::: theorem Refusing an invitation politely {#rule-refusing}
A polite refusal usually has three or four parts:
1. **appreciation**: *Thanks for asking.* / *That's very kind of you.* / *I'd love to, but…*
2. **a softened refusal**: *I'm afraid I can't.* / *I don't think I can make it.*
3. **a reason**: *I'm working on Saturday.* / *I've already got plans.*
4. (often) **an alternative**: *Maybe another time?* / *How about next weekend instead?*
:::

::: proof
A bare *No* or *I can't* sounds like a rejection of the person, not of the date. Appreciation shows that the invitation itself was welcome; the reason shows that the problem is the situation; the alternative shows interest in a future meeting. The reason does not need to be detailed — *I've got plans* is enough, and asking for more detail would be rude. Leaving out the alternative is a polite way of signalling that you are not interested in another date; this is understood, not stated.
:::

::: example Refusing without offending {#ex-refuse}
Your classmate Diego invites you: *We're having a little party for my birthday on Saturday — would you like to come?* You cannot go, because your cousin is getting married that day. Write two refusals: one that is too short, and one that is polite.
::: solution
Too short: *No, I can't.* — it sounds as if you do not want to come, and it says nothing about the invitation.

Polite: *Oh, thanks so much for inviting me! I'd love to, but I'm afraid I can't — my cousin's getting married on Saturday. Happy birthday in advance, though — maybe we could get a coffee next week to celebrate?*

The polite version has all four parts: appreciation (*thanks so much*, *I'd love to*), a softened refusal (*I'm afraid I can't*), a reason (*my cousin's getting married*), and an alternative (*a coffee next week*). Wishing him a happy birthday adds warmth.
:::
:::

::: example A real invitation or a friendly phrase? {#ex-vague}
At the end of a meeting, three colleagues say goodbye to Nkechi. Which of them is really inviting her, and how should she answer each?

1. *We should get lunch sometime!*
2. *A few of us are going for lunch at one — do you want to come?*
3. *You must come over for dinner one day.*
::: solution
Look for the details that make a plan: a time, a place, a question that needs an answer.

1. No fixed time (*sometime*), and no question: this is a friendly phrase that means 'I like you and would be happy to see you again'. Answer in the same spirit: *Yes, definitely — that'd be nice!* If Nkechi wants to make it real, she can add a detail: *Are you free next Tuesday?*
2. A time (*at one*), the people (*a few of us*) and a question (*do you want to come?*): a real invitation that needs a yes or no. *I'd love to — where are you going?* or *Thanks, but I've got a call at one. Next time!*
3. *You must…* sounds strong, but *one day* is vague; like (1), it is usually a warm phrase, not yet an invitation. *That would be lovely!* is the right answer. A real invitation would follow later with a date.

Taking a vague phrase as a fixed plan, or a real invitation as a vague phrase, are both common misunderstandings — for English speakers too.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **请 and *please*.** 请 + verb is a polite request in Chinese; *Please* + verb is an instruction in English. Use *Could you…?*
- **麻烦你 and *Sorry to bother you*.** 麻烦你帮我…… is a softener that apologises for the trouble; English uses *Sorry to bother you, but could you…?* or *Would you mind…?*
- **借.** One Chinese verb, two English ones: *lend* and *borrow* ([[#rule-lend-borrow]]).
- **客气.** In many Chinese settings, refusing an offer at first and insisting as a host are both signs of politeness. In many English-speaking settings, a host offers once or twice and takes *No, thank you* at its word, while a guest who wants something says *Yes, please*. Neither convention is better; knowing both avoids misunderstandings.
- **不客气 and *You're welcome*.** The answer to *Thank you* is *You're welcome*, *No problem*, *Not at all* (BrE, formal) or *My pleasure*; in Britain, a smile and *That's OK* are also common.
:::

::: history
Why do English speakers say *Could you possibly…?* rather than *Do it*? An influential answer was given by the anthropologist Penelope Brown and the linguist Stephen Levinson, first in a long essay of 1978 and then in the book *Politeness: Some Universals in Language Usage* (1987). They proposed that everyone has **face** — a public self-image — with two sides: the wish to be liked and approved of, and the wish not to be imposed on. Requests threaten the second, so speakers use forms that give the listener room to refuse: questions about ability (*Could you…?*), softeners (*possibly*, *just*), apologies (*Sorry to bother you*). Their claim that these strategies are universal has been widely debated — researchers working on Chinese and Japanese have argued that face is more about the group than the individual — but their three factors of distance, power and the size of the request are still used to explain politeness in many languages.
:::

## Where this leads

The modal verbs used here are taught in [[core-grammar/first-modals]] and, in depth, in [[modality/modality-politeness]]. Requests and offers in shops and restaurants are practised in [[everyday-english/shopping-eating-out]]; on the phone and in messages in [[everyday-english/phone-messages]]. The theory behind them — speech acts, face and indirectness — is the subject of [[pragmatics/speech-acts]], [[pragmatics/politeness-face]] and [[pragmatics/requests-refusals]], and writing requests in emails is taught in [[pragmatics/writing-to-people]].

::: summary
- Ask for permission with *Can/Could/May I…?* or *Do you mind if I…?*; ask someone to act with *Can/Could/Would you…?* or *Would you mind -ing?*
- Use more indirect forms and softeners (*please*, *possibly*, *just*, *I was wondering if*) for strangers, people with more power and bigger requests ([[#rule-request-forms]], [[#def-softener]]); *Could you…, please?* is safe almost everywhere.
- *Would you mind…?* is answered *No, not at all* to agree ([[#rule-would-you-mind]]).
- *Borrow from*, *lend to*: *Could I borrow your pen?* = *Could you lend me your pen?* ([[#rule-lend-borrow]]).
- *Please* + imperative is still an instruction; imperatives that offer (*Have a seat*, *Help yourself*) are warm and polite.
- Offer with *Would you like…?*, *Can I get you…?*, *Shall I…?* (AmE *Should I…?*); answer *Yes, please* / *No, thank you*, and say yes the first time if you mean yes ([[#rule-yes-please]]).
- Refuse invitations with appreciation, a softened refusal, a reason and often an alternative ([[#rule-refusing]]).
:::

::: exercise Find the problem {level=2}
Each exchange has a problem of politeness or meaning. Explain it and give a better version.

(a) **A:** *Would you mind turning the music down?* **B:** *Yes, sure!* (B agrees and turns it down)

(b) **A:** *Would you like some tea?* **B:** *Thank you.* (B does not want any)

(c) **A:** *Could you borrow me your phone charger?*
::: solution
(a) *Yes* answers 'Yes, I mind', which is a refusal, so the words contradict the action. Better: *No, not at all — sorry!* or *Sure — sorry about that!* (*Sure* alone is common and understood as agreement.)

(b) *Thank you* alone usually sounds like acceptance, so B may get tea. Better: *No, thank you, I'm fine.*

(c) *Borrow* is the wrong verb: the charger goes from B to A. Better: *Could you lend me your phone charger?* or *Could I borrow your phone charger?*
:::
:::

::: exercise A request by message {level=3}
Write a short message (3–5 sentences) to a classmate you do not know well, asking to borrow their notes from a lecture you missed. Make it polite, explain why you need them, and offer something in return.
::: hint
Use a greeting, a reason, *I was wondering if…* or *Would you mind…?*, and an offer such as *I'm happy to…*.
:::
::: solution
A model answer:

*Hi Hannah, it's Zhao Lin from the Monday economics class. I was ill last week and missed the lecture on inflation — I was wondering if you'd mind sharing your notes? I'd only need them for a day or two. I'm happy to send you mine from the week you were away, if that's useful. Thanks so much!*

The message identifies the writer (they do not know each other well), gives a reason, uses a very polite form (*I was wondering if you'd mind…*), limits the request (*a day or two*), and offers something back — all ways of making a bigger request easier to accept.
:::

::: exercise A visit {level=3}
Write a dialogue (10–12 turns) in which you visit a friend's home. The friend makes at least two offers and one invitation; you accept one offer, refuse one offer politely, and refuse the invitation with all four parts of [[#rule-refusing]].
::: solution
A model answer:

**Friend:** *Come in! Let me take your coat.*

**You:** *Oh, thanks.*

**Friend:** *Have a seat. Can I get you something to drink — tea, coffee, juice?*

**You:** *A cup of tea would be lovely, thanks.*

**Friend:** *Milk and sugar?*

**You:** *Just milk, please.*

**Friend:** *Here you go. And would you like a biscuit?*

**You:** *No, thank you — I've just had lunch.*

**Friend:** *By the way, a few of us are going hiking on Sunday. Would you like to come?*

**You:** *Oh, thanks for asking! I'd love to, but I'm afraid I can't this Sunday — I've got an exam on Monday and I need to study. Are you going again soon? I'd really like to come next time.*

**Friend:** *Sure, we'll let you know.*

Offers: *Let me take your coat*, *Can I get you…?*, *Would you like a biscuit?*; acceptance with *would be lovely, thanks*; refusal with *No, thank you* and a reason; the invitation refused with appreciation, *I'm afraid I can't*, a reason and an alternative.
:::
