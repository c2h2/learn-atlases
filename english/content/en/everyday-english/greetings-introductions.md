On her first morning in a new office, Li Na walks to the lift. A colleague she met yesterday passes her in the corridor and says:

**Tom:** *Hi, Li Na! How are you?*

Li Na starts to answer — *Well, I'm a bit tired, because I didn't sleep very well, and …* — but Tom has already smiled, said *Good, good!* and walked on. Was he rude? Did he not want to know?

He was not rude, and he did not really ask a question. In English, *How are you?* is very often part of a greeting, like *Hello*. It expects a short answer — *Fine, thanks. And you?* — and nothing more. This lesson is about these small, fixed exchanges: saying hello, answering *How are you?*, introducing yourself and other people, using names and titles, and saying goodbye. They are short, but people notice them, because they are the first thing they hear from you.

## Saying hello

English has a few common greetings. The choice depends on the time of day and on how well you know the person.

| greeting | when | register |
|---|---|---|
| *Hello.* | any time, anyone | neutral: safe everywhere |
| *Hi.* | any time | informal: friends, colleagues, people your age |
| *Hey.* | any time | very informal: friends; common among younger speakers |
| *Good morning.* | until about 12 noon | neutral to formal |
| *Good afternoon.* | from 12 noon to about 6 p.m. | neutral to formal |
| *Good evening.* | from about 6 p.m. | formal: hotels, restaurants, the start of a talk |
| *Morning!* | until about 12 noon | informal (short for *Good morning*) |

The words *formal* and *informal* will be used in every lesson of this course, so here is what they mean.

::: definition Formal and informal register {#def-register}
The **register** of a phrase is the kind of situation it suits. **Formal** language is used with people we do not know, with older people or people in a higher position, and in official situations (a job interview, a hotel reception, a business email). **Informal** language is used with friends, family and colleagues we know well. **Neutral** language fits almost everywhere.
:::

Register is not a matter of right and wrong. *Hey!* is perfect for a friend and strange for a job interviewer; *Good evening, madam* is perfect for a hotel receptionist and strange for your sister. When you are not sure, choose the neutral form: *Hello*, *Good morning*.

People often add the other person's name: *Hi, Kwame!*, *Good morning, Ms Haddad.* Using the name makes the greeting warmer.

::: theorem Good night is not a greeting {#rule-good-night}
*Good morning*, *Good afternoon* and *Good evening* are said when you **meet** someone. *Good night* is said only when you **leave** in the evening or when someone goes to bed. It is a way of saying goodbye.
:::

::: proof
*Good evening* and *Good night* both refer to the evening, but they have different jobs. *Good evening* opens a conversation; *Good night* closes one. A receptionist says *Good evening* when a guest arrives at 9 p.m. and *Good night* when the same guest goes up to their room. The same is true of *Good day*: in most of Britain and North America it now sounds old-fashioned, though it is still used as a greeting in Australia (*G'day*).
:::

::: quiz
It is 8 p.m. You arrive at a friend's dinner party. What do you say to the host's parents, whom you have not met before?
- [ ] *Good night!*
- [x] *Good evening!*
- [ ] *Hey!*
- [ ] *Good afternoon!*
::: solution
*Good evening* is the greeting for the evening, and it is polite enough for people you do not know. *Good night* is only for leaving; *Hey* is too informal for people you have just met who are older than you; *Good afternoon* is for the time before about 6 p.m.
:::
:::

## *How are you?*

In many situations a greeting has two parts: *Hello* and a question about the other person.

- *How are you?* — neutral, used everywhere
- *How are you doing?* — informal, especially in American English
- *How's it going?* — informal
- *How are things?* — informal, to someone you already know
- *(Are) you all right?* or *Alright?* — informal, common in England as a greeting, not a worried question
- *What's up?* — very informal, especially among young people; the usual answer is *Not much* or *Nothing much*

::: definition Greeting question {#def-greeting-question}
A **greeting question** is a question that is part of a greeting. The speaker does not expect real information. The listener answers briefly, usually positively, and often asks the same question back.
:::

Here is a typical exchange between two colleagues:

**Priya:** *Morning, Tom. How are you?*

**Tom:** *Fine, thanks. And you?*

**Priya:** *Good, thanks.*

The whole exchange takes about three seconds. Common answers are:

- *Fine, thanks.* / *Good, thanks.* / *Very well, thank you.* (more formal)
- *Not bad.* / *Pretty good.* (informal)
- *OK, thanks.* / *All right.*
- *Not too bad.* (informal, often British: it means 'quite well')

The return question is usually short: *And you?*, *How about you?*, *You?* (informal). With *And* **you**, the stress falls on *you*, because the important information is the change of person.

::: theorem Answering a greeting question {#rule-how-are-you}
When *How are you?* is part of a greeting, (1) answer in a few words, (2) usually say something positive or neutral, (3) add *thanks*, and (4) ask the question back: *Fine, thanks. And you?*
:::

::: proof
The question is a way of being friendly, not a request for news, so a long answer breaks the rhythm of the greeting. A neutral answer is also possible when you are not well: *Oh, not too bad.* or *Bit tired, actually!* — short, and said with a smile. The question becomes a real question when the situation shows it: a friend who knows you were ill, a doctor, or a colleague who says *How are you **really**?* or *How are you feeling now?* Then a full answer is right.
:::

::: example Real question or greeting? {#ex-real-question}
Decide whether *How are you?* is a greeting question or a real question in each situation, and give a suitable answer.

1. A shop assistant says: *Hi, how are you today?* and starts to scan your shopping.
2. Your friend Omar visits you in hospital: *So, how are you? How's the leg?*
3. A colleague passes you on the stairs: *Hey, how's it going?*
::: solution
1. A greeting question. The assistant is being friendly, and there is a queue. Answer: *Good, thanks. How are you?*
2. A real question. Omar came because you were hurt, and he adds a second, specific question (*How's the leg?*). Answer with information: *Much better, thanks. It still hurts a bit when I walk, but the doctor says I can go home on Friday.*
3. A greeting question. You are both moving, and *How's it going?* is informal. Answer: *Not bad, thanks! You?* — and keep walking.

The clues are the place (a shop, the stairs), whether the speaker stops to listen, and whether the question is followed by a more specific one.
:::
:::

::: warning *How do you do?* is not *How are you?*
✗ **Mr Okafor:** *How do you do?* — **Wang Fang:** *I'm fine, thank you.*

✓ **Mr Okafor:** *How do you do?* — **Wang Fang:** *How do you do?*

*How do you do?* is a very formal greeting, used mainly in British English and mainly by older speakers, when people are introduced for the first time. It is not a question about health: the traditional answer is to say *How do you do?* back. Today most people say *Nice to meet you* instead. You need to understand *How do you do?*, but you do not need to use it.
:::

## Introducing yourself

When you meet someone for the first time, you say hello and give your name. Common ways are:

- *I'm Ana.* — neutral, the most common
- *My name's Ana Souza.* — neutral, a little more careful
- *Hi, I'm Ana — Ana Souza.* — the first name, then the full name
- *I'm Ana. I'm from Brazil.* / *I'm Ana. I'm a new student.* — with one piece of information

The other person answers with their own name and a fixed phrase:

- *Nice to meet you.* — neutral, the most common
- *Pleased to meet you.* — a little more formal
- *Good to meet you.* — informal
- *Nice to meet you too.* — the answer to *Nice to meet you*; stress on *too*

Here are two first meetings, one informal and one formal.

**Ana:** *Hi, I'm Ana.*

**Kwame:** *Hi, Ana. I'm Kwame.*

**Ana:** *Nice to meet you, Kwame.*

**Kwame:** *Nice to meet you too. Are you in this class as well?*

**Ana:** *Yes, I just joined this week.*

**Ms Haddad:** *Good morning. I'm Leila Haddad, the office manager.*

**Chen Jie:** *Good morning, Ms Haddad. I'm Chen Jie. I start in the design team today.*

**Ms Haddad:** *Ah, welcome. Pleased to meet you.*

**Chen Jie:** *Pleased to meet you too.*

Notice that the informal meeting uses only first names, while in the formal one Chen Jie uses a title and family name (*Ms Haddad*), because Ms Haddad is a manager she has not met before. In many English-speaking workplaces the manager would quickly say *Please, call me Leila*, and from then on first names are normal.

### Introducing other people

To introduce one person to another, English uses *this is*, even though the person is standing next to you:

- *Kwame, this is my friend Li Wei. Li Wei, this is Kwame.*
- *Mum, this is Sarah, from my course.*
- *Have you met Elena?* — informal: 'Do you know Elena?'
- *Do you two know each other?* — informal
- *I'd like you to meet Dr Mensah.* — formal
- *Can I introduce my colleague, Carlos Ruiz?* — formal

When you introduce two people, say each name to the other and, if you can, add one piece of information that helps them start talking: *Elena, this is Li Wei. He's just moved here from Chengdu. Li Wei, Elena works in the library.*

::: theorem *This is* in introductions {#rule-this-is}
In introductions, use *This is* + name (not *He is* or *She is*) for the person you are introducing, and give both names: *Tom, this is Priya. Priya, this is Tom.* The same *this is* is used on the phone to give your own name (see [[everyday-english/phone-messages]]).
:::

::: proof
*She is Maria* is a correct sentence, but it identifies someone we are already talking about (*Who's the woman in the blue coat? — She's Maria, my neighbour.*). An introduction presents a new person, and English does this with *this*, the word that points to something near the speaker. Chinese 这是 works in the same way, so for Chinese speakers this pattern is easy once it is noticed. On *be* in these sentences, see [[core-grammar/be-and-have]].
:::

::: example Building an introduction {#ex-introduce}
You are at a friend's birthday party. Your classmate Hannah, who is studying medicine, arrives. You are talking to your cousin Zhang Min, who is a nurse. Introduce them to each other.
::: solution
Step 1: get attention and say the first name to the person who is already there. *Min, this is Hannah.*

Step 2: add a piece of information about Hannah. *We're in the same English class. She's studying medicine.*

Step 3: turn to Hannah and introduce Min. *Hannah, this is my cousin, Zhang Min. She's a nurse — so you two have something in common!*

Step 4: they greet each other. **Hannah:** *Hi, Min. Nice to meet you.* **Zhang Min:** *Nice to meet you too.*

The register is informal (a party, people of the same age), so first names are right. Calling her *Min* rather than *Zhang Min* is natural for a family member; to Hannah, the full name is useful the first time.
:::
:::

::: quiz
You are introducing your manager, Dr Kowalski, to a visitor at a formal meeting. Which is the most suitable?
- [ ] *Hey, this is Anna.*
- [ ] *She is Dr Kowalski.*
- [x] *I'd like you to meet Dr Anna Kowalski, our head of research.*
- [ ] *Have you two met before? This is Anna, OK?*
::: solution
The situation is formal, so the formal introduction *I'd like you to meet…* with the title and full name fits, and the job title helps the visitor. *Hey* and *OK?* are too informal; *She is Dr Kowalski* does not work as an introduction (use *This is…*).
:::
:::

## Names and titles

English names usually have two or three parts.

::: definition Parts of a name {#def-name-parts}
A person's **first name** (also **given name**; on forms in the US also **forename**) is the name chosen for them, such as *Sarah*. Their **surname** (also **family name**, or **last name**, especially in American English) is the name they share with their family, such as *Cohen*. Some people also have a **middle name** between the two. Their **full name** is all of these together: *Sarah Ruth Cohen*.
:::

In English-speaking countries the first name comes first and the family name last, which is why the family name is often called the *last name*. In Chinese names the order is the other way round: in *Li Wei*, *Li* is the family name and *Wei* the given name. This atlas writes Chinese names family name first, as they are written in Chinese; international newspapers and journals increasingly do the same. In everyday life, however, Chinese people living or working in English-speaking countries make different choices: some keep the Chinese order (*Li Wei*), some reverse it (*Wei Li*), and some use an English given name as well (*David Li*). Forms sometimes ask you to write your surname in capital letters, *LI Wei*, so that the order is clear.

The safest thing is to ask:

- *Sorry, which is your first name?*
- *What would you like me to call you?*
- *How do you pronounce your name?*

### Titles

A **title** is a word such as *Mr* or *Dr* used before a person's name in formal or polite situations.

| title | used for | pronunciation (GB / GA) |
|---|---|---|
| *Mr* | a man | /ˈmɪstə/ / /ˈmɪstər/ |
| *Mrs* | a married woman (traditionally) | /ˈmɪsɪz/ |
| *Ms* | a woman, whether married or not | /mɪz/ or /məz/ |
| *Miss* | a girl or a young unmarried woman | /mɪs/ |
| *Dr* | a medical doctor or a person with a doctorate | /ˈdɒktə/ / /ˈdɑːktər/ |
| *Professor* | a professor (in the US also other university teachers) | /prəˈfesə/ / /prəˈfesər/ |

In British English these short forms are written without a full stop (*Mr Smith*, *Dr Okafor*); in American English with one (*Mr. Smith*, *Dr. Okafor*). *Ms* is now the normal choice in business when you do not know whether a woman is married, and many women prefer it in all situations. Some people prefer *Mx* /mɪks/, a title that does not show gender.

::: theorem Title + family name {#rule-title-surname}
A title goes before the **family name**, or before the full name: *Ms Chen*, *Ms Chen Jie*, *Mr Walsh*, *Mr Tom Walsh*. A title is not used before the first name alone: not *Ms Jie*, not *Mr Tom*.
:::

::: proof
The title and the family name together are the formal way of naming a person; the first name alone is the informal way. Mixing the two (*Mr Tom*) is a common learner error. A few exceptions exist: in parts of the southern United States, *Miss* + first name (*Miss Laura*) is used by children to adults and in some workplaces, and *Sir* + first name is the form for British knights (*Sir Paul*). These are special uses, not the general rule. With Chinese names, the rule means that *Li Wei* is *Mr Li* (if Li Wei is a man), never *Mr Wei*.
:::

Titles are mostly used for people you do not know well, in formal situations, and in schools. In many English-speaking workplaces and universities, people use first names even with managers and professors once they have been introduced. When you are not sure, start with the title, and wait for the other person to say *Call me Rachel* or *Please, it's Rachel*.

A few forms of address are used **without** a name: *Sir* (to a man) and *Madam* (to a woman) by shop and hotel staff in formal service; *Ma'am* (to a woman) mostly in American English. *Doctor* can be used alone to a doctor: *Thank you, Doctor.*

::: warning *Teacher* is not a title
✗ *Good morning, teacher.*

✓ *Good morning, Ms Clarke.* (school) — ✓ *Good morning, Dr Mensah.* / ✓ *Hi, Rachel.* (university, if she uses first names)

In Chinese, 老师 can be used to address a teacher, and to show respect to other people too. In English, *teacher* is the name of a job, not a form of address. Children in British schools often call their teachers *Miss* or *Sir*; in American schools children use the title and family name (*Mr. Lopez*). Adult learners and university students use the title and family name, or the first name if the teacher asks them to.
:::

::: example Choosing a form of address {#ex-address}
How would you greet each person in the morning?

1. Your new neighbour, Mrs Margaret Brown, about 70 years old, whom you meet for the first time.
2. Your classmate Mehmet Yilmaz, 20 years old.
3. Your doctor, Dr Fatima Bello, at an appointment.
4. Your colleague Wang Fang, who has worked next to you for a year.
::: solution
1. *Good morning, Mrs Brown.* She is older and you have just met, so use a title and her family name. If she says *Oh, call me Margaret*, then use *Margaret*.
2. *Hi, Mehmet!* or *Morning, Mehmet!* A classmate of the same age: first name, informal greeting.
3. *Good morning, Dr Bello.* A professional situation: title + family name.
4. *Morning, Fang!* or *Hi, Wang Fang!* A colleague you know well: informal greeting and the given name (or the full name, which is common among Chinese colleagues). *Ms Fang* would be wrong: the title goes with the family name, *Wang*.
:::
:::

## Saying goodbye

Ending a meeting also has its fixed phrases.

| goodbye | register and use |
|---|---|
| *Goodbye.* | neutral to formal; can sound final |
| *Bye.* / *Bye-bye.* | informal and very common; *bye-bye* is also used to children |
| *See you.* / *See you later.* / *See you tomorrow.* | informal; *later* is used even if you will not meet again that day |
| *See you on Monday.* / *See you next week.* | informal, when you know when you will meet |
| *Take care.* | informal and friendly |
| *Have a nice day.* / *Have a good weekend.* | neutral, often to people in shops and at work |
| *Good night.* | in the evening, when leaving or going to bed |
| *Cheers!* | British, very informal (also means 'thanks') |

At the end of a **first** meeting, people often say *Nice to meet you* again, or *Nice meeting you* or *It was nice to meet you*. The answer is *You too.*

**Kwame:** *I have to go — my bus is in five minutes. Nice meeting you, Ana.*

**Ana:** *You too! See you in class on Thursday.*

**Kwame:** *Yeah, see you Thursday. Bye!*

**Ana:** *Bye!*

Notice that Kwame does not just say *Bye* and leave. He first gives a reason (*my bus is in five minutes*). English speakers usually prepare the end of a conversation like this; the last lesson of this course, [[everyday-english/small-talk]], shows how.

::: example Formal or informal goodbye? {#ex-goodbye}
Choose a suitable way to say goodbye in each situation, and explain your choice.

1. Leaving a job interview with Mr Patel.
2. Leaving your friend's flat at 11 p.m.
3. Paying in a café where you often go.
::: solution
1. *Thank you very much for your time, Mr Patel. Goodbye.* — The situation is formal, so thank him, use the title and family name, and choose *Goodbye* rather than *Bye*. Shaking hands is common at the end of an interview.
2. *Thanks for tonight — it was great. Good night! See you soon.* — Informal, and it is late in the evening, so *Good night* fits.
3. *Thanks! See you tomorrow!* or *Thanks, have a nice day!* — Informal and friendly; *See you tomorrow* shows that you are a regular customer.
:::
:::

::: quiz
At the end of your first meeting, a new colleague says *It was nice to meet you.* What is the most natural answer?
- [ ] *Nice to meet you.*
- [x] *You too.*
- [ ] *Me too.*
- [ ] *I'm fine, thanks.*
::: solution
*You too* means 'It was nice to meet **you** too'. *Me too* would mean 'I was also nice to meet', which is not what you want to say. *Nice to meet you* repeated is possible but sounds like a new greeting; *I'm fine* answers a different question.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Greeting questions are different.** Chinese greetings such as 吃了吗？ or 去哪儿啊？ are also not real questions, but their English translations *Have you eaten?* and *Where are you going?* are real questions in English: the first can sound like an invitation to a meal, the second like a personal question. In English, use *Hi, how are you?* for the same job.
- **你好 and *How are you?*** 你好 is a greeting, not a question, and is translated *Hello*. *How are you?* is often translated 你好吗？, but 你好吗？ is a real question in Chinese, used much less often than *How are you?* in English.
- **Names.** Chinese puts the family name first (*Li Wei*), English the given name first (*Sarah Cohen*). Titles go with the family name in both languages: 王老师, 陈经理 — *Ms Wang*, *Mr Chen*. But English has no titles of job or position such as 经理 or 老师 to use in greetings, apart from *Doctor* and *Professor*.
- **您 and *you*.** English has only one word for *you*. Politeness is shown in other ways: titles and family names, *please* and *thank you*, and polite forms such as *Could you…?* (see [[everyday-english/requests-offers]]).
:::

::: history
*Goodbye* is a shortened form of *God be with you*. Letters of the late sixteenth century already write it as one word, in spellings such as *godbwye*, and *God* later changed to *good*, probably under the influence of greetings like *good day* and *good night*. *Hello* is much younger. It developed in the nineteenth century from earlier calls such as *hallo* and *hollo*, used to get someone's attention, and it became the usual greeting partly through the telephone (see the historical note in [[everyday-english/phone-messages]]). Older people in Britain may still use *hullo*, another spelling of the same word.
:::

## Where this leads

The next lesson, [[everyday-english/personal-information]], continues the first meeting: where you are from, what you do, how to spell your name. Ending a conversation politely is the subject of [[everyday-english/small-talk]], and introducing yourself on the phone of [[everyday-english/phone-messages]]. The verb *be* in *I'm Ana* and *This is Tom* is explained in [[core-grammar/be-and-have]]; the sounds of names and the stress of phrases such as *Nice to meet **you*** in [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics]]. Later, [[pragmatics/politeness-face]] explains why forms of address and greetings matter so much, and [[listening-and-conversation/turn-taking]] shows how a conversation continues after the greeting.

::: summary
- Choose greetings by time and register: *Hello* is safe everywhere, *Hi* and *Hey* are informal, *Good morning/afternoon/evening* are neutral to formal.
- *Good night* is only for leaving or going to bed, never for meeting ([[#rule-good-night]]).
- *How are you?* is usually a greeting question: answer briefly and positively and ask it back — *Fine, thanks. And you?* ([[#rule-how-are-you]]).
- Introduce yourself with *I'm…* or *My name's…*; answer *Nice to meet you* with *Nice to meet you too*.
- Introduce others with *This is…*, giving both names and, if possible, one piece of information ([[#rule-this-is]]).
- Titles (*Mr*, *Ms*, *Dr*) go with the family name: *Ms Chen*, never *Ms Jie* ([[#rule-title-surname]]); *teacher* is not a form of address.
- End a first meeting with *Nice meeting you* — *You too*, and prepare the end of a conversation with a reason before *Bye*.
:::

::: exercise Change the register {level=2}
This introduction is very informal. Rewrite it for a formal situation: a visitor, Dr Hannah Becker, meets the director, Mr Carlos Ruiz, at a conference.

**Assistant:** *Hey Carlos, this is Hannah.*

**Carlos:** *Hi Hannah! How's it going?*

**Hannah:** *Good! You?*
::: hint
Use a formal introduction, titles and full names, and *Pleased to meet you*.
:::
::: solution
A possible answer:

**Assistant:** *Mr Ruiz, I'd like you to meet Dr Hannah Becker, from the University of Leeds. Dr Becker, this is Carlos Ruiz, our director.*

**Mr Ruiz:** *Good morning, Dr Becker. Pleased to meet you.*

**Dr Becker:** *Pleased to meet you too, Mr Ruiz.*

The changes: *I'd like you to meet* instead of *this is* for the first introduction; titles and full names; one piece of information about each person; *Good morning* and *Pleased to meet you* instead of *Hi* and *How's it going?*. (*Can I introduce…?* is also correct.) In practice, Mr Ruiz might then say *Please, call me Carlos.*
:::

::: exercise Write a dialogue {level=3}
Write a short dialogue (8–10 turns) at a party. You meet a new person, Yusuf, introduce yourselves, and then introduce Yusuf to your friend Mei Ling. End with a goodbye.
::: hint
Use *Hi, I'm…*, *Nice to meet you*, *This is…*, one piece of information about each person, and *Nice meeting you* — *You too* at the end.
:::
::: solution
A model answer (yours will be different):

**You:** *Hi, I'm Zhou Lei. I don't think we've met.*

**Yusuf:** *No, I don't think so. I'm Yusuf. Nice to meet you.*

**You:** *Nice to meet you too. How do you know Sam?*

**Yusuf:** *We work together. And you?*

**You:** *We were at university together. Oh — Mei Ling! Come and meet Yusuf. Yusuf, this is my friend Mei Ling. Mei Ling, this is Yusuf — he works with Sam.*

**Mei Ling:** *Hi, Yusuf. Good to meet you.*

**Yusuf:** *You too.*

… (later) …

**Yusuf:** *Well, I should go — I have an early start tomorrow. It was nice meeting you both.*

**You:** *You too! Good night.*

Check your dialogue: does each new person give a name, does each *Nice to meet you* get an answer, does the introduction use *this is* and both names, and does the goodbye come after a reason?

::: exercise Introduce yourself {level=3}
Prepare and say aloud a 30-second introduction of yourself for the first meeting of an English class. Include a greeting, your name (say which part is your family name), where you are from, and one more fact. Then write what you would say if the teacher answered *Nice to meet you. What should we call you?*
::: solution
A model answer:

*Hello, everyone. My name's Liu Yang. Liu is my family name and Yang is my first name, so in English it's sometimes written Yang Liu. I'm from Harbin, in the north-east of China, and I'm studying engineering. I like playing basketball, and I'm looking forward to practising my English with you.*

To the teacher's question: *Please call me Yang.* (or *Most people call me Yang*, or *You can call me Lucy — it's my English name.*)

When you say it aloud, keep a steady speed, stress the important words (your **name**, the **place**, your **subject**), and pause briefly after each sentence.
:::

::: exercise Greeting or question? {level=2}
For each situation, say whether the speaker most probably wants real information. Explain why in one sentence.

(a) A receptionist: *Good morning! How are you today?*

(b) Your mother on the phone, after you started a new job: *So how are you? How's the new job?*

(c) A friend you see in the street: *Hey! How are things?*
::: solution
(a) No — a greeting question from a stranger at work; answer *Fine, thanks. And you?*

(b) Yes — she knows your situation and asks a specific follow-up question, so tell her your news.

(c) Usually not, though a friend may stop and listen; start short (*Good, thanks! You?*) and say more if your friend stops to talk.
