Wang Jun arrives at the front desk of a language school in Dublin to register for a course.

**Receptionist:** *Can I have your name, please?*

**Wang Jun:** *Wang Jun.*

**Receptionist:** *Thanks. Could you spell that for me?*

**Wang Jun:** *W–A–N–G, J–U–N.*

**Receptionist:** *Sorry — was that G or J at the end of the first name?*

**Wang Jun:** *N. J–U–N. The J is at the start.*

**Receptionist:** *Ah, OK. And is Wang your first name or your surname?*

Two short questions, and two small problems: which letter is which when spoken, and which part of the name is the family name. Giving personal information — your name, where you are from, what you do, your family, your email address — is the second step of almost every first meeting, and it is also what forms, receptionists and new colleagues ask for. This lesson teaches the questions and answers, how to spell aloud with the English names of the letters, and how to give an email address so that the other person writes it down correctly.

## Asking and answering about yourself

These are the commonest questions in a first conversation, with typical answers.

| question | typical answers |
|---|---|
| *What's your name?* (direct, e.g. at a desk) / *Can I have your name, please?* (polite) | *Wang Jun.* / *It's Ana Souza.* / *My name's Kwame Mensah.* |
| *Where are you from?* | *I'm from Kenya.* / *I'm from Nanjing, in the east of China.* / *Originally from Lima, but I live in Toronto now.* |
| *Where do you live?* | *In Leeds.* / *I live in a flat near the station.* (BrE) / *I live in an apartment downtown.* (AmE) |
| *What do you do?* | *I'm a nurse.* / *I work in a hospital.* / *I'm an engineer.* / *I'm retired.* / *I'm looking for a job at the moment.* |
| *Where do you work?* | *At a primary school.* / *I work for a small design company.* / *I work from home.* |
| *Are you a student?* / *What are you studying?* | *Yes, I'm studying chemistry.* / *I'm in my second year.* / AmE also: *What's your major?* — *I'm majoring in history.* |
| *Have you got any brothers or sisters?* (BrE) / *Do you have any brothers or sisters?* (BrE and AmE) | *Yes, I've got an older sister.* / *I have two younger brothers.* / *No, I'm an only child.* |

Here is how these questions sound in a real first conversation, at a language exchange evening.

**Elena:** *So where are you from, Wang Jun?*

**Wang Jun:** *I'm from China — from Qingdao. It's on the coast, in the east.*

**Elena:** *Oh, nice. And what do you do?*

**Wang Jun:** *I'm a software developer. I'm here for six months to improve my English. How about you? Are you from Dublin?*

**Elena:** *No, I'm from Bulgaria originally, but I've lived here for five years. I work at the university — I'm a lab technician.*

**Wang Jun:** *That sounds interesting. Do you like it?*

**Elena:** *Yes, I love it.*

Notice three things. First, the answers are short, but they are a little longer than one word: *I'm from China — from Qingdao. It's on the coast.* An extra detail gives the other person something to ask about. Second, each person asks the question back (*How about you?*). Third, the answers use the same verb as the question: *Where do you **live**?* — *I **live** in…*; *What do you **do**?* — *I'**m** a…* (the grammar of these questions is in [[core-grammar/questions-and-negatives]]).

::: theorem *What do you do?* asks about a job {#rule-what-do-you-do}
*What do you do?* (also *What do you do for a living?*) means 'What is your job?'. It does not ask what you are doing now (*What are you doing?*) or what you do in your free time (*What do you do in your free time?*). Answer with a job, *I'm a student*, or another situation: *I'm retired*, *I'm looking for work*, *I look after my children*.
:::

::: proof
The question uses the present simple, the tense for permanent situations and regular activities ([[core-grammar/present-simple]]), and a person's main regular activity is their job. *What are you doing?* uses the present continuous for something in progress now ([[core-grammar/present-continuous]]). Some people find *What do you do?* too direct as a first question, because not everyone has a job they want to talk about; in a first conversation it is common, but it is kind to accept any answer without surprise.
:::

::: theorem *A* or *an* before a job {#rule-job-article}
With *be*, a singular job or position takes *a* or *an*: *I'm **a** nurse.* *She's **an** engineer.* *He's **a** student.* Use *an* before a vowel sound: *an architect*, *an IT manager* (/ˌaɪ ˈtiː/). The plural has no article: *They're teachers.*
:::

::: proof
English singular countable nouns need a determiner such as *a*, *the* or *my*; a job after *be* says what kind of person someone is, so it takes *a/an* ([[nouns-and-articles/a-and-the]]). The choice between *a* and *an* depends on the first **sound**, not the first letter: *a university* (/ˌjuː-/), *an hour* (silent *h*). *Work* + *as* also takes the article: *I work as a translator.* After *work in* or *work for*, the noun is a place or a company, not a job: *I work in a hospital.*
:::

::: warning Do not leave out *a* before a job
✗ *I'm student.* ✗ *My mother is doctor.*

✓ *I'm a student.* ✓ *My mother is a doctor.*

This is one of the most common mistakes of Chinese speakers, because Chinese says 我是学生 with no word for *a*. The sentence without *a* is understood, but it sounds wrong to every English speaker. Learn the job together with its article: *a student*, *an engineer*, *a nurse*.
:::

::: quiz
At a party, someone asks you *What do you do?* You are a university student. Which answer fits best?
- [ ] *I'm drinking orange juice.*
- [ ] *I'm student.*
- [x] *I'm a student. I'm studying economics.*
- [ ] *I do my homework.*
::: solution
*What do you do?* asks about your job or main occupation, so the answer is *I'm a student*, with the article *a*, and an extra detail helps the conversation. *I'm drinking orange juice* answers *What are you doing?*; *I'm student* has no article; *I do my homework* is a strange answer to a question about your occupation.
:::
:::

::: example From one word to a good answer {#ex-extend}
At a welcome event, a new colleague asks Kim Soo-ah three questions, and she answers each with one word: *Where are you from?* — *Korea.* *Where do you live?* — *Brighton.* *What do you do?* — *Marketing.* Improve her answers so that the conversation can continue.
::: solution
Step 1: make each answer a short sentence, which sounds friendlier than a single word. *I'm from Korea.* / *I live in Brighton.* / *I work in marketing.*

Step 2: add one detail that the other person can ask about. *I'm from Korea — from Busan, by the sea.* / *I live in Brighton, so I come here by train every day.* / *I work in marketing. I've just joined the team that plans the online adverts.*

Step 3: ask one question back, so that the conversation goes both ways. *How about you? Do you live near here?*

One-word answers are not wrong — they are normal at a reception desk, where the other person only needs the facts. In a conversation, though, a series of one-word answers can sound as if you do not want to talk.
:::
:::

## Family

The words for close family are:

| | female | male | either |
|---|---|---|---|
| your parents' parents | *grandmother* (*grandma*, *granny*) | *grandfather* (*grandpa*, *granddad*) | *grandparent* |
| your parents | *mother* (*mum* BrE, *mom* AmE) | *father* (*dad*) | *parent* |
| your parents' other children | *sister* | *brother* | — (*sibling* is formal) |
| your parents' brothers and sisters | *aunt* | *uncle* | — |
| your aunt's or uncle's children | — | — | *cousin* |
| your children | *daughter* | *son* | *child* (plural *children*) |
| your brother's or sister's children | *niece* | *nephew* | — |

To talk about your family, use *have* or *have got* (British English) with a number, and *be* for jobs and ages:

- *I've got two sisters.* (BrE) / *I have two sisters.* (BrE and AmE)
- *My older sister is a teacher, and my younger brother is still at school.*
- *My parents live in Hangzhou. My dad's an engineer and my mum works in a bank.*
- *I don't have any brothers or sisters. I'm an only child.*
- *We're a big family — I've got lots of cousins.*

*Have got* is common in British speech, *have* in American English and in writing everywhere; both are correct. In questions, *Have you got…?* is British and *Do you have…?* is used everywhere. See [[core-grammar/be-and-have]].

::: example Describing a family {#ex-family}
Use the family figure above. Imagine you are the person marked "me", and your name is Ana. Write four sentences about your family that answer *Tell me about your family.*
::: solution
Step 1: say how many brothers and sisters you have. *I've got one brother and one sister.* (or *I have one brother and one sister.*)

Step 2: add who is older or younger. *My brother is older than me, and my sister is younger.* — English has no single word for 'older brother' or 'younger sister', so *older* and *younger* are added. (British English also uses *elder* before a noun: *my elder brother*.)

Step 3: mention the wider family. *My dad has one sister, my aunt. She's married, and she has one son — my cousin.*

Step 4: add one detail. *My cousin and I are the same age, so we're very close.*

The sentences use *have* or *have got* for family members and *be* for descriptions, and they give a little more than the question strictly needs, which keeps the conversation going.
:::
:::

## Spelling aloud

Every letter of the alphabet has a **name**, which is how you say it when you spell. The name is often different from the sound the letter usually stands for in words.

::: definition Letter name {#def-letter-name}
The **name** of a letter is the word used to say that letter on its own: the letter *h* is called /eɪtʃ/, although it usually stands for the sound /h/ in words such as *hat*. Spelling aloud means saying the names of the letters one after another: *Jun* is spelled /dʒeɪ – juː – en/.
:::

The 26 letter names fall into groups by their vowel sound. Learning them in these groups is easier than learning them in alphabetical order, because the letters in each group rhyme.

| vowel | letters and their names |
|---|---|
| /eɪ/ as in *day* | A /eɪ/, H /eɪtʃ/, J /dʒeɪ/, K /keɪ/ |
| /iː/ as in *see* | B /biː/, C /siː/, D /diː/, E /iː/, G /dʒiː/, P /piː/, T /tiː/, V /viː/, (Z /ziː/ in American English) |
| /e/ as in *bed* | F /ef/, L /el/, M /em/, N /en/, S /es/, X /eks/, (Z /zed/ in British English) |
| /aɪ/ as in *my* | I /aɪ/, Y /waɪ/ |
| /əʊ/ GB, /oʊ/ GA as in *go* | O |
| /uː/ as in *you* | Q /kjuː/, U /juː/, W /ˈdʌbəljuː/ |
| /ɑː/ GB, /ɑːr/ GA as in *car* | R |

Some speakers in Ireland, Australia and parts of Britain say H as /heɪtʃ/; both forms are used. The letter Z is the clearest difference between British and American English: *zed* in British English (and in Canada, Australia and many other countries), *zee* in American English.

::: theorem How to spell aloud clearly {#rule-spelling}
1. Say the letters in small groups, with a short pause between the parts of a name: *W–A–N–G* … *J–U–N*.
2. For two identical letters together, British speakers usually say *double* + the letter (*double L*, *double O*); American speakers more often say the letter twice (*L, L*). Both are understood everywhere.
3. Say *capital* before a capital letter if it matters, and say *hyphen*, *space* or *apostrophe* where the name has one: *O, apostrophe, B–R–I–E–N* (*O'Brien*).
4. To make a letter clear, add a word that begins with it: *B for brother*, *P for Paris*, or use the spelling alphabet below.
:::

::: proof
Many letter names sound almost the same, especially on a phone or in a noisy place: B, D, E, G, P, T and V all end in /iː/, and F and S, M and N differ only in their consonant. Groups and pauses help the listener to keep their place, and a word such as *brother* removes the doubt because it gives the letter in a longer, clearer context. Rule 2 is a habit, not a strict rule: *double* is also heard in American English, and *L, L* in British English.
:::

For very clear spelling — on a bad phone line, at an airport, when a mistake would be expensive — many organisations use the international radiotelephony spelling alphabet, adopted by the International Civil Aviation Organization: *Alfa, Bravo, Charlie, Delta, Echo, Foxtrot, Golf, Hotel, India, Juliett, Kilo, Lima, Mike, November, Oscar, Papa, Quebec, Romeo, Sierra, Tango, Uniform, Victor, Whiskey, X-ray, Yankee, Zulu.* (*Alfa* and *Juliett* are spelled this way on purpose, so that speakers of other languages pronounce them correctly.) In everyday life, any clear word will do: *J for Japan*, *G for Germany*.

::: example Spelling a name at a hotel {#ex-spelling}
Chen Jie arrives at a hotel. The receptionist cannot find her booking and asks her to spell her name. Write what she says, and explain each choice.
::: solution
**Receptionist:** *Could you spell your surname for me, please?*

**Chen Jie:** *Yes, it's Chen: C–H–E–N.* — She says first which part she is spelling (*surname*), because the receptionist may expect the family name last.

**Receptionist:** *C–H–E–N. And your first name?*

**Chen Jie:** *Jie: J for Japan, I, E.* — J and G are easy to confuse, so she adds a word that starts with J.

**Receptionist:** *Ah, I see — it's here under "Jie Chen". Sorry about that!*

The booking was under the English order, given name first. By naming the parts (*surname*, *first name*) and making the difficult letter clear, Chen Jie solved both problems.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which letter name has the same vowel sound as the letter A?
- [ ] E
- [ ] I
- [x] K
- [ ] R
::: solution
A is /eɪ/, and K is /keɪ/: they rhyme. The /eɪ/ group is A, H, J and K. E is /iː/, I is /aɪ/, and R is /ɑː/ (GB) or /ɑːr/ (GA).
:::
:::

::: quiz
A British receptionist checks a surname: *R, U, double S, E, double L?* Which name is she spelling?
- [ ] *Rusel*
- [ ] *Russel*
- [x] *Russell*
- [ ] *Rousell*
::: solution
*Double S* means two S's together and *double L* two L's together: R–U–S–S–E–L–L, *Russell*. The word *double* always refers to the letter that follows it.
:::
:::

## Email addresses

An email address is read aloud in a fixed way. Take the address *li.na_88@example.com*:

*li dot na underscore eight eight at example dot com*

| symbol | name | notes |
|---|---|---|
| @ | *at* | never *a* or *circle a* |
| . | *dot* | in an email or web address; in a number it is *point* (*3.5* = *three point five*) |
| _ | *underscore* | |
| - | *hyphen* or *dash* | both are common in addresses |
| / | *slash* (*forward slash*) | in web addresses |

::: theorem Giving an email address {#rule-email}
Say *at* for @ and *dot* for the full stop; say the parts that are ordinary words as words (*example*, *com*) and spell the parts that are not (*L–I dot N–A*); say *all lower case* or *all one word* if that helps; and ask the listener to read it back: *Could you read that back to me?*
:::

::: proof
An email address must be exactly right, but some parts are hard to hear: names from other languages, numbers, and symbols. Saying a common word as a word is quicker and clearer than spelling it: *example* is said as a word, *L–I* is spelled. (This atlas uses *example.com*, *example.org* and *example.net* in its addresses because these names are reserved for examples and belong to no real person or business.) Reading back is the best check, because the person who writes it down then says what they actually wrote.
:::

**Wang Jun:** *My email's wang dot jun at example dot com. That's W–A–N–G dot J–U–N.*

**Receptionist:** *So W–A–N–G dot J–U–N at example dot com.*

**Wang Jun:** *That's right.*

Phone numbers, dates of birth and other numbers are the subject of the next lesson, [[everyday-english/numbers-in-conversation]].

## Filling in a form

Forms use short labels instead of questions. Here is a registration form, filled in by Wang Jun.

| field | what it asks | Wang Jun's answer |
|---|---|---|
| *Title* | *Mr*, *Mrs*, *Ms*, *Miss*, *Dr*, *Mx* | *Mr* |
| *First name(s)* / *Given name(s)* / *Forename(s)* | your given name | *Jun* |
| *Surname* / *Family name* / *Last name* | your family name | *WANG* |
| *Date of birth* (*DOB*) | day, month, year | *14/03/1998* (BrE order: day/month/year) |
| *Nationality* | the country you are a citizen of | *Chinese* |
| *Address* | your address in the country | *Flat 4, 21 Harbour Road, Dublin* |
| *Postcode* (BrE) / *ZIP code* (AmE) / *Eircode* (Ireland) | the code for your street | (his code) |
| *Email* | | *wang.jun@example.com* |
| *Occupation* | your job | *Software developer* |
| *Signature* | your name written by hand | (signed) |

Many forms say *Please write in BLOCK CAPITALS* (or *capital letters*): write every letter as a capital, *WANG JUN*, so that it is easy to read. *Nationality* asks for an adjective (*Chinese*, *Brazilian*, *Kenyan*), not the name of the country (*China*). The order of the date matters: British forms usually write day/month/year and American forms month/day/year, so *03/04/2000* is 3 April in Britain but March 4 in the United States; see [[everyday-english/numbers-in-conversation]].

::: example Completing a form from a conversation {#ex-form}
Read what Ms Fatima Bello says at a gym reception, then fill in the form fields *Title*, *First name*, *Surname*, *Occupation* and *Email*.

*Hi, I'd like to join the gym. My name's Fatima Bello — that's B–E–double L–O. I'm a pharmacist. My email is f underscore bello at example dot org.*
::: solution
- *Title*: *Ms* — she gives no title, so *Ms* is the safe choice; ask her if the form requires one.
- *First name*: *Fatima* — the name she says first, in the English order.
- *Surname*: *BELLO* — *double L* means two L's: B–E–L–L–O. Many forms want the surname in capitals.
- *Occupation*: *Pharmacist* — the job without the article *a*, because a form field is a label, not a sentence.
- *Email*: *f_bello@example.org* — *underscore* is _, *at* is @, *dot* is the full stop.

Notice the last point about articles: *I'm **a** pharmacist* in a sentence, but just *Pharmacist* in a form.
:::
:::

::: warning First name and surname in the wrong boxes
✗ First name: *Li* — Surname: *Wei* (for Li Wei, whose family name is Li)

✓ First name: *Wei* — Surname: *LI*

On English forms, the first box is usually for the given name, whatever order you use in your own language. Read the labels, not the order of the boxes, and write the surname in capitals to make it clear. If your passport shows your name in a particular order, use the same names on official forms.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **No article in Chinese.** 我是学生 has no word for *a*, so *I'm student* is a very natural transfer; in English the article is required ([[#rule-job-article]]).
- **Family words.** Chinese has separate words for older and younger brothers and sisters (哥哥, 弟弟, 姐姐, 妹妹) and for relatives on the father's and the mother's side (叔叔, 舅舅, 姑姑, 姨, 堂兄, 表妹 …). English has far fewer: *brother*, *sister*, *uncle*, *aunt* and *cousin* cover them all. Add *older* or *younger* when it matters, or say *my mum's sister*, *my dad's brother*.
- **Letter names and Pinyin.** Pinyin uses the same Latin letters as English, but the English letter names are different: the English letter *i* is called /aɪ/ (like *eye*) and *e* is called /iː/ (like the Pinyin syllable *yi*). This is why E and I are often confused; G /dʒiː/ and J /dʒeɪ/ are another common pair.
- **Personal questions.** Some questions that are polite and normal in many Chinese conversations — age, income, whether someone is married — are often avoided in a first conversation in English, especially with adults. *What do you do?* and *Where are you from?* are safe; see [[everyday-english/small-talk]] and [[pragmatics/small-talk-norms]].
:::

::: history
The sign @ was used in trade long before computers, to mean 'at a price of' (*5 apples @ 10p*). In 1971 the American engineer Ray Tomlinson, working on the ARPANET, a forerunner of the internet, sent messages between two computers and chose @ to separate the user's name from the name of the computer. This is why we read the symbol *at*: *wang.jun at example.com* is Wang Jun "at" the computer called *example.com*. The spelling alphabet that begins *Alfa, Bravo, Charlie* came into use for international aviation in the 1950s, after several earlier alphabets had been tested with speakers of different languages.
:::

## Where this leads

Numbers in personal information — phone numbers, dates of birth, ages, prices — are taught in [[everyday-english/numbers-in-conversation]]. Talking more fully about your life, your routine and your interests is the subject of [[everyday-english/talking-about-yourself]]. The questions *Where do you live?* and *What do you do?* are formed as explained in [[core-grammar/questions-and-negatives]], and *a* and *an* in [[nouns-and-articles/a-and-the]]. The sounds of the letter names are taught in [[sounds-of-english/long-vowels-diphthongs]], and filling in forms and writing short messages return in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/emails-messages]].

::: summary
- The key questions are *Where are you from?*, *Where do you live?*, *What do you do?* and *Have you got / Do you have any brothers or sisters?*; answer briefly, add one detail, and ask back.
- *What do you do?* asks about your job ([[#rule-what-do-you-do]]); jobs after *be* take *a/an*: *I'm a student* ([[#rule-job-article]]).
- English family words do not show older or younger, or the father's or mother's side: add *older*, *younger*, *my mum's…*.
- Letter names come in rhyming groups; Z is *zed* (BrE) and *zee* (AmE); E/I and G/J are easy to confuse.
- Spell in groups, use *double* (BrE) or repeat the letter (AmE), and add a word to make a letter clear ([[#rule-spelling]]).
- Read an email address with *at*, *dot*, *underscore*, *hyphen*, and ask for it to be read back ([[#rule-email]]).
- On forms, the first-name box is for the given name; *Nationality* takes an adjective; the date order differs between BrE and AmE.
:::

*Good morning. I'm Dr Priya Nair. I'm Indian, and I work as a dentist here in Sydney.*
::: solution

::: exercise A first conversation {level=3}
Write a dialogue of 10–12 turns between you and a new classmate, Carlos, who is from Mexico. Ask and answer about where you are from, where you live, what you do, and your families. Each of you should ask at least one question back.
::: hint
Use the table at the start of the lesson. Give short answers with one extra detail, and use *How about you?* or *And you?* to ask back.
:::
::: solution
A model answer:

**You:** *Hi, I'm Tang Yu. You're Carlos, right?*

**Carlos:** *That's right. Nice to meet you, Tang Yu.*

**You:** *Nice to meet you too. Where are you from, Carlos?*

**Carlos:** *I'm from Mexico — from Guadalajara. How about you?*

**You:** *I'm from Xiamen, in the south-east of China. Do you live near the school?*

**Carlos:** *Yes, I live with a host family about ten minutes away. And you?*

**You:** *I've got a room in a student flat near the river. What do you do in Mexico?*

**Carlos:** *I'm a civil engineer. I build bridges, actually! What about you?*

**You:** *I'm a student. I'm studying accounting. Have you got any brothers or sisters?*

**Carlos:** *Yes, I've got two older sisters. Both of them are teachers. You?*

**You:** *No, I'm an only child.*

Check: each answer gives a detail; each question is asked back at least once; jobs have *a/an*; *have got* or *have* is used for family.
:::

::: exercise Spell your own name {level=3}
(a) Write how you would spell your own full name aloud to a receptionist on the phone, saying which part is the family name and making any difficult letters clear. (b) Write your email address as you would say it, and the sentence you would use to ask the receptionist to check it.
::: solution
A model answer for the name Xu Jiaqi and the address *xu.jiaqi@example.com*:

(a) *My surname is Xu: X for X-ray, U. My first name is Jiaqi: J for Japan, I, A, Q for Quebec, I.*

(b) *My email is xu dot jiaqi at example dot com — that's X–U dot J–I–A–Q–I. Could you read that back to me, please?*

Points to check in your answer: you name the parts (*surname*, *first name*); you make letters that are easy to confuse clear (here X, J, Q); you use *at* and *dot*; you ask for a read-back.
:::

::: exercise Personal or not? {level=2}
You meet a new colleague, Mr Daniel Okoro, about 50 years old, for the first time. Which of these questions are normal in English at a first meeting, and which are usually avoided? (a) *Where are you from?* (b) *How old are you?* (c) *How much do you earn?* (d) *Which team are you in?* (e) *Are you married?*
::: solution
Normal: (a) and (d). Usually avoided at a first meeting with an adult: (b), (c) and (e), which many people find too personal. People may tell you these things themselves later; you can respond with interest, but it is better not to ask first.
:::
