At a language exchange evening, Li Jun meets Rosa, a Spanish teacher who is learning Chinese.

**Rosa:** *So what do you do in your free time?*

**Li Jun:** *I like hiking. Last weekend I go to the mountains with my friends, and next month I go to Yunnan.*

Rosa understands — but she has to work out the times herself, because every verb is in the same form: *I go*. In Chinese this would be natural: 上个周末我跟朋友去爬山，下个月我去云南, where *上个周末* and *下个月* carry the time and the verb 去 does not change. In English the verb itself must change: *Last weekend I **went** to the mountains, and next month I'**m going** to Yunnan.*

A first conversation moves through three kinds of time very quickly: what you **usually** do (your routine and your likes), what you **did** (last weekend, your last holiday), and what you **are going to do** (your plans). This lesson teaches the phrases for each — and the verb forms that go with them, in their simplest everyday use. The grammar of each form is taught in *Core Grammar*: [[core-grammar/present-simple]], [[core-grammar/past-simple]] and [[core-grammar/talking-about-the-future]].

## Your routine

A **routine** is what you do regularly — every day, every week. English uses the present simple for routines.

- *I get up at seven and have breakfast at home.*
- *I usually take the bus to work, but on Fridays I cycle.*
- *I finish work at half past five and get home at about six.*
- *In the evening I cook dinner, and sometimes I watch a film.*
- *I go to bed at about eleven.*
- *At weekends* (BrE) / *On weekends* (AmE) *I play football and visit my parents.*

With *he*, *she* and *it*, add *-s*: *My sister **works** in a hospital. She **gets** up at five.* Questions and negatives use *do* and *does*: *What time **do** you get up? She **doesn't** work on Sundays.* See [[core-grammar/present-simple]].

### How often?

::: definition Adverb of frequency {#def-frequency}
An **adverb of frequency** says how often something happens: *always*, *usually*, *often*, *sometimes*, *rarely*, *hardly ever*, *never*. Longer **expressions of frequency** do the same job: *every day*, *once a week*, *twice a month*, *three times a year*, *every other day* ('one day yes, one day no'), *now and then* ('sometimes').
:::

::: widget graph
nodes: never@0,0; hardly ever@1.4,0; rarely@2.8,0; sometimes@4.2,0; often@5.6,0; usually@7,0; always@8.4,0
edges: never>hardly ever; hardly ever>rarely; rarely>sometimes; sometimes>often; often>usually; usually>always
title: From never to always
caption: The adverbs of frequency in order, from least to most often. The scale is an order, not a set of exact numbers: *often* and *usually* are not fixed percentages, and speakers differ. *Hardly ever* and *rarely* are close; *rarely* is a little more formal.
:::

::: theorem Where adverbs of frequency go {#rule-frequency-position}
A one-word adverb of frequency usually goes
1. **before** the main verb: *I **usually** take the bus.* *She **never** eats meat.*
2. **after** *be*: *I'm **always** late on Mondays.* *He's **often** tired.*
3. **after** the first auxiliary: *I've **never** been to Japan.* *I can **sometimes** work from home.*

Expressions of more than one word (*every day*, *once a week*) usually go at the **end**: *I go swimming **twice a week**.* *Sometimes*, *usually* and *often* can also begin a sentence for emphasis: ***Sometimes** I walk to work.*
:::

::: proof
The pattern is the same as for other short adverbs of this kind in English: they sit in the middle of the clause, next to the verb they describe. *Be* is treated differently because it is not a main verb of action; the adverb follows it as it follows an auxiliary. *Never* and *always* almost never begin an ordinary sentence (✗ *Always I get up early*); *Never* at the start is possible only in a formal, emphatic structure with inversion (*Never have I seen such a mess*), taught in [[complex-sentences/emphasis-inversion]].
:::

::: warning Word order with *always* and *never*
✗ *I always am tired after work.* ✗ *Always I drink coffee in the morning.* ✗ *She goes never to the gym.*

✓ *I'm always tired after work.* ✓ *I always drink coffee in the morning.* ✓ *She never goes to the gym.*

In Chinese, 总是 and 从不 come before the verb and also before 是 (我总是很累), so the position after *be* is the one most often missed. Remember the two patterns: adverb + main verb (*always drink*), *be* + adverb (*am always*).
:::

::: example Describing a routine from notes {#ex-routine}
Ana made these notes about her week. Turn them into a short spoken description of her routine.

*Mon–Fri: up 6.30 — gym 3×/week before work — bus to office (always) — lunch at desk (usually) — home 6 — cook (most days) / takeaway Fri. Weekends: sleep late, see friends, Sun: call parents.*
::: solution
Step 1: choose the present simple for regular actions, and *I* as the subject.

Step 2: turn each note into a clause, putting the frequency words in their places — one-word adverbs before the verb, longer expressions at the end:

*On weekdays I get up at half past six. I go to the gym three times a week, before work. I always take the bus to the office, and I usually have lunch at my desk. I get home at about six. Most days I cook dinner, but on Fridays I usually get a takeaway. At the weekend I sleep late and see my friends, and on Sundays I always call my parents.*

Step 3: check the details: *three times a week* at the end; *always* and *usually* before the main verb; *at the weekend* (BrE; AmE *on the weekend*); *get a takeaway* (BrE; AmE *get takeout*).
:::
:::

## Likes and dislikes

From strongest liking to strongest dislike:

| | + *-ing* or a noun |
|---|---|
| *I love…* | *I love cooking.* / *I love jazz.* |
| *I really like…* / *I'm really into…* (informal) | *I'm really into photography.* |
| *I like…* / *I enjoy…* | *I enjoy hiking.* |
| *I quite like…* (BrE: 'I like it a little') | *I quite like dancing.* |
| *I don't mind…* ('it's OK, I have no problem with it') | *I don't mind cleaning.* |
| *I'm not keen on…* (BrE) / *I'm not a big fan of…* | *I'm not keen on spicy food.* |
| *I don't like…* / *I don't really like…* | *I don't really like football.* |
| *I can't stand…* / *I hate…* | *I can't stand waiting in queues.* |

*Like*, *love* and *hate* can also be followed by *to* + verb, especially in American English and for habits (*I like to get up early*); the *-ing* form is more common for activities we enjoy. *Enjoy*, *don't mind* and *can't stand* take only *-ing*: ✗ *I enjoy to swim*. See [[verb-patterns/ing-or-infinitive]].

To ask:

- *What do you like doing in your free time?* / *What do you do in your free time?*
- *What kind of music do you like?* / *What sort of films do you like?*
- *Do you like cooking?* — *Yes, I do. I love it.* / *Not really.* / *It's OK.*
- *Are you into sport?* (informal)

::: theorem *I like* and *I'd like* {#rule-like-would-like}
*I like…* talks about what you enjoy in general: *I like swimming* ('I enjoy it; I often do it'). *I'd like…* (*I would like*) means 'I want' now or at a particular time, and is the polite form of a wish or a request: *I'd like to go swimming this afternoon.* *I'd like a coffee, please.*
:::

::: proof
*Would* adds the idea of 'in this case, now', so *I'd like* is about a particular situation, not a general taste. The two are easy to confuse because the contraction *'d* is short and often hard to hear in fast speech; the following words help: *I'd like **to**…* + a verb, or *I'd like **a**…* + a noun, versus *I like …ing*. A question with *Would you like…?* is an offer or invitation ([[everyday-english/requests-offers]]); a question with *Do you like…?* asks about tastes.
:::

### Agreeing: *Me too* and *Me neither*

**Rosa:** *I love Chinese food.* — **Li Jun:** *Me too!* / *So do I!*

**Rosa:** *I don't like getting up early.* — **Li Jun:** *Me neither.* / *Neither do I.*

::: theorem *Me too* or *Me neither*? {#rule-me-too}
To agree with a **positive** statement, say *Me too* (informal) or *So do I* / *So am I* / *So have I*. To agree with a **negative** statement, say *Me neither* (informal; in American English also *Me either*) or *Neither do I* / *Nor do I*. In *So…* and *Neither…*, use the auxiliary of the first sentence: *I'm tired. — So am I.* *I can't swim. — Neither can I.*
:::

::: proof
*Me too* adds you to a positive statement; after a negative statement it would add you to the opposite, so English switches to *neither* ('not either'). Chinese 我也是 and 我也不 work differently: 我也是 can agree with both positive and negative statements in some contexts, and this is why ✗ *I don't like coffee. — Me too* is a frequent learner error. Note the inversion in *So do I* (*so* + auxiliary + subject); *I do so* is a different structure.
:::

::: quiz
Your friend says: *I can't stand horror films.* You agree. What do you say?
- [ ] *Me too.*
- [ ] *So can I.*
- [x] *Neither can I.*
- [ ] *I can't too.*
::: solution
The statement is negative (*can't*), so agree with *neither* + the same auxiliary: *Neither can I* (or, informally, *Me neither*). *Me too* and *So can I* agree with positive statements; *I can't too* is not English (*I can't either* is possible).
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence talks about a general taste rather than a wish for now?
- [x] *I like going to the theatre.*
- [ ] *I'd like to go to the theatre tonight.*
- [ ] *Would you like to go to the theatre?*
- [ ] *I'd like a ticket for tonight, please.*
::: solution
*I like going…* (present simple of *like* + *-ing*) describes what you enjoy in general. The others use *would like*, which is about a particular occasion: a wish for tonight, an invitation, and a request at a ticket office.
:::
:::

::: example More than *I like music* {#ex-likes}
Asked *What do you like doing in your free time?*, Sun Mei answers: *I like music. I like sport. I don't like shopping.* The answer is correct but flat. Make it more natural and more informative.
::: solution
Step 1: use different verbs from the scale, so the listener knows how strong each feeling is: *love*, *quite like*, *can't stand* instead of *like* three times.

Step 2: make each like specific, with *-ing* or a kind: not *music* but *playing the guitar*, *listening to old rock music*; not *sport* but *badminton*.

Step 3: add a short detail or reason to one of them, which gives the listener something to ask about.

Result: *I love music — I play the guitar a bit, and I'm really into old rock bands. I quite like badminton too; I play with some friends from work on Thursdays. But I can't stand shopping, especially in big crowds!*

The listener now has three possible follow-up questions (*Which bands?*, *Where do you play?*, *Why not shopping?*). Notice that *I'm really into* is informal and *can't stand* is strong: both are normal in friendly conversation, but in an interview *I really enjoy* and *I'm not very keen on* would sound better.
:::
:::

## Last weekend

On Monday mornings, colleagues and classmates often ask about the weekend. The answer uses the **past simple**, because the weekend is a finished time.

- *How was your weekend?* / *Did you have a good weekend?* / *What did you get up to at the weekend?* (BrE, informal)
- *It was great, thanks. I went to the beach on Saturday.*
- *Not bad. I didn't do much — I just relaxed at home.*
- *Busy! I helped my brother move house.*
- *Quiet. I stayed in and caught up on some sleep.*

Many common verbs are irregular in the past: *go* → *went*, *have* → *had*, *see* → *saw*, *do* → *did*, *get* → *got*, *make* → *made*, *eat* → *ate*, *buy* → *bought*, *meet* → *met*, *take* → *took*. Questions and negatives use *did* + the base form: *What **did** you **do**? I **didn't go** out.* See [[core-grammar/past-simple]].

A good answer is longer than *It was good*: it gives one or two facts, an opinion, and perhaps a question back.

::: example Telling about the weekend {#ex-weekend}
Chen Hao's colleague asks *How was your weekend?* Chen Hao's notes in Chinese are: 周六和女朋友去看了一个展览，挺有意思；晚上吃火锅；周日在家，下雨。 Write a natural answer of four or five sentences.
::: solution
Step 1: give a short evaluation first, as English speakers usually do: *It was really nice, thanks.*

Step 2: tell the main events in order, in the past simple, with time words: *On Saturday my girlfriend and I went to an exhibition at the art museum — it was really interesting. In the evening we had hotpot with some friends.*

Step 3: add the quieter part: *Sunday was quiet. It rained all day, so I just stayed at home.*

Step 4: ask back: *How about you? Did you do anything fun?*

Notes: *went*, *had*, *rained*, *stayed* are all past simple, even though the time words (*on Saturday*, *in the evening*) already show the time — English requires both. *Hotpot* is the usual English name for 火锅. *It was really interesting* gives an opinion, which makes the answer sound personal.
:::
:::

## Plans

English has several ways to talk about the future; three are enough for everyday conversation.

| form | use | example |
|---|---|---|
| *be going to* + verb | a plan or intention you have already made | *I'm going to learn to drive this summer.* |
| present continuous (*be* + *-ing*) | an arrangement with a time, often with other people | *I'm meeting Sam for lunch on Friday.* |
| *will* / *'ll* + verb | a decision made now; a prediction; an offer | *I think I'll stay in tonight.* |
| *might* + verb | a possibility, not yet decided | *I might go to the cinema.* |

::: theorem Talking about plans {#rule-future-forms}
For something you have **decided** to do, use *be going to*: *I'm going to visit my grandparents.* For something already **arranged** with a time or other people, the present continuous is also common: *I'm visiting my grandparents on Sunday.* For something **not decided yet**, use *might* or *I'm thinking of* + *-ing*: *I might visit them, or I'm thinking of visiting them.* Use *will* when you decide at the moment of speaking: *Oh, it's raining — I'll take the bus.*
:::

::: proof
The present continuous treats a future arrangement as if it were already in progress, because the preparations have been made (a ticket is bought, a friend has agreed). *Going to* points from the present intention to the future action. The difference between *going to* and the present continuous is small, and in many sentences both are correct; *will* is different, because it is not about a previous plan. These forms and more are taught in [[core-grammar/talking-about-the-future]] and [[tense-and-aspect/future-forms]].
:::

Common questions about plans:

- *What are you doing this weekend?* / *Have you got any plans for the weekend?* (BrE) / *Do you have any plans…?*
- *What are you going to do after you graduate?*
- *Are you going anywhere for the holidays?* (BrE) / *for vacation?* (AmE)

::: example One conversation, three times {#ex-three-times}
Correct Li Jun's answer from the start of the lesson and extend it into a natural turn, using the present, the past and the future.
::: solution
Original: *I like hiking. Last weekend I go to the mountains with my friends, and next month I go to Yunnan.*

Step 1 — the routine or taste stays in the present simple, and can be made fuller: *I love hiking. I go most weekends if the weather's good.*

Step 2 — the past event goes into the past simple: *Last weekend I went to the mountains near the city with some friends. It was hard work, but the views were amazing.*

Step 3 — the plan uses *be going to* or the present continuous, because it is already decided: *And next month I'm going to Yunnan for a week* — or *I'm going to go to Yunnan* (both are correct; *going to go* is often shortened to *going*).

Step 4 — ask back: *Do you like hiking?*

The time words (*last weekend*, *next month*) stay; what changes is the verb. A Chinese speaker can use the time word as a reminder: when you say *last*, *yesterday* or *ago*, check that the verb is in the past.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Time in the verb.** Chinese verbs do not change for time; time words (昨天, 下个月) and particles such as 了 and 过 do the work. In English the verb changes **as well as** the time word: *Yesterday I **went**…*, *Tomorrow I'**m going**…*. Learners who know the rule often still say *Yesterday I go* in fast speech: the time word feels like enough.
- **The *-s* of the present.** *He works*, *she likes*: Chinese has no ending for the person, so the *-s* is easy to forget, especially in speech.
- **我也是.** It can follow positive and negative statements in Chinese; English chooses *Me too* or *Me neither* ([[#rule-me-too]]).
- **喜欢 + verb.** 我喜欢游泳 is *I like swimming* (or *I like to swim*), not ✗ *I like swim*.
- **打算 and 准备.** Both are usually *be going to* (*I'm going to…*) or *plan to* (*I'm planning to…*).
:::

::: history
The topics of this lesson — yourself, your home, your free time, your travel — were not always the centre of language courses. Many twentieth-century textbooks were organised around grammar: one tense per chapter, illustrated by sentences on any subject. In 1975 Jan van Ek's *The Threshold Level*, written for the Council of Europe, listed instead the situations and topics an adult learner would really need, including personal identification, house and home, free time and entertainment, travel, and food and drink, and the things a learner should be able to *do* in each. That list shaped the communicative textbooks of the following decades, and its descendant, the Common European Framework of Reference (2001), still describes the lowest levels largely as the ability to talk about yourself and your everyday life.
:::

## Where this leads

The forms used here are taught fully in [[core-grammar/present-simple]], [[core-grammar/past-simple]] and [[core-grammar/talking-about-the-future]], and later in [[tense-and-aspect/simple-and-progressive]] and [[tense-and-aspect/future-forms]]; the difference between 了 and the English past is discussed in [[tense-and-aspect/tense-and-chinese]]. *Like* + *-ing* and *to* belongs to [[verb-patterns/ing-or-infinitive]]. Talking about yourself is the material of small talk ([[everyday-english/small-talk]]), and telling a longer story about the past is practised in [[listening-and-conversation/storytelling]].

::: summary
- Routines use the present simple: *I get up at seven*, *she works in a hospital* (with *-s* for *he/she/it*).
- Adverbs of frequency go before the main verb and after *be*: *I usually walk*, *I'm always late*; longer expressions go at the end: *twice a week* ([[#rule-frequency-position]]).
- Likes and dislikes: *love*, *like*, *enjoy*, *don't mind*, *not keen on*, *can't stand* + *-ing* or a noun; *I like* (in general) ≠ *I'd like* (now) ([[#rule-like-would-like]]).
- Agree with *Me too / So do I* after a positive statement and *Me neither / Neither do I* after a negative one ([[#rule-me-too]]).
- Last weekend: the past simple, even with a time word — *Last weekend I went…*; give facts, an opinion and a question back.
- Plans: *be going to* for decisions, the present continuous for arrangements, *might* for possibilities, *will* for decisions made now ([[#rule-future-forms]]).
:::

::: exercise A self-introduction {level=3}
Prepare and say aloud a one-minute talk about yourself for a new English class. Include your routine (with at least three adverbs or expressions of frequency), two likes and one dislike, something you did last weekend, and a plan for the next few months.
::: hint
Organise it in four short parts: routine → likes → last weekend → plans. Check each verb against its time.
:::
::: solution
A model answer:

*Hi, I'm Wu Ting. I'm a nurse, so my routine changes a lot — some weeks I work nights. When I work days, I usually get up at six and I'm at the hospital by seven. I go running twice a week, and I hardly ever watch TV. In my free time I love baking and I really enjoy reading crime novels, but I can't stand long phone calls! Last weekend I baked a cake for my nephew's birthday — it was a dinosaur, and it was a disaster, but he loved it. In the summer I'm going to take a two-week course in Edinburgh, so I'm trying to improve my listening before I go.*

Checklist: present simple for routines (*get up*, *go running*), frequency (*usually*, *twice a week*, *hardly ever*), likes and a dislike with *-ing*, past simple for last weekend (*baked*, *was*, *loved*), and *be going to* / present continuous for the plan.
:::

::: exercise Ask and answer {level=3}
Write five questions you could ask a new classmate — one about routine, one about likes, one about last weekend, one about plans, and one follow-up question — and a natural answer to each.
::: solution
A model answer:

(1) *What time do you usually get up?* — *Around seven on weekdays, but at the weekend I sleep in.*

(2) *What kind of music do you like?* — *I'm really into hip hop at the moment, but I listen to all sorts.*

(3) *Did you do anything nice at the weekend?* — *Yes, I went to a friend's wedding on Saturday. It was lovely.*

(4) *Have you got any plans for the holidays?* — *I'm going home to see my family, and I might travel a bit with my sister.*

(5) Follow-up to (3): *Oh, nice! Where was it?* — *In a small village by the sea. The weather was perfect.*

Each question uses the form that fits its time: present simple with *usually*, *like* for tastes, *did* for the past, *have you got any plans* / *going to* for the future. The follow-up question picks up a detail from the answer — the subject of [[everyday-english/small-talk]].
