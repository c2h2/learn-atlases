Two people are waiting for a lift (BrE; AmE *elevator*) in an office building. They have never met.

**Aisha:** *Busy day?*

**Wang Lei:** *Yes.*

Silence. The lift arrives, and both look at their phones. Now the same moment again:

**Aisha:** *Busy day?*

**Wang Lei:** *Yes, pretty busy — I've had meetings since eight. How about you?*

**Aisha:** *Not too bad, actually. It's always quiet on Fridays in our team.*

**Wang Lei:** *Lucky you! Which floor are you on?*

Nothing important has been said in either version. Aisha does not need to know about Wang Lei's meetings, and he does not need to know which floor she works on. But the second version has done something the first has not: two strangers have become two people who might say hello next time. That is the job of **small talk** — short, light conversation about safe topics, whose purpose is social rather than informational.

This lesson teaches how to start small talk, what to talk about (and what usually to avoid), how to keep a conversation going by giving a little more than the answer and asking follow-up questions, how to show that you are listening, and how to end a conversation without seeming rude. It uses the forms from the earlier lessons — greetings ([[everyday-english/greetings-introductions]]), talking about yourself ([[everyday-english/talking-about-yourself]]) — and puts them together into conversation.

## What small talk is for

::: definition Small talk {#def-small-talk}
**Small talk** is brief, friendly conversation about uncontroversial topics — the weather, the weekend, the place you are in, travel, work in general — whose main purpose is to create or keep a social relationship, not to exchange important information. It is used with strangers in shared situations (queues, lifts, waiting rooms), with acquaintances and colleagues, and at the start of meetings, calls and parties.
:::

Small talk is often called *chit-chat* (informal) and, by linguists, **phatic communion** (see the historical note). It matters more in English-speaking cultures than many learners expect: silence with someone you know slightly — a colleague in a lift, a neighbour at the bus stop — can feel uncomfortable or even unfriendly, and a few sentences of small talk are the normal way to fill it. In meetings, the first few minutes of small talk (*Did you have a good weekend?*) are part of the meeting, not a waste of time.

## Starting

Small talk usually starts from something both speakers can see or share.

| type | examples |
|---|---|
| the weather | *Lovely day, isn't it?* / *Cold this morning, isn't it?* / *Can you believe this rain?* |
| the situation | *Busy in here today!* / *Have you been waiting long?* / *Is this the queue for tickets?* |
| the event | *How do you know Sarah?* (at a party) / *Have you been to one of these conferences before?* |
| the time of week | *Did you have a good weekend?* / *Any plans for the weekend?* / *Nearly Friday!* |
| something positive | *I like your bag — where's it from?* / *That was a great talk, wasn't it?* |

Many openers are **comments with a question tag** (*isn't it?*, *wasn't it?*), said with a **falling** tone. They are not real questions: the speaker expects agreement, and the answer is another comment: *Lovely day, isn't it? — Yes, gorgeous. Much better than last week.*

::: theorem Question tags in small talk {#rule-tags}
A question tag repeats the auxiliary of the sentence (or *do/does/did*) and the pronoun, with the opposite polarity: positive sentence + negative tag (*It's cold, **isn't it?***), negative sentence + positive tag (*It isn't very warm, **is it?***). In small talk, the tag is said with a **falling** tone, which invites agreement; a **rising** tone would make it a real question, asking for information.
:::

::: proof
The tag turns a statement into a shared observation: the speaker says what they think and gives the other person an easy turn — agreeing. *Lovely day, isn't it?* leaves out *It's* at the start, which is normal in casual speech. Agreeing is also done with the same auxiliary: *It's cold, isn't it? — It is!* / *Yes, isn't it?* The forms of tags are taught in [[core-grammar/questions-and-negatives]]; the role of the falling and rising tone is in [[connected-speech/intonation-meaning]].
:::

## Safe topics and risky topics

::: theorem Choosing a topic {#rule-topics}
With people you do not know well, the safest topics are the shared situation, the weather, travel, food, the weekend and holidays, films, books and sport in general, and work or study in general terms. Topics that are often avoided with strangers and new acquaintances in English-speaking cultures include someone's **salary** or the price of their home, their **age**, **weight** or appearance (except a compliment on clothes), **religion**, **politics**, and whether they are married or plan to have **children**. These are tendencies, not laws: among friends, and in some families and regions, many of them are normal.
:::

::: proof
Small talk aims at easy agreement, so it chooses topics where people are unlikely to disagree or feel judged. Questions about money, age, body and family plans can suggest that the other person is being measured or compared, and political and religious opinions can lead to disagreement. If someone raises one of these topics themselves (*I've just turned forty!*), it is fine to respond (*Oh, happy birthday!*). If someone asks *you* a question you prefer not to answer, a light reply moves the talk on: *Oh, that's a secret!* / *Enough, I hope!* — said with a smile.
:::

::: example Choosing what to say {#ex-topics}
At a friend's barbecue, Leila meets Zhang Wei for the first time. Which of these openers are good choices, and which are risky? Suggest a better version for the risky ones.

1. *How do you know Tom?*
2. *How much do you earn as an engineer?*
3. *These burgers are amazing, aren't they?*
4. *You're not married yet? How old are you?*
5. *Have you lived in Manchester long?*
::: solution
1. Good: the shared situation (the host), and a natural way to begin at a party.
2. Risky: salary is private. Better: *What kind of engineering do you do?* or *Do you enjoy it?* — interest in the work, not the pay.
3. Good: a positive comment on the shared situation, with a falling tag that invites agreement.
4. Risky: marriage and age are personal, and *yet* suggests that something is wrong. Leave both out; if family comes up, the other person will raise it.
5. Good: a general, friendly question about the place, which leads easily to follow-up questions (*Where were you before?*).

The good openers have three things in common: they are about something shared or general, they are easy to answer, and they lead somewhere.
:::
:::

::: quiz

- [ ] *You look tired. Did you sleep badly?*
- [x] *Morning! Did you have a good weekend?*
- [ ] *What do you think about the election?*
- [ ] *How much is your rent?*
::: solution
*Did you have a good weekend?* is the standard Monday opener: friendly, easy to answer and safe. Commenting on someone looking tired can seem critical, the election may lead to disagreement, and rent is a question about money.

## Keeping it going

### Give more than the answer

::: theorem Answer plus one {#rule-answer-plus}
In small talk, do not answer a question with a single word. Give the answer **plus** one or two extra pieces of information or an opinion, and often **ask back**: *Did you have a good weekend? — Yes, really nice, thanks. We went to the coast — it was freezing, but beautiful. How about you?*
:::

::: proof
A one-word answer (*Yes.*, *Fine.*) gives the other person nothing to respond to, so they must think of a new topic, and it can sound as if you do not want to talk — as Wang Lei's *Yes* did at the start of the lesson. The extra detail offers a **hook**: something the other person can ask about (*Which beach?*, *Did you swim?*). Asking back (*How about you?* / *And you?* / *What did you get up to?*) gives them a turn and shows interest. Two or three sentences are enough; a long monologue is the opposite mistake.
:::

### Ask follow-up questions

::: definition Follow-up question {#def-follow-up}
A **follow-up question** asks for more about something the other person has just said, rather than starting a new topic: *I've just got back from Portugal. — Oh, lovely! Where did you go?* Follow-up questions are usually short, often *wh*-questions (*Where…?*, *What was it like?*, *How long…?*), and show that you were listening.
:::

### Show that you are listening

While the other person speaks, English speakers show attention with short **response tokens** (also called backchannels): *Mm.*, *Yeah.*, *Right.*, *Really?*, *Oh, wow!*, *Oh no!*, *That's great!*, *I know!* Silence while someone tells a story can make them think you are not interested — or did not understand. Short reactions also show how you feel: *Oh, lovely!* (good news), *Oh no — what happened?* (bad news), *No way!* (informal surprise). See [[listening-and-conversation/turn-taking]].

::: example From an interview to a conversation {#ex-interview}
Paulo and Yuki are colleagues at a training day. Paulo's conversation feels like an interview. Rewrite it so that it sounds like small talk.

**Paulo:** *Where are you from?* **Yuki:** *Osaka.* **Paulo:** *What's your job?* **Yuki:** *I'm a designer.* **Paulo:** *Do you like London?* **Yuki:** *Yes.*
::: solution
The problem is on both sides: Paulo asks a list of unrelated questions, and Yuki answers with the minimum, so there are no hooks and no questions back.

**Paulo:** *So where are you from originally?*

**Yuki:** *Osaka, in Japan. But I've been in London for about three years now.*

**Paulo:** *Oh, really? I've always wanted to go to Osaka. How are you finding London?*

**Yuki:** *I love it, actually — although I still miss the food at home! Have you been here long?*

**Paulo:** *Mm, about five years. I'm originally from Lisbon.*

**Yuki:** *Oh, nice! So we're both immigrants.*

Changes: Yuki adds a detail to each answer (*three years*, *I miss the food*); Paulo reacts (*Oh, really?*) and asks follow-up questions about what she said (*How are you finding London?*) instead of moving to a new topic; both ask back. Note *How are you finding…?* ('what do you think of… so far?'), a friendly question to newcomers.
:::
:::

::: warning Questions that sound like a test
✗ *Do you like Chinese food?* (asked as the first question to every foreign guest) ✗ *Can you use chopsticks?*

✓ *Have you tried anything good since you got here?* ✓ *What's your favourite place to eat around here?*

Questions that check whether a visitor can do or likes something from your own culture are kindly meant, but asked again and again they can feel like a test the guest must pass. Open questions about their experience are warmer. The same is true the other way: ✗ *Do you eat dog?* or ✗ *Is it true that everyone in China…?* are questions that put the other person in the position of defending a whole country.
:::

::: example Dealing with an awkward question {#ex-awkward}
At a neighbour's party, a guest asks Olu three questions he would rather not answer. Write a light reply to each that keeps the conversation friendly and moves it on.

1. *So how much do you make in your job?*
2. *How old are you, if you don't mind me asking?*
3. *What do you think of the government?*
::: solution
The aim is to avoid the question without making the other person feel foolish: a smile, a little humour, and a new question.

1. *Ha — not enough! But I really like the work. What about you — what do you do?* — a joke instead of a number, something positive, and a question back that changes the topic.
2. *Oh, old enough to need a rest after a party like this! Do you live in the building too?* — humour about age instead of the age itself, then a question about the shared situation.
3. *Oh, I try to stay out of politics at parties! Have you tried the cake, by the way? It's amazing.* — a gentle, open statement of the convention, and *by the way* to change the topic.

None of the replies says *That's a rude question*: the guest may come from a place where such questions are normal, and a light reply keeps the evening pleasant for both.
:::
:::

## Ending

Small talk often ends by itself — the lift arrives, the meeting starts. When it does not, English speakers use a **pre-closing** ([[everyday-english/phone-messages]]) and a reason to leave, then a friendly goodbye.

- *Anyway, I'd better get back to work. Nice talking to you!*
- *Well, I should go and say hello to Maria. Good to meet you, Zhang Wei.*
- *Right, I'll let you get on. See you around!*
- *Oh, that's my bus. Have a good weekend!*
- *Anyway, I'll let you get back to the party. Enjoy the rest of the evening!*

::: theorem Ending a conversation politely {#rule-ending}
To end small talk, (1) use a pre-closing word (*Anyway*, *Well*, *Right*), (2) give a reason that is about time or another task, not about the other person (*I'd better get back to work*), and (3) close warmly: *(It was) nice talking to you*, *Good to meet you*, *See you later*, *Have a good weekend*. When you leave someone at a party, it is kind to introduce them to someone else first.
:::

::: proof
As at the end of a phone call, a sudden exit can feel like rejection, so the reason presents leaving as necessary rather than chosen, and the final phrase makes the conversation sound worthwhile. The reason does not have to be important — *I need another drink* is a perfectly acceptable party reason. *Nice to meet you* is said at the start of a first meeting; at the end, the usual form is *Nice to **have met** you* or, more often, *(It was) nice meeting you* / *Good to meet you* ([[everyday-english/greetings-introductions]]).
:::

::: example A complete conversation {#ex-complete}
At a conference coffee break, Fatima, a researcher, starts a conversation with Dong Ming, whom she does not know. Write a short complete conversation (opening, two topics, closing) and label its parts.
::: solution
**Fatima:** *The coffee's surprisingly good, isn't it?* — opener: a comment on the shared situation, with a falling tag.

**Dong Ming:** *It is! Much better than last year's.* — agreement plus a detail (a hook: he was here last year).

**Fatima:** *Oh, so you came last year too? I'm Fatima, by the way.* — follow-up question and introduction.

**Dong Ming:** *Dong Ming — nice to meet you. Yes, this is my third time. Is it your first?*

**Fatima:** *Yes, first time. It's a bit overwhelming! Which talks would you recommend?* — answer plus a feeling, and a new question on the shared topic.

**Dong Ming:** *Definitely the one on sleep this afternoon — I heard the speaker last year, and she was brilliant.*

**Fatima:** *Great, I'll go to that. Thanks!* — response token and thanks.

**Fatima:** *Oh — they're calling us back in. Well, it was really nice meeting you.* — pre-closing (*Well*) with a reason (the session).

**Dong Ming:** *You too. Maybe see you at the talk!* — warm close, with a possible next meeting.

The conversation lasts about a minute, contains no important information, and leaves both speakers with a contact at the conference: small talk doing its job.
:::
:::

::: quiz
At a party, you want to end a conversation with someone you have just met. Which is the most polite?
- [ ] *OK, bye.*
- [ ] *I'm bored. I'll talk to someone else.*
- [x] *Anyway, I should go and find my friend — it was really nice meeting you!*
- [ ] *Nice to meet you!* (and walk away in the middle of their sentence)
::: solution
The third answer uses a pre-closing (*Anyway*), a reason that is not about the other person (*find my friend*) and a warm close (*it was really nice meeting you*). *OK, bye* is too sudden, the second is rude, and walking away mid-sentence is rude whatever you say.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **吃了吗？** A traditional Chinese greeting asks about food; its English counterparts are *How are you?* and *How's it going?*, which, like 吃了吗, are not real requests for information ([[everyday-english/greetings-introductions]]). Translated literally, *Have you eaten?* is understood as a real question, and may sound like an invitation.
- **Personal questions.** Questions such as 你多大了？, 结婚了吗？, 一个月多少钱？ or 你胖了 are, in many Chinese settings, signs of friendly interest. In English small talk with strangers and new colleagues, they are usually avoided ([[#rule-topics]]); wait until the other person mentions the topic.
- **Compliments.** A common Chinese response to a compliment is to deny it (哪里哪里). In English, the usual response is to accept it with thanks: *I love your jacket! — Oh, thanks! I got it in a sale.* Denying (*No, it's ugly*) can sound as if you think the other person's taste is poor. See [[pragmatics/apologies-thanks-compliments]].
- **Silence.** Comfortable silence between people who know each other slightly is more common in many Chinese settings than in many English-speaking ones. A short comment — *Busy day?* — is the English way to make the silence friendly.
- **The weather.** Talk about the weather is famous in Britain, but it is not a sign of having nothing to say: it is the most shared, least risky topic there is.
:::

::: history
The word for small talk used by linguists comes from anthropology. In 1923 the Polish-born anthropologist Bronisław Malinowski, who had studied life in the Trobriand Islands, wrote a supplement to C. K. Ogden and I. A. Richards's book *The Meaning of Meaning*. In it he described a kind of talk in which 'ties of union are created by a mere exchange of words' — greetings, remarks about the weather, questions whose answers nobody needs — and called it **phatic communion**, from a Greek word for speaking. His point was that language is not only for passing on thoughts: some of it exists to make silence friendly and to keep people connected. Later linguists, including Roman Jakobson, spoke of the **phatic function** of language, and researchers who recorded real conversation found that small talk is not 'empty' at all: it is where relationships at work and among neighbours are made and kept.
:::

::: summary
- Small talk is short, friendly talk about safe topics whose purpose is social ([[#def-small-talk]]).
- Start from something shared: the weather, the situation, the event, the weekend; comments with a falling question tag invite agreement ([[#rule-tags]]).
- With strangers, avoid salary, age, weight, religion, politics and family plans unless the other person raises them ([[#rule-topics]]).
- Answer with more than one word — the answer plus a detail — and ask back ([[#rule-answer-plus]]).
- Ask follow-up questions about what the other person said, and show attention with response tokens (*Really?*, *Oh, nice!*) ([[#def-follow-up]]).
- End with a pre-closing, a reason about time or another task, and a warm close: *Anyway, I'd better go — nice talking to you!* ([[#rule-ending]]).
:::

You are waiting in a doctor's waiting room, and the appointments are running late. Write a short conversation (8–10 turns) with another patient you do not know. A model answer:

**You:** *Oh no. Have you got somewhere to be?*

**Patient:** *Only work — I told them I'd be in by eleven. Do you come to this practice often?*

**You:** *Not really. I've only just moved to the area.*

**Patient:** *Oh, really? Where did you move from?*

**You:** *From Leeds. I've got a new job here.*

**Patient:** *That's great! How are you finding it so far?*

**You:** *So far so good, thanks — it's a nice area.*

**Receptionist:** *Ms Huang?*

**You:** *Oh, that's me. Nice talking to you — I hope you're not waiting much longer!*

Opener with a falling tag about the shared situation; follow-up questions (*Have you got somewhere to be?*, *Where did you move from?*); response tokens (*Oh no*, *Oh, really?*, *That's great!*); and an ending with a reason (the name is called) and a warm close.

::: exercise A first day {level=3}
On your first day in a new job, you meet a colleague in the kitchen. Write a conversation (12–14 turns) that moves through three topics — the office, the weekend, and an interest you share — and ends politely. Then explain which turns give hooks, which are follow-up questions, and where the pre-closing is.
::: solution
A model answer:

**Colleague:** *Hi — you must be new. I'm Sofia.*

**You:** *Hi, Sofia. Yes, it's my first day — I'm Lin Yue. I'm in the design team.*

**Colleague:** *Oh, welcome! How's it going so far?*

**You:** *Good, thanks, although I keep getting lost — this building's like a maze, isn't it?*

**Colleague:** *It is! It took me a month to find the second kitchen. Did you have a good weekend, at least?*

**You:** *Yes, thanks. I went to a climbing wall with some friends — my arms still hurt!*

**Colleague:** *Climbing? No way — I climb too! Where do you go?*

**You:** *There's a place near the river. Do you know it?*

**Colleague:** *Yes, I go there on Thursdays. You should come with us one week.*

**You:** *I'd love to! Thanks.*

**Colleague:** *Great. Anyway, I'd better get back — I've got a meeting at ten.*

**You:** *Of course. Nice to meet you, Sofia — and thanks for the tip about the kitchen!*

**Colleague:** *Any time. See you later!*

Hooks: *I keep getting lost* (leads to the building), *I went to a climbing wall* (leads to the shared interest). Follow-up questions: *How's it going so far?*, *Where do you go?*. Question tag: *like a maze, isn't it?*. Pre-closing: *Anyway, I'd better get back*, with a reason about time (*a meeting at ten*). In three short topics, a colleague has become a possible climbing partner.
