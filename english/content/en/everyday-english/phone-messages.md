Kwame's phone rings. He does not know the number.

**Kwame:** *Hello?*

**Caller:** *Hello, is that Kwame Asante?*

**Kwame:** *Speaking.*

**Caller:** *Oh, hi — this is Liu Fang from the language school. I'm calling about your course next week.*

In under ten seconds the two speakers have done four things: answered, checked who is speaking, said who is calling, and given the reason for the call. They have done it with phrases that are used on the phone and almost nowhere else: *Is that…?*, *Speaking*, *This is…*, *I'm calling about…*. Phone calls are hard for learners — there is no face to read, the sound can be poor, and the other person may speak fast — but they are also very regular. The same short sequence of turns opens almost every call, and the same few phrases close it.

This lesson teaches those phrases: how to start and end a call, how to ask for someone and take or leave a message, what to say when the line is bad, and how to write short texts and chat messages that sound natural. It builds on spelling names and saying numbers ([[everyday-english/personal-information]], [[everyday-english/numbers-in-conversation]]) and on polite requests ([[everyday-english/requests-offers]]).

## Starting a call

### Answering

- At home or on a mobile (BrE) / cell phone (AmE): *Hello?* — with rising intonation, as a question. Many people also answer with their name if they see who is calling: *Hi, Sam!*
- At work, the person answering often gives the name of the organisation and their own name: *Good morning, Riverside Dental Practice, Maria speaking. How can I help?*

### Saying who you are

::: theorem *This is…* and *It's…* on the phone {#rule-this-is}
On the phone, say who you are with *This is…* (neutral and formal) or *It's…* (friendly, informal): *Hello, this is Liu Fang from the language school.* *Hi, it's Tom!* Do not say ✗ *I am Liu Fang* at the start of a call. To ask who the other person is, British English usually says *Is that…?* and American English *Is this…?*: *Is that Kwame?* (BrE) / *Is this Kwame?* (AmE). If you are the person asked for, answer *Speaking* or *Yes, this is he/she* (formal) — or simply *Yes, it's me* to someone you know.
:::

::: proof
On the phone, the speaker's voice is the only evidence of who is speaking, and English treats it as something being pointed at: *this* (here, near me) for the caller's own voice, *that* (there, away from me) for the other voice in British usage. *I am X* is grammatically correct but sounds like a face-to-face introduction, slightly odd on the phone; *My name is X* is fine when the person does not know you at all (*Hello, my name is Kwame Asante, and I'm calling about…*). The forms *Who is this?* and *Who's calling?* follow the same pattern; *Who's calling, please?* is the polite way for a receptionist to ask, while *Who is this?* on its own can sound abrupt.
:::

### Asking for someone

- *Could I speak to Ms Okafor, please?* / *Can I speak to Ana?* / *Is Ana there?* (informal)
- *I'd like to speak to someone about my booking, please.*

Answers from the person who answered:

- *Speaking.* — 'I'm the person you asked for.'
- *Just a moment, please.* / *Hold on a second.* (informal) / *Hold the line, please.* (formal) — 'wait'
- *I'll put you through.* — 'I'll connect you to her phone.'
- *Who's calling, please?* / *Can I ask who's calling?*
- *I'm afraid she's not available at the moment / she's in a meeting / she's out of the office today. Can I take a message?*

::: definition Opening sequence {#def-opening}
The **opening sequence** of a phone call is the short, regular series of turns at its start: (1) the phone rings and is answered (*Hello?*); (2) the speakers identify each other (*Hi, it's Tom. — Oh, hi Tom!*); (3) between friends, they greet each other and ask *How are you?*; (4) the caller gives the **reason for the call** (*I'm calling about…* / *I just wanted to ask…*). In calls between strangers or at work, step 3 is short or missing.
:::

:::

::: example A call to a dentist's office {#ex-business-call}
Cao Min needs to change a dental appointment. Write the beginning of the call, from the receptionist's answer to the moment Cao Min gives her reason, and explain each turn.
::: solution
**Receptionist:** *Good morning, Riverside Dental Practice, Maria speaking. How can I help?* — the organisation, the speaker's name, an offer of help.

**Cao Min:** *Oh, good morning. My name's Cao Min — that's C-A-O, M-I-N.* — she does not know the receptionist, so *My name's…* is right; she spells her name at once, because it will be unfamiliar ([[everyday-english/personal-information]]).

**Receptionist:** *Thank you, Ms Cao.*

**Cao Min:** *I've got an appointment on Thursday at ten, and I'm calling to ask if I could move it to next week.* — the reason, with a polite indirect request ([[everyday-english/requests-offers]]).

There is no *How are you?*: in a short business call between strangers, the speakers move straight from identification to the reason. *I'm calling to…* / *I'm calling about…* / *I'm ringing about…* (BrE) are the standard ways to give the reason.
:::
:::

## Messages

When the person is not there, the caller can **leave a message**, and the person who answered **takes a message**.

**Receptionist:** *I'm afraid Mr Ibrahim's in a meeting. Can I take a message?*

**Caller:** *Yes, please. Could you ask him to call me back? It's Hannah Novak, from the city library.*

**Receptionist:** *Could you spell your surname, please?*

**Caller:** *N-O-V-A-K.*

**Receptionist:** *And your number?*

**Caller:** *It's 01632 960 418.*

**Receptionist:** *Let me read that back: oh-one-six-three-two, nine-six-oh, four-one-eight.*

**Caller:** *That's right.*

**Receptionist:** *I'll make sure he gets the message.*

::: theorem What a message needs {#rule-message}
A clear phone message — to a person or on **voicemail** (a recorded message service) — contains:
1. **who** is calling: name, and organisation if needed;
2. **why**: the reason in one sentence;
3. **what** you want: *Could you call me back?* / *No need to call back.*
4. **how** to reach you: the number, said slowly in groups, often twice;
5. **when**: a time, if it matters: *I'm free after three.*
Then a closing: *Thanks, bye.*
:::

::: proof
A voicemail cannot ask questions, so everything the listener needs must be in the message, in the order in which it is useful: first who, so the listener knows whether to pay attention; last the number, because it is the thing to write down. Saying the number twice, slowly and in groups ([[everyday-english/numbers-in-conversation]]), saves the listener from replaying the message. When you take a message for someone else, check the same five points and **read back** the name and number: errors in numbers are common, and a message with a wrong number is useless.
:::

::: example A voicemail {#ex-voicemail}
Hannah calls Mr Ibrahim's mobile, and the voicemail answers. Write her message: she wants to talk about a talk he is giving at the library on 14 March, she needs an answer by Friday, and she is free every afternoon.
::: solution
*Hello, Mr Ibrahim, this is Hannah Novak from the city library. I'm calling about your talk on the fourteenth of March — I just need to check a couple of details with you. Could you call me back before Friday, if possible? I'm free any afternoon this week. My number is 01632 960 418 — that's oh-one-six-three-two, nine-six-oh, four-one-eight. Thanks very much. Bye.*

Who (*this is Hannah Novak from the city library*), why (*about your talk on the fourteenth of March*), what (*Could you call me back…?*), when (*before Friday*, *any afternoon*), how (the number, twice). The date is said in the spoken form, *the fourteenth of March* (BrE; AmE *March fourteenth*). The message is short — about twenty seconds — which is what listeners prefer.
:::
:::

## When the line is bad

Phone calls often go wrong for technical reasons. These phrases deal with them politely:

- *Sorry, I didn't catch that. Could you say it again?* / *Sorry, could you repeat that?*
- *Could you speak up a bit, please?* ('louder') / *Could you speak a bit more slowly, please?*
- *Sorry, you're breaking up.* ('the sound keeps stopping') / *The line's really bad.* / *I can hardly hear you.*
- *I'm losing you…* / *You've gone very quiet.*
- *I'll call you back.* — when the call is too bad to continue; *We got cut off* — when the call stopped by itself.
- *Can you hear me now?* / *Is that better?*

Checking information is as important as hearing it: *Sorry, was that fifteen or fifty?* ([[everyday-english/numbers-in-conversation]]); *Did you say Tuesday?*; *So that's ten o'clock on Thursday, at the main entrance — is that right?* More ways of asking for clarification are in [[listening-and-conversation/clarification]].

::: quiz
On a call, you hear: *…so meet me at the — kkhh — at half past — kkhh.* What is the most useful thing to say?
- [ ] *What?*
- [ ] *Repeat.*
- [x] *Sorry, you're breaking up — where and when did you say?*
- [ ] *Yes, OK.*
::: solution
Say what the problem is (*you're breaking up*) and ask for exactly the information you missed (*where and when*). *What?* and *Repeat* are understood but sound abrupt; *Yes, OK* hides the problem, and you will not know where to go.
:::
:::

## Ending a call

::: definition Pre-closing {#def-preclosing}
A **pre-closing** is a turn that signals that a conversation is coming to an end, before anyone says goodbye: *OK, then…*, *Right…*, *Anyway…*, *Well, I'd better let you go*, *I'll let you get on* (BrE), *So, see you on Saturday*. The other speaker either accepts it (*OK, great*) and the closing follows, or raises a new topic (*Oh — before you go…*).
:::

::: theorem Closing a call politely {#rule-closing}
Do not end a call with *Bye* alone after the last piece of information. Use a pre-closing ([[#def-preclosing]]), sum up any arrangement, and thank the other person: *OK, great — so I'll see you at ten on Thursday. Thanks for calling. — No problem. Bye! — Bye!* At work, a common closing is *Is there anything else I can help you with? — No, that's everything, thanks.*
:::

::: proof
Ending a conversation suddenly can feel like rejection, so speakers prepare it together over several turns; the sociologists Emanuel Schegloff and Harvey Sacks described this in a 1973 study of how conversations close. The pre-closing gives the other speaker a chance to add something; the summary of the arrangement prevents misunderstandings; the thanks closes on a warm note. *Bye-bye* is common in British English and on the phone; the final goodbye is often said twice, once by each speaker, on a falling tone.
:::

::: example Closing a call between friends {#ex-closing}
Ji-woo has been talking on the phone with her friend Lucas about Saturday's football match. Write a natural closing in five or six turns.
::: solution
**Ji-woo:** *Anyway, I'd better let you go — I know you're busy.* — the pre-closing, with a kind reason: she says she is leaving for **his** benefit.

**Lucas:** *Yeah, I should get back to work. So — Saturday, two o'clock, outside the stadium?* — he accepts and sums up the arrangement.

**Ji-woo:** *Perfect. I'll bring the tickets.*

**Lucas:** *Great. Thanks for organising it!*

**Ji-woo:** *No problem. See you Saturday!*

**Lucas:** *See you! Bye!*

Six turns to say goodbye may seem long, but each has a job: preparing the end, checking the plan, thanking, and saying goodbye. *I'd better let you go* is a friendly pre-closing because it presents the end of the call as a favour to the other person.
:::
:::

::: warning *I'll call you back* and *I'll call back*
✗ *I'll call back you later.* ✗ *Please call me back later* (to a receptionist — sounds like an order).

✓ *I'll call you back later.* ✓ *Could you ask her to call me back, please?*

*Call back* is a phrasal verb; with a pronoun, the pronoun goes in the middle: *call **you** back*, *call **her** back*. *I'll call back later* (no object) means 'I'll phone this number again'. And as always, a request to a stranger goes into a question with *could* ([[everyday-english/requests-offers]]). In British English *ring* is also common: *I'll ring you back.*
:::

## Texts and chat messages

Text messages and chat messages are written, but they are close to speech: short, informal and quick.

| informal message | meaning |
|---|---|
| *Running 10 mins late, sorry!* | 'I'm running ten minutes late.' — subject and *I'm* left out |
| *On my way* / *omw* | 'I'm on my way.' |
| *Thx* / *Thanks!* / *Ta* (BrE, informal) | 'Thank you.' |
| *pls* | 'please' |
| *btw* | 'by the way' |
| *lol* | 'laughing out loud' — often just 'that's funny' or a friendly tone |
| *asap* | 'as soon as possible' — can sound urgent or bossy |
| *np* | 'no problem' |
| *Sounds good!* / *Perfect!* | agreement |
| *x* (BrE) | a kiss, at the end of messages to friends and family |

::: theorem Matching a message to the reader {#rule-message-register}
The shortest forms — no subject, no full stop, abbreviations — suit friends, family and close colleagues. With people you do not know well, with older people, and at work, write complete sentences with a greeting and the reader's name, use *please* and *thanks* in full, and avoid abbreviations: *Hi Mr Lee, this is Ana Silva, Daniel's mum. Would it be possible to change Daniel's lesson to Thursday this week? Thanks very much, Ana.*
:::

::: proof
The short forms are signs of closeness: they say 'we know each other well enough not to need the usual politeness'. Used with a stranger, they can seem careless or rude. Texts have no tone of voice, so a very short reply such as *OK.* — with a full stop — can feel cold to some readers; *OK!* or *OK, thanks* is warmer. Conventions change fast and vary between age groups, so the safest rule is to copy the style of the person you are writing to. Longer written messages and emails are taught in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/emails-messages]] and [[pragmatics/writing-to-people]].
:::

::: example From full English to a text, and back {#ex-texts}
(a) Turn this into a natural text to a close friend: *I am sorry, but I am going to be about fifteen minutes late because the bus has not arrived. I will see you at the café.*

(b) Turn this text into a suitable message to a teacher you do not know well: *cant come tmrw sick sorry*
::: solution
(a) *So sorry — bus hasn't come! Running about 15 mins late. See you at the café x* — subject and *I'm* left out, numbers as figures, a short apology, a contraction (*hasn't*), and, between close friends in Britain, an *x*.

(b) *Hello Ms Ortiz, this is Kenji Sato from your Tuesday evening class. I'm sorry, but I'm ill and won't be able to come to tomorrow's lesson. Could you let me know what I should prepare for next week? Thank you, Kenji* — a greeting with the teacher's name, the writer's name and class (she may not have his number saved), full sentences with *I'm* and apostrophes, a reason, a polite request, and a closing with thanks.

The information is the same; the form depends on how well the writer knows the reader.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A new colleague whose number you do not have texts you: *Hi, it's Ramón from the third floor — could you send me the meeting notes when you get a chance?* Which reply is best?
- [ ] *k*
- [ ] *Who r u?*
- [x] *Hi Ramón, sure — I'll send them this afternoon.*
- [ ] *Send notes asap.*
::: solution
Ramón has introduced himself and asked politely, so a friendly, complete reply with his name is right. *k* is very short and can seem rude to someone you do not know; *Who r u?* asks a question he has already answered; *Send notes asap* misunderstands who is sending.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **喂.** Chinese 喂 answers the phone and also gets attention when the line is bad (喂？喂？). English *Hello?* answers; for a bad line, use *Hello? Can you hear me?* — repeating *Hello? Hello?* sharply can sound impatient.
- **我是… and *This is…*.** 我是刘芳 is *This is Liu Fang* or *It's Liu Fang* on the phone, not ✗ *I am Liu Fang*.
- **你是谁？** The direct question is fine in Chinese between people who know each other; in English, *Who is this?* can sound suspicious. *Sorry, who's calling?* or *Sorry, I don't have your number saved* is softer.
- **Voice messages.** Sending short voice messages in chat apps is very common among Chinese users; in many English-speaking workplaces a text is still the default, and a long voice message can feel like extra work for the listener. Ask, or follow what the other person does.
- **好的 and *OK*.** 好的 is a neutral, polite acknowledgement; English *OK.* alone with a full stop can read as cold in a text. Add a word: *OK, thanks!* / *Sounds good.*
:::

::: history
What should you say when you pick up a telephone? When the telephone was new, there was no agreed answer. Alexander Graham Bell, who patented the telephone in 1876, preferred the sailors' call *Ahoy!*. Thomas Edison, in a letter of 1877 about a telephone line, suggested *Hello* — a variant of older calls such as *hallo* and *hullo*, used to attract attention — and it was *hello* that spread, first among telephone users and then into everyday greetings. More than a century later, on 3 December 1992, a British engineer sent the first text message from a computer to a mobile phone; it said *Merry Christmas*. Within a few years text messaging had developed its own short forms — many of which, such as *thx* and *btw*, had already been used on computer networks.
:::

## Where this leads

Phone calls and video meetings at a higher level are the subject of [[listening-and-conversation/calls-online-meetings]], and asking for repetition and clarification is developed in [[listening-and-conversation/clarification]]. Written messages and emails are taught in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/emails-messages]] and [[pragmatics/writing-to-people]]. The conversation skills of the next lesson, [[everyday-english/small-talk]], are also used in the friendly part of a phone call.

::: summary
- Answer with *Hello?*; at work, give the organisation and your name.
- Say who you are with *This is…* or *It's…*; ask *Is that…?* (BrE) or *Is this…?* (AmE); the person asked for says *Speaking* ([[#rule-this-is]]).
- A call opens with answer, identification, (between friends) *How are you?*, and the reason: *I'm calling about…* ([[#def-opening]]).
- A message gives who, why, what you want, how to reach you, and when; read numbers back ([[#rule-message]]).
- For a bad line: *Sorry, you're breaking up*, *I didn't catch that*, *I'll call you back*.
- Close with a pre-closing, a summary and thanks, not *Bye* alone ([[#def-preclosing]], [[#rule-closing]]).
- In texts, match the reader: short forms for close friends, full sentences with a greeting and name for everyone else ([[#rule-message-register]]).
:::
