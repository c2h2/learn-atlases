Zhou Lei buys a scarf at a market stall in London.

**Stallholder:** *That one's fifteen pounds.*

Zhou Lei hands over a fifty-pound note. The stallholder looks surprised, smiles, and gives him thirty-five pounds in change. Zhou Lei is surprised too: he heard *fifty*, and thought he was paying exactly the right amount.

Numbers are some of the first words a learner knows, so why are they so hard in conversation? Because in real life they come fast, in the middle of other words, and often only once; because some pairs, such as *fifteen* and *fifty*, differ only in a stressed syllable and one sound; and because prices, times, dates and phone numbers each have their own way of being said, which differs between British and American English. This lesson is about using numbers in conversation: saying them clearly, understanding them at normal speed, and — most important — checking them.

The words for numbers themselves (*twelve*, *twentieth*, *a hundred*, *a third*) are taught in [[core-vocabulary/numbers-dates-money]]. Here we use them.

## Big numbers

In English, large numbers are said in groups of three digits, which are separated in writing by a comma: *2,500* is *two thousand five hundred*; *36,000* is *thirty-six thousand*; *1,200,000* is *one million two hundred thousand* (or *one point two million*).

Between the hundreds and the rest of the number, British English always says *and*; American English often leaves it out:

- *120* — *a hundred and twenty* (BrE, also AmE) / *one hundred twenty* (AmE)
- *2,350* — *two thousand three hundred and fifty* (BrE) / *two thousand three hundred fifty* (AmE)
- *105* — *a hundred and five* / *one hundred five*

*A hundred* and *one hundred* mean the same; *a* is more common in conversation, *one* when you want to be precise.

::: theorem Thousands, not ten-thousands {#rule-big-numbers}
English counts large numbers in thousands, millions and billions — steps of 1,000. There is no English word for 万 (10,000) or 亿 (100,000,000). So:

| Chinese | figures | English |
|---|---|---|
| 一万 | 10,000 | *ten thousand* |
| 三万五千 | 35,000 | *thirty-five thousand* |
| 十万 | 100,000 | *a hundred thousand* |
| 一百万 | 1,000,000 | *a million* |
| 一千万 | 10,000,000 | *ten million* |
| 一亿 | 100,000,000 | *a hundred million* |
| 十亿 | 1,000,000,000 | *a billion* |
:::

::: proof
The comma in *35,000* shows where the English word *thousand* goes: everything before the comma is said as a number and followed by *thousand*. Chinese groups digits in fours (万, 亿), so a Chinese speaker converting a large number in their head has to move the boundary by one digit — a step where mistakes are easy, even for advanced speakers. The safe method is to write the number in figures with commas and read it from the commas. In speech, a number word after another number has no plural *-s*: *three thousand people*, not *three thousands people*; but *thousands of people* ('a very large number') is correct.
:::

::: example From 万 to *thousand* {#ex-big-number}
Liang Yu tells an English-speaking colleague that her city has 二百三十五万 people. How does she say the number in English?
::: solution
Step 1: write the number in figures. 二百三十五万 = 235 × 10,000 = 2,350,000.

Step 2: put in the commas from the right, every three digits: 2,350,000.

Step 3: read from the left, one group at a time, adding the word for each comma: *two* **million**, *three hundred and fifty* **thousand**. The last group (000) is not said.

So: *My city has two million three hundred and fifty thousand people*, or, more naturally in conversation, *two point three five million people*, or, rounded, *about two point four million*. The mistake to avoid is *two hundred and thirty-five ten thousand*, a word-for-word translation of 二百三十五万, which English speakers cannot understand.
:::
:::

Decimals are read with *point*, and each digit after the point is said separately: *3.75* is *three point seven five*, not *three point seventy-five*. The figure 0 has several names: *zero* (everywhere, especially in science and in American English), *nought* (British, in numbers such as *0.5*, *nought point five*), and *oh* (in phone numbers, years and room numbers, like the letter O).

## *Fifteen* or *fifty*?

The numbers 13–19 and 30–90 sound alike in pairs: *thirteen* and *thirty*, *fourteen* and *forty*, and so on. Two things tell them apart.

::: theorem How *-teen* and *-ty* differ {#rule-teen-ty}
1. **Stress.** When said alone or at the end of a phrase, *-teen* numbers are stressed on the second syllable: *fif**teen*** (oO), /ˌfɪfˈtiːn/. *-ty* numbers are stressed on the first: *fifty* (Oo), /ˈfɪfti/.
2. **The ending.** *-teen* has a long vowel and ends in /n/: /tiːn/. *-ty* has a short, weak vowel and no /n/: /ti/. In General American, the *t* of *thirty*, *forty* and *eighty* is often a quick *d*-like sound (a flap), [ˈθɜːrɾi], which never happens in *thirteen* /ˌθɜːrˈtiːn/.
:::

::: proof
Stress is not completely reliable. When a *-teen* number comes before a stressed noun, its stress usually moves to the first syllable, so that the two strong syllables are not next to each other: *She's fif**teen***, but *fifteen people* is said Oo Oo, with the stress on *fif-* and *peo-*. In that position *fifteen* and *fifty* have the same stress pattern, and only the long vowel and the final /n/ separate them. This is why the clearest way to give one of these numbers is at the end of a sentence (*The price is fif**teen***), and why the best way to understand one is to check it ([[#rule-check]]). Word stress and stress shift are explained in [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics]] and [[connected-speech/word-stress-patterns]].
:::

::: example Saying a number so it cannot be misheard {#ex-teen-ty}
A hotel receptionist has to tell a guest that her room is 314 and breakfast is from 7.30 until 10.15. How can she say this so that the guest does not hear *340* or *10.50*?
::: solution
Step 1: give each number its own place near the end of a phrase, where its normal stress is kept: *Your room is three-one-**four**.* Room numbers are usually said in digits (*three-one-four*) or as *three fourteen*; the digits avoid the *-teen/-ty* problem completely.

Step 2: for the time, stress the second syllable and say the final /n/ clearly: *Breakfast is from seven thirty until ten fif**teen**.*

Step 3: add a second form that cannot be confused: *…until ten fifteen — a quarter past ten.* *A quarter past* can only mean 15 minutes.

Step 4: invite a check: *Is that OK?* — if the guest repeats *Quarter past ten, great*, both know the number arrived.
:::
:::

::: quiz
At a station you hear: *The train to Bristol leaves from platform fif**teen**.* The stress is on the last syllable, and you hear a clear /n/ at the end. Which platform is it?
- [ ] 50
- [x] 15
- [ ] 5
- [ ] 1.5
::: solution
At the end of a phrase, a *-teen* number keeps its stress on *-teen* and ends with a clear /n/: *fif**teen***. If the speaker had meant 50, the stress would be on the first syllable and there would be no /n/: *fifty* (Oo). If you are not sure, check: *Fifteen — one five?*
:::
:::

## Prices

| written | said |
|---|---|
| £4.99 | *four pounds ninety-nine* / *four ninety-nine* |
| £1.50 | *one pound fifty* / *a pound fifty* / *one fifty* |
| 80p | *eighty pence* / *eighty p* (/piː/, informal) |
| \$3.25 | *three dollars and twenty-five cents* / *three twenty-five* |
| \$0.75 | *seventy-five cents* |
| €12 | *twelve euros* |
| ¥200 | *two hundred yuan* (/juˈɑːn/, also *two hundred renminbi*) |

::: theorem Short forms of prices {#rule-prices}
In conversation, prices are usually shortened: the word *pounds*, *dollars* or *euros* is often left out when the coins part follows (*four ninety-nine*), and the coins word (*pence*, *cents*) is almost always left out after it. The full form (*four pounds and ninety-nine pence*) is used only when being very careful.
:::

::: proof
The listener already knows the currency from the situation, so the shortest form is enough. Chinese does the same: 三块五 is not *three yuan and five jiao* but simply 'three fifty'. Informal words also exist: *quid* for pounds in British English (*It cost me twenty quid*; the plural is also *quid*), and *bucks* for dollars in American English (*ten bucks*). They are common in speech but too informal for a shop to use to a customer.
:::

**Customer:** *Excuse me, how much is this mug?*

**Assistant:** *That's six fifty.*

**Customer:** *Six fifty? OK, I'll take it. Can I pay by card?*

**Assistant:** *Sure.*

The customer repeats the price as a question — a quick, natural check. More about shopping is in [[everyday-english/shopping-eating-out]].

## Times

There are two ways to say the time: the traditional way with *past* and *to*, and the digital way, which reads the numbers.

| time | traditional | digital |
|---|---|---|
| 7.00 | *seven o'clock* | *seven* |
| 7.05 | *five past seven* (AmE also *five after seven*) | *seven oh five* |
| 7.15 | *(a) quarter past seven* (AmE also *(a) quarter after seven*) | *seven fifteen* |
| 7.30 | *half past seven* (BrE informal *half seven*) | *seven thirty* |
| 7.40 | *twenty to eight* (AmE also *twenty of eight*) | *seven forty* |
| 7.45 | *(a) quarter to eight* (AmE also *(a) quarter of eight*) | *seven forty-five* |
| 7.52 | *eight minutes to eight* | *seven fifty-two* |

::: theorem *Past* and *to* {#rule-past-to}
In the traditional system, minutes from 1 to 30 are counted **past** the hour that has started; minutes from 31 to 59 are counted **to** the next hour. With 5, 10, 20 and 25 the word *minutes* is usually left out (*ten past six*); with other numbers it is said (*seven minutes past six*). *O'clock* is used only with exact hours.
:::

::: proof
The traditional system follows the face of a clock: the minute hand moves *past* the top of the clock and then towards it again. The digital system follows a digital display and is used more and more, especially for exact times (*the train leaves at 7.52*) and in American English. Both are understood everywhere. One trap: British *half seven* means 7.30 (half past seven), not 6.30.
:::

To ask the time: *What time is it?* / *What's the time?* (BrE) / *Have you got the time?* (BrE) / *Do you have the time?* — the last two are polite requests to a stranger, not questions about whether they are free.

For the part of the day, add *in the morning*, *in the afternoon*, *in the evening* or *at night*, or use *a.m.* (before 12 noon) and *p.m.* (after 12 noon): *I start work at nine in the morning* / *at 9 a.m.* Timetables, tickets and many countries use the 24-hour clock (*18.30*); in conversation English speakers usually change it to the 12-hour clock (*half past six*, *six thirty*), although *eighteen thirty* is also heard for timetables. *12 p.m.* is noon and *12 a.m.* midnight, but because these are often confused, people prefer *12 noon* and *12 midnight*.

::: example Reading a timetable aloud {#ex-times}
Your friend Fatima, who does not read English well, asks you to tell her the departure times on a British railway timetable: 06.45, 13.20 and 21.55. Say each time in the traditional and in the digital way.
::: solution
- 06.45: traditional *a quarter to seven in the morning*; digital *six forty-five* (*a.m.*). The minutes are past 30, so the traditional form counts *to* the next hour.
- 13.20: traditional *twenty past one in the afternoon*; digital *one twenty* (*p.m.*), or from the 24-hour timetable *thirteen twenty*.
- 21.55: traditional *five to ten in the evening*; digital *nine fifty-five* (*p.m.*), or *twenty-one fifty-five*.

To change from the 24-hour clock, subtract 12 from hours after 12 and add *p.m.* or *in the afternoon/evening*. Both systems are correct; for train times the digital form is clearer because it gives the exact minute.
:::
:::

## Dates

| written (BrE) | said (BrE) | written (AmE) | said (AmE) |
|---|---|---|---|
| *3 May 2026* | *the third of May, twenty twenty-six* (or *May the third*) | *May 3, 2026* | *May third, twenty twenty-six* |
| *03/05/2026* | (day/month/year) | *05/03/2026* | (month/day/year) |

Years are usually said in two pairs: *1998* is *nineteen ninety-eight*, *2026* is *twenty twenty-six*. The years 2000–2009 are said with *thousand*: *two thousand*, *two thousand and eight* (BrE) or *two thousand eight* (AmE). Years from 2010 are said both ways: *twenty twenty-six* or *two thousand and twenty-six*. A year is never read digit by digit (✗ *two zero two six*).

::: theorem Numerical dates are ambiguous {#rule-date-order}
In British English (and most of the world) a date in figures is day/month/year; in American English it is month/day/year. *03/05/2026* is 3 May in Britain and March 5 in the United States. When it matters, write the month as a word (*3 May 2026*, *May 3, 2026*) or use the international order year–month–day (*2026-05-03*).
:::

::: proof
Both orders are standard in their own countries, and neither is wrong; the problem appears only between them. A date with a day above 12 cannot be misread (*25/12* can only be 25 December), but the dates from the 1st to the 12th of every month can. The year–month–day order, used in the international standard ISO 8601 and also in Chinese (2026年5月3日), goes from the largest unit to the smallest and is unambiguous.
:::

Prepositions with dates and times — *at* 7.30, *on* Monday, *on* 3 May, *in* May, *in* 2026 — are taught in [[core-grammar/prepositions-time-place]].

## Phone numbers

Phone numbers are said one digit at a time, in groups, with a short pause between the groups. The examples use numbers reserved for fiction and examples.

- UK: *07700 900371* — *oh seven seven oh oh, nine oh oh, three seven one* (or *double seven, double oh*)
- US: *(202) 555-0147* — *two oh two, five five five, oh one four seven*

::: theorem Saying a phone number {#rule-phone}
Say each digit separately (*three seven one*, not *three hundred and seventy-one*); group the digits as they are written, pausing between groups; say 0 as *oh* (or *zero*); and in British English use *double* for two identical digits next to each other (*double seven*) and sometimes *triple* for three. Many speakers let their voice rise slightly at the end of each group and fall at the end of the last one, so the listener knows when the number is finished.
:::

::: proof
Digits said one by one are shorter and harder to mishear than full number words, and grouping lets the listener write while you pause. *Double* is a British habit (*double five*); Americans more often say the digit twice (*five five*), but both are understood. The rise at the end of each group is the same intonation as in a list (*apples ↗, oranges ↗ and ↘bananas*); intonation is taught in [[connected-speech/intonation-basics]].
:::

## Checking numbers

Because numbers are easy to mishear, English speakers check them constantly — and so should you. Checking is not a sign of bad English; it is what careful speakers do.

::: theorem Check every important number {#rule-check}
When a number matters (a price, a time, a date, a phone number, an address), check it in one of these ways:
1. **Repeat it as a question**: *Fifteen?* / *Seven forty-five?*
2. **Ask about the confusable pair**: *Sorry, fifteen or fifty?* / *Did you say thirteen or thirty?*
3. **Ask for the digits**: *Fifteen — is that one five?*
4. **Read it back**: *Let me read that back to you: oh seven seven oh oh …*
5. **Ask for it again**: *Sorry, could you say that again?* / *Could you repeat the last part?*
:::

::: proof
Repeating or reading back makes the other person hear what you understood, so any mistake comes out at once. Asking about the pair (*fifteen or fifty?*) is especially effective, because the speaker answers with extra stress on the difference: *Fif**teen**! One five.* All these checks are quick and polite; the phrase *Sorry* at the start makes them sound friendly. Asking for clarification more generally is the subject of [[listening-and-conversation/clarification]].
:::

**Receptionist:** *Your appointment is on the thirteenth at ten forty.*

**Mehmet:** *Sorry — the thirteenth or the thirtieth?*

**Receptionist:** *The thir**teenth**. One three. Next Tuesday.*

**Mehmet:** *Great — Tuesday the thirteenth, ten forty. Thanks.*

::: warning Do not just nod
✗ **Assistant:** *That's fifty pounds.* — **Customer:** (not sure if it was *fifteen* or *fifty*, nods and pays with a card)

✓ **Customer:** *Sorry, fifty? Five oh?*

Many learners do not check because they are worried that it shows they did not understand. But a wrong number costs more than a question: a missed train, a wrong amount, a phone number that does not work. A quick *Sorry, fifteen or fifty?* sounds natural and competent.
:::

::: example Taking down a phone message {#ex-phone-number}
A colleague, Mr Ruiz, calls and leaves his number. Write a dialogue in which you take it down and check it: *01632 960 418* (a number reserved for examples).
::: solution
**Mr Ruiz:** *Could you ask Ms Chen to call me back? My number's oh one six three two, nine six oh, four one eight.*

**You:** *Sorry, could you say that a bit more slowly?* — Ask for a slower repeat; this is normal.

**Mr Ruiz:** *Sure. Oh one six three two … nine six oh … four one eight.*

**You:** *Let me read that back: oh one six three two, nine six oh, four one eight.* — Read it back in the same groups.

**Mr Ruiz:** *That's right.*

**You:** *Thank you. I'll ask her to call you back.*

Each step reduces the risk of an error: the slower repeat lets you write; reading back in groups lets him check each part.
:::
:::

A British friend says: *Let's meet at half six.* What time does she mean?
- [ ] 5.30
- [x] 6.30
- [ ] 6.00
- [ ] 7.30
::: solution
British *half six* is short for *half past six*, 6.30. (Speakers of some other languages say "half seven" for 6.30, which is a common source of confusion; in English it is always half **past**.)

::: quiz
How is the price \$12.40 most often said in an American shop?
- [ ] *twelve point four dollars*
- [ ] *one two four zero*
- [x] *twelve forty*
- [ ] *twelve dollars and four cents*
::: solution
In conversation, prices are shortened: *twelve forty*. *Twelve dollars and forty cents* is also correct but long. *Point* is not used in prices, and *four cents* would be \$12.04.

::: note English and Chinese
- **万 and 亿.** Chinese counts large numbers in fours, English in threes ([[#rule-big-numbers]]). Practise by reading numbers with commas: *35,000* — *thirty-five* (before the comma) *thousand*.
- **Years.** Chinese reads a year digit by digit (二〇二六年); English reads it in pairs (*twenty twenty-six*) or with *thousand* (*two thousand and six*).
- **Date order.** Chinese dates go from largest to smallest: 2026年5月3日. British English goes from smallest to largest (*3 May 2026*), and American English puts the month first (*May 3, 2026*).
- **Phone numbers.** Chinese speakers say 幺 for 1 in phone and room numbers to avoid confusing 一 and 七; English has no special word for 1, but *double* and the read-back do the same job of preventing errors.
- **Prices.** 三块五 and *three fifty* are shortened in the same way, so this part is easy — but remember that *point* is not used in prices.
:::

::: history
The word *billion* used to mean different things in Britain and the United States. In the traditional British usage, as in several European languages, a billion was a million million (10¹²); in American usage it was a thousand million (10⁹). The American meaning spread through finance and science, and in 1974 the British Prime Minister, Harold Wilson, told Parliament that official British statistics would use *billion* to mean a thousand million. Today *billion* means a thousand million in all varieties of English, but older British books may still use the old meaning, and *milliard* (a thousand million) survives in other European languages.
:::

Points to check: *£1.2 billion* is read with the number first and *pounds* at the end; *billion* means a thousand million; *85,000* has *thousand* after *eighty-five*; *18 March* is said with *the* … *of* in British English.
