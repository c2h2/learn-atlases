Our language, wrote Jonathan Swift, "is extremely imperfect; that its daily Improvements are by no means in proportion to its daily Corruptions". The complaint was made in 1712, in the *Proposal* described in [[history-of-english/dictionaries-grammars]]. In every generation since, someone has announced that English is being ruined — by slang, by Americanisms, by newspapers, by radio, by television, by text messages, by the internet. Yet the English of 1712 had not decayed by 1812, nor that of 1912 by 2012; it had changed. The history in the chapters before this one shows that change is the normal state of a living language: Old English became Middle English, *thou* gave way to *you*, *do* entered questions, *gotten* went out of British English and stayed in American.

This last chapter turns from the past to the present. Language change does not stop when the history books do; it is going on now, in the vocabulary, the grammar and the pronunciation of English, and linguists can observe it as it happens. The chapter explains how they do so, what is changing, what the internet has and has not done, and what it means that most people who use English today did not learn it as their first language. For a learner, these are practical matters: the English you hear and read is not the English of older textbooks, and knowing which changes are established, which are in progress and which are merely complained about helps you choose what to use.

## Watching change happen

::: definition Change in progress; real time and apparent time {#def-change}
A **change in progress** is a change in a language that is still spreading through its speakers: some use the new form, some the old, and the proportion is shifting. It can be studied in two ways:

- in **real time**, by comparing the language of the same community (or comparable texts) at different dates — for example, matched samples of written English from 1961 and 1991;
- in **apparent time**, by comparing speakers of different ages at one date, on the assumption that people's basic usage changes little after adolescence, so that older speakers show the language as it was when they were young.
:::

::: theorem Evidence for a change in progress {#rule-evidence}
A claim that some feature of English is changing is well supported when:

1. it is based on **counts** in comparable samples (corpora of the same size and kinds of text, or matched groups of speakers), not on individual examples;
2. the difference between dates or age groups is **consistent** across registers or regions, or its limits are stated;
3. **real-time** and **apparent-time** evidence agree;
4. the comparison measures the **rate** of use (per million words, or as a percentage of the contexts where the form could occur), not raw numbers.

A claim based on noticing a few new examples — "Everyone says *less people* now" — is not evidence of change, because people notice new forms more than old ones.
:::

::: proof
The best-known real-time evidence for written English comes from the "Brown family" of corpora: one million words of American English printed in 1961 (the Brown Corpus, compiled at Brown University) and its British match (LOB), with later samples built on the same design from 1991–1992 (FLOB and Frown, compiled at Freiburg). Because the four corpora sample the same kinds of text in the same proportions, differences between them can be attributed to time. Geoffrey Leech and his colleagues analysed them in *Change in Contemporary English: A Grammatical Study* (2009). Apparent-time evidence comes from sociolinguistic interviews, a method developed by William Labov in the 1960s ([[linguistics/variation-change]]). Point 4 matters because corpora and populations differ in size: a form that occurs twice as often in a corpus twice as large has not become more frequent. Swift's complaints and their modern successors fail all four tests.
:::

## New words

Vocabulary changes fastest. Dictionaries add new words and senses several times a year, and most of them are made from material that English already has.

These are the processes of the course *Word Formation* ([[word-formation/compounds]], [[word-formation/conversion]], [[word-formation/shortening]]), applied to new things. Two observations about them are well established. First, compounding and new senses of existing words are far more common than entirely new roots: *cloud*, *tweet*, *stream*, *viral* and *mouse* are old words with new meanings. Second, many new words are short-lived. Lists of "words of the year" chosen by dictionary publishers — Oxford chose *selfie* for 2013 and, unusually, an emoji (the "face with tears of joy") for 2015; Merriam-Webster chose *they* for 2019 — record what was noticed in one year, not what will last.

::: example Identifying the process {#ex-processes}
Name the word-formation process behind each word and give its parts or source: *vlog*, *podcast*, *to text*, *phishing*, *karaoke*, *GIF*, *influencer* (in its social-media sense).
::: solution
1. *vlog* — **blending** (or clipping and compounding): *video* + *blog*, itself a clipping of *weblog* (*web* + *log*).
2. *podcast* — **blending**: *iPod* (a brand name) + *broadcast*.
3. *to text* — **conversion**: the noun *text* (a text message) used as a verb.
4. *phishing* — **respelling** of *fishing* (fishing for passwords), probably on the model of *phreaking*, an older hackers' word: a creative coinage.
5. *karaoke* — **borrowing** from Japanese (カラオケ, ‘empty orchestra’).
6. *GIF* — **acronym** (*Graphics Interchange Format*); its pronunciation, with /ɡ/ or /dʒ/, is itself a well-known dispute.
7. *influencer* — **new sense** of an old derivative (*influence* + *-er*, recorded since the seventeenth century in the general sense ‘one who influences’).

Most of these combine old material in new ways; only *karaoke* is a loanword. That is the normal pattern of English vocabulary growth today.
:::
:::

## Grammar

Grammar changes more slowly than vocabulary, and changes in progress are often visible only in counts.

::: theorem Some grammatical changes in progress {#rule-grammar-change}
The following changes are supported by corpus evidence for twentieth- and early twenty-first-century English (Leech et al. 2009 and later studies):

1. **The core modals have declined** in frequency in both British and American written English between 1961 and the 1990s, *must*, *shall* and *may* most of all, while **semi-modals** such as *have to*, *need to*, *want to* and *be going to* have grown, especially in American English and in speech ([[modality/what-modals-do]]).
2. **The progressive** (*is doing*) has become more frequent, and appears with more verbs of state than before (*I'm loving it*, *I'm wanting to ask you*).
3. ***Whom*** has continued to decline, replaced by *who* or by a zero relative except after prepositions in formal writing.
4. **The *get*-passive** (*He got arrested*) has increased, mainly in informal registers.
5. **Singular *they*** for a person of unspecified gender (*Someone left their umbrella*), used by good writers for centuries, has been accepted by major style guides (the Associated Press in 2017, the American Psychological Association in 2019); its use for a specific person who does not identify as *he* or *she* is newer and spreading.
6. **Quotative *be like*** (*She was like, "Really?"*), first discussed by linguists in the early 1980s in American English, has spread to young speakers in Britain, Canada, Australia and elsewhere.
:::

::: proof
These changes share a direction that Leech and his colleagues described as **colloquialisation**: written English, especially in news and popular writing, has moved closer to the patterns of speech — more contractions, more semi-modals, more progressives, fewer formal forms such as *whom* and *shall*. A second tendency, **densification**, works the other way: more noun sequences (*government health policy review*) in news and academic writing, packing information into fewer words. The changes are tendencies across large samples, not rules: *shall* is still normal in British questions such as *Shall I open the window?*, *must* has not disappeared, and *whom* remains expected after a preposition in formal writing (*to whom it may concern*). Point 5 shows how usage and authority interact: singular *they* was common in speech and in good writing (Jane Austen used it) long before style guides accepted it ([[nouns-and-articles/agreement]]). Point 6 shows how a feature can spread internationally in a generation, through media and through young speakers' contacts.
:::

::: quiz
Which change is supported by comparisons of the Brown-family corpora (1961 and 1991–1992)?
- [ ] *Must* and *shall* became more frequent.
- [x] The core modals declined while semi-modals such as *need to* and *want to* increased.
- [ ] The progressive became less frequent.
- [ ] *Whom* became more frequent.
::: solution
Leech et al. (2009) found that the core modals, especially *must*, *shall* and *may*, declined in written British and American English between 1961 and the early 1990s, while semi-modals rose. The progressive increased and *whom* declined.
:::
:::

::: example Reading a corpus comparison {#ex-corpus}
A student compares two corpora of newspaper English. (The figures are invented for this exercise.) In corpus A, from 1965, with 800,000 words, *shall* occurs 240 times; in corpus B, from 2015, with 1,200,000 words, it occurs 180 times. Has *shall* become less frequent, and by how much? What else would you want to know?
::: solution
1. **Raw numbers mislead**: 240 and 180 come from corpora of different sizes ([[#rule-evidence]], point 4).
2. **Normalise** to a rate per million words: A: 240 ÷ 0.8 = **300** per million; B: 180 ÷ 1.2 = **150** per million.
3. **Compare**: the rate has halved, a fall of 50%. (The raw counts suggested only a 25% fall.)
4. **What else to check**: whether the two corpora sample the same kinds of newspaper text (a change in the mix of reports, editorials and letters would change the rate); whether the fall is consistent in each section; whether *shall* in fixed uses (*Shall we …?*, legal *shall*) behaves differently from *shall* as a plain future; and whether other evidence — real-time corpora of other registers, apparent-time speech data — agrees ([[#rule-evidence]], points 2 and 3).

Only with these checks can the result be called a change in the language rather than a change in the sample.
:::
:::

::: warning Not every complaint is a change, and not every change is decline
✗ *Young people no longer use grammar.* ✗ *Text messages are destroying spelling.*
✓ Every generation uses a grammar; the young often use **newer** forms (*be like*, *I'm loving it*), which older speakers notice and dislike. Studies of texting and literacy have generally **not** found that texting harms children's spelling or reading; one study of British schoolchildren (Plester, Wood and Joshi, 2009) found that knowing text abbreviations was associated with *better* literacy scores, though such correlations do not show causes.

✗ *English was at its best in Shakespeare's time and has declined ever since.*
✓ Each period's English is complete for its users' purposes. Shakespeare's English lacked *its*, used double comparatives and negatives, and had no fixed spelling ([[history-of-english/early-modern-english]]). "Change" is a description; "decline" is a judgement.
:::

## Pronunciation

The reference accents themselves are changing. The *Longman Pronunciation Dictionary* (Wells), whose symbols this atlas uses, records many of these changes in its notes; Cruttenden's *Gimson's Pronunciation of English* (8th edition, 2014), which named the modern British standard General British, changed some of its symbols to match them.

- **GB vowels.** The final vowel of *happy* and *city* is now a tense /i/ for most speakers, closer to the vowel of *see* than to the vowel of *sit* (LPD writes it /i/). The vowel of *goose* has moved forward in the mouth for many younger speakers (*GOOSE-fronting*). The CURE vowel /ʊə/ is giving way to /ɔː/: *sure*, *poor* and *tour* are increasingly /ʃɔː/, /pɔː/, /tɔː/. The TRAP vowel has become more open, nearer [a], and SQUARE is often a monophthong [ɛː]: Cruttenden writes /a/ and /ɛː/ for them, while LPD keeps /æ/ and /eə/ ([[sounds-of-english/long-vowels-diphthongs]]).
- **GB consonants.** The glottal stop for /t/ before consonants (*football*, *Gatwick*) is now normal in GB; between vowels (*water* as [wɔːʔə]) it is common in London and many cities but still regional or informal. *Th*-fronting (*fink* for *think*) is spreading among young speakers in England, though it is not part of GB.
- **GA.** The merger of the vowels of *cot* and *caught* has spread across much of the United States and Canada; in the cities of the Great Lakes, a chain shift of the short vowels (the "Northern Cities Shift", studied by Labov and colleagues from the 1970s) is a reminder that chain shifts like the Great Vowel Shift ([[history-of-english/great-vowel-shift]]) still happen.
- **New urban accents.** In London, the multi-ethnic speech of young people, described by Jenny Cheshire, Paul Kerswill and colleagues as **Multicultural London English** (2011), is spreading features from the speech of many immigrant communities; "Estuary English", a name coined by David Rosewarne in 1984 for a supposed spread of London features into the speech of the south-east, is now seen by many researchers as part of a wider pattern.

::: example Hearing a change in apparent time {#ex-apparent}
In a recorded interview study, 80% of speakers aged 60–80 pronounced *poor* with /ʊə/ and 20% with /ɔː/; among speakers aged 18–30, the figures were 15% and 85%. (These figures are invented for this exercise.) What can and cannot be concluded?
::: solution
**What the data suggest.** A large age difference in the direction of /ɔː/ is consistent with a change in progress: the younger the speakers, the more they use the new form. This is apparent-time evidence ([[#def-change]]).

**What cannot be concluded without more evidence.**
1. That the change is not **age-grading** — a pattern that individuals change as they grow older. Some features are used more by the young and abandoned later. Real-time evidence (recording the same community again twenty years later, or comparing with older recordings) would decide ([[#rule-evidence]], point 3).
2. That the result holds for other words: *poor* may behave differently from *sure*, *tour* or *cure*; the study should count all the words of the lexical set.
3. That it holds for other social groups or regions: the sample must be described.

For CURE-lowering in GB, real-time evidence exists — dictionaries and recordings over several decades show /ɔː/ increasing — so the change is well established. The exercise shows how one figure becomes evidence only with the right comparisons.
:::
:::

## The internet and writing

The internet has produced more written English, by more people, in more informal settings, than ever before. David Crystal (*Language and the Internet*, 2001; *Txtng: The Gr8 Db8*, 2008) argued that computer-mediated communication is best seen as a **new medium** with registers of its own, mixing features of speech and writing: messages are typed, but they are often quick, interactive and informal like conversation, and they develop conventions to supply what speech has and writing lacks — emoticons and emoji for facial expression, capital letters for shouting, repeated letters for intonation (*sooo*), punctuation for tone (a full stop at the end of a short message can sound cold). Abbreviations such as *lol* and *btw* are not new in kind: abbreviation is as old as writing, and many abbreviations of text messaging (*u* for *you*, *gr8*) became less common once phones had full keyboards and predictive text.

::: theorem New media add registers; they do not replace the standard {#rule-media}
1. Informal online writing is a **register**, with its own conventions, appropriate in its setting, like conversation.
2. Writers who use it usually also control the standard written register, and switch between them as they switch between speech styles; what matters is **choosing** the register for the reader and the purpose ([[collocation-and-register/register]]).
3. The standard written language changes slowly, and mainly through its own registers (journalism, business, academic writing), some of which have become more informal (colloquialisation, [[#rule-grammar-change]]).
:::

::: example Choosing the register {#ex-register-online}
Rewrite this message for each recipient: *hey!! running late sooo sorry 🙈 b there in 10*. (a) A close friend. (b) A professor you are meeting for the first time. (c) A colleague, in a work chat.
::: solution
(a) To a friend the message is fine as it is; at most, *Running late, so sorry! Be there in 10* if your friend dislikes emoji.
(b) To a professor: *Dear Professor Chen, I am very sorry, but I am running about ten minutes late. I will be there as soon as possible. Best wishes, Li Na* — full sentences, no emoji, a greeting and a closing ([[pragmatics/writing-to-people]]).
(c) To a colleague in a work chat: *Sorry, running about 10 minutes late — be there soon.* — short and polite, informal but standard, without the repeated letters and emoji.

The content is the same in all three; the register changes. Knowing all three registers, and when to use each, is part of knowing English today.
:::
:::

## Whose English?

The largest change in the situation of English is not inside the language but in its users. Estimates such as Crystal's ([[history-of-english/spread-of-english#rule-power]]) agree that people who use English as a second or foreign language now far outnumber its first-language speakers. Most conversations in English in the world today — between a Chinese engineer and a German supplier, a Brazilian student and a Korean one — may involve no native speaker at all. Research on **English as a lingua franca** (ELF), such as Jennifer Jenkins's work on pronunciation (*The Phonology of English as an International Language*, 2000) and Barbara Seidlhofer's corpus of ELF conversations in Vienna (VOICE), has studied what makes such communication succeed: mutual intelligibility and accommodation to the listener matter more than closeness to a British or American model ([[world-englishes/lingua-franca]]).

::: quiz
Which statement about English today is best supported by the evidence in this chapter?
- [ ] English is changing faster than ever before because of the internet.
- [ ] Native speakers are the majority of English users.
- [x] Second- and foreign-language users of English outnumber first-language speakers.
- [ ] Grammar is no longer changing.
::: solution
All the estimates agree that second- and foreign-language users outnumber first-language speakers. There is no good evidence that English is changing faster than in earlier periods — the changes between 1100 and 1500 were far larger — and grammar is still changing, as [[#rule-grammar-change]] shows.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese speakers are now among the largest groups of English users, and Chinese has begun to contribute to English in new ways. Some are older expressions linked with contact in China: *lose face* ([[history-of-english/spread-of-english#ex-chinese-loans]]) and, more doubtfully, *long time no see*, often explained as a calque of 好久不见 through Chinese Pidgin English, although its earliest records, from around 1900, are American and its origin is debated. Some are recent: in 2018 the *Oxford English Dictionary* added *add oil* (from 加油), labelled as Hong Kong English. Researchers distinguish **China English** — the systematic features of English as used in China, such as terms for Chinese things and ideas (*the Spring Festival*, *hukou*) — from **Chinglish**, which usually means errors or unidiomatic translations; the line is debated, and the history of this course suggests caution. Every variety of English, including American English, was once called a corruption by someone. What decides whether a form becomes part of the language is not its origin but whether many people use it and understand it ([[world-englishes/english-in-china]], [[collocation-and-register/false-friends-chinglish]]).
:::

Finally, machine translation and large language models are now writing and translating very large amounts of English. Whether this will slow change (by favouring the most frequent forms in their training data), speed it, or spread particular styles is not yet known; it is too early for evidence of the kind described in [[#rule-evidence]].

::: history
Complaints about the decline of English have a history as long as the history of the standard. In his essays of 1710–1712, Swift blamed fashionable young men and poets for clipping words (*mob* for *mobile vulgus*, *rep* for *reputation*) — *mob* survived. Henry Alford's *A Plea for the Queen's English* (1864) attacked newspaper style; H. W. Fowler's *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage* (1926) mixed sensible advice with personal dislikes, and Sir Ernest Gowers's *Plain Words* (1948) attacked official jargon. Linguists have answered with evidence: *Language Myths* (1998), edited by Laurie Bauer and Peter Trudgill, collected short essays by specialists on claims such as "English is being ruined" and "Some languages are harder than others". Modern usage guides, such as the *Merriam-Webster Dictionary of English Usage* (1989) and Pam Peters's *Cambridge Guide to English Usage* (2004), base their advice on corpus evidence of how careful writers actually write.
:::

## Where this leads

This is the last chapter of the course. The study of change in progress continues in [[linguistics/variation-change]] and [[linguistics/corpus-linguistics]]; the varieties of English around the world are the subject of the course *World Englishes* ([[world-englishes/models]]); and the literature written in English across these periods is read in *Reading Literature* ([[reading-literature/literary-periods]]). For choosing register in your own writing, see [[collocation-and-register/register]] and [[style-and-rhetoric/word-choice]].

::: summary
- Language change never stops; complaints of "decline" are as old as the standard (Swift 1712) and confuse change with deterioration.
- Change in progress is studied in **real time** (comparable corpora such as Brown, LOB, Frown and FLOB) and **apparent time** (speakers of different ages); good evidence uses counts, rates and comparable samples ([[#def-change]], [[#rule-evidence]]).
- New vocabulary comes mostly from compounding, blending, clipping, acronyms, conversion and new senses; borrowing is a smaller source.
- Grammatical changes include the decline of core modals and rise of semi-modals, more progressives, less *whom*, more *get*-passives, accepted singular *they* and quotative *be like* — a general **colloquialisation** ([[#rule-grammar-change]]).
- GB and GA pronunciation is changing: tense *happY*, GOOSE-fronting, CURE → /ɔː/, glottal stops, the *cot*–*caught* merger, new urban accents.
- The internet has added informal written registers; it has not replaced the standard ([[#rule-media]]).
- Most users of English are now second- or foreign-language users, and English as a lingua franca follows norms of intelligibility.
:::

::: exercise Evaluate the evidence {level=2}
A newspaper writes: "Nobody says *whom* any more: I heard *Who did you see?* three times on the bus today." Explain what is wrong with this as evidence, and what better evidence would show.
::: solution
It is anecdotal: three examples, from one place, on one day, chosen because the writer noticed them ([[#rule-evidence]], point 1). It gives no rate (how often *who* and *whom* occurred where either was possible) and no comparison with an earlier date. Better evidence: counts of *who* and *whom* in object positions in comparable corpora of different dates (real time), or in the speech of different age groups (apparent time). Such counts do show *whom* declining — but in formal writing, especially after prepositions, it is still used, so "nobody says *whom*" is also an overstatement.
:::
:::

::: exercise Explain colloquialisation {level=2}
Define *colloquialisation* and give three changes in written English that illustrate it.
::: solution
**Colloquialisation** is the movement of written English, especially news and popular writing, toward the patterns of speech. Illustrations (any three): more contractions (*don't*, *it's*); the rise of semi-modals (*have to*, *need to*) at the expense of core modals (*must*, *shall*); more progressives; fewer uses of *whom*; more *get*-passives; more first- and second-person pronouns.
:::
:::

::: exercise Singular *they* {level=2}
Explain the difference between the two uses of singular *they* in (a) *Every student must bring their own laptop* and (b) *Alex is my colleague; they work in marketing*, and say which is older.
::: solution
In (a), *they* refers to a person of unspecified gender, here a quantified expression (*every student*); this use is centuries old, found in good writers such as Jane Austen, and accepted by major style guides. In (b), *they* refers to a specific, known person who does not identify as *he* or *she*; this use is recent and spreading, and was recognised by dictionaries and style guides from the late 2010s (Merriam-Webster's word of the year for 2019). (a) is older.
:::

::: exercise Write a reply {level=3}
A reader's letter to a newspaper says: "Text messages and the internet are destroying the English language." Write a reply of 150–200 words, using at least three ideas from this chapter and one from an earlier chapter of the course.
::: solution
A model answer: "The claim is familiar: in the 1710s Jonathan Swift thought English was being corrupted by fashionable young men and their clipped words, and in the nineteenth century the complaint was about newspapers. English has always changed, and change is not destruction. Old English lost its case endings, Shakespeare's English had no *its*, and the *do* of our questions was new in the sixteenth century. The internet has added new informal registers of writing, with their own conventions — emoji, abbreviations, punctuation for tone — but most people who use them also write standard English when the situation requires it, just as we speak differently to friends and in interviews. Studies have generally not found that texting harms children's literacy; one British study even found the opposite association. Where written English is changing, as in the decline of *shall* and *whom* measured in corpora between 1961 and 1991, the changes began before the internet and follow long-term tendencies toward the patterns of speech. English is not being destroyed; it is being used by more people, in more ways, than ever before."
:::

Design a small study of one change in progress in English. Choose a feature (for example *whom*/*who*, *have got*/*have*, CURE as /ʊə/ or /ɔː/, quotative *be like*), and describe in 150–200 words: your question, your data (real or apparent time), what you will count and how, and one limitation. You may use a free corpus interface such as the corpora in [[collocation-and-register/using-a-corpus]].
::: hint
Follow the four points of [[#rule-evidence]]: counts, consistency, real and apparent time, rates.

::: solution
A model design: "**Question**: Is quotative *be like* replacing *say* among English-speaking students at my university? **Data**: apparent time — recorded informal conversations (with permission) of twenty speakers aged 18–25 and twenty aged 45–65, each telling a story about a recent conversation. **What I count**: every quotation of speech or thought in the stories, and which introducer is used (*say*, *go*, *be like*, *think*, zero). **Measure**: the percentage of quotations introduced by *be like* in each age group, not the raw number, because some speakers quote more than others. **Comparison**: published results for other communities (for example Tagliamonte and D'Arcy's studies of Canadian English). **Limitation**: a difference between age groups could be age-grading rather than change, since speakers may use *be like* less as they grow older; only a repeat study in ten or twenty years would show real-time change. The sample is also small and from one place." Your study should state a clear question and count the variable in all the contexts where it could occur.
