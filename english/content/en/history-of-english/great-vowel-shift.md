Why is the letter *i* pronounced /aɪ/ in *bite* and *five*, when in almost every other European language that writes with the Latin alphabet it stands for a vowel like the one in *beet*? Why do *meet* and *meat* sound the same although they are spelled differently? Why is it *five* but *fifth*, *wise* but *wisdom*, *divine* but *divinity*? And why, when you read Chaucer aloud with the rules of [[history-of-english/middle-english#rule-chaucer]], does *lyf* ‘life’ sound like modern *leaf*?

The answer to all four questions is one set of changes in the long vowels of English, which took place roughly between 1400 and 1700 and is known as the **Great Vowel Shift**. It happened just as English spelling was being fixed by scribes and printers, so the spelling largely records the vowels as they were before the shift. This chapter sets out what changed, in what order and when, what the evidence is, what is still debated, and how the shift explains the vowel letters of [[sounds-of-english/spelling-and-sound]] and the long vowels and diphthongs of [[sounds-of-english/long-vowels-diphthongs]]. It uses the phonemic symbols of [[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet]]; reconstructed values are given in the same symbols, but they are hypotheses, not recordings.

## The long vowels before the shift

Late Middle English, the English of Chaucer, had seven long monophthongs (in addition to short vowels and several diphthongs). Their spellings were close to the "continental" values of the Latin alphabet:

| Middle English vowel | usual spelling | example (ME spelling) | modern word | modern GB |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| /iː/ | *i*, *y* | *lyf*, *bite* | *life*, *bite* | /aɪ/ |
| /eː/ ("close *e*") | *e*, *ee* | *mete*, *swete* | *meet*, *sweet* | /iː/ |
| /ɛː/ ("open *e*") | *e*, *ee* (later *ea*) | *mete*, *clene* | *meat*, *clean* | /iː/ |
| /aː/ | *a* | *name*, *mak(e)* | *name*, *make* | /eɪ/ |
| /ɔː/ ("open *o*") | *o*, *oo* (later *oa*) | *boot*, *hoom* | *boat*, *home* | /əʊ/ |
| /oː/ ("close *o*") | *o*, *oo* | *bote*, *roote* | *boot*, *root* | /uː/ |
| /uː/ | *ou*, *ow* | *hous*, *now* | *house*, *now* | /aʊ/ |

Notice that Middle English wrote *mete* for both ‘meat’ and ‘meet’, but the two had different vowels — open /ɛː/ and close /eː/ — as Chaucer's rhymes show. The later spellings *ea* and *ee* were introduced in the sixteenth century to keep them apart, at a time when they were still pronounced differently.

## What changed

::: definition Chain shift; the Great Vowel Shift {#def-gvs}
A **chain shift** is a set of related sound changes in which one sound moves into the position of another, which in turn moves, so that the distinctions between words are kept although every sound has changed. The **Great Vowel Shift** (元音大推移) is the chain shift in which the long vowels of late Middle English were **raised** by one step — /eː/ to /iː/, /oː/ to /uː/, /ɛː/ to /eː/, /ɔː/ to /oː/, /aː/ to /æː/ and later /ɛː/ — while the two highest vowels, /iː/ and /uː/, which could not be raised further, became **diphthongs**, which then lowered their first element: /iː/ → /ei/ → /əi/ → /aɪ/, /uː/ → /ou/ → /əu/ → /aʊ/. The name was given by the Danish linguist Otto Jespersen (*A Modern English Grammar on Historical Principles*, Part I, 1909).
:::

::: theorem The main changes and their approximate dates {#rule-gvs}
| word | late ME (c. 1400) | c. 1550 | c. 1650 | modern GB |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *bite* | /iː/ | /əi/ (or /ei/) | /əi/ | /aɪ/ |
| *meet* | /eː/ | /iː/ | /iː/ | /iː/ |
| *meat* | /ɛː/ | /ɛː/ | /eː/ | /iː/ |
| *name* | /aː/ | /æː/ | /ɛː/ | /eɪ/ |
| *boat* | /ɔː/ | /ɔː/ | /oː/ | /əʊ/ (GA /oʊ/) |
| *boot* | /oː/ | /uː/ | /uː/ | /uː/ |
| *house* | /uː/ | /əu/ (or /ou/) | /əu/ | /aʊ/ |

1. The **first stage**, in the fifteenth century, affected the close mid vowels and the high vowels: /eː/ → /iː/ and /oː/ → /uː/, while /iː/ and /uː/ began to diphthongise.
2. The **second stage**, in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, raised the open and low vowels: /ɛː/ → /eː/, /ɔː/ → /oː/, /aː/ → /æː/ → /ɛː/.
3. **Later changes**, mostly after 1700, completed the modern values: /eː/ (from *meat*) merged with /iː/ (from *meet*); /ɛː/ (from *name*) rose to /eː/ and became the diphthong /eɪ/; /oː/ became /əʊ/ (GB) or /oʊ/ (GA); the first elements of /əi/ and /əu/ lowered to give /aɪ/ and /aʊ/.

All dates are approximate, and the changes went at different speeds in different social groups and regions.
:::

::: proof
The values come from several kinds of evidence that support one another:

- **Writers on pronunciation** (orthoepists) of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries described the vowels of their own speech, often in detail: John Hart (*An Orthographie*, 1569), William Bullokar (1580), John Wallis (*Grammatica Linguae Anglicanae*, 1653) and Christopher Cooper (1685) are among the most important. Their descriptions are not always easy to interpret, and some were describing a conservative or idealised pronunciation, but together they show the stages.
- **Rhymes** of poets show which vowels were identical in their speech. Alexander Pope rhymes *obey* with *tea* in *The Rape of the Lock* (1714): *Here thou, great Anna! whom three realms obey, / Dost sometimes counsel take — and sometimes tea.* The rhyme shows that for Pope, the *meat*-vowel of *tea* had not yet joined the *meet*-vowel /iː/; it was still a mid vowel close to that of *obey*.
- **Spellings** by less educated writers, who spelled as they spoke, and **puns**, which need identical sounds.
- **Transcriptions** of English words in other alphabets, and the treatment of English words borrowed into other languages, and vice versa.
- **Modern dialects**, which preserve intermediate or unshifted stages (see below).

The reconstruction combines these into a single sequence. Its outline is secure; the exact values at each stage, and especially the dates, remain approximate.
:::

In Chaucer's English, which modern word had the vowel /iː/ (as in modern *see*)?
- [ ] *see*
- [x] *five*
- [ ] *sea*
- [ ] *say*
::: solution
Chaucer's *fyve* had /iː/, which became the diphthong /aɪ/ in the shift. *See* had /eː/ (raised to /iː/ in the shift), *sea* had /ɛː/ (raised later, merging with *see* by about 1700), and *say* had a diphthong /ai/.

::: example One word through a thousand years {#ex-house}
Trace the word *house* from Old English to modern GB and GA, giving its spelling and its vowel at each stage, and say which changes affected the sound and which the spelling.
::: solution
1. **Old English** (c. 900): *hūs*, /huːs/. The spelling *u* has its Latin value.
2. **Middle English** (c. 1300–1400): *hous*, /huːs/. The **sound** is unchanged; the **spelling** has changed, because scribes trained in French wrote /uː/ as *ou* ([[history-of-english/norman-french]]). A final *-e* was added in many texts (*house*), partly by analogy with forms that had a pronounced ending.
3. **c. 1500–1600**: still spelled *house*; the vowel has begun to diphthongise in the first stage of the shift: /hous/ or /həus/, according to how the orthoepists are read.
4. **c. 1650–1700**: /həus/; the first element lowers over the following century.
5. **Modern**: /haʊs/ in GB and GA (with fronter or more raised variants in many accents, and /huːs/ in Scots, which never took part in this change — [[#rule-debates]]).

The spelling stopped changing at stage 2 or 3; the sound went on. That is why *ou* now spells /aʊ/: the letters record the scribal convention of the fourteenth century, the sound the shift of the following three.
:::
:::

## The debates

The Great Vowel Shift is one of the most studied changes in the history of any language, and several of its central questions are still open.

::: theorem The positions in the main debates {#rule-debates}
1. **Push chain or drag chain?** In a **drag chain**, the high vowels /iː/ and /uː/ diphthongised first, leaving gaps that the mid vowels moved up to fill; this was Jespersen's view. In a **push chain**, the mid vowels /eː/ and /oː/ rose first and pushed the high vowels out into diphthongs; this was argued by Karl Luick (from 1896). Some scholars propose that the upper half of the shift was a push chain and the lower half a drag chain, or that the two halves were separate changes.
2. **One shift or several?** Robert Stockwell and Donka Minkova (1988 and later) argued that the "Great Vowel Shift" is a modern construct joining changes that took place over three centuries, by different mechanisms, and that it is misleading to call it a single event. Roger Lass, in *The Cambridge History of the English Language*, vol. III (1999), defended treating the first stage, at least, as a coherent chain shift.
3. **When?** The shift is usually dated about 1400–1700, but the start may have been earlier in some places, and the later changes went on into the nineteenth century. Different dates for its stages are given in different handbooks.
4. **Why?** No single cause is accepted. Proposals include the internal pressure of a crowded vowel system, contact between dialects in London as migrants arrived from the Midlands and the East after the Black Death, and social motives — speakers adopting or exaggerating pronunciations associated with a rising class.
:::

::: proof
The evidence for a push chain comes mainly from the north. In Northern Middle English, the vowel /oː/ had already been fronted (to a vowel like /øː/) before the shift, so there was no back mid vowel to rise into the position of /uː/ — and in the north and in Scots, /uː/ did **not** diphthongise: Scots *hoose* ‘house’, *oot* ‘out’ and *doon* ‘down’ keep /uː/. If the diphthongisation of /uː/ had come first and dragged /oː/ after it, the absence of /oː/ should have made no difference; it seems that /uː/ moved only where it was pushed. On the front side, however, northern /iː/ did diphthongise, which fits a drag chain or an independent change better. The incoherence argument rests on the dates: if the raising of /ɛː/ and /aː/ took place a century or more after the raising of /eː/ and /oː/, the speakers of 1650 were not taking part in the "same" change as those of 1450. Both positions agree on the facts in [[#rule-gvs]]; they disagree about how to describe and explain them. This is a normal state for historical linguistics, and the way a careful history should present it.
:::

::: warning What the shift was not
✗ *The Great Vowel Shift changed all the vowels of English around 1500.*
✓ It changed the **long** vowels (and only some of them in some regions), over **about three centuries**. Short vowels were not part of it: the vowels of *bit*, *bet*, *bat*, *pot*, *put* changed little, and the changes that did affect short vowels (for example /ʊ/ → /ʌ/ in *cut*, *but*, in the seventeenth century) were separate.

✗ *The shift is why English spelling is irregular.*
✓ It is one cause among several. Others are the borrowing of French and Latin spellings, scribal conventions (*o* for *u* in *love* — [[history-of-english/norman-french]]), the loss of consonants such as the *k* of *knight*, and the learned respellings of the Renaissance (*debt*, *doubt* — [[history-of-english/early-modern-english]]).
:::

English is not unique. German and Dutch underwent a similar diphthongisation of their high long vowels: Middle High German *mîn niuwez hûs* ‘my new house’ became modern German *mein neues Haus*, and Dutch *huis* ‘house’ and *mijn* ‘my’ have diphthongs too. The German change began earlier and in the south-east, and it did not include the raising of the mid vowels, so it is not the same chain; but it shows that diphthongising high long vowels is a common development, not an English peculiarity.

## Exceptions and later developments

Several groups of words did not follow the main pattern, or changed again afterwards. They are why the same spelling can have several modern values.

- ***great*, *break*, *steak***: words with ME /ɛː/ that did not join the *meet*-vowel when the merger took place, but kept the mid vowel and now have /eɪ/, the vowel of *name*. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries both pronunciations were heard in many words of this group; standard English kept /iː/ in almost all of them and /eɪ/ in these three.
- ***head*, *bread*, *dead*, *death*, *sweat***: words with ME /ɛː/ that were **shortened** to /e/, mostly before /d/, /t/ and /θ/, at various dates, and so escaped the shift. Their spelling with *ea* records the old long vowel.
- ***good*, *foot*, *book*, *stood*** and ***blood*, *flood***: words with ME /oː/ that became /uː/ in the shift and were then shortened to /ʊ/. In *blood* and *flood* the shortening came early enough that the vowel then took part in the change of /ʊ/ to /ʌ/; in *good* and *foot* it came after that change was over; in *food*, *moon* and *root* there was no shortening. A few words still vary between speakers: *room* and *roof* are heard with /uː/ and /ʊ/.
- ***pane*** and ***pain***: the vowel of *name* (from ME /aː/) merged in the seventeenth century with the old diphthong /ai/ of *day* and *pain*, so that *pane* and *pain*, *made* and *maid* became homophones.

::: example Explaining *ea* {#ex-ea}
Explain the three pronunciations of *ea* in *meat* /miːt/, *bread* /bred/ and *great* /ɡreɪt/.
::: solution
All three words had the Middle English open vowel /ɛː/, which sixteenth-century spelling marked with *ea* to distinguish it from the close /eː/ of *meet*.

1. *meat*: /ɛː/ rose to /eː/ in the second stage of the shift and merged with /iː/ around 1700. This is the regular development, shared by most *ea* words (*sea*, *clean*, *teach*).
2. *bread*: the vowel was shortened to /e/ before the shift reached it — shortening before /d/ was common — so it stayed a short front vowel. The spelling *ea* records the earlier long vowel.
3. *great*: the vowel took part in the raising to /eː/ but did not join the final merger with /iː/; it later became /eɪ/ together with the vowel of *name*. Only *great*, *break* and *steak* (and *yea*) follow this path in standard English.

One spelling, three histories. The spelling was fixed at a stage when all three words still had the same vowel.
:::
:::

## Why the spelling no longer fits

::: theorem Spelling records the pre-shift vowels {#rule-spelling}
English spelling of the long vowels was largely settled between about 1450 and 1650, by scribes and printers who continued the conventions of late Middle English. It therefore shows, roughly, the values **before** the shift:

| spelling | pre-shift value | modern value |
|---|---|---|
| *i*, *y* + consonant + *e* (*bite*) | /iː/ | /aɪ/ |
| *ee* (*meet*) | /eː/ | /iː/ |
| *ea* (*meat*) | /ɛː/ | /iː/ (*great*: /eɪ/; *bread*: /e/) |
| *a* + consonant + *e* (*name*) | /aː/ | /eɪ/ |
| *oa*, *o* + consonant + *e* (*boat*, *home*) | /ɔː/ | /əʊ/ (GA /oʊ/) |
| *oo* (*boot*) | /oː/ | /uː/ (*good*: /ʊ/; *blood*: /ʌ/) |
| *ou*, *ow* (*house*, *now*) | /uː/ | /aʊ/ |
:::

::: proof
The printers who followed Caxton (1476) did not invent a new spelling to match the pronunciation of their time; they used the spellings that scribes had used, and those spellings had been formed when the long vowels had their continental values. The *e* added to mark a long vowel (*name*, *bite*) comes from the same period: it was once a pronounced syllable ([[sounds-of-english/spelling-and-sound#rule-silent-e]]). During the sixteenth century reformers such as John Hart proposed new spellings to match the new pronunciation, but they were not adopted. So the vowel letters of English mean what they meant in the fifteenth century, and the "names" of the letters — *a* /eɪ/, *e* /iː/, *i* /aɪ/, *o* /əʊ/ — are themselves the products of the shift.
:::

::: theorem Alternations from the shift {#rule-alternations}
Many pairs of related words differ in their vowels because one member had a long vowel, which shifted, and the other a short vowel, which did not:

| long vowel → shifted | short vowel → unshifted |
|---|---|
| *divine* /aɪ/ | *divinity* /ɪ/ |
| *wise* /aɪ/, *five* /aɪ/, *child* /aɪ/ | *wisdom* /ɪ/, *fifth* /ɪ/, *children* /ɪ/ |
| *serene* /iː/, *keep* /iː/, *meet* /iː/ | *serenity* /e/, *kept* /e/, *met* /e/ |
| *sane* /eɪ/, *nation* /eɪ/ | *sanity* /æ/, *national* /æ/ |
| *profound* /aʊ/ | *profundity* /ʌ/ |
| *goose* /uː/ | *gosling* /ɒ/ |

The short vowels arose earlier, in Old and Middle English, by **shortening** of long vowels before two consonants (*wisdom*, *fifth*, *kept*) and in the third syllable from the end (*divinity*, *serenity*, *sanity*). When the shift came, the long vowels moved and the short ones stayed.
:::

::: example Pairs of related words {#ex-pairs}
Explain the vowel difference in *five* / *fifteen* and in *heal* / *health*.
::: solution
1. *five* had ME /iː/ (Old English *fīf*), which became /aɪ/ in the shift. *Fifteen* (Old English *fīftēne*) had its first vowel shortened before the cluster *ft* in Old or early Middle English, so it was short /ɪ/ when the shift happened, and stayed /ɪ/. The same explains *fifth* and *fifty*.
2. *heal* (Old English *hǣlan*) had ME /ɛː/, raised in the shift and merged with /iː/: /hiːl/. *Health* (Old English *hǣlþ*) had its vowel shortened before the cluster *lth*, so it was short /e/ when the shift came, and stayed /e/: /helθ/. The same pattern is in *clean* / *cleanse* /klenz/ and *deal* / *dealt*.

The pairs show why a learner should not expect related words to share a vowel: the spelling (*fi-*, *plea-*) shows their relationship, while the pronunciation shows their history. Stress and suffixes that create these pairs in modern English are described in [[word-formation/suffixes]].
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which pair shows a vowel difference that the Great Vowel Shift explains?
- [x] *crime* /aɪ/ – *criminal* /ɪ/
- [ ] *cat* /æ/ – *cats* /æ/
- [ ] *sing* /ɪ/ – *sang* /æ/
- [ ] *bed* /e/ – *bedroom* /e/
::: solution
In *crime* the vowel was long in Middle English and shifted to /aɪ/; in *criminal*, with the stress on the first of three syllables, the vowel was short and did not shift. *Sing* – *sang* is an alternation of ablaut, thousands of years older ([[history-of-english/english-and-its-relatives#rule-ablaut]]); the other pairs have no vowel difference.

::: example Reading a poem with its old rhymes {#ex-pope}
In Pope's couplet *Here thou, great Anna! whom three realms obey, / Dost sometimes counsel take — and sometimes tea*, the rhyme no longer works in modern English. Explain why it worked in 1714, and say what this shows about the date of the *meat*–*meet* merger.
::: solution
*Obey* (from French *obéir*) had a vowel that in Pope's time was a long mid front vowel, close to /eː/, on its way to modern /eɪ/. *Tea*, a recent borrowing of the seventeenth century (from Min Chinese *te*, through Dutch), was given the vowel of the *meat* class, which in the speech Pope followed was still /eː/, not yet /iː/. So *obey* and *tea* rhymed.

This shows that the final merger of the *meat* class with the *meet* class (/eː/ → /iː/) was not yet complete for all speakers in the early eighteenth century, even in educated London English. The pronunciation *tay* for *tea* is recorded in that period, and it survives in some Irish English today. A single rhyme is weak evidence, because poets sometimes rhymed by eye or by tradition, but many rhymes of this kind together are strong evidence ([[#rule-gvs]], stage 3).
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese readers know a very similar effect from classical poetry. In Du Mu's 《山行》, *远上寒山石径斜，白云生处有人家。停车坐爱枫林晚，霜叶红于二月花。*, the rhyme words 斜, 家 and 花 rhymed in Tang Chinese, but in Mandarin 斜 is *xié* while 家 and 花 are *jiā* and *huā*; teachers often ask pupils to read 斜 as *xiá* to keep the rhyme. As with Pope's *obey* and *tea*, the poem records a sound system that has since changed. The difference is in the writing. Chinese characters do not show sounds directly, so the change is invisible on the page and appears only when the poem is read aloud. English letters do show sounds, so a spelling fixed before the shift now shows the wrong ones — the "silent" history behind *bite*, *meat* and *house*. Both systems record the past; English shows it in every word.
:::

::: history
The changes of the shift were first worked out by nineteenth-century philologists who used the descriptions of the sixteenth- and seventeenth-century orthoepists. Alexander Ellis's *On Early English Pronunciation* (5 volumes, 1869–1889) collected the evidence on a huge scale; Henry Sweet's *History of English Sounds* (1874, revised 1888) set it out in phonetic terms. Karl Luick's work from the 1890s and Otto Jespersen's grammar of 1909, which gave the change its name, established the chain-shift picture. E. J. Dobson's *English Pronunciation 1500–1700* (1957; second edition 1968) reinterpreted the orthoepists in great detail, often dating the changes earlier. The modern debate was opened by Stockwell and Minkova's critique (1988), answered by Lass (1999) and others, and it continues, now with the methods of sociolinguistics: Labov's studies of vowel shifts in progress in American cities ([[linguistics/variation-change]]) show chain shifts happening today and give historians a model of how such changes spread.
:::

## Where this leads

The shift happened during the transition to **Early Modern English**, whose printing, spelling and vocabulary are the subject of [[history-of-english/early-modern-english]]. Modern long vowels and diphthongs, with their GB and GA values, are described in [[sounds-of-english/long-vowels-diphthongs]]; the spelling patterns that the shift explains, in [[sounds-of-english/spelling-and-sound]]; and chain shifts in progress today, in [[linguistics/variation-change]] and [[history-of-english/english-today]].

::: summary
- The **Great Vowel Shift** (c. 1400–1700) raised the long vowels of Middle English by one step and turned the highest, /iː/ and /uː/, into diphthongs that became /aɪ/ and /aʊ/ ([[#def-gvs]]).
- It happened in stages: /eː/ and /oː/ first, /ɛː/, /ɔː/ and /aː/ later, with the *meat*–*meet* merger around 1700 and further changes after ([[#rule-gvs]]).
- The evidence is orthoepists' descriptions, rhymes (Pope's *obey* – *tea*), spellings, puns and dialects; the values are reconstructions.
- Push chain or drag chain, one shift or several, the dating and the causes are **debated**; Scots *hoose* and *doon* are key evidence ([[#rule-debates]]).
- Spelling was fixed at about the same time and records the **pre-shift** values: *bite*, *meet*, *meat*, *name*, *boat*, *boot*, *house* ([[#rule-spelling]]).
- Alternations like *divine* – *divinity*, *five* – *fifth*, *sane* – *sanity* exist because short vowels did not shift ([[#rule-alternations]]).
- Exceptions (*great*, *bread*, *good*, *blood*) come from incomplete mergers and shortenings.
:::

::: exercise How many long vowels? {level=1 check="7"}
How many long monophthongs did late Middle English have, as listed in this chapter?
::: solution
**7**: /iː/, /eː/, /ɛː/, /aː/, /ɔː/, /oː/, /uː/.
:::
:::

::: exercise Explain the pairs {level=2}
Explain the vowel differences in: *mine* ‘a pit for coal or metal’ / *mineral*; *south* / *southern*; *mean* / *meant*.
::: solution
*mine* had ME /iː/, shifted to /aɪ/; *mineral* (stressed on the first of three syllables) had a short /ɪ/, which did not shift. *south* had ME /uː/, shifted to /aʊ/; *southern* had a shortened vowel before *th* + *r* (/ʊ/, later /ʌ/), which did not shift. *mean* had ME /ɛː/, raised and merged with /iː/; *meant* had a vowel shortened before the cluster *nt*, so it stayed /e/.
:::
:::

::: exercise Which value of *oo*? {level=2}
Explain the three pronunciations of *oo* in *food* /uː/, *good* /ʊ/ and *flood* /ʌ/.
::: solution
All three had ME /oː/, raised to /uː/ in the shift. *Food* kept /uː/. *Flood* was shortened to /ʊ/ early, before the seventeenth-century change of /ʊ/ to /ʌ/, and then took part in that change. *Good* was shortened to /ʊ/ later, after that change was over, so it kept /ʊ/.
:::
:::

::: exercise The northern evidence {level=2}
Scots has *hoose* ‘house’ and *doon* ‘down’ with /uː/. Explain how this evidence is used in the debate about the shift.
::: solution
In the north, ME /oː/ had already moved forward before the shift, so there was no back mid vowel below /uː/. Northern /uː/ did not diphthongise. Supporters of a **push chain** argue that this is what we should expect if the mid vowel had to rise and push /uː/ out of its place: with no pushing vowel, /uː/ stayed. Under a **drag chain**, /uː/ would have diphthongised first in any case, and the absence of /oː/ should have made no difference. The evidence therefore supports a push chain, at least for the back vowels ([[#rule-debates]]).
:::

::: exercise Reconstruct a line {level=3}
Read the line *The holy blisful martir for to seke* (Chaucer, c. 1387) in three ways — with Chaucer's pronunciation, with a pronunciation of about 1600, and with modern pronunciation — for the words *holy* and *seke* (‘seek’). Give the vowel of each word at each date and explain.
::: hint
*holy* had ME /ɔː/; *seke* ‘seek’ had ME /eː/. Use the table in [[#rule-gvs]].
:::
::: solution
- *holy*: c. 1387 /ɔː/ (/ˈhɔːli/); c. 1600 /oː/ (raised one step in the second stage); modern /əʊ/ (GB) or /oʊ/ (GA), after the later diphthongisation.
- *seke* ‘seek’: c. 1387 /eː/ (/ˈseːkə/, with final *-e* pronounced in verse); c. 1600 /iː/ (raised in the first stage, and final *-e* silent); modern /iː/.

The line shows that the two halves of the shift reached different words at different times: *seek* had its modern vowel by Shakespeare's time, *holy* not until much later.
:::

::: exercise Write an explanation {level=3}
A classmate asks: "Why does English write *i* for /aɪ/, when every other language uses it for /iː/?" Write an answer of 120–180 words that uses the Great Vowel Shift and the history of printing, and mentions at least one point that is still debated.
::: solution
A model answer: "English once used *i* exactly as other European languages do. In Chaucer's time, around 1400, *bite* and *life* were pronounced with /iː/, like the vowel of modern *beet*. Between about 1400 and 1700 the long vowels of English changed in a series known as the Great Vowel Shift: they moved up in the mouth, and the highest ones, /iː/ and /uː/, became diphthongs, which eventually became /aɪ/ and /aʊ/. During the same period, spelling was being fixed, especially after Caxton brought printing to England in 1476, and printers kept the spellings scribes had used before the shift. So *i* still shows the old vowel /iː/, while the pronunciation has moved on. Scholars still debate whether the change began with the high vowels moving out of the way (a drag chain) or with the mid vowels pushing them (a push chain), and why it happened at all."
