Around 1600, English was the language of a medium-sized kingdom on the edge of Europe. Baugh & Cable cite estimates of five to seven million speakers, almost all of them in England and the Lowlands of Scotland; even in the British Isles, Welsh, Cornish, Irish and Scottish Gaelic were the first languages of large regions. Educated Europeans learned Latin, French or Italian, not English. Four centuries later, English is the first language of several hundred million people on every continent, an official or working language in dozens of countries, the main language of international science, aviation and business, and the foreign language most often taught in schools around the world, China included.

No feature of the language explains this. English was not chosen because it is easy, logical or beautiful; it spread because of who spoke it and what they did — settlement, trade, conquest, empire, and later the economic, technological and cultural power of the United States. This chapter follows that spread: first within the British Isles, then to North America, the Caribbean, Australasia, South Asia, Africa and East Asia. It shows how each new variety took shape from the dialects of its settlers and the languages around them, and what English borrowed on the way. The varieties themselves are described in [[world-englishes/models]] and the chapters after it; here the subject is how they came to exist.

## Three ways of using English

::: definition First, second and foreign language {#def-enl}
English is used in a country as:

- a **first language** (or native language, ENL), spoken by most people from childhood: the United Kingdom, Ireland, the United States, Canada, Australia, New Zealand;
- a **second language** (ESL), not the first language of most people but used inside the country for government, education, law or the media, often as an official language: India, Nigeria, Kenya, Singapore, the Philippines;
- a **foreign language** (EFL), learned at school and used mainly for contact with other countries: China, Japan, Brazil, Germany.

Braj Kachru (1985) drew the three as concentric circles: the **Inner Circle** of first-language countries, the **Outer Circle** of second-language countries, which are mostly former British or American colonies, and the **Expanding Circle** of the rest of the world. The model is widely used and widely criticised — it classifies countries rather than speakers, and the boundaries are now blurred — as [[world-englishes/models]] explains.
:::

The circles correspond roughly to two different histories. In the Inner Circle, English came with **settlers** who became the majority. In the Outer Circle, it came with **administrators**, merchants, missionaries and teachers who remained a minority, and it was learned by local people as the language of power and education. The Expanding Circle learned English later, mainly in the twentieth century, as the language of international communication.

## The British Isles

English spread first to the other countries of the British Isles, and there too it spread by power rather than by settlement alone.

- **Wales**: the Laws in Wales Acts of 1535 and 1542 incorporated Wales into the English legal system and required those holding public office to use English. Welsh nevertheless remained the language of most of the population until the nineteenth century; a Welsh Bible (1588) gave it a written standard, and it is still spoken today by several hundred thousand people.
- **Cornwall**: Cornish declined from the sixteenth century and ceased to be a community language in the eighteenth (Dolly Pentreath, who died in 1777, is traditionally named its last native speaker, though the claim is disputed).
- **Ireland**: English had been spoken in the towns of the east since the twelfth century, but the decisive spread followed the Tudor conquest and the "plantations" of English and Scottish settlers, above all in Ulster from 1609. After the Great Famine of 1845–1852, with death and emigration, the use of Irish fell sharply; by 1900 most Irish people spoke English as their first language.
- **Scotland**: the Lowlands spoke **Scots**, a sister language of English descended from Northern Middle English ([[history-of-english/middle-english]]), with its own literature. After the Union of the Crowns in 1603, when James VI of Scotland became James I of England, and the Acts of Union of 1707, the English of England increasingly became the written standard in Scotland too. Gaelic remained the language of the Highlands and Islands.

## North America

The first lasting English settlement in America was at Jamestown, Virginia, in 1607; the Puritan settlers of the *Mayflower* landed at Plymouth, Massachusetts, in 1620. Over the next century and a half, settlers came from every part of the British Isles — East Anglia and the south of England to New England, the south and west of England to Virginia, Ulster Scots to the Appalachian back country — together with Dutch, German, French and Swedish settlers and, by force, hundreds of thousands of enslaved Africans. American English took shape in this mixture.

::: theorem How a colonial variety forms {#rule-new-variety}
A new variety of English in a settler colony develops from:

1. **Dialect mixing**: settlers from different regions of the home country, with different dialects, live together; their children, and their children's children, form a new, levelled variety, keeping features that were common or socially prestigious and dropping many local ones;
2. **Retention**: some features of the settlers' English survive in the colony after they have changed at home;
3. **Innovation**: new words and senses for new things, and new changes in pronunciation and grammar;
4. **Contact**: borrowing from the languages of the indigenous peoples and of other immigrants.
:::

::: proof
The model is supported by the best-documented case, New Zealand English, which formed in the nineteenth century and was recorded in the speech of the first native-born generations in the 1940s; Peter Trudgill (*New-Dialect Formation*, 2004) used these recordings to show how a new accent emerged from a mixture of British dialects within two or three generations. The same processes explain the main features of American English. Its **retentions** include *gotten* (common in British English until the eighteenth century), *fall* for ‘autumn’ (used in England in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries), *I guess* in the sense ‘I suppose’ (Chaucer has *I gesse*) and the pronunciation of *r* after vowels ([[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet#rule-rhotic]]), which the south-east of England lost in the eighteenth century — though the coastal cities of New England and the South, in close contact with London, also became non-rhotic. The idea that American English as a whole is "older" than British English (sometimes called "colonial lag") is too simple: each variety has kept some old features and innovated in others.
:::

American English borrowed from the languages around it:

- **Native American languages**: *raccoon*, *moose*, *skunk*, *moccasin*, *squash*, *tomahawk*, and thousands of place names, among them about half the names of the states (*Massachusetts*, *Michigan*, *Ohio*);
- **Dutch**, from the colony of New Netherland (New York): *cookie*, *boss*, *coleslaw*, *Santa Claus*, *Yankee* (probably);
- **Spanish**, in the south-west: *canyon*, *ranch*, *rodeo*, *mosquito*, *patio*;
- **African languages**, through the enslaved population: *gumbo*, *okra*, possibly *tote*; the origin of *OK* (first recorded in Boston in 1839, as an abbreviation of a jocular misspelling *oll korrect*) is often claimed for African or other sources, but the evidence supports the abbreviation.

After independence, Webster's dictionary and speller gave American English its own written standard ([[history-of-english/dictionaries-grammars#rule-webster]]), and immigration from Germany, Ireland, Scandinavia, Italy, Eastern Europe and later Latin America and Asia made the United States the largest English-speaking country in the world.

::: example Retention or innovation? {#ex-retention}
Classify each American usage as a probable **retention** of older British English, an **innovation**, or a **borrowing**, and give the British equivalent: *fall* (‘autumn’), *gotten*, *cookie*, *elevator*, *canyon*, *I guess* (‘I suppose’).
::: solution
- *fall* — retention: *fall of the leaf* and *fall* for the season were used in England in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries; British English later preferred *autumn* (from French).
- *gotten* — retention: British English used *gotten* as the past participle of *get* until the eighteenth century, and still has it in *ill-gotten*.
- *cookie* — borrowing from Dutch *koekje* ‘little cake’; British *biscuit*.
- *elevator* — innovation: a Latin-based word given a new sense in nineteenth-century America; British *lift*.
- *canyon* — borrowing from Spanish *cañón*; British English has no equivalent, and uses *canyon* too.
- *I guess* — retention of a Middle English sense of *guess*, ‘suppose’; British English usually says *I suppose* or *I think*.

The list shows the four processes of [[#rule-new-variety]] working together. None of these makes one variety more correct than the other.
:::
:::

::: example Why some Americans drop their *r* {#ex-rhotic}
Most American accents pronounce the *r* of *car* and *hard*, but traditional accents of eastern New England (Boston) and of parts of the coastal South did not. Explain the pattern with the processes of [[#rule-new-variety]].
::: solution
1. **Starting point.** In the seventeenth century, when the colonies were founded, almost all English accents pronounced *r* after vowels. Most American English kept it: a **retention**.
2. **Change at home.** During the eighteenth century the *r* after vowels was lost in the prestigious English of London and the south-east of England ([[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet#rule-rhotic]]).
3. **Contact with London.** The Atlantic ports — Boston, Charleston, Savannah — kept close commercial and social ties with London, and their upper classes sent their sons to be educated in England. The new London fashion was imitated and spread in these cities and their regions: an **innovation** borrowed from the home country after settlement.
4. **The interior.** Areas settled from Pennsylvania and the back country, with many Ulster Scots and northern English, whose dialects kept *r*, and with less contact with London fashion, stayed rhotic. Westward migration spread this rhotic English across most of the continent.

In the twentieth century, non-rhotic pronunciation lost prestige in the United States and has declined even in Boston and the South — a reminder that prestige, not the sound itself, decides which pronunciation spreads. (The details of the eighteenth-century spread are debated; the outline is standard.)
:::
:::

## Australia, New Zealand and South Africa

On his first Pacific voyage, James Cook reached the east coast of Australia in 1770; his party recorded the word *kangaroo* from the Guugu Yimithirr language of north Queensland. The British established a penal colony at Sydney with the "First Fleet" in 1788, and free settlers followed in growing numbers in the nineteenth century. In New Zealand, British sovereignty dates from the Treaty of Waitangi (1840) with Māori chiefs, whose English and Māori texts differ in important ways. In South Africa, the British took the Cape from the Dutch in 1806, and British settlers arrived from 1820. Each of these varieties formed by dialect mixing ([[#rule-new-variety]]), largely from the English of south-east England, and each borrowed from local languages: Australian English from Aboriginal languages (*kangaroo*, *boomerang*, *koala*), New Zealand English from Māori (*kiwi*, *haka*, *kia ora*), South African English from Afrikaans and Bantu languages (*trek*, *veld*, *indaba*).

## South Asia

The East India Company received its royal charter on 31 December 1600. For two centuries it was a trading company with growing territories; after the Indian Rebellion of 1857, the British Crown took over direct rule. English became the language of government and higher education, and the key decision was made in 1835, when the Governor-General adopted the recommendation of Thomas Babington Macaulay's *Minute on Education* to fund education in English rather than in Sanskrit, Arabic and Persian. Macaulay's aim was explicit: *We must at present do our best to form a class who may be interpreters between us and the millions whom we govern; a class of persons, Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and in intellect.*

::: example Reading a colonial document {#ex-macaulay}
Analyse Macaulay's sentence from 1835 quoted above. What does it say about the purpose of English education, and how does the later history of English in India both fulfil and contradict it?
::: solution
**The purpose.** English was to create an intermediate class — *interpreters* between the British rulers and the *millions* they governed — and that class was to adopt English culture (*taste*, *opinions*, *morals*, *intellect*) while remaining Indian by birth. Elsewhere in the same *Minute*, Macaulay dismissed Indian learning with contempt, claiming that a single shelf of a good European library was worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia. The document is a statement of colonial power and of cultural arrogance, and is read that way in India today.

**What followed.** An English-educated class did emerge, and it ran much of the administration, the law and the universities. But it also used English for its own purposes: leaders of the independence movement, from the Indian National Congress (founded 1885) onward, argued in English, and English became a neutral link language between speakers of India's many languages. After independence (1947), the Official Languages Act of 1963 kept English alongside Hindi for official purposes. Indian English developed its own norms of vocabulary, grammar and pronunciation ([[world-englishes/south-asian-englishes]]). The language imposed for one purpose was adopted for others — a pattern repeated across the Outer Circle.
:::
:::

English in turn borrowed many words from Indian languages, collected in Henry Yule and A. C. Burnell's glossary *Hobson-Jobson* (1886): *bungalow*, *shampoo*, *pyjamas*, *jungle*, *loot*, *thug*, *cot*, *chutney*.

## Africa and the Caribbean

The Atlantic slave trade, in which English ships played a leading part in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, carried millions of Africans to the Caribbean and the Americas. On plantations where people of many African languages were forced to work together under English-speaking owners, new languages arose: **pidgins**, simplified contact languages with no native speakers, and **creoles**, which develop when a pidgin or a contact variety becomes the first language of a community — such as Jamaican Creole, Gullah in South Carolina and Georgia, and Krio in Sierra Leone, founded as a settlement for freed slaves in 1787. How such languages form, and why they are full languages rather than "broken English", is the subject of [[world-englishes/pidgins-creoles]]. In nineteenth-century Africa, British colonial rule made English the language of administration in Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, Uganda and elsewhere; at independence many of these states kept English as an official language, often because it belonged to no single ethnic group ([[world-englishes/african-caribbean-englishes]]).

## East Asia and China

English reached China by sea. In the eighteenth century, foreign trade was confined to Canton (Guangzhou) under the "Canton system" (1757–1842), and English and Chinese merchants communicated in **Chinese Pidgin English**, a contact language with mostly English vocabulary and Chinese-influenced grammar. The word *pidgin* itself is usually explained as a Chinese pronunciation of *business*. After the First Opium War, the Treaty of Nanking (1842) ceded Hong Kong to Britain and opened five ports to foreign trade; Hong Kong remained a British colony until 1997, and Singapore, founded as a British trading post by Stamford Raffles in 1819, kept English as its main working language after independence. For the European side of the age of empire, see the [European History Atlas](/learn/europe/lesson.php?c=nations&l=empires).

::: example Chinese words in English {#ex-chinese-loans}
Explain the origin of each English word or phrase, and how sure we can be: *tea*, *kowtow*, *chopsticks*, *ketchup*, *gung-ho*, *lose face*.
::: solution
1. *tea* — from the southern Min (Amoy, Xiamen) pronunciation *te* of 茶, brought to Europe by Dutch traders in the seventeenth century. Languages that got the word overland or through Portuguese Macau often have the *cha* form (Portuguese *chá*, Russian *chai*). Certain.
2. *kowtow* — from 叩头 *kòutóu* ‘knock the head’, recorded in English from the early nineteenth century, at first for the ritual prostration before the emperor; now ‘behave obsequiously’. Certain.
3. *chopsticks* — a pidgin compound: *chop* ‘quick’ (Chinese Pidgin English, probably from Cantonese 急 *gap*) + *stick*, recorded from the late seventeenth century; a translation of the idea behind 筷子 (*kuài* sounds like 快 ‘quick’). Probable.
4. *ketchup* — usually derived from a southern Min word for a fish sauce (something like *kê-tsiap*), reaching English through Malay in the late seventeenth century. Generally accepted, but the details are debated.
5. *gung-ho* — from 工合 *gōnghé*, the name of the Chinese Industrial Cooperatives, adopted as a motto by a US Marine unit in the early 1940s and misunderstood as ‘work together, be enthusiastic’; now ‘very (perhaps too) enthusiastic’. Well documented.
6. *lose face* — a **calque** (loan translation) of 丢脸 *diū liǎn*, recorded in English from the late nineteenth century among foreigners in China. Probable.

Words from Chinese are few compared with those from French or Latin, but they record real episodes of contact: the tea trade, diplomacy, the pidgin of Canton, war. Some newer ones are discussed in [[history-of-english/english-today]].
:::

## Why English became a global language

::: theorem Global languages spread by power, not by structure {#rule-power}
A language becomes international because of the **power of its speakers** — political, military, economic, technological and cultural — not because of any property of the language itself. English became global through two successive powers: the British Empire, which by the early twentieth century ruled about a quarter of the world's land and people, and the United States, which became the leading economic, scientific and cultural power of the twentieth century. Once established, a global language is maintained by its usefulness: the more people use it, the more useful it is to learn.
:::

::: proof
This is the argument of David Crystal's *English as a Global Language* (1997; 2nd edition 2003). The evidence is comparative. Latin, Greek, Arabic, Spanish and French each became international languages when their speakers held power over large territories or dominated trade and learning, and each lost ground when that power declined; none of them spread because of its grammar. English spread in the same way, with a few twentieth-century additions: the dominance of American films, music and television, the use of English in aviation and shipping, in international organisations founded after 1945, in science (where English became the main language of publication during the twentieth century, overtaking German and French), and finally on the internet. Crystal (2003) estimated, with wide margins, roughly 320–380 million first-language speakers, 300–500 million second-language speakers and 500 million to a billion foreign-language users; all such figures depend on how "speaker" is defined and are uncertain.
:::

::: warning "English spread because it is easy"
✗ *English became the world language because its grammar is simple and it has no genders or cases.*
✓ English spread because of the power of Britain and then of the United States. Its spelling is notoriously difficult, its phrasal verbs and idioms are hard for learners, and its "simple" morphology is balanced by complex word order, articles and tense–aspect distinctions ([[tense-and-aspect/tense-and-chinese]]). Languages with complex grammar, such as Latin and Arabic, have also been global languages.

A related mistake: ✗ *American English is a corrupted form of British English.* ✓ Both developed from the English of the seventeenth century; each has kept some older features and changed others ([[#rule-new-variety]]).
:::

::: quiz
In Kachru's model, which circle does Singapore belong to?
- [ ] Inner Circle
- [x] Outer Circle
- [ ] Expanding Circle
- [ ] None
::: solution
Singapore is a former British colony where English is an official language and the main language of education and government, though not historically the first language of most of the population: the Outer Circle. (In fact, many young Singaporeans now grow up speaking English at home, which is one of the reasons Kachru's circles are criticised — [[world-englishes/models]].)
:::

::: quiz
Which of these is the best explanation of why English is the main language of international science today?
- [ ] English has a larger vocabulary than other languages.
- [ ] English is grammatically simpler than German or French.
- [x] The scientific, economic and political weight of English-speaking countries, especially the United States, in the twentieth century.
- [ ] English was chosen by an international treaty.
::: solution
Before the twentieth century, much important science was published in German and French. The shift to English came with the scientific and economic dominance of the United States and the disruption of European science by two world wars; once English journals and conferences were the centre, scientists everywhere had reason to publish in English ([[#rule-power]]). No treaty chose English, and vocabulary size and grammar are not the cause.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
English teaching in China has a long history of its own. The Tongwen Guan (同文馆), founded in Beijing in 1862, trained interpreters for the government; Yung Wing (容闳) graduated from Yale in 1854, the first Chinese graduate of an American university, and led the Chinese Educational Mission that sent 120 boys to study in the United States from 1872. In the twentieth century English competed with Japanese and Russian, and since the late 1970s it has become the main foreign language of Chinese schools and universities. China is therefore in Kachru's Expanding Circle, with an unusually large number of learners. Two lessons from the history of the spread are useful for a Chinese learner. First, English belongs to all its users, not only to its first-language speakers: Indian, Nigerian and Singaporean English are not mistakes but varieties. Second, "standard English" is not one thing: an exam may expect British or American norms, but English as it is used between, for example, a Chinese engineer and a Brazilian one follows norms of intelligibility rather than of either country ([[world-englishes/lingua-franca]], [[world-englishes/english-in-china]]).
:::

::: history
The study of the new varieties began with dictionaries and glossaries of their vocabulary: John Pickering's *Vocabulary … of Words and Phrases Supposed to Be Peculiar to the United States* (1816), *Hobson-Jobson* (1886) for Anglo-Indian English, and E. E. Morris's *Austral English* (1898). H. L. Mencken's *The American Language* (1919) celebrated American English as a language in its own right. The academic field of "World Englishes" took shape in the 1980s with Braj Kachru's work and the journal *World Englishes* (from 1981 under an earlier title); Edgar Schneider's *Postcolonial English* (2007) proposed a "dynamic model" of five phases through which new Englishes develop, from foundation to differentiation. Research has moved from describing varieties as departures from British norms to describing them on their own terms.
:::

## Where this leads

The varieties that resulted are described in the course *World Englishes*: [[world-englishes/models]], [[world-englishes/british-american]], [[world-englishes/other-first-language-varieties]], [[world-englishes/south-asian-englishes]], [[world-englishes/southeast-asian-englishes]], [[world-englishes/african-caribbean-englishes]], [[world-englishes/pidgins-creoles]] and [[world-englishes/english-in-china]]. What is happening to English now, as a language used by more second- and foreign-language speakers than first-language ones, is in [[history-of-english/english-today]].

::: summary
- English was spoken by perhaps five to seven million people around 1600; it is now a first, second or foreign language for well over a billion, by uncertain estimates ([[#def-enl]]).
- It spread first within the British Isles (Wales 1535–1542, Ireland, Scotland 1603 and 1707), largely at the expense of Celtic languages.
- In settler colonies — North America from 1607, Australia from 1788, New Zealand from 1840 — new varieties formed by **dialect mixing**, **retention**, **innovation** and **contact** ([[#rule-new-variety]]).
- In South Asia, Africa and parts of East Asia, English came with administration and education and became a second language, used for new purposes after independence (Macaulay 1835; India 1947 and 1963).
- Contact with China produced Chinese Pidgin English and loans such as *tea*, *kowtow* and *lose face*.
- English became global because of the **power** of Britain and then the United States, not because of its structure ([[#rule-power]]).
:::

::: exercise Explain a retention {level=2}
American English has *gotten* and British English mostly *got* as the past participle of *get*. Explain which is older and why American English has it.
::: solution
*Gotten* is the older form, the strong past participle in *-en* (like *forgotten*, *written*). It was normal in British English until the eighteenth century, when *got* became usual there; American English, founded in the seventeenth century, kept *gotten* (beside *got* in the sense of possession, *I've got a car*). It is a **retention** ([[#rule-new-variety]], point 2), not an American invention.
