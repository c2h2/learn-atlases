Put four words for ‘father’ side by side: English *father*, Dutch *vader*, Latin *pater*, Sanskrit *pitár-*. The resemblance is obvious, but it is not enough to prove anything — English *bad* and Persian *bad* also look alike and mean the same, and they have nothing to do with each other. Now add four words for ‘foot’: *foot*, *voet*, *pes* (stem *ped-*), *pad-*; and four for ‘fish’: *fish*, *vis*, *piscis* and, from Old Irish, *íasc*. Something more interesting appears. Wherever Latin and Sanskrit have *p* at the start of a word, English has *f* and Dutch *f* or *v*. This is not a resemblance but a **correspondence**, repeated across hundreds of words, and a correspondence that regular cannot be chance. It is the evidence that English, Dutch, Latin and Sanskrit are descended from one ancestor language that nobody wrote down.

This chapter explains how linguists group languages into families, and where English sits in the largest family in the world: Indo-European. It sets out the Germanic branch to which English belongs, the sound law that first separated Germanic from its relatives, and the method — the comparative method — by which a language spoken more than five thousand years ago can be partly reconstructed. It also explains why English vocabulary looks so un-Germanic on the page, and why it is nevertheless classified as a Germanic language. Many examples come from the Latin and Greek roots of [[word-formation/greek-latin-roots]]: here you see that some of those borrowed roots are distant cousins of native English words.

## Language families

Languages change all the time, and they change differently in different places. When a community splits — by migration, by conquest, by mountains or seas — its speech goes on changing in each part, and after enough centuries the parts no longer understand each other. Latin did this within recorded history: the Latin spoken across the western Roman Empire became, over about a thousand years, Portuguese, Spanish, Catalan, French, Italian, Romanian and many other languages. In that case we have the ancestor in writing. In most cases we do not, and the ancestor has to be inferred from its descendants.

::: definition Language family, cognate {#def-family}
A **language family** is a group of languages descended from a single earlier language, their **proto-language** (原始语). The languages of a family are **genetically related** — a metaphor from biology, meaning descent, not genes. A **branch** is a subgroup of a family with a later common ancestor of its own (Germanic, Romance, Slavic).

Words in related languages that are descended from the same word of the proto-language are **cognates** (同源词): English *father*, German *Vater*, Latin *pater*. Cognates need not look alike — English *hundred* and Latin *centum* are cognates — and words that look alike need not be cognates.
:::

Two other relations between words must be kept apart from cognacy. A **loanword** is a word taken from another language: English *paternal* is not a cognate of Latin *pater* but a borrowing from it (through medieval Latin *paternalis*). And some words resemble each other by **chance**: with a few dozen sounds and thousands of words in each language, coincidences are unavoidable.

::: theorem The comparative method {#rule-comparative}
Languages are shown to be related, and their proto-language is reconstructed, by the **comparative method**:

1. Collect words of **basic vocabulary** — numerals, kinship terms, body parts, pronouns, common verbs — which are rarely borrowed.
2. Look for **regular sound correspondences**: a sound in one language matched by the same sound in the other, in the same positions, in many words (Latin *p-* : English *f-* in *pater*/*father*, *pes*/*foot*, *piscis*/*fish*, *plenus*/*full*).
3. Explain exceptions by **conditions** (the sound's position, the stress, the neighbouring sounds) or by borrowing, not by special pleading.
4. Reconstruct for each correspondence a sound in the proto-language from which every attested sound can be derived by plausible changes. Reconstructed forms are written with an asterisk in linguistics books; in this atlas we mark them with the word *Proto-Indo-European* (PIE) or *Proto-Germanic* and write them in italics: PIE *ph₂tḗr* ‘father’ (the *h₂* stands for a lost consonant; see the historical note).
5. Confirm relationships also by **shared grammar** — irregular forms and endings especially, because these are almost never borrowed.
:::

::: proof
The method works because sound change is, to a high degree, **regular**: when a sound changes in a community, it changes in every word in which it occurs in the same conditions. This principle was stated as a law by the "Neogrammarians" of Leipzig in 1878, and although it has known limits (some changes spread through the vocabulary word by word, as [[linguistics/variation-change]] shows), it holds well enough over centuries that a correspondence repeated in dozens of basic words is overwhelmingly unlikely to be chance. Resemblance alone proves nothing, because chance and borrowing both produce it. Regular correspondences in basic vocabulary, together with shared irregularities in grammar, are what chance and borrowing do not produce. Note what the method cannot do: it reconstructs a system of correspondences, not a recording. We do not know how PIE sounded in detail, only which distinctions it must have made.
:::

::: warning Look-alikes are not evidence
✗ *English* bad *and Persian* bad *mean the same, so the two words are related.* They are not: the resemblance is a coincidence, and English *bad* has no known cognates at all.
✗ *Latin* deus *and Greek* theos *(‘god’) must be cognates.* They are not: the Greek *th-* does not correspond regularly to Latin *d-*.
✗ *English* day *comes from Latin* dies. It does not: *day* is Germanic (German *Tag*), and Latin *dies* belongs with *deus* and the name *Zeus*.
✓ *English* ten *and Latin* decem *are cognates*, although they look less alike, because *t* : *d* is a regular correspondence (*two* : *duo*, *tooth* : *dent-*, *eat* : *edere*).

Always ask whether a similarity is regular, whether it appears in basic words, and whether one language could have borrowed the word from the other.
:::

::: example Testing a claimed cognate {#ex-test}
Someone claims that English *much* and Spanish *mucho* (‘much’) are cognates. Test the claim with [[#rule-comparative]].
::: solution
1. **Basic vocabulary?** Yes, both are common words — so borrowing is unlikely, and the claim deserves a test.
2. **Regular correspondence?** Spanish comes from Latin, so look at Latin *multum* ‘much’. For the words to be cognates, Latin *-lt-* would have to correspond to English *-ch*; no other pair of words shows such a correspondence.
3. **Each word's own history.** English *much* comes from Old English *micel* ‘great’ (Scots still has *mickle*), which is cognate with Greek *megas* ‘great’ — Grimm's *g* → *k*. Spanish *mucho* comes from Latin *multum*, by a regular Spanish change of *-ult-* to *-uch-* (as in *cuchillo* ‘knife’ from Latin *cultellum*).
4. **Conclusion.** The two words have different ancestors; their present likeness is a coincidence produced by separate changes. The true cognate of *much* in Latin is *magnus* ‘great’ — which English also has, borrowed, in *magnify* and *magnitude*.
:::
:::

## The Indo-European family

Most languages of Europe, and many of South and West Asia, belong to one family, **Indo-European** (印欧语系), named after its easternmost and westernmost branches in the age before European expansion. Its living languages are spoken as a first language by nearly half the people of the world.

| branch | languages (ancient → modern) |
|---|---|
| Indo-Iranian | Sanskrit, Avestan → Hindi–Urdu, Bengali, Punjabi, Marathi; Persian, Pashto, Kurdish |
| Hellenic | Ancient Greek → Modern Greek |
| Italic | Latin → the Romance languages: Portuguese, Spanish, Catalan, French, Italian, Romanian |
| Celtic | Gaulish, Old Irish → Irish, Scottish Gaelic, Welsh, Breton |
| Germanic | Gothic, Old Norse, Old English → English, German, Dutch, Frisian, the Scandinavian languages |
| Balto-Slavic | Old Church Slavonic → Russian, Polish, Czech, Serbian, Bulgarian; Lithuanian, Latvian |
| Armenian | Classical Armenian → Armenian |
| Albanian | Albanian |
| Anatolian (extinct) | Hittite, Luwian |
| Tocharian (extinct) | Tocharian A and B |

Two branches are known only from ancient texts. **Hittite**, the language of an empire in what is now Turkey, is preserved on clay tablets written in cuneiform from the second millennium BC; it was identified as Indo-European by the Czech scholar Bedřich Hrozný in 1915. **Tocharian** is known from Buddhist manuscripts of roughly the sixth to eighth centuries AD found in the Tarim Basin, in today's Xinjiang, near Kucha (库车) and Turpan (吐鲁番): the easternmost Indo-European language of the ancient world, spoken far inside Central Asia.

Where and when Proto-Indo-European was spoken is one of the longest debates in the subject. The **steppe hypothesis**, associated with Marija Gimbutas and, in its modern form, with David Anthony (*The Horse, the Wheel, and Language*, 2007), places it on the grasslands north of the Black and Caspian Seas, perhaps around 4500–3000 BC; the shared PIE vocabulary for wheels, axles and wagons suggests a date after the invention of wheeled transport. The **Anatolian hypothesis** of Colin Renfrew (1987) links the spread of the family to the spread of farming from Anatolia several thousand years earlier. Since 2015, studies of ancient DNA have shown large migrations from the steppe into Europe in the third millennium BC, which most scholars take as support for the steppe hypothesis, at least for the European branches; the question is not closed. For a learner the important point is a modest one: the reconstructed language is real, in the sense that its descendants are, but every statement about its speakers is an inference.

Which pair is evidence that two languages are related?
- [ ] English *bad* and Persian *bad*, both ‘bad’
- [ ] English *paternal* and Latin *paternus*, both ‘of a father’
- [x] English *three*, *thin*, *thou* beside Latin *tres*, *tenuis*, *tu*
- [ ] English *mama* and Mandarin *māma*
::: solution
The third answer shows a **regular** correspondence — English *th-* matching Latin *t-* — in several basic words. *Bad* is a chance resemblance; *paternal* is a loanword from Latin, so it shows contact, not descent; and words like *mama* and *papa* arise independently in many languages because they are among the first syllables babies produce.

## Grimm's law: how Germanic separated

What makes Germanic a branch is a set of changes that its languages share and no other branch has. The best known is a shift in the consonants, worked out by the Danish scholar Rasmus Rask (1818) and stated systematically by Jacob Grimm in the second edition of his *Deutsche Grammatik* (1822). It is known in English as **Grimm's law**, or the First Germanic Consonant Shift.

::: theorem Grimm's law {#rule-grimm}
In the development from Proto-Indo-European to Proto-Germanic, three series of stop consonants shifted, as a chain:

1. The voiceless stops ***p, t, k*** became fricatives ***f, θ, h***: Latin *pater* – *father*; Latin *tres* – *three*; Latin *centum* (with *c* = /k/) – *hundred*; Latin *cornu* – *horn*.
2. The voiced stops ***b, d, g*** became voiceless ***p, t, k***: Latin *decem* – *ten*; Latin *dent-* – *tooth*; Latin *genu* – *knee*; Latin *granum* – *corn*. (PIE *b* was rare, so examples of *b* → *p* are few.)
3. The "voiced aspirated" stops ***bh, dh, gh*** became ***b, d, g***: Sanskrit *bhrātar-* – *brother*; Sanskrit *dhā-* ‘put’ – *do*; the root of Latin *hostis* (PIE *ghosti-*) – *guest*.

Latin, Greek and Sanskrit did not undergo this shift (though each had changes of its own: PIE *bh* is *f* in Latin *frater* and *ph* in Greek *phrātēr*), so their consonants are usually closer to those of the proto-language.
:::

::: proof
The evidence is the regularity of the correspondences across basic vocabulary: once the three series are recognised, dozens of cognates line up — *foot* : *ped-*, *fish* : *piscis*, *fire* : Greek *pyr*, *thin* : *tenuis*, *heart* : *cord-*, *hound* : *canis*, *two* : *duo*, *eat* : *edere*, *cold* : *gelidus*, *bear* (‘carry’) : Sanskrit *bhar-*. The changes form a chain: if the voiced stops had become voiceless before the old voiceless stops became fricatives, *ten* would now begin with *th*. So the order is part of the hypothesis. The shift also explains pairs **inside** English, where one word is inherited and the other borrowed from Latin: *father* / *paternal*, *foot* / *pedal*, *fish* / *piscine*, *three* / *triple*, *heart* / *cordial*, *hound* / *canine*, *tooth* / *dental*, *ten* / *decimal*, *corn* / *grain*. Each pair is one PIE root that has reached English twice: once by inheritance, with Grimm's law, and once by borrowing, without it.
:::

Grimm's law had exceptions, and explaining them was a triumph of the method. Why does Gothic have *fadar* ‘father’, with *d*, but *brōþar* ‘brother’, with *þ* (= /θ/), when both words had PIE *t*? In 1875, in a paper published two years later, the Danish linguist Karl Verner showed that the answer lay in the position of the stress in the proto-language, which Sanskrit preserves: *pitár-*, stressed after the *t*, but *bhrā́tar-*, stressed before it. Where the PIE stress did not immediately precede, the new fricatives became voiced. **Verner's law** turned an apparent exception into a confirmation of regularity. It also explains an alternation still visible in modern English: *was* and *were*. The *s* and the *r* are the same original sound; in the plural, where the old stress had fallen differently, it became *z* and then *r*. (The *th* of modern *father* is a much later English change: Old English had *fæder*, and *father* with /ð/ became usual only around 1500.)

::: example Applying Grimm's law {#ex-grimm}
Use Grimm's law to find the native English cognate of each Latin word, and then give an English word borrowed from the Latin one.

1. Latin *pes*, *ped-* ‘foot’
2. Latin *cor*, *cord-* ‘heart’
3. Latin *genu* ‘knee’
4. Latin *tres* ‘three’
5. Latin *frater* ‘brother’
::: solution
1. *p* → *f*, *d* → *t*: **foot**. Borrowed: *pedal*, *pedestrian*.
2. *k* → *h*, *d* → *t*: **heart** (the vowel differs, as vowels often do). Borrowed: *cordial* ‘from the heart, warm’, and through French *courage*.
3. *g* → *k*: **knee**. The *k* was pronounced in Old English and is still written ([[sounds-of-english/spelling-and-sound#rule-silent]]); German *Knie* still pronounces it. Borrowed: *genuflect* ‘bend the knee’.
4. *t* → *θ*: **three**. Borrowed: *triple*, *trio*, *trinity*.
5. This one is a trap: Latin *f-* is itself the Latin development of PIE *bh*, which Grimm's law made *b* in Germanic: **brother**. Borrowed: *fraternal*, *fraternity*. Comparing Latin and English directly gives *f* : *b*, which is regular, but only because both come from *bh*.

The pairs show why English vocabulary is said to have two layers: a native Germanic word for the everyday thing, and a Latin-based word for the learned or abstract adjective.
:::
:::

## The Germanic branch

Proto-Germanic was probably spoken in southern Scandinavia and the coasts of northern Germany in the last centuries BC. Its speakers were in contact with the Romans, from whom they borrowed words such as the ancestor of English *street* (Latin *strata*), *wine* and *cheese*; the earliest Germanic writing is in runes, carved from about the second century AD. One of the most famous early inscriptions was on a golden horn found at Gallehus in Denmark and dated to about AD 400: *ek hlewagastiz holtijaz horna tawido* ‘I, Hlewagastiz, son of Holt (or: of Holt), made the horn’. You can see *horn* in *horna*, and *ek* is the ancestor of *I* (compare German *ich*).

::: definition The Germanic subgroups {#def-germanic}
The Germanic languages are usually divided into three groups:

- **East Germanic**, now extinct, known mainly from **Gothic**, through the Bible translation made by Bishop Wulfila in the fourth century — the earliest substantial text in any Germanic language.
- **North Germanic**: **Old Norse** and its descendants Icelandic, Faroese, Norwegian, Danish and Swedish.
- **West Germanic**: **Old English**, Old Frisian, Old Saxon, Old Dutch and Old High German, and their descendants English, Scots, Frisian, Dutch (with Afrikaans), Low German, German and Yiddish.

English belongs with Frisian, Old Saxon and some features of Dutch in a subgroup sometimes called **North Sea Germanic** (or *Ingvaeonic*); within it, English and Frisian are particularly close.
:::

What makes all of these "Germanic", besides Grimm's law? Several features that the other branches lack:

- **Stress on the first syllable of the root.** PIE stress could fall on different syllables of a word; Germanic fixed it on the first syllable of the root. This is why native English words are stressed at the start (*father*, *mother*, *happy*, *water*), why prefixes like *be-* and *for-* are unstressed (*become*, *forget*), and why many endings were later weakened and lost — the story of the next chapters.
- **A past tense made with a dental suffix.** Besides the old pattern of vowel change (*sing* – *sang*), Germanic created a new past tense with a *-d-* or *-t-* suffix: Gothic *nasida* ‘saved’, Old English *lufode* ‘loved’, modern *walked*, German *sagte*. This **weak** past is the source of the regular *-ed* of today; the older vowel-change type survives in the **strong** verbs.
- **Two tenses.** Germanic verbs had only a present and a past form; the future and the perfect were expressed with other words. Modern English *will go* and *have gone* are later developments, not inheritances of single forms.
- **Strong and weak adjectives**, a double set of adjective endings depending on whether a determiner comes before (German still has *guter Wein* beside *der gute Wein*). English lost the distinction in the Middle Ages.
- **Words without known cognates outside Germanic**, such as *sea*, *ship* and *wife*. Some scholars have suggested that they were borrowed from a non-Indo-European language spoken where Germanic arose; this "substrate" idea is unproven.

::: theorem Strong verbs keep an Indo-European pattern {#rule-ablaut}
The vowel alternation of English strong verbs — *sing*, *sang*, *sung*; *drive*, *drove*, *driven*; *ride*, *rode*, *ridden* — is not an English invention but a regular system called **ablaut** (vowel gradation), inherited from Proto-Indo-European, where the vowels *e*, *o* and zero alternated within a root. Greek shows the same system: *leípō* ‘I leave’, *léloipa* ‘I have left’, *élipon* ‘I left’. Learners meet ablaut today as a list of irregular verbs; historically it was the regular way of forming tenses, and the *-ed* type was the newcomer.
:::

::: proof
The evidence is the match of whole patterns: the same three-way vowel alternation in the same roots across Gothic, Old English, Old Norse and Old High German, and the same kind of alternation in Greek, Latin and Sanskrit. It is the kind of evidence described in [[#rule-comparative]], point 5: irregular-looking grammar shared across languages, which could not have been borrowed. In Old English there were seven classes of strong verbs, each with its own vowel series, and many more strong verbs than today. Over the next thousand years, many moved to the weak type — *helpan*, *healp* became *help*, *helped*; *climb* had *clomb* — while a few went the other way (*dive*, *dove* in American English; *wear*, *wore*). The irregular verbs of a learner's list are what remains of a once-regular system ([[history-of-english/old-english]]).
:::

### English and its closest relatives

Dutch and German share most of English's Germanic vocabulary, but German has undergone a second consonant shift, the **High German consonant shift** (roughly the sixth to eighth centuries AD), which English, Dutch and Low German did not. It explains many differences between English and German cognates:

| English | Dutch | German | shift in German |
|---|---|---|---|
| *ten* | *tien* | *zehn* | *t* → *z* /ts/ |
| *water* | *water* | *Wasser* | *t* → *ss* |
| *pepper* | *peper* | *Pfeffer* | *p* → *pf*, *ff* |
| *make* | *maken* | *machen* | *k* → *ch* |
| *that* | *dat* | *das* | *θ* → *d* (a separate change) |

English also shares changes with Frisian alone. In both, *k* before a front vowel became *ch*: *cheese* and *church* beside West Frisian *tsiis* and *tsjerke*, but Dutch *kaas* and *kerk*. And both lost an *n* before a fricative, lengthening the vowel: *five*, *us*, *other*, *tooth*, *goose* beside German *fünf*, *uns*, *ander*, *Zahn*, *Gans*.

::: example Matching cognates across Germanic {#ex-germanic}
Give the English cognate of each German word, and say which sound difference explains the change: *Zunge*, *Apfel*, *Pfund*, *Herz*, *machen*, *Fuß*.
::: solution
1. *Zunge* — **tongue**: *t* → German *z* /ts/ at the start of a word.
2. *Apfel* — **apple**: *p* → German *pf* after a consonant or at the start, *pf*/*ff* elsewhere.
3. *Pfund* — **pound**: *p* → *pf*. (Both come early from Latin *pondo*, a weight, borrowed before the High German shift — which is how we know the shift is later than the borrowing.)
4. *Herz* — **heart**: *t* → *z*. (The *h* in both is Grimm's *k* → *h*: compare Latin *cord-*.)
5. *machen* — **make**: *k* → *ch* /x/ after a vowel.
6. *Fuß* — **foot**: *t* → *ß* /s/ after a vowel. (The *f* in both is Grimm's *p* → *f*: Latin *ped-*.)

German shows both shifts in a single word: *Fuß* has Grimm's *f* and the High German *ß*; English *foot* has only Grimm's *f*. That is the relative chronology the method recovers: first the Germanic shift, much later the High German one.
:::
:::

## Why English does not look Germanic

Open an English dictionary at any page and most of the headwords will be of French or Latin origin. Open an English novel and most of the running words will be Germanic. Both statements are true, and the difference between them matters for classification.

::: theorem Classification rests on inheritance, not on vocabulary counts {#rule-inheritance}
A language is classified by what it **inherited** — its basic vocabulary, its sound correspondences, its core grammar — not by the proportion of borrowed words in its dictionary. English is a West Germanic language because:

1. its basic vocabulary is overwhelmingly Germanic: pronouns (*I*, *you*, *we*), numerals (*one* to *ten*), kinship terms (*father*, *mother*, *son*, *daughter*), body parts (*hand*, *foot*, *head*, *heart*), common verbs (*be*, *have*, *do*, *go*, *come*, *eat*, *see*) and function words (*the*, *and*, *of*, *in*);
2. its grammar descends from Old English: strong and weak verbs, the genitive *-s*, the comparison *-er*, *-est*, the modals;
3. its sound system shows the Germanic changes, including Grimm's law and initial stress in native words.

The large Romance and Latin vocabulary is the result of **contact** — borrowing — after English had become a separate language.
:::

::: proof
The point is clearest from counts made in two different ways. A count of the **headwords** of a dictionary treats *the* and *ubiquitous* equally; it is dominated by the rare, learned words that make up most of any dictionary, and these are largely French and Latin. The best-known such count, by Finkenstaedt and Wolff (*Ordered Profusion*, 1973), used the roughly 80,000 words of the *Shorter Oxford English Dictionary* and found French and Latin each the source of a little over a quarter of them, and the Germanic stock (Old English, Old Norse, Dutch) about a quarter. A count of **running text**, by contrast, counts each word every time it occurs, and the most frequent words in any English text — *the*, *of*, *and*, *to*, *a*, *in*, *is*, *it* — are nearly all native. Durkin (*Borrowed Words*, 2014) shows how much the picture changes with the method: borrowed words become rarer the further up the frequency list one goes, although some very common words, such as *people*, *very* and *use*, are French. Neither count decides the classification. Vocabulary can be borrowed in huge quantities, as English shows; the basic vocabulary and the core grammar are what languages pass on from one generation to the next.
:::

::: example Sorting a sentence by origin {#ex-sorting}
Classify every word of this sentence as Germanic (inherited from Old English or borrowed from Old Norse) or Romance/Latin: *The young doctor gave her patient some simple advice about his diet.*
::: solution
Germanic: *the*, *young*, *gave* (Old English *giefan*; the *g-* of the modern form is due to Norse — see [[history-of-english/vikings-norse]]), *her*, *some*, *about*, *his*. Romance/Latin: *doctor* (Latin, through French), *patient* (French, from Latin), *simple* (French), *advice* (French *avis*), *diet* (French, from Latin and ultimately Greek).

Seven Germanic words and five Romance ones; but notice which is which. The Germanic words are the grammatical frame — articles, pronouns, the verb, the preposition — and the Romance words are the content nouns and the adjective. Take the Romance words out and you can still see the structure of an English sentence: *The young ___ gave her ___ some ___ ___ about his ___.* Take the Germanic words out and nothing is left but a list. That is what it means to say that English is a Germanic language with a very large borrowed vocabulary.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese belongs to a different family, **Sino-Tibetan** (汉藏语系), together with Tibetan, Burmese and many languages of southern China and the Himalayas. No relationship between Sino-Tibetan and Indo-European has been demonstrated by the comparative method, so resemblances between English and Chinese words are either chance or borrowing: *tea* comes from a southern Min form *te* of 茶, and Chinese 沙发 *shāfā* from English *sofa*. A learner should not look for cognates between the two languages.

The method itself, however, works in Chinese exactly as it does in English. The varieties of Chinese — Mandarin, Wu, Yue (Cantonese), Min, Hakka and others — differ as much as many European languages do, and they show regular correspondences. Words that end in *-p*, *-t* or *-k* in Cantonese — 十 *sap*, 一 *jat*, 六 *luk* — end in a vowel in Mandarin — *shí*, *yī*, *liù* — and the correspondence holds through hundreds of characters. As with Latin and English, the simplest explanation is a common ancestor that had final stops, and the old rhyme books such as the 《切韵》 (AD 601) confirm it. The Swedish sinologist Bernhard Karlgren reconstructed Middle Chinese in the early twentieth century using exactly the comparative method of this chapter, applied to modern dialects and to those rhyme books. If you know two varieties of Chinese, you already know what a regular correspondence feels like.
:::

::: history
The idea that languages form families is older than the comparative method, but the turning point is usually placed in 1786. In an address to the Asiatic Society in Calcutta on 2 February 1786, published in 1788, the judge and orientalist Sir William Jones said of Sanskrit that it bears to Greek and Latin "a stronger affinity, both in the roots of verbs and in the forms of grammar, than could possibly have been produced by accident; so strong indeed, that no philologer could examine them all three, without believing them to have sprung from some common source, which, perhaps, no longer exists". Jones was not the first to notice the resemblance, and some of his other groupings were wrong, but his emphasis on grammar and his "common source, which, perhaps, no longer exists" set the agenda. In the next half-century Rasmus Rask, Franz Bopp and Jacob Grimm built the comparative method; August Schleicher drew the first family trees of Indo-European in the 1850s and 1860s and even tried to write a fable in reconstructed PIE. In 1879 the young Ferdinand de Saussure argued, from internal patterns alone, that PIE must have had sounds that no daughter language preserved; when Hittite was deciphered in the twentieth century, it turned out to have a consonant written *ḫ* in some of the predicted places. A prediction about an unrecorded language, confirmed by a language discovered later, is the strongest evidence that the method is more than clever guessing.
:::

## Where this leads

English became a separate language when Germanic settlers brought their dialects to Britain in the fifth century: that is the beginning of [[history-of-english/old-english]]. Its North Germanic cousins came back to England with the Vikings ([[history-of-english/vikings-norse]]), and its Romance cousins with the Normans ([[history-of-english/norman-french]]) and with the scholars of the Renaissance ([[history-of-english/early-modern-english]]). How sound changes spread in a community and through the vocabulary is the subject of [[linguistics/variation-change]]; how the Latin and Greek roots of borrowed words are used today is in [[word-formation/greek-latin-roots]].

::: summary
- A **language family** is a group of languages descended from one proto-language; **cognates** are words descended from the same ancestor word, and they need not look alike ([[#def-family]]).
- Relationship is proved by **regular sound correspondences** in basic vocabulary and by shared irregular grammar, not by resemblance, which chance and borrowing also produce ([[#rule-comparative]]).
- English is an **Indo-European** language of the **West Germanic** branch, closest to Frisian, then to Dutch, Low German and German ([[#def-germanic]]).
- **Grimm's law** shifted PIE *p t k* to *f θ h*, *b d g* to *p t k*, and *bh dh gh* to *b d g*: *father* / *pater*, *ten* / *decem*, *brother* / *bhrātar-* ([[#rule-grimm]]). Verner's law explained its exceptions and survives in *was* / *were*.
- Germanic also fixed the stress on the root syllable and created the dental (*-ed*) past; the vowel changes of strong verbs (*sing*, *sang*, *sung*) are inherited from PIE ([[#rule-ablaut]]).
- English has many French and Latin words, but its classification rests on its inherited basic vocabulary and grammar ([[#rule-inheritance]]).
- Pairs such as *foot* / *pedal* and *heart* / *cordial* are one PIE root reaching English twice: inherited and borrowed.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Cognate, loan or chance? {level=1}
For each pair, say whether the English word is a cognate of the other word, a borrowing from it, or a chance look-alike.

1. English *mother* – Latin *mater*
2. English *maternal* – Latin *maternus*
3. English *bad* – Persian *bad*
4. English *night* – German *Nacht*
5. English *table* – Latin *tabula*
::: solution
1. Cognate (inherited from the same PIE word; Grimm's law: *t* → *θ*, later *th* /ð/).
2. Borrowing: *maternal* came into English from Latin (through French or medieval Latin) in the late Middle Ages.
3. Chance look-alike.
4. Cognate: both are inherited from Proto-Germanic (and English *gh* once spelled the same /x/ sound as German *ch*).
5. Borrowing: *table* came from Latin through French.

In how many of these pairs does the English word show Grimm's *p* → *f*? *father* / *pater*; *pen* / *penna*; *fish* / *piscis*; *fire* / Greek *pyr*; *pepper* / *piper*.

Each Latin root reached English twice. Give the native English word and the borrowed Latinate word for each meaning.

1. ‘tooth’ (Latin *dent-*)

::: exercise Explain an exception {level=2}
Latin *piper* ‘pepper’ and Latin *planta* ‘plant’ begin with *p*, and so do English *pepper* and *plant*. Does this contradict Grimm's law? Explain.
::: solution
No. Grimm's law applies to words **inherited** from Proto-Indo-European into Germanic. *Pepper* and *plant* were **borrowed** from Latin — *pepper* early, by the Germanic peoples in contact with Rome, *plant* in Old English times — after the shift had already happened, so their *p* was never shifted. (If they were inherited cognates of the Latin words, they would begin with *f*.) Borrowing is one of the standard explanations of an apparent exception in the comparative method ([[#rule-comparative]], point 3). German *Pfeffer* shows that the borrowing happened before the High German shift, which affected it.
:::

::: exercise Is English "really" Romance? {level=3}
A classmate argues: "More than half the words in an English dictionary come from French and Latin, so English is really a Romance language." Write a reply of 120–180 words that explains what the figures actually measure and why linguists classify English as Germanic.
::: solution
A model answer: "The figure is roughly right for dictionary headwords — counts such as Finkenstaedt and Wolff's (1973) put French and Latin together above half — but it measures the wrong thing. A dictionary lists every word once, so rare and learned words like *ubiquitous* count as much as *the*. In running text, where each word is counted every time it is used, the most frequent words — articles, pronouns, prepositions, auxiliaries, numbers and common verbs — are almost all Germanic, and they make up most of what we say and write. More importantly, languages are classified by what they inherit, not by what they borrow. English inherited its basic vocabulary and its grammar — strong verbs, the dental past, the genitive *-s* — from Proto-Germanic, and its native words show Grimm's law. The French and Latin words came later, through contact. English is a Germanic language with an unusually large borrowed vocabulary."
:::

::: exercise Reconstruct a correspondence {level=3}
Here are words for four numbers in three languages:

| | Language A | Language B | Language C |
|---|---|---|---|
| ‘two’ | *duo* | *two* | *dva* |
| ‘three’ | *tres* | *three* | *tri* |
| ‘ten’ | *decem* | *ten* | *desyat'* |
| ‘tooth’ | *dent-* | *tooth* | — |

(A is Latin, B English, C Russian.) State the correspondences for the first consonant, say which language has probably changed, and why.
::: hint
Look at the columns: in how many languages does each consonant appear? Then remember the chain in [[#rule-grimm]].
:::
::: solution
There are two correspondences. (1) Latin *d* : English *t* : Russian *d* (*duo* / *two* / *dva*; *decem* / *ten* / *desyat'*; *dent-* / *tooth*). (2) Latin *t* : English *th* : Russian *t* (*tres* / *three* / *tri*). In each, Latin and Russian agree, and English is the odd one out. The simplest hypothesis is that the proto-language had *d* and *t*, as Latin and Russian do, and that English (with the rest of Germanic) changed them: *d* → *t* and *t* → *θ*. Two languages from different branches would have had to make the same change independently for the opposite to be true, which is less likely. This is Grimm's law reconstructed from scratch — and the order of the changes follows too: if *d* had become *t* first, the new *t* of *two* would have gone on to become *th*.
:::
