In 1755 Samuel Johnson published *A Dictionary of the English Language* in two large volumes. Among its forty thousand entries is his own profession:

> *Lexicographer. A writer of dictionaries; a harmless drudge, that busies himself in tracing the original, and detailing the signification of words.*

and a definition that has been quoted ever since:

> *Oats. A grain, which in England is generally given to horses, but in Scotland supports the people.*

The jokes are famous, but they are not typical: almost all of Johnson's definitions are careful, plain and supported by quotations from English writers. What was new was the ambition — a single person setting out the whole vocabulary of the language, with its meanings and its best usage. Behind it lay a question that the eighteenth century asked with great urgency: who decides what is correct English? France had an Academy; Italy had the Accademia della Crusca. England had none, and it still has none. Its standard was settled instead by dictionaries, grammars and usage guides written by private individuals and sold by booksellers.

This chapter tells that story — the first English dictionaries, the calls for an academy, Johnson, the grammarians whose rules learners still meet, Webster's American standard and the *Oxford English Dictionary* — and it uses the story to separate two kinds of statement about English that are easily confused: descriptions of what speakers do, and prescriptions of what they should do ([[linguistics/what-linguistics-studies#rule-describe]]).

## The first English dictionaries

Before 1600, dictionaries in England were bilingual: Latin–English word lists for schools, and dictionaries of French and Italian. The first **monolingual** English dictionary was Robert Cawdrey's *A Table Alphabeticall* (1604), a small book of about 2,500 "hard usuall English wordes, borrowed from the Hebrew, Greeke, Latine, or French", explained with short synonyms, for "Ladies, Gentlewomen, or any other unskilfull persons". It was a response to the Renaissance borrowing of [[history-of-english/early-modern-english]]: readers needed help with words like *abdicate* and *ingenious*.

::: definition Hard-word dictionary; general dictionary {#def-dictionaries}
A **hard-word dictionary** explains only difficult, usually borrowed, words; the seventeenth-century English dictionaries (Cawdrey 1604, John Bullokar 1616, Henry Cockeram 1623, Thomas Blount 1656, Edward Phillips 1658) were of this kind. A **general dictionary** aims to include the common words of the language as well; the first widely used one was Nathan Bailey's *Universal Etymological English Dictionary* (1721). A **historical dictionary** records the history of each word and sense with dated quotations — the principle of the *Oxford English Dictionary*. A **learner's dictionary** is written for those learning English as a second language, with simple definitions, grammar codes and examples.
:::

## An academy for English?

::: theorem The aim of "ascertaining" the language {#rule-ascertain}
Many writers of the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries believed that English needed to be **ascertained** — fixed in a permanent form — for three reasons:

1. it seemed to lack rules: spelling, grammar and pronunciation varied, and there was no grammar or dictionary of authority;
2. it seemed to be **decaying**: writers feared that the English of their time would soon be as hard to read as Chaucer's, so their own works would not last;
3. other nations had institutions to regulate their languages: the Accademia della Crusca in Florence published its dictionary in 1612, and the Académie française was founded in 1635.

The proposed remedy was an academy, or at least an authoritative dictionary and grammar.
:::

::: proof
The fear of decay was understandable: readers of 1700 could see how much English had changed since Chaucer, and they did not know that the changes of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries — the loss of final *-e*, the vowel shift, mass borrowing — had been unusually large. Proposals came from the Royal Society, which set up a committee on the language in 1664, from Daniel Defoe (*An Essay upon Projects*, 1697) and most famously from Jonathan Swift, whose *Proposal for Correcting, Improving and Ascertaining the English Tongue* (1712), addressed to the head of the government, the Earl of Oxford, asked for a society to fix the language. Nothing came of it: the government fell in 1714, and English institutions were in any case suspicious of regulation in the French manner. The work fell to private authors, which is why English still has no official regulator. As Johnson's career shows, the belief behind point 2 — that a language could be fixed — did not survive contact with the evidence.
:::

## Johnson's *Dictionary*

A group of London booksellers commissioned the dictionary in 1746, and Johnson published a *Plan of a Dictionary of the English Language* in 1747, addressed to Lord Chesterfield. Working with six assistants in a house off Fleet Street, he produced the work in under nine years: published on 15 April 1755, it contained about 42,000 entries and some 114,000 quotations.

Its innovations were in method more than in size. Johnson **illustrated** every sense with quotations from the best English writers, mostly from Sidney and Shakespeare (the 1580s) to his own day; he **divided** the senses of common words carefully (the verb *take* has well over a hundred numbered senses); and he gave **etymologies** and some notes on usage. His Preface records how the work changed his mind. He had hoped, he admits, to fix the language; he ended convinced that no dictionary could:

> *When we see men grow old and die at a certain time one after another, from century to century, we laugh at the elixir that promises to prolong life to a thousand years; and with equal justice may the lexicographer be derided, who being able to produce no example of a nation that has preserved their words and phrases from mutability, shall imagine that his dictionary can embalm his language, and secure it from corruption and decay, that it is in his power to change sublunary nature, or clear the world at once from folly, vanity, and affectation.*

A little later he dismisses the academies in one sentence: *sounds are too volatile and subtile for legal restraints; to enchain syllables, and to lash the wind, are equally the undertakings of pride, unwilling to measure its desires by its strength.*

::: example Description and prescription in Johnson {#ex-johnson}
Here are four entries or remarks from Johnson's *Dictionary*. Classify each as mainly **descriptive** (recording usage) or **prescriptive** (judging it), and say what each shows about his method.

1. *Network. Any thing reticulated or decussated, at equal distances, with interstices between the intersections.*
2. *Oats. A grain, which in England is generally given to horses, but in Scotland supports the people.*
3. He labels many words *low* or *cant* (‘vulgar slang’); *fun*, for example, is called *a low cant word*.
4. In the Preface: *I do not form, but register the language; I do not teach men how they should think, but relate how they have hitherto expressed their thoughts.*
::: solution
1. **Descriptive**, though unhelpful: it tries to define the word exactly, but uses harder words than the one defined (*reticulated* ‘made like a net’, *decussated* ‘crossed in an X’). It shows the risk of defining common words with Latinate ones — the opposite of the plain definitions of modern learner's dictionaries ([[core-vocabulary/using-a-dictionary]]).
2. **Descriptive** in form, with a **judgement** in tone: it records a fact about use (oats were indeed a staple food in Scotland), but the joke expresses a Londoner's prejudice. Johnson's personal voice appears in a few such entries.
3. **Prescriptive**: the labels *low* and *cant* judge words by their social standing. Modern dictionaries keep the idea of register labels (*informal*, *slang*, *offensive*) but intend them as descriptions of how a word is received, not as condemnations.
4. **Descriptive principle**: Johnson presents the lexicographer as a recorder of how people have expressed their thoughts, not a legislator — although, as items 2 and 3 show, he did not always keep to it.

His *Dictionary* is a mixture, typical of its time: a description of the usage of good writers, with the author's judgements added. The idea that a dictionary should record all usage neutrally belongs to the nineteenth century.
:::
:::

## The grammarians

Grammars of English for English speakers began in the late sixteenth century (William Bullokar's *Pamphlet for Grammar*, 1586) and multiplied in the eighteenth, when a growing middle class wanted to speak and write "correctly". Two of the most influential were Robert Lowth's *A Short Introduction to English Grammar* (1762) and Lindley Murray's *English Grammar* (1795), which was used in schools in Britain and America for decades. Joseph Priestley's *Rudiments of English Grammar* (1761) took a more descriptive line, appealing to the usage of good writers rather than to reason.

Many of the rules that learners still meet in style guides come from this period. They were usually justified by **logic**, by the model of **Latin**, or by the **taste** of the grammarian.

::: theorem The origins of some prescriptive rules {#rule-prescriptive}
| rule | first prominent statement | its basis |
|---|---|---|
| Do not end a sentence with a preposition (*the man I spoke to*) | John Dryden criticised Ben Jonson for it (1672); Lowth (1762) called the construction natural but less graceful in solemn style | the Latin model, where prepositions cannot be stranded; the word *preposition* ‘placed before’ |
| Two negatives make a positive (✗ *I didn't see nothing*) | Lowth (1762): two negatives "destroy one another" | logic and the model of mathematics; most dialects and older English (Chaucer) use multiple negation for emphasis |
| *Shall* for the future in the first person, *will* in the second and third | John Wallis's grammar, in Latin (1653) | a description of one variety, later taught as a rule |
| Do not split an infinitive (*to boldly go*) | an anonymous letter of 1834 and Victorian usage writers | the Latin model, where the infinitive is one word |
| *You was* is wrong | Lowth (1762), condemning writers who used it | agreement with the plural form; *you was* for one person had been common in good eighteenth-century writing |
:::

::: proof
These rules were statements about what the authors preferred, not about what English speakers, even the best writers, did. Lowth himself made this clear in the case of the preposition, writing that the construction "prevails in common conversation, and suits very well with the familiar style in writing" but that the preposition before the relative is "more graceful" in the "solemn and elevated Style". In later grammars and in schools, his preference hardened into a rule. Modern descriptive grammars (Quirk et al. 1985; Huddleston & Pullum 2002) and usage guides based on evidence (Swan, *Practical English Usage*) show that stranded prepositions, split infinitives and *will* in the first person are normal in standard English, while multiple negation is standard in many dialects but not in standard written English. Some of the eighteenth-century rules thus became real norms of the written standard (single negation), while others never described anyone's English (no split infinitives). The difference matters for a learner, as the warning below explains.
:::

::: warning Zombie rules
✗ *Never end a sentence with a preposition.* ✗ *Never split an infinitive.* ✗ *Never begin a sentence with* And *or* But.
✓ These are not rules of English but style preferences from eighteenth- and nineteenth-century grammarians. *What are you looking at?* is normal at every level of English; *At what are you looking?* is stiff. *She decided to gradually reduce the dose* is standard; moving *gradually* may be clumsier or ambiguous.

But not every old rule is a zombie. Single negation (*I didn't see anything*), *I was* / *you were* and *fewer* with plural nouns in formal writing are real conventions of standard English today, expected in exams and formal writing. Learn which is which from evidence-based guides ([[academic-writing/academic-style]]), not from lists of prohibitions.
:::

::: example Multiple negation before and after the grammarians {#ex-negation}
Chaucer says of the Knight in the *General Prologue* (c. 1387): *He nevere yet no vileynye ne sayde / In al his lyf unto no maner wight.* Count the negatives, translate, and explain why a grammarian of 1762 would have objected and why Chaucer's readers would not.
::: solution
There are four negative words: *nevere*, *no* (*vileynye*), *ne* (before *sayde*) and *no* (*maner wight*). Translation (made for this atlas): ‘He never yet said anything rude in all his life to any kind of person.’

For Chaucer and his readers, each negative **reinforced** the others: multiple negation was the normal way to make a strong negative statement in Middle English, as it still is in many dialects of English and in languages such as French (*ne … jamais rien*) and Spanish (*no dijo nada*). Lowth (1762) treated negatives as logical operators, so that two of them "destroy one another" — a principle from logic and mathematics, not from English usage ([[#rule-prescriptive]]). In the written standard, single negation had in fact become usual by the seventeenth century, before Lowth wrote, so his rule codified a change already under way and gave it a logical justification it did not need. Today it is a real convention of standard written English, but it is not a law of logic: *I didn't see nobody* is perfectly clear to every English speaker.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which statement describes Lowth's view of ending a sentence with a preposition?
- [ ] He said it was always a grammatical error.
- [x] He said it was natural in conversation and familiar writing, but less graceful than the alternative in solemn style.
- [ ] He said it was correct only in poetry.
- [ ] He did not discuss it.
::: solution
Lowth (1762) treated the stranded preposition as an idiom that English is "strongly inclined to", acceptable in conversation and familiar writing, and expressed a preference for the preposition before the relative in formal style. Later writers turned his preference into a prohibition.
:::

### Pronouncing dictionaries

The eighteenth century also tried to standardise pronunciation. Thomas Sheridan's *Course of Lectures on Elocution* (1762) and John Walker's *Critical Pronouncing Dictionary* (1791) taught the pronunciation of educated London as a model, and condemned provincial accents — a social judgement that became deeply rooted in Britain. The phrase *received pronunciation*, in the sense ‘accepted in polite society’, was used by Alexander Ellis in 1869; Daniel Jones's *English Pronouncing Dictionary* (1917) described the accent with phonetic precision, and Jones later adopted the name *Received Pronunciation*. Its modern successor is the General British of this atlas ([[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet#def-gb-ga]]).

## Webster and American English

Noah Webster (1758–1843), a Connecticut schoolteacher, believed that the new United States needed its own standard of English. His *Dissertations on the English Language* (1789) declared: *As an independent nation, our honor requires us to have a system of our own, in language as well as government.* His spelling book, the first part of *A Grammatical Institute of the English Language* (1783) — the "blue-backed speller" — sold in tens of millions of copies over the following century and taught generations of Americans to read. His *Compendious Dictionary* (1806) and above all his *American Dictionary of the English Language* (1828), with about 70,000 entries, established American spellings and included American words.

::: theorem Webster's spellings: what survived {#rule-webster}
Webster proposed many spelling reforms; American English adopted those that were **simple**, **systematic** and already **in use** by some writers:

1. ***-or*** for ***-our***: *color*, *honor*, *labor* (both spellings existed in eighteenth-century Britain; Johnson wrote *honour* but *error* and *horror*);
2. ***-er*** for ***-re***: *center*, *theater*, *meter*;
3. ***-se*** for ***-ce*** in some nouns: *defense*, *offense*, *license*;
4. a **single consonant** before an ending on unstressed syllables: *traveler*, *canceled* (British *traveller*, *cancelled*);
5. dropping the final *k* of *-ick*: *music*, *public* (a change that British English also made).

More radical proposals, such as *tung* for *tongue* and *wimmen* for *women*, which appeared in some of his works, were rejected and dropped from his own later dictionaries.
:::

::: proof
The pattern of success and failure shows how spelling reform works in a language without an academy. Changes that affected a whole class of words by a simple rule, and that had precedents in existing usage, could be adopted by printers and schools; changes to single common words, which made familiar words look strange, were not. Webster's authority came from the commercial success of his speller and dictionary, not from any office. The result is the set of differences that British and American spelling still show ([[world-englishes/british-american]]). Note also *-ize*: it is not an Americanism. Both *-ize* and *-ise* are used in British English, and the Oxford dictionaries and Oxford University Press prefer *-ize* ("Oxford spelling"), on the ground that the suffix comes from Greek *-izein*.
:::

::: example British or American? {#ex-spelling}
Say whether each spelling is British, American or both, and give the other form if there is one: *colour*, *center*, *realize*, *traveller*, *defence*, *music*, *program* (computer), *catalogue*.
::: solution
- *colour* — British; American *color* ([[#rule-webster]], 1).
- *center* — American; British *centre* (2).
- *realize* — both: American, and British "Oxford spelling"; British also *realise*.
- *traveller* — British; American *traveler* (4).
- *defence* — British; American *defense* (3).
- *music* — both; *musick* is obsolete everywhere (5).
- *program* — both, for a computer program; British *programme* for a television programme or a plan.
- *catalogue* — British and also American; American *catalog* is common, and was one of the shorter spellings Webster favoured.

The pattern: most differences affect whole classes of words and go back to Webster's choices of 1806–1828; a few, like *program*, have their own histories.
:::
:::

## The *Oxford English Dictionary*

In November 1857 the clergyman and scholar Richard Chenevix Trench read two papers to the Philological Society in London, *On Some Deficiencies in Our English Dictionaries*. He argued that a dictionary should be "an inventory of the language": it should record every word, good or bad, with its history shown by dated quotations — not choose the words its author approved. The Society resolved to make such a dictionary. After false starts, the Scottish schoolmaster and philologist **James Murray** became editor in 1879, and the first part (*A–Ant*) of *A New English Dictionary on Historical Principles* appeared in 1884. Murray died in 1915, at work on the letter *T*; the editors Henry Bradley, William Craigie and C. T. Onions completed the dictionary in 1928. Reissued as the *Oxford English Dictionary* in 1933 with a supplement, it recorded over 400,000 words and phrases, illustrated by about 1.8 million quotations, many sent in by volunteer readers on paper slips. A second edition followed in 1989, an online edition in 2000, and a full revision, the third edition, is in progress.

::: theorem The principles of a historical dictionary {#rule-historical}
1. **Every word in use** is included, whatever its register, within stated limits (the *OED* excluded most specialised technical terms and dialect words at first).
2. **Each sense is documented** by dated quotations from real texts, from its first recorded use to the latest (or to its disappearance).
3. **Senses are ordered historically**, the earliest first, so the entry shows how the meaning developed.
4. **The dictionary describes**: it may label a use as *rare*, *colloquial* or *disputed*, but it does not decide what is correct.
:::

The descriptive principle had a long and not always comfortable future. When Merriam-Webster published *Webster's Third New International Dictionary* in 1961, with fewer usage labels and entries for words such as *ain't*, many reviewers attacked it for abandoning standards. The quarrel showed that dictionary users often want a judge, while lexicographers had become recorders. Learner's dictionaries meet both needs: A. S. Hornby's *Idiomatic and Syntactic English Dictionary* (Tokyo, 1942), reissued by Oxford in 1948 as the *Advanced Learner's Dictionary*, described the grammar of words for foreign learners, and the *Collins COBUILD English Language Dictionary* (1987) was the first to be based systematically on a large computer corpus of real usage ([[collocation-and-register/using-a-corpus]]).

::: example Reading an entry on historical principles {#ex-entry}
A historical dictionary gives these senses of *nice*, earliest first (simplified): (1) ‘foolish, ignorant’ (from about 1300); (2) ‘fussy, fastidious’ (fourteenth century); (3) ‘precise, subtle’ (*a nice distinction*, sixteenth century); (4) ‘agreeable, pleasant’ (eighteenth century). Its source is Old French *nice* ‘silly, simple’, from Latin *nescius* ‘ignorant’. Explain how the order of senses helps a reader, and which senses a learner needs.
::: solution
The historical order shows a chain of meaning: ‘ignorant’ → ‘foolish’ → ‘fussy about small things’ → ‘attentive to small differences, precise’ → ‘pleasing’ (perhaps through ‘refined, delicate’). Each sense grows from the one before, and the Latin *ne-* ‘not’ + *scire* ‘know’ explains the start. For a historian, the order is the point of the dictionary. For a learner, it is a problem: the commonest modern sense comes last. That is why learner's dictionaries order senses by **frequency**, not by date. A learner needs sense (4) for everyday English and sense (3) for formal and academic English (*a nice point*, *a nice balance*); senses (1) and (2) appear only in older literature. The example also shows why *nice* is not "wrong" in its modern sense, as some usage writers once claimed: every sense of a word was new once.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
China's dictionaries and standards have a very different history. The 《康熙字典》 (1716) was compiled by order of the Kangxi Emperor; in the People's Republic, standards are set by the state — the promotion of 普通话 from 1956, the simplification of characters (汉字简化方案, 1956) and the 汉语拼音方案 (1958) — and the 《现代汉语词典》, edited by the Institute of Linguistics of the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, carries semi-official authority. English has nothing of this kind. No body decides English spelling or grammar; the standard is the consensus of publishers, dictionary-makers, schools and examiners, and it differs between countries. For a learner, this means that "Is it correct?" often needs the reply "In which variety, and in which register?" — and that the best authority is a good modern dictionary and an evidence-based usage guide. The first English–Chinese dictionary, by the missionary Robert Morrison (*A Dictionary of the Chinese Language*, Macau, 1815–1823), was itself an act of description by an outsider: it recorded the usage of others rather than laying down rules.
:::

::: history
The *Oxford English Dictionary* was made by hand. Murray built a corrugated-iron shed in his garden in Oxford, which he called the Scriptorium, and lined it with pigeon-holes for the slips sent in by volunteers — over a thousand readers in Britain, America and the colonies, who read assigned books and copied out sentences that used words in ways worth recording. One of the most productive was W. C. Minor, an American surgeon confined to Broadmoor asylum after killing a man. Murray reckoned that the dictionary would take ten years; it took more than seventy. Its method — the systematic collection of real examples — anticipated the corpus linguistics of the twentieth century, in which the slips became computer files of billions of words.
:::

## Where this leads

By 1800, English had a standard written form, a body of grammars and dictionaries, and two national centres, London and the United States. Its spread over the next two centuries is told in [[history-of-english/spread-of-english]], and the role of dictionaries and usage guides today in [[history-of-english/english-today]]. Using a modern dictionary is taught in [[core-vocabulary/using-a-dictionary]]; descriptive and prescriptive statements are distinguished in [[linguistics/what-linguistics-studies]]; British and American differences are set out in [[world-englishes/british-american]].

::: summary
- The first monolingual English dictionaries (Cawdrey 1604 and successors) explained **hard words**; Bailey (1721) was the first widely used **general** dictionary ([[#def-dictionaries]]).
- Writers such as Swift (1712) wanted an academy to **ascertain** English; none was founded, and English still has no official regulator ([[#rule-ascertain]]).
- **Johnson's *Dictionary*** (1755) defined about 42,000 words with quotations; its Preface concluded that no dictionary can fix a living language.
- Eighteenth-century grammarians (Lowth 1762, Murray 1795) laid down rules from Latin, logic and taste; some became real norms of the written standard, others are **zombie rules** ([[#rule-prescriptive]]).
- **Webster** (1783–1828) gave American English its own spellings; the systematic ones survived (*color*, *center*, *traveler*) ([[#rule-webster]]).
- The ***OED*** (1884–1928) recorded every word with dated quotations, ordered historically, and made description the principle of English lexicography ([[#rule-historical]]); learner's dictionaries (1942, 1987) order senses by use and frequency.
:::

::: exercise Advise a writer {level=3}
A colleague asks you to correct this sentence for a formal report: *We need to carefully consider which suppliers we can rely on, and we didn't get no replies from two of them.* Rewrite it, change only what standard formal English requires, and explain each decision with reference to this chapter.
::: solution
Model rewrite: *We need to carefully consider which suppliers we can rely on, and we received no replies from two of them.* (Or: *… and we did not receive any replies from two of them.*)

Decisions:
1. *to carefully consider* — **kept**. The split infinitive is standard; moving the adverb (*to consider carefully*) is possible, but not required. The ban is a zombie rule ([[#rule-prescriptive]]).
2. *rely on* at the end — **kept**. *On which we can rely* is stiffer and not more correct.
3. *didn't get no replies* — **changed**. Multiple negation is not used in standard written English; this is a real convention. *Received no* or *did not receive any* also suits the formal register better than *get*, and the contraction *didn't* is usually avoided in a formal report ([[academic-writing/academic-style]]).

A good editor changes what the register and the standard require and leaves the rest alone.
