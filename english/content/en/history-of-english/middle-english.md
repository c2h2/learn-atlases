These are the opening lines of Geoffrey Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales*, written in London in the late 1380s. The text follows the spelling of W. W. Skeat's edition (1894), which is based on the Ellesmere manuscript of the early fifteenth century:

> *Whan that Aprille with his shoures sote*
> *The droghte of Marche hath perced to the rote,*
> *And bathed every veyne in swich licour,*
> *Of which vertu engendred is the flour;*
> *Whan Zephirus eek with his swete breeth*
> *Inspired hath in every holt and heeth*
> *The tendre croppes, and the yonge sonne*
> *Hath in the Ram his halfe cours y-ronne,*
> *And smale fowles maken melodye,*
> *That slepen al the night with open yë,*
> *(So priketh hem nature in hir corages):*
> *Than longen folk to goon on pilgrimages,*
> *(And palmers for to seken straunge strondes)*
> *To ferne halwes, couthe in sondry londes;*
> *And specially, from every shires ende*
> *Of Engelond, to Caunterbury they wende,*
> *The holy blisful martir for to seke,*
> *That hem hath holpen, whan that they were seke.*

A translation made for this atlas: ‘When April with its sweet showers has pierced the drought of March to the root, and bathed every vein in such moisture as has the power to bring forth the flower; when Zephyrus, the west wind, too, with his sweet breath has breathed life into the tender shoots in every wood and heath, and the young sun has run his half-course in the Ram, and little birds make melody that sleep all night with open eye (so nature spurs them in their hearts) — then people long to go on pilgrimages, and pilgrims to seek foreign shores, to far-off shrines, known in various lands; and especially, from every shire's end of England, to Canterbury they go, to seek the holy blessed martyr who has helped them when they were sick.’

Compare this with the *Beowulf* of [[history-of-english/old-english#ex-beowulf]]. A modern reader needs a glossary for Chaucer, but not a grammar: the word order is mostly modern, the case endings have nearly gone, and most words are recognisable, many of them French (*licour*, *vertu*, *engendred*, *inspired*, *tendre*, *melodye*, *nature*, *corages*, *pilgrimages*, *straunge*, *martir*). In three centuries, English had changed more than in any period before or since. This chapter explains how English returned to the uses that French and Latin had taken from it, how its grammar was simplified, how different its dialects were, and how to read Chaucer aloud.

## The return of English

::: definition Middle English {#def-middle-english}
**Middle English** (中古英语) is the English language from about 1100 to about 1500 (Baugh & Cable: 1150–1500). It is the period in which English lost most of its inflections, took in thousands of French words, existed in strongly differing regional dialects with no single written standard, and returned to official and literary use. Its end is conventionally marked by the introduction of printing (1476) and by the changes in pronunciation described in [[history-of-english/great-vowel-shift]].
:::

English never stopped being spoken; what it lost after 1066 was **status** — use by the powerful and in writing ([[history-of-english/norman-french]]). Its return was gradual, and historians mark it by a series of dated events.

::: theorem Stages in the return of English {#rule-return}
1. **1204**: King John loses Normandy to the king of France. The nobility of England increasingly has to choose between its English and its French lands, and its identity becomes English.
2. **1258**: Henry III issues a proclamation confirming the Provisions of Oxford in English as well as French — the first royal document in English since the Conquest.
3. **1337–1453**: the Hundred Years' War between England and France makes French the language of the enemy, although it remains the language of culture.
4. **1348–1350**: the Black Death kills perhaps a third or more of the population. The labour shortage that follows raises the economic and social position of the English-speaking classes.
5. **1362**: the Statute of Pleading orders that cases in the law courts be pleaded in English (the records are still kept in Latin, and the statute itself is in French).
6. **1385**: John Trevisa, translating a Latin chronicle, notes that since the Black Death schoolmasters have taught boys to construe Latin into English rather than French, and that now "in al þe gramerscoles of Engelond childern leveþ Frensch and construeþ and lurneþ an Englysch" (‘in all the grammar schools of England, children leave French and construe and learn in English’).
7. **1399**: Henry IV claims the throne in a speech in English, and from **1417** Henry V uses English in his letters. From the **1420s** and **1430s**, English replaces French in the records of London guilds and in the documents of the royal Chancery.
:::

::: proof
These dates come from surviving documents, which are the evidence; they do not mark sudden changes. Each event records a shift already under way in the usage of particular groups: the royal administration, lawyers, schools, merchants. Two qualifications matter. First, French stayed a language of high culture, of some law and of correspondence well into the fifteenth century, and "Law French" survived in the courts until the eighteenth. Second, the main competitor of English in writing was Latin as much as French: most records were in Latin, and English replaced both. The political background — the loss of Normandy, the war, the plague — explains why the English-speaking majority and the English identity of the ruling class gained ground at the same time.
:::

## A simpler grammar

The grammatical change between Old and Middle English is the largest in the history of the language. It is often called the change from a **synthetic** to an **analytic** language ([[history-of-english/old-english#def-inflection]]).

::: definition Levelling {#def-levelling}
**Levelling** is the reduction of the variety of inflectional endings, so that forms which were once distinct become identical. In late Old English and early Middle English, the unstressed vowels *a*, *o*, *u* and *e* of endings all became a single vowel, written *e* and pronounced /ə/, and final *-m* became *-n*; many endings then fell together (*-a*, *-u*, *-e* → *-e*; *-an*, *-on*, *-um* → *-en*) and later often disappeared. Distinctions of case and gender that depended on these endings were lost.
:::

The results, by the time of Chaucer:

- **Nouns** had, in most cases, only two forms in the singular and plural: *ston*, *stones* (the genitive *stones* written the same as the plural). The plural ending *-es* spread from the *stān* class to most nouns. In the south, the weak plural *-en* was for a time competing: Chaucer has *eyen* ‘eyes’ and *shoon* ‘shoes’, and many southern texts have *hosen*, *housen*, *treen*. It lost, except in *oxen*, *children* and *brethren*.
- **Grammatical gender** disappeared, replaced by **natural gender**: *he* for men, *she* for women, *it* for things. The definite article became the single uninflected *the*.
- **Adjectives** kept, in Chaucer's English, only a final *-e* in the plural and in the "weak" position after a determiner: *the yonge sonne*, *smale fowles*. By 1500 this too had gone.
- **Word order** became the main signal of grammatical relations: subject–verb–object in statements, with prepositions (*of*, *to*, *by*, *with*) doing the work of the old cases: Old English *þǣm biscope* became *to the bishop*.
- **Verbs** kept the most inflections: *I singe*, *thou singest*, *he singeth*, *we singen*; past *song*, participle *(y)songen*. Many strong verbs became weak (*helpen*, *helped* beside older *holpen*). New patterns with auxiliaries grew in frequency: the perfect with *have*, the future with *shal* and *wol*, the passive with *be*.

::: theorem Why the endings went {#rule-endings}
The loss of inflections had a phonetic cause and a structural consequence, with contact as an accelerating factor:

1. **Stress.** Germanic stress on the first syllable of the root left endings unstressed, and unstressed vowels weakened to /ə/ and were lost ([[history-of-english/english-and-its-relatives#def-germanic]]).
2. **Function.** As endings became identical, they no longer showed case; word order and prepositions took over, which made the endings redundant and easier to lose.
3. **Contact.** In the north and east, contact with Old Norse probably accelerated the change ([[history-of-english/vikings-norse#rule-contact]]), and the levelled northern forms spread south.
:::

::: proof
The order of events supports this account. The weakening of unstressed vowels is visible in late Old English spellings that confuse the endings (*-an* written for *-on*, *-um* for *-an*); northern texts show the loss earliest and southern ones latest; and fixed word order becomes more frequent as the endings disappear. Which came first — fixed word order, making endings redundant, or lost endings, forcing fixed order — is debated, and the answer is probably that each reinforced the other over several generations. French had no part in the loss of English endings: the change was well advanced before most French words arrived, and it was fastest in areas of little French influence.
:::

::: example Old English and Middle English compared {#ex-compare}
Here is the beginning of the Lord's Prayer in a Wycliffite translation of the 1380s (the earlier version of the first complete English Bible), beside the Old English of [[history-of-english/old-english]]:

> Old English: *Fæder ure þu þe eart on heofonum, si þin nama gehalgod. To becume þin rice.*
> Middle English: *Oure fadir that art in heuenes, halewid be thi name; thi kyngdoom come to.*

(*u* in the manuscript spelling *heuenes* is the letter *v*.) Identify three grammatical changes and two changes of vocabulary.
::: solution
**Grammar.**
1. *heofonum*, dative plural with *-um*, has become *heuenes*: the ending *-es* now marks only the plural, and the preposition *in* alone shows the place.
2. The relative *þe* has been replaced by *that* (*that art*), a demonstrative turned relative.
3. Word order: the possessive now comes before the noun (*oure fadir* for *fæder ure*), the modern order.
4. The participle has lost its prefix: Old English *gehalgod*, Middle English *halewid* (the *ge-* had weakened to *y-* and then disappeared in this dialect).

**Vocabulary.**
1. *rice* ‘kingdom’ has been replaced by *kyngdoom*, a native compound.
2. *si* ‘be’ (a subjunctive of *be*) has been replaced by *be*.

Spelling has changed too: *þ* is giving way to *th* (*that*, *thi*), and *u*/*v* is a single letter. The Middle English sentence can almost be read as modern English; the Old English one cannot.
:::
:::

## Dialects

With no national written standard, scribes wrote the English of their own region, and Middle English texts show the dialects more clearly than those of any later period. Scholars usually distinguish five main areas: **Northern** (north of the Humber, including Lowland Scotland, where it developed into Scots), **East Midland**, **West Midland**, **Southern** and **Kentish**.

| feature | Northern | Midland | Southern |
|---|---|---|---|
| ‘he sings’ | *he singes* | *he singeth* (*-es* in parts) | *he singeth* |
| ‘they sing’ | *thai singes* | *thei singen* | *hi singeth* |
| ‘they’ | *thai* | *thei*, *they* | *hi*, *heo* |
| ‘she’ | *scho* | *she*, *sche* | *heo*, *ho* |
| ‘I’ | *I*, *ik* | *I* | *ich*, *I* |
| ‘stone’, ‘home’ | *stane*, *hame* | *stoon*, *hoom* | *stoon*, *hoom* |
| ‘fox’, ‘fat’ | *fox* | *fox* | *vox*, *vat* (initial *f* voiced) |
| ‘singing’ (participle) | *singand* | *singende*, *singinge* | *singinde* |

Modern English shows traces of the variation. *Vixen* ‘female fox’ and *vat* are southern forms with voiced *v*; the Scots *hame* and *stane* keep the northern vowel; and the standard language mixes forms from different areas — northern *they*, Midland *she* and *-en*-less plurals, northern *-s* in the third person (later — [[history-of-english/early-modern-english]]).

The northern speech was hard for southerners to follow. Trevisa (1385) wrote that "Al þe longage of þe Norþhumbres, and specialych at ȝork, ys so scharp, slyttyng, and frotyng, and unschape, þat we Souþeron men may þat longage unneþe undurstonde" (‘All the language of the Northumbrians, and especially at York, is so sharp, piercing and grating, and unformed, that we southern men can hardly understand that language’).

::: example Eggs or eyren? {#ex-eggs}
In the prologue to his translation *Eneydos* (1490), the printer William Caxton tells a story about merchants sailing down the Thames who stopped in Kent and asked a woman for food:

> *And specyally he axyd after eggys. And the good wyf answerde that she coude speke no frenshe. And the marchaunt was angry, for he also coude speke no frenshe, but wolde haue hadde egges; and she vnderstode hym not. And thenne at laste a nother sayd that he wolde haue eyren. Then the good wyf sayd that she vnderstod hym wel.*

Translate the passage and explain the misunderstanding from what you know of Norse and native forms.
::: solution
Translation (made for this atlas): ‘And in particular he asked for eggs. And the good woman answered that she could speak no French. And the merchant was angry, for he could not speak French either, but wanted to have eggs; and she did not understand him. And then at last another said that he wanted *eyren*. Then the good woman said that she understood him well.’

*Egges* is the Norse form ([[history-of-english/vikings-norse]]), which had spread from the north and east into the speech of the merchant, probably a Londoner; *eyren* is the native southern plural of *ey* (Old English *ǣg*, plural *ǣgru*), still used in Kent. To the Kentish woman, an unfamiliar word for an everyday thing sounded foreign, so she assumed it was French. (*Axyd* is the old form of *ask*, from Old English *āxian* beside *āscian*, which survives in many dialects today.) Caxton tells the story to explain his own problem as a printer: "Loo, what sholde a man in thyse dayes now wryte, egges or eyren?" Which dialect should a book for the whole country use? That question leads to the standard language of [[history-of-english/early-modern-english]].
:::
:::

::: quiz
In a Middle English text you find *scho singes* ‘she sings’ and *stane* ‘stone’. Where was it most probably written?
- [x] In the north of England or Scotland
- [ ] In London
- [ ] In Kent
- [ ] In the south-west
::: solution
*Scho*, the third-person *-es* and the vowel *a* in *stane* (Old English *ā* kept unrounded) are all Northern features. A London text would have *she singeth* and *stoon*; a southern or Kentish one *heo singeth*.
:::
:::

::: example Locating a text by its forms {#ex-locate}
The three sentences below were written for this atlas, each in the forms of one dialect area (they are not quotations). They all mean ‘They go home, and she sings’. Locate each, and say which forms decide.

1. *Thai gas hame, and scho singes.*
2. *Hi goth hoom, and heo singeth.*
3. *Thei goon hoom, and she singeth.*
::: solution
1. **Northern**: *thai* (the Norse pronoun in its northern form), plural *gas* with *-s*, *hame* with unrounded *a*, *scho*, third-person *-es*. Every form points north.
2. **Southern**: *hi* (the native pronoun), plural *goth* with *-th*, *hoom* with rounded *o*, *heo*, *-eth*.
3. **Midland**, probably East Midland or London: *thei* (Norse, arrived from the north), plural *goon* with *-n*, *hoom*, *she*, *-eth*.

The method is the one used by the *Linguistic Atlas of Late Mediaeval English*: no single form locates a text, because forms spread and scribes copied each other's spellings, but a **combination** of forms that overlap in only one area does. Notice that the third sentence is the nearest to modern Standard English — except for *goon*, which later lost its ending (*go*), and *singeth*, which gave way to the northern *sings*.
:::
:::

## Reading Chaucer aloud

Geoffrey Chaucer (c. 1343–1400) was the son of a London wine merchant and spent his life in royal service — as a page, a soldier, a diplomat in France and Italy, a customs official and a clerk of the king's works. He wrote in the English of London, which in his time had absorbed many features of the East Midlands. His major works include *Troilus and Criseyde* (mid-1380s) and the *Canterbury Tales*, a collection of stories told by pilgrims on the road to the shrine of Thomas Becket at Canterbury, begun around 1387 and unfinished at his death.

::: theorem Pronouncing Chaucer's English {#rule-chaucer}
Chaucer's pronunciation can be reconstructed in outline from spelling, rhyme, metre and later developments. As a guide:

1. **Long vowels have their pre-shift values** ([[history-of-english/great-vowel-shift]]): *i*/*y* is /iː/ (*lyf* ‘life’ /liːf/), *ee* /eː/ or /ɛː/ (*swete* /ˈsweːtə/, *breeth* /brɛːθ/), *a* /aː/ (*name* /ˈnaːmə/), *oo* /oː/ or /ɔː/ (*sote* /ˈsoːtə/, *goon* /ɡɔːn/), *ou*/*ow* /uː/ (*shoures* /ˈʃuːrəs/, *flour* /fluːr/).
2. **Final *-e* is pronounced** as /ə/ where the metre needs it, and usually elided before a vowel or *h*: *sote*, *rote*, *swete* have two syllables.
3. **Consonants are all pronounced**: the *k* of *knight*, the *gh* of *night* and *droghte* (/x/ or /ç/, as in German), the *w* of *write*, the *l* of *half*; *r* is trilled or tapped, and pronounced after vowels.
4. **French words keep French stress**, at least sometimes: *naTURE*, *coRAges*, *meloDYE*.
5. **Metre**: most lines of the *Tales* have ten syllables, or eleven with an unstressed final syllable, alternating unstressed and stressed (iambic pentameter); rhyme confirms the pronunciation of the final words.
:::

::: proof
The evidence is of four kinds. **Spelling** before the vowel shift was close to the continental values of the Latin alphabet, as in [[history-of-english/old-english#rule-oe-spelling]]. **Rhyme** shows which vowels were identical: Chaucer rhymes *sote* with *rote* and *seke* ‘seek’ with *seke* ‘sick’, a rhyme of identical sounds with different meanings that he and his contemporaries admired. **Metre** shows where final *-e* counts: *Whan that Aprille with his shoures sote* has eleven syllables only if *Aprille*, *shoures* and *sote* keep their final vowels. And the **later history** of each vowel, as recorded by sixteenth- and seventeenth-century writers on pronunciation, works back to these values. The reconstruction is an approximation — the exact qualities of the vowels, and how consistently final *-e* was pronounced in speech rather than verse, are debated — but its outline is secure.
:::

::: example Scanning and sounding two lines {#ex-scan}
Mark the stressed syllables in the first two lines of the *General Prologue*, count the syllables, and give a rough reconstructed pronunciation.
::: solution
Stress (capitals) and syllables:

*Whan THAT a-PRIL-le WITH his SHOU-res SO-te* — 11 syllables: five stresses, alternating, with an unstressed eleventh syllable (a "feminine ending").

*The DROGH-te OF Mar-CHE hath PER-ced TO the RO-te* — the line has 11 syllables if *Marche* keeps its *-e*; many readers elide it (*Marche hath* → *March' hath*), giving the regular count. Either way there are five stresses, on *DROGH*, *OF* (lightly), *MARCH*, *PER* and *RO*.

A rough reconstruction (GB-style symbols): /ʍan θat aˈprilə wiθ his ˈʃuːrəs ˈsoːtə/ — /ðə ˈdruxtə ɔf ˈmartʃ(ə) haθ ˈpɛrsəd toː ðə ˈroːtə/.

Notice what changes compared with modern English: *shoures* has /uː/ (modern *showers* /aʊ/), *sote* and *rote* /oː/ (modern *sweet* and *root* are different words or vowels), *droghte* keeps its /x/, and the final *-e*s make the line longer and more musical. The *wh-* of *whan* was probably a voiceless [ʍ], as it still is in Scottish English.
:::
:::

::: warning Reading Chaucer with modern vowels
✗ Reading *sote*, *rote* and *flour* with the modern vowels of *note*, *rote* and *flower*, and dropping every final *-e*: the lines lose their syllables and their rhymes.
✓ Read the long vowels with their "continental" values, pronounce the final *-e* where the line needs a syllable, and pronounce every consonant.

The opposite mistake is to treat the reconstruction as certain. Recordings of Chaucer by specialists differ in detail; what they agree on is that his English sounded much more like the vowels of German or Italian than like any modern English accent.
:::

## Toward a standard

By Chaucer's death, London English was becoming the basis of a written standard, for reasons of population and power: London was the largest city, the seat of the court and of government, and the destination of migrants. The English of London itself had changed: in the thirteenth century it had many Southern and Essex features, but in the fourteenth, with heavy migration from the East Midlands and later the Central Midlands, it took on Midland forms. In an influential article of 1963, M. L. Samuels distinguished several "types" of fifteenth-century written English, of which the latest, the language of documents produced by the royal **Chancery** from the 1430s, he called **Chancery Standard** and saw as a main source of later standard spelling. More recent research (by Michael Benskin and Laura Wright among others) has questioned whether Chancery documents were as uniform or as influential as Samuels suggested, and stresses the many writing offices, merchants and printers who contributed. The standard emerged from a convergence over a century, completed by printing, not from a single office.

::: note English and Chinese
The situation of Middle English is easy to picture for a Chinese reader. There was no single "correct" English: each region spoke and wrote its own, and speakers from the north and south could understand each other only with difficulty, as speakers of different Chinese varieties may. The written standard that later emerged was based on the variety of the capital and the centre of government, as 官话 and later 普通话 were based on the speech of Beijing and the north. There are also differences. Chinese characters allowed a written language to be read across dialects for two thousand years, while English writing, being alphabetic, recorded each dialect's sounds, so a national written standard had to be built anew. And English standardisation was not decided by any government or academy: it happened through the practice of scribes, merchants and printers ([[history-of-english/dictionaries-grammars]]).
:::

::: history
Chaucer was admired in his own lifetime and immediately after: his follower Thomas Hoccleve called him his "maister", and William Caxton printed the *Canterbury Tales* around 1476, one of the first books from his Westminster press, and again around 1483. John Dryden, in the preface to his *Fables* (1700), called him "the father of English poetry" while believing, wrongly, that his verse was irregular — because by then the final *-e* had long been silent, and readers could no longer hear Chaucer's metre. The recovery of Chaucer's pronunciation was one of the achievements of nineteenth-century philology: Alexander Ellis and Henry Sweet worked on Middle English sounds, F. J. Furnivall's Chaucer Society (founded 1868) printed the manuscripts, and W. W. Skeat's *Complete Works* (1894) became the standard edition for decades. The mapping of Middle English dialects culminated in the *Linguistic Atlas of Late Mediaeval English* (1986), edited by Angus McIntosh, M. L. Samuels and Michael Benskin, which located over a thousand texts by their linguistic forms alone.
:::

## Where this leads

Middle English ends with two changes: the Great Vowel Shift, which changed the long vowels just as spelling was becoming fixed ([[history-of-english/great-vowel-shift]]), and printing, which spread the London standard ([[history-of-english/early-modern-english]]). The metre of Chaucer's verse, iambic pentameter, is explained in [[reading-literature/metre-rhythm]], and the literary periods in [[reading-literature/literary-periods]]. How dialects differ and change is the subject of [[linguistics/variation-change]].

::: summary
- **Middle English** (c. 1100–1500) is the period of levelled inflections, mass borrowing from French, strong dialect differences and the return of English to official use ([[#def-middle-english]]).
- English returned in stages, from the loss of Normandy (1204) and the proclamation of 1258 to the Statute of Pleading (1362), English in schools (by 1385) and Chancery English (1430s) ([[#rule-return]]).
- Unstressed endings weakened and merged (**levelling**), gender gave way to natural gender, *-es* plurals spread, and word order and prepositions took over the work of case ([[#def-levelling]], [[#rule-endings]]).
- Dialects differed strongly: Northern *scho singes*, *stane*; Southern *heo singeth*, *stoon*, *vox*; the standard language mixed features from several areas.
- Chaucer's London English is read with pre-shift long vowels, pronounced final *-e* and all consonants ([[#rule-chaucer]]).
- A written standard grew from London English in the fifteenth century; the role of the Chancery is debated.
:::

Chaucer writes *eyen* ‘eyes’ and *shoon* ‘shoes’, but modern English has *eyes* and *shoes*. Explain, and give two modern words that keep the older type.

::: exercise Read the evidence of rhyme {level=2}
Chaucer rhymes *sote* with *rote* and *seke* ‘seek’ with *seke* ‘sick’. What do these rhymes tell us about his pronunciation, compared with modern English?
::: solution
In modern English, *sweet* and *root* (the descendants of *sote* and *rote*, although *sweet* comes from a related form) do not rhyme, and *seek* and *sick* have different vowels. In Chaucer's English, *sote* and *rote* had the same long vowel /oː/ and ending /ə/, and *seke* ‘seek’ and *seke* ‘sick’ were identical in sound (/ˈseːkə/). Since then, the vowel of *sick* has been shortened and the vowel of *seek* raised by the Great Vowel Shift, so the two words have separated. Rhymes show which sounds a poet treated as identical, and so they are evidence for reconstruction ([[#rule-chaucer]]).
:::

::: exercise Translate and comment {level=3}
Translate lines 9–12 of the *General Prologue* (from *And smale fowles* to *pilgrimages*) into modern English prose, and then list three features of Middle English grammar that they show.
::: hint
Look at *smale*, *maken*, *slepen*, *priketh hem*, *hir* and *longen … to goon*.
:::
::: solution
Translation: ‘And little birds sing melodies, birds that sleep all night with their eyes open (so nature spurs them in their hearts): then people long to go on pilgrimages.’

Features (any three):
1. **Plural adjective in *-e***: *smale fowles*, the remnant of adjective inflection.
2. **Plural verbs in *-en***: *maken*, *slepen*, *longen* (the Midland present plural).
3. **Infinitive in *-n***: *goon* ‘to go’.
4. **Third-person singular *-eth***: *priketh*.
5. **Native object and possessive pronouns** *hem* ‘them’ and *hir* ‘their’, beside the Norse subject *they* elsewhere in the passage.
6. **Subject after the verb** in *Than longen folk* (‘then long people’), an inversion after an opening adverb, as in Old English verb-second order.
:::

::: exercise Recite and explain {level=3}
Learn the first four lines of the *General Prologue* by heart and record yourself reading them with the reconstructed pronunciation of [[#rule-chaucer]]. Then write 80–120 words explaining two differences between your reading and a reading with modern pronunciation, and how we know about them.
::: solution
A model explanation: "In my reading, *shoures* has the vowel /uː/ and *sote* and *rote* the vowel /oː/, as in Chaucer's time, while in modern English the descendants of these words have /aʊ/ (*showers*) and /iː/ or /uː/ (*sweet*, *root*). We know the older values because the spelling followed continental values before the Great Vowel Shift, because Chaucer's rhymes pair words that had the same vowel then, and because writers on pronunciation in the sixteenth century describe the stages of change. I also pronounced the final *-e* in *Aprille*, *sote* and *rote*, which makes the first line eleven syllables long; the metre shows that the *-e* was counted in verse, although it is silent today." A good recording keeps the rhythm of five stresses per line and pronounces the *gh* of *droghte*.
