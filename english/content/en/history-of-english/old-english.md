Here is the Lord's Prayer as it was written in England around the year 1000, in the West Saxon translation of the Gospels (Matthew 6:9–13). The spelling is that of the manuscripts, with the old letters *þ* and *ð* (both ‘th’) and *æ* (the vowel of *cat*):

> *Fæder ure þu þe eart on heofonum, si þin nama gehalgod. To becume þin rice. Gewurþe ðin willa on eorðan swa swa on heofonum. Urne gedæghwamlican hlaf syle us to dæg. And forgyf us ure gyltas, swa swa we forgyfað urum gyltendum. And ne gelæd þu us on costnunge, ac alys us of yfele. Soþlice.*

A word-for-word translation made for this atlas: ‘Father our, thou that art in heavens, be thy name hallowed. Come thy kingdom. Be done thy will on earth as in heavens. Our daily loaf give us today. And forgive us our guilts, as we forgive our guilty ones. And do not lead thou us into temptation, but release us from evil. Truly.’

At first sight the passage looks foreign. Look again and many words appear: *fæder* ‘father’, *ure* ‘our’, *þu* ‘thou’, *eart* ‘art’, *on*, *þin* ‘thine, thy’, *nama* ‘name’, *willa* ‘will’, *eorðan* ‘earth’, *swa* ‘so’, *hlaf* ‘loaf’ (here ‘bread’), *us*, *to dæg* ‘today’, *and*, *forgyf* ‘forgive’, *we*, *gyltas* ‘guilts’, *of*. Other words are lost (*rice* ‘kingdom’, still in German *Reich*; *syle* ‘give’, the ancestor of *sell*; *ac* ‘but’), and the endings — *heofon-um*, *eorð-an*, *gylt-as*, *gylt-end-um* — show a grammar that English has largely given up.

This is **Old English**, the language of England from the settlements of the fifth century to the generations after the Norman Conquest. This chapter shows how to read it: its letters and sounds, its inflections, its vocabulary and its dialects, and the features of modern English — irregular plurals, strong verbs, *whom*, the possessive *'s* — that are its survivals.

## The settlements

::: definition Old English {#def-old-english}
**Old English** (古英语), also called **Anglo-Saxon**, is the English language from the arrival of Germanic settlers in Britain in the fifth century to about 1100 (Baugh & Cable date the period 450–1150). Its speakers called it *Englisc* and their land *Angelcynn* ‘the English people’ and later *Englaland* ‘land of the Angles’. It was a group of West Germanic dialects ([[history-of-english/english-and-its-relatives#def-germanic]]), close to Old Frisian and Old Saxon.
:::

Roman rule in Britain ended early in the fifth century. The Britons of the former province spoke a Celtic language, the ancestor of Welsh and Cornish, and many in the towns spoke Latin. Germanic raiders and settlers had been crossing the North Sea before then; during the fifth and sixth centuries they settled across most of what is now England. Our main written account comes three centuries later: the Northumbrian monk **Bede** finished his Latin *Ecclesiastical History of the English People* in 731. Bede names three peoples — **Angles**, **Saxons** and **Jutes**, from what are now Schleswig-Holstein, northern Germany and Jutland — and dates the first arrival to the years around 449, when, he says, a British ruler invited Germanic warriors to defend him and they turned against him. Archaeologists and historians now see a longer and more varied process: migration in many waves and on different scales, settlement beside the Britons as well as their displacement, and Britons who became English by adopting the newcomers' language. The political story of post-Roman Britain belongs to the [European History Atlas](/learn/europe/lesson.php?c=after-rome&l=successor-kingdoms); for the language, the outcome is what matters. By about 600, Germanic speech dominated most of lowland Britain, and the kingdoms that emerged — Northumbria, Mercia, East Anglia, Essex, Kent, Wessex and others — were English-speaking.

The Celtic language of the Britons left surprisingly little trace in English vocabulary: a handful of words such as *brock* ‘badger’ and *combe* ‘valley’, and many names of places and rivers — *Kent*, *London* (probably), *Thames*, *Avon* (Welsh *afon* means ‘river’, so *River Avon* is ‘river river’). Whether Brittonic speech influenced English grammar in less visible ways — for example, the later rise of the progressive (*is going*) and of *do* in questions — is a live debate, but it is not proven.

Christianity came to the English kingdoms from two directions: from Ireland and Scotland in the north, and from Rome with the mission of Augustine, who landed in Kent in 597. With it came the Latin alphabet, Latin learning and writing on parchment. Most Old English that survives was written by churchmen.

## Reading Old English

Old English was written in the Latin alphabet with some additions. Earlier, and for short inscriptions, the English used **runes** (the *futhorc*), and two runic letters passed into the manuscripts.

| letter | name | value | modern spelling |
|---|---|---|---|
| *þ* | thorn (a rune) | /θ/ or /ð/ | *th* |
| *ð* | eth | /θ/ or /ð/ (used interchangeably with *þ*) | *th* |
| *æ* | ash | /æ/ (long *ǣ* /æː/) | *a*, *ea* |
| *ƿ* | wynn (a rune) | /w/ | *w* (modern editions print *w*) |

::: theorem How Old English spelling was pronounced {#rule-oe-spelling}
Old English spelling was much closer to its pronunciation than modern spelling is. As a working guide:

1. **Every letter is pronounced.** *cniht* ‘boy, servant’ (modern *knight*) begins with /kn/; *writan* ‘write’ with /wr/; final *-e* is a vowel.
2. Vowels have their "continental" values (as in German or Italian); long vowels, written with a macron in modern textbooks (*stān*, *hūs*), are held longer: *hūs* /huːs/ ‘house’, *stān* /stɑːn/ ‘stone’, *wīf* /wiːf/ ‘woman, wife’.
3. *c* is /k/, but /tʃ/ next to front vowels in many words: *cirice* /ˈtʃiritʃe/ ‘church’, *cild* ‘child’. *sc* is /ʃ/: *scip* ‘ship’, *fisc* ‘fish’. *cg* is /dʒ/: *brycg* ‘bridge’.
4. *g* is /ɡ/, but /j/ next to front vowels in many words: *gēar* ‘year’, *dæg* ‘day’.
5. *h* is /h/ at the start of a word but a fricative like the *ch* of Scottish *loch* or German *ich* elsewhere: *niht* ‘night’, *hlāf* ‘loaf’ (with /hl/).
6. *f*, *s* and *þ*/*ð* are voiceless at the start and end of words but **voiced** between voiced sounds: *wulf* /wulf/ but *wulfas* /ˈwulvas/ ‘wolves’; *hūs* /huːs/ but *hūsian* with /z/.
:::

::: proof
The rule explains modern spellings that seem irregular. The silent *k* of *knight*, *know* and *knee*, the *w* of *write* and *wrong*, and the *gh* of *night* and *daughter* were pronounced in Old English and long after; the spelling was settled before they were lost ([[sounds-of-english/spelling-and-sound#rule-silent]]). Point 6 explains the alternations *wolf* – *wolves*, *knife* – *knives*, *house* (/s/) – *houses* (/z/) and *bath* – *bathe*: Old English had no separate phonemes /v/, /z/ and /ð/, only voiced variants of /f/, /s/ and /θ/ between vowels. The guide is approximate: our knowledge of Old English pronunciation comes from spelling, from verse, from comparison with related languages and from later developments, and specialists disagree on details such as the exact quality of the diphthongs *ea* and *eo*.
:::

::: warning *Ye olde*
✗ *Ye Olde Tea Shoppe* shows how people spoke in Old English times.
✓ The *Y* in *Ye* is a late form of the letter *þ*. Late medieval scribes wrote *þ* so that it looked like *y*, and early printers, whose type came from the Continent and had no *þ*, used *y* for it: *ye* = *þe* = *the*. Nobody ever said "yee" for *the*. Nor is *olde shoppe* Old English: it is a modern imitation of the spelling of the fifteenth to seventeenth centuries. Real Old English looks like the Lord's Prayer above.

A second confusion: Shakespeare and the King James Bible are not "Old English". Their language is **Early Modern English** ([[history-of-english/early-modern-english]]), and a modern reader can follow it with a few notes. Old English must be learned almost like a foreign language.
:::

## An inflected language

::: definition Inflection, case, grammatical gender {#def-inflection}
An **inflection** is an ending (or internal change) that marks a grammatical category of a word: number, tense, person, case. A **synthetic** or inflecting language expresses many relations by inflections; an **analytic** language expresses them by word order and separate words (prepositions, auxiliaries). Old English was largely synthetic; modern English is largely analytic.

**Case** is the marking of a noun's or pronoun's role in the sentence. Old English had four main cases: **nominative** (subject), **accusative** (direct object), **genitive** (possession and other relations, like modern *'s* or *of*) and **dative** (indirect object, and after many prepositions), with traces of a fifth, the instrumental.

**Grammatical gender** is a classification of nouns that decides the forms of the words agreeing with them. Old English nouns were masculine, feminine or neuter, and the gender was largely arbitrary: *wīf* ‘woman’ was neuter, *wīfmann* ‘woman’ masculine (because its second part, *mann*, was), *stān* ‘stone’ masculine, *sunne* ‘sun’ feminine and *mōna* ‘moon’ masculine.
:::

Nouns fell into several classes (declensions), each with its own set of endings. The two most important were the "strong" *a*-declension and the "weak" *n*-declension:

| | *stān* ‘stone’ (strong, masc.) | | *nama* ‘name’ (weak, masc.) | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | singular | plural | singular | plural |
| nominative | *stān* | *stān-as* | *nam-a* | *nam-an* |
| accusative | *stān* | *stān-as* | *nam-an* | *nam-an* |
| genitive | *stān-es* | *stān-a* | *nam-an* | *nam-ena* |
| dative | *stān-e* | *stān-um* | *nam-an* | *nam-um* |

The definite article (which also served as a demonstrative, ‘that’) was inflected too: masculine nominative *se*, accusative *þone*, genitive *þæs*, dative *þǣm*; feminine nominative *sēo*; neuter *þæt*; plural *þā*. Adjectives agreed with their nouns in gender, number and case, and had strong and weak forms.

::: theorem Case does the work of word order {#rule-case}
Because case endings show who does what, Old English word order was freer than modern word order. *Se cyning lufode þone biscop* and *Þone biscop lufode se cyning* both mean ‘The king loved the bishop’: *se* marks the subject, *þone* the object. Order was not free, however. Typical patterns were:

1. **Verb second** in main clauses: the finite verb came second, after the subject or after an adverb or object placed first — *Þā cōm hē* ‘then came he’ (compare German *Dann kam er*).
2. **Verb late** in subordinate clauses: *þā hē þone biscop geseah* ‘when he the bishop saw’.
3. **Subject–verb–object** was already the most common order in simple main clauses beginning with the subject.
:::

::: proof
The evidence is the prose of the period, which shows all three patterns frequently, together with the endings that make them unambiguous. When the endings were weakened and lost in late Old English and early Middle English ([[history-of-english/middle-english]]), SVO order took over the work of showing the subject and object; *The bishop loved the king* now means something different from *The king loved the bishop*. Modern English keeps traces of verb second after some opening adverbs (*Here comes the bus*, *Never have I seen such a thing* — [[complex-sentences/emphasis-inversion]]), and keeps case only in pronouns: *he* / *him* / *his*, *who* / *whom* / *whose*.
:::

### Verbs

Old English verbs were either **strong**, forming the past by a change of vowel (ablaut — [[history-of-english/english-and-its-relatives#rule-ablaut]]), or **weak**, forming it with a dental suffix:

| type | infinitive | past (singular) | past (plural) | past participle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| strong, class I | *rīdan* ‘ride’ | *rād* | *ridon* | *geriden* |
| strong, class III | *singan* ‘sing’ | *sang* | *sungon* | *gesungen* |
| strong, class III | *helpan* ‘help’ | *healp* | *hulpon* | *geholpen* |
| weak | *lufian* ‘love’ | *lufode* | *lufodon* | *gelufod* |
| weak | *fremman* ‘do, perform’ | *fremede* | *fremedon* | *gefremed* |

The present tense was marked for person and number: *ic singe*, *þū singst*, *hē singþ*, *wē*/*gē*/*hīe singaþ*. The ending *-þ* of the third person singular is the *-eth* of the King James Bible (*he singeth*); the modern *-s* came from the north ([[history-of-english/vikings-norse]]). The prefix *ge-* of the past participle survives in German (*gesungen*) and, in English, only as the *y-* of Chaucer's *yronne* ‘run’ and of the archaic *yclept* ‘called’.

### Pronouns

| | 1st | 2nd | 3rd masc. | 3rd fem. | 3rd neut. | plural 1st | plural 2nd | plural 3rd |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| nom. | *ic* | *þū* | *hē* | *hēo* | *hit* | *wē* | *gē* | *hīe* |
| acc. | *mē* | *þē* | *hine* | *hīe* | *hit* | *ūs* | *ēow* | *hīe* |
| gen. | *mīn* | *þīn* | *his* | *hire* | *his* | *ūre* | *ēower* | *hira* |
| dat. | *mē* | *þē* | *him* | *hire* | *him* | *ūs* | *ēow* | *him* |

Old English also had **dual** pronouns for exactly two people: *wit* ‘we two’, *git* ‘you two’. Notice that the third person plural began with *h-* (*hīe*, *hira*, *him*): the modern *they*, *their*, *them* are Norse ([[history-of-english/vikings-norse]]). Modern *him* comes from the old dative, which took over the work of the accusative *hine*; *it* is *hit* without its *h*; *its* did not exist (*his* served for both masculine and neuter — still in the King James Bible).

::: quiz
In Old English *Se hund biton þone mann* would be ungrammatical, but *Þone mann bāt se hund* is fine. Who bites whom in the second sentence?
- [x] The dog bites the man.
- [ ] The man bites the dog.
- [ ] It is ambiguous.
- [ ] Nobody bites anyone: it is a question.
::: solution
*Se* is the nominative (subject) form of the masculine article, and *þone* the accusative (object) form. So *se hund* ‘the dog’ is the subject and *þone mann* ‘the man’ the object, although the object comes first. *Bāt* is the singular past of *bītan* ‘bite’; the first sentence is wrong because *biton* is plural and the subject is singular. In modern English only word order can show the difference.
:::
:::

::: example Parsing an Old English sentence {#ex-parse}
Analyse the sentence *Se cyning sealde þǣm biscope þone stān.* (*cyning* ‘king’, *sellan*, past *sealde* ‘give’, *biscop* ‘bishop’.) Identify the case of each noun phrase, its role, and translate. Then reorder it to start with the bishop and say whether the meaning changes.
::: solution
1. *Se cyning*: *se* = masculine nominative → **subject**: ‘the king’.
2. *sealde*: past singular of *sellan* → the verb ‘gave’. (Old English *sellan* meant ‘give, hand over’; its descendant *sell* has narrowed to ‘give for money’.)
3. *þǣm biscope*: *þǣm* = dative article, *-e* = dative singular ending → **indirect object**, the receiver: ‘(to) the bishop’.
4. *þone stān*: *þone* = masculine accusative → **direct object**: ‘the stone’.

Translation: ‘The king gave the bishop the stone.’ Reordered, *Þǣm biscope sealde se cyning þone stān* still means the same: the endings, not the order, show the roles (the order would add emphasis on the bishop, roughly ‘It was to the bishop that the king gave the stone’). In modern English *The bishop gave the king the stone* reverses the meaning, because order now does the job of the endings.
:::
:::

### What survives

Most of the inflections disappeared, but several survive as the "irregularities" of modern grammar:

- **Plurals.** The modern regular *-s* is the Old English *-as* of the *stān* class, which spread to almost all nouns. The weak plural *-an* survives in *oxen* and, doubly marked, in *children* (Old English *cild*, plural *cildru*, plus *-en*); *brethren* is similar. Plurals with a vowel change — *foot* / *feet*, *goose* / *geese*, *tooth* / *teeth*, *man* / *men*, *mouse* / *mice* — come from an earlier sound change (*i*-mutation) in which an ending with *i* fronted the vowel of the root before disappearing. *Sheep* and *deer* continue neuter nouns that had no plural ending.
- **The genitive *'s*** is the Old English genitive ending *-es* of the *stān* class (*stānes*); the apostrophe is a printers' addition of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, not a sign that a letter was lost from *his*.
- **Adverbs** from case forms: *once*, *twice* and *needs* (*needs must*) are old genitives; *seldom* is an old dative plural (*seldum*); and the *the* of *the more the merrier* is not the article but the old instrumental *þȳ* ‘by that much’.
- **Pronoun case**: *he*, *him*, *his*; *who*, *whom* (the old dative *hwǣm*), *whose* (genitive *hwæs*).
- **Strong verbs**: most of the "irregular verbs" learners memorise.

## Vocabulary

The vocabulary of Old English was almost entirely Germanic. Where modern English uses a word from French or Latin, Old English usually had a native word, often a **compound** of native parts. The *Dictionary of Old English* project at the University of Toronto, whose electronic corpus contains essentially all surviving Old English, counts about three million words of running text; within that, the native resources of word-formation do most of the work that borrowing does today.

| Old English | literal meaning | modern equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| *tungolcræft* | ‘star-craft’ | astronomy |
| *lǣcecræft* | ‘physician-craft’ | medicine |
| *leorningcniht* | ‘learning-servant’ | disciple |
| *godspel* | ‘good news’ (a translation of Greek *euangelion*) | gospel |
| *hǣlend* | ‘healer, one who saves’ | Saviour |
| *þrines* | ‘three-ness’ | Trinity |
| *hlāford* (*hlāf* + *weard*) | ‘loaf-guardian’ | lord |
| *hlǣfdige* | ‘loaf-kneader’ (traditionally explained so) | lady |

Poetry added compounds of its own, **kennings** — short metaphors in place of a plain noun: *hronrād* ‘whale-road’ for the sea, *bānhūs* ‘bone-house’ for the body.

Old English did borrow, mostly from Latin, in layers that can be dated by their form and meaning:

- **On the Continent**, before the settlement of Britain, through contact with the Roman Empire: *strǣt* ‘street’ (Latin *strata*), *wīn* ‘wine’, *cīese* ‘cheese’ (*caseus*), *butere* ‘butter’, *mīl* ‘mile’, *pund* ‘pound’, *cycene* ‘kitchen’.
- **In Britain**, from the Latin of the former province: *ceaster* ‘fortified town’ (*castra*), surviving in place names in *-chester*, *-caster* and *-cester*.
- **With Christianity**, from 597 on: *biscop* ‘bishop’ (*episcopus*), *prēost* ‘priest’, *munuc* ‘monk’, *mynster* ‘minster’, *scōl* ‘school’, *candel*, *mæsse* ‘mass’, *engel* ‘angel’.

::: quiz
Old English *cīese* ‘cheese’ comes from Latin *caseus*, and German *Käse* from the same word. What does that suggest about when it was borrowed?
- [x] Before the settlement of Britain, while the ancestors of English and German speakers were still neighbours on the Continent
- [ ] With Christianity, after 597
- [ ] From the Normans, after 1066
- [ ] In the Renaissance
::: solution
A Latin word found in several West Germanic languages, and in English with the early sound changes (here *c* → /tʃ/ before a front vowel, which also gave *church*), was most likely borrowed on the Continent, through trade with the Roman Empire. Christian loans such as *biscop* come later and are mostly about the church; Norman and Renaissance loans keep forms much closer to French and Latin.
:::
:::

::: example Finding modern English in Ælfric {#ex-aelfric}
Around 1000 the teacher and abbot **Ælfric** wrote a Latin *Colloquy*, a dialogue to help boys practise Latin, in which a master questions people of different trades. A later hand added an Old English translation between the lines. Here is the start of the ploughman's part, in the English of that gloss:

> *Hwæt sægest þu, yrþlingc? Hu begæst þu weorc þin? — Eala, leof hlaford, þearle ic deorfe. Ic ga ut on dægræd þywende oxon to felda, and iugie hig to syl.*

Find every word that survives in modern English (in any form), then translate.
::: solution
Surviving words: *hwæt* → *what*; *sægest* → *sayest*, *say*; *þu* → *thou*; *hu* → *how*; *weorc* → *work*; *þin* → *thine*; *leof* → *lief* (‘dear’, archaic: *I'd as lief*); *hlaford* → *lord*; *ic* → *I*; *ga* → *go*; *ut* → *out*; *on* → *on*; *dægræd* → *day* + *red* (‘day-red’: dawn); *oxon* → *oxen*; *to* → *to*; *felda* → *field*; *and* → *and*. *Begæst* contains *gæst*, a form of *gān* ‘go’ (*begān* ‘go about, practise’). Lost words: *yrþlingc* ‘ploughman’ (from *yrþ* ‘ploughing’, related to *earth*), *eala* ‘oh’, *þearle* ‘very, hard’, *deorfe* ‘toil’, *þywende* ‘driving’, *iugie* ‘yoke’ (in fact related to modern *yoke*), *hig* ‘them’, *syl* ‘plough’.

A translation made for this atlas: ‘What do you say, ploughman? How do you do your work? — Oh, dear lord, I work very hard. I go out at daybreak driving the oxen to the field, and yoke them to the plough.’

Notice *oxon*, with the weak plural that modern *oxen* keeps, and *hig* ‘them’, with the native *h-* that the Norse *them* replaced.
:::
:::

## Poetry: *Beowulf*

About 30,000 lines of Old English poetry survive, most of them in four manuscripts written around the year 1000. The longest poem is ***Beowulf***, 3,182 lines long, preserved in a single manuscript in the British Library (Cotton Vitellius A.xv). It tells of a Scandinavian hero who kills the monster Grendel and Grendel's mother, and in old age dies fighting a dragon. When the poem was composed is disputed: proposals range from the eighth century to the time of the manuscript itself.

::: example The opening of *Beowulf* {#ex-beowulf}
Here are the first three lines, in the spelling of the manuscript with modern word division and punctuation:

> *Hwæt! We Gardena in geardagum,*
> *þeodcyninga þrym gefrunon,*
> *hu ða æþelingas ellen fremedon.*

Glossary: *Gār-Dene* ‘Spear-Danes’; *gēar-dagas* ‘days of yore’; *þēod-cyning* ‘king of a people’; *þrym* ‘glory, power’; *gefrignan* ‘learn by asking, hear of’; *æþeling* ‘prince’; *ellen* ‘courage’; *fremman* ‘do, perform’. Identify the case or form of the words in italics in the glossary as they appear in the text, translate, and describe the metre.
::: solution
**Forms.** *Gardena* is genitive plural (*-a*): ‘of the Spear-Danes’. *geardagum* is dative plural (*-um*) after *in*: ‘in days of yore’. *þeodcyninga* is genitive plural: ‘of the kings of the people’. *þrym* is accusative, object of *gefrunon*. *gefrunon* is the past plural of *gefrignan*, agreeing with *we*. *æþelingas* is nominative plural (*-as*, the ending that became *-s*). *ellen* is accusative. *fremedon* is the weak past plural (*-ed-on*) of *fremman*.

**Translation** (made for this atlas): ‘Listen! We have heard of the glory of the Spear-Danes in days of old, of the kings of the people — how the princes performed deeds of courage.’ (*Hwæt* is literally ‘what’, used to call for attention.)

**Metre.** Each line has two half-lines separated by a pause, each with two strongly stressed syllables. The halves are bound by **alliteration** — the same initial sound on stressed syllables — not by rhyme: ***G**ār-Dena* / ***g**ēardagum*; ***þ**ēodcyninga* / ***þ**rym*; and in line 3 two words beginning with vowels, ***æ**þelingas* / ***e**llen*, because in Old English verse any vowel alliterates with any other. Alliteration suits a language with stress on the first syllable of the root ([[history-of-english/english-and-its-relatives#def-germanic]]); it survives in modern phrases such as *safe and sound*, *kith and kin* and *time and tide*.
:::
:::

## Dialects and the written standard

Old English had four main dialects, known from texts: **Northumbrian** and **Mercian** (together called *Anglian*), **West Saxon** and **Kentish**. Most surviving texts are in West Saxon, because Wessex, under **King Alfred** (reigned 871–899) and his successors, became the leading English kingdom. Alfred, after the Viking wars ([[history-of-english/vikings-norse]]), complained that learning had decayed so far that few men could understand their Latin services, and he set out to translate into English the books he thought most necessary for all to know, among them Pope Gregory's *Pastoral Care* and Boethius' *Consolation of Philosophy*. A century later, the school of Bishop Æthelwold at Winchester and writers such as Ælfric used a regular literary language, **Late West Saxon**, which was written across England — the first standard written English.

::: example Two dialects of one poem {#ex-caedmon}
Bede tells the story of **Cædmon**, a cowherd at the abbey of Whitby who was given the gift of song in a dream. Bede gives the hymn in Latin; some manuscripts add it in English. Here are the first lines in an early Northumbrian copy (eighth century) and in a later West Saxon one:

> Northumbrian: *Nu scylun hergan hefaenricaes uard, / metudæs maecti end his modgidanc*
> West Saxon: *Nu sculon herigean heofonrices weard, / meotodes meahte and his modgeþanc*

List the differences and say which are differences of spelling and which of dialect.
::: solution
Translation (made for this atlas): ‘Now [we] must praise the guardian of the heavenly kingdom, the might of the Creator and his purpose.’ (The Northumbrian copy leaves the subject *we* unexpressed.)

**Spelling** differences, reflecting different scribal habits and dates: Northumbrian *u* for *w* (*uard* / *weard*), *d* for *þ* (*modgidanc* / *modgeþanc*), *ae* for *e* in the genitive endings (*hefaenricaes*, *metudæs* / *-es*).

**Dialect** differences in sound: the vowel of ‘guardian’ — Northumbrian *uard* with *a*, West Saxon *weard* with the diphthong *ea* (the same contrast as Anglian *ald* / West Saxon *eald*); *maecti* / *meahte* ‘might’, again *a*-type vowel against *ea*; *end* / *and*. Some differences, such as *scylun* / *sculon*, mix both.

The exercise shows why West Saxon texts should not be taken as "the" Old English: they record one dialect in one written tradition, and the dialect that later dominated was another.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese readers sometimes ask why English speakers cannot read their own language of a thousand years ago, when an educated Chinese reader can read Tang poetry, written more than a thousand years ago. Part of the answer is the writing system. Chinese characters record words, not sounds, so a Tang poem looks much the same today although its pronunciation has changed greatly; read aloud in Mandarin, many of its rhymes no longer rhyme. English letters record sounds, so every change in pronunciation, if spelling follows it, makes the old texts look stranger. The other part is grammar and vocabulary: Old English differs from modern English in its inflections and in most of its vocabulary, roughly as Classical Chinese (文言) differs from modern written Chinese (白话) — enough to need study, not a foreign language.

For the grammar, Chinese is a good guide to what English became rather than to what it was. Modern English, like Chinese, relies mainly on word order and separate function words; Old English relied on endings, like Latin or Russian. Grammatical gender — *wīf* neuter, *stān* masculine — has no counterpart in Chinese, and like German gender it was a property of the word, not of the thing.
:::

::: history
After the Norman Conquest, Old English became unreadable within a few generations, and its manuscripts were mostly forgotten until the sixteenth century, when Protestant scholars around Archbishop Matthew Parker collected them to show that the early English church had used the vernacular. Laurence Nowell, whose name is written in the *Beowulf* manuscript, was one of these collectors. Many manuscripts were gathered by Sir Robert Cotton, whose library was damaged by fire at Ashburnham House in Westminster in 1731: the *Beowulf* manuscript survived with scorched edges, which crumbled further before the leaves were mounted in paper frames in the nineteenth century. The Icelandic-Danish scholar Grímur Thorkelin had two transcripts made in 1787, which preserve letters since lost, and published the first edition in 1815. The first grammar of Old English written in English was Elizabeth Elstob's *Rudiments of Grammar for the English-Saxon Tongue* (1715), which she wrote in English rather than Latin, partly, she explained, so that women, who were rarely taught Latin, could use it. The scientific study of the language came in the nineteenth century with the comparative method ([[history-of-english/english-and-its-relatives]]).
:::

## Where this leads

From the late eighth century, Scandinavian raiders and then settlers brought Old Norse into contact with Old English: [[history-of-english/vikings-norse]]. After 1066, French became the language of power ([[history-of-english/norman-french]]), and by the time English returned to official use, its inflections had largely gone ([[history-of-english/middle-english]]). The phonology behind *wolf* / *wolves* and the modern allophones of English sounds are treated in [[linguistics/phonetics-phonology]]; modern irregular plurals and verbs, the survivors of Old English grammar, in [[nouns-and-articles/countable-uncountable]] and [[core-grammar/past-simple]].

::: summary
- **Old English** (c. 450–1100) was the West Germanic speech of the Angles, Saxons and Jutes who settled in Britain after Roman rule ended; Bede (731) is the main early written source ([[#def-old-english]]).
- It was written with the extra letters *þ*, *ð*, *æ* and *ƿ*, and every letter was pronounced: the *k* of *cniht*, the *h* of *niht* ([[#rule-oe-spelling]]).
- It was **inflected**: four cases, three grammatical genders, strong and weak nouns, adjectives and verbs ([[#def-inflection]]); case endings allowed a freer word order, with verb-second main clauses ([[#rule-case]]).
- Survivals include *-s* plurals and *'s* (from *-as*, *-es*), *oxen*, *feet*, *whom*, *once*, *seldom*, *the more the merrier*, and the strong verbs.
- Its vocabulary was almost all Germanic, rich in compounds (*godspel*, *hlāford*) and kennings; Latin loans came in three layers (continental, British, Christian); Celtic left mainly place names.
- West Saxon was the written standard around 1000, but modern Standard English descends mainly from the Anglian (Mercian) English of the Midlands via London.
:::

Rewrite in modern letters and give the modern English word: *þæt*, *ðing*, *ƿæter*, *fæder*, *scip*, *cirice*.
::: solution

Which of these texts are in Old English? *Beowulf*; Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales*; Ælfric's *Colloquy* gloss; Shakespeare's *Hamlet*; the King James Bible; Cædmon's Hymn.
::: solution

::: exercise Strong or weak? {level=2}
Classify each Old English verb as strong or weak from its past form, and give the modern past tense: *rīdan* – *rād*; *lufian* – *lufode*; *singan* – *sang*; *helpan* – *healp*; *fremman* – *fremede*; *bītan* – *bāt*.
::: solution
Strong (vowel change): *rīdan* – *rād* (modern *rode*), *singan* – *sang* (*sang*), *helpan* – *healp* (modern *helped*: the verb has become weak), *bītan* – *bāt* (*bit*). Weak (dental suffix): *lufian* – *lufode* (*loved*), *fremman* – *fremede* (the verb is lost; it meant ‘performed’).
:::
:::

::: exercise Explain the irregularities {level=2}
Explain, from Old English, why modern English has: (a) *feet*, not *foots*; (b) *oxen*; (c) *wolves* with *v*; (d) *whom*.
::: solution
(a) *Feet* continues an Old English plural (*fēt*) formed by *i*-mutation: an earlier ending with *i* fronted the root vowel and then disappeared. (b) *Oxen* keeps the plural ending *-an* of the weak declension (*oxa*, *oxan*), which elsewhere gave way to *-s*. (c) Old English *f* was voiced between vowels, so *wulfas* had a [v]; when the ending lost its vowel, the [v] stayed and became a separate phoneme. (d) *Whom* is the old dative of *who* (*hwǣm*), which took over the accusative function — the same thing happened with *him*.
:::
:::

::: exercise Translate with a glossary {level=2}
Translate: *Ic seah þone cyning on þǣm felda.* (*sēon*, past *seah* ‘see’; *cyning* ‘king’; *feld* ‘field’.) Identify the case of *þone cyning* and *þǣm felda*, and say why.
::: solution
‘I saw the king in the field.’ *Þone cyning* is accusative, because it is the direct object of *seah*. *Þǣm felda* is dative, because the preposition *on*, meaning location (‘in, on’), takes the dative. (The *-a* of *felda* is the dative singular of this noun, which belonged to a different class from *stān*.)
:::

::: exercise Analyse the Lord's Prayer {level=3}
Using the Lord's Prayer at the start of this chapter, find: (1) three words with an ending that has since disappeared and say what each ending marked; (2) two words that survive with a changed meaning; (3) two words that have been replaced, with their replacements and the source of each replacement.
::: hint
Look at *heofonum*, *eorðan*, *gyltas*, *gyltendum*; at *hlaf* and *syle*; at *rice* and *costnunge*.
:::
::: solution
A model answer.
(1) *heofon-um*: dative plural after *on* (location); *eorð-an*: dative singular after *on*; *gylt-end-um*: dative plural, the indirect object of *forgyfað* (‘forgive [to] our debtors’); *gedæghwamlic-an*: an adjective ending agreeing with *hlaf*. All are lost; modern English uses prepositions and word order.
(2) *hlaf* ‘bread, loaf’ survives as *loaf*, narrowed to ‘a shaped piece of bread’; *syle* (from *sellan* ‘give’) survives as *sell*, narrowed to ‘give for money’; *gyltas* ‘sins, debts’ survives as *guilts*, now mostly uncountable *guilt*.
(3) *rice* ‘kingdom’ was replaced by *kingdom*, a native compound (*cyning* + *-dōm*), which already existed in Old English; *costnung* ‘temptation’ by *temptation*, from French and Latin; *alys* ‘release’ by *deliver*, from French; *soþlice* ‘truly’ by *Amen*, from Hebrew through Latin and Greek.
:::

::: exercise Read the evidence {level=3}
A website says: "The Anglo-Saxons arrived in 449 and killed or drove out all the Britons; that is why there are almost no Celtic words in English." Write 120–180 words evaluating this claim, using what the chapter says about Bede, archaeology and language.
::: solution
A model answer: "The claim takes the date 449 from Bede, who wrote nearly three centuries later and gave it as an approximate date for the first arrival, not for the whole settlement. Modern historians and archaeologists describe a longer process, in many waves over the fifth and sixth centuries, with very different outcomes in different regions; in many places Britons probably stayed and gradually adopted the English language. The lack of Celtic loanwords does not prove extermination. When a socially dominant group's language replaces another, the speakers who shift often bring few words with them, especially if their own language had low prestige; place names and river names, which do survive in large numbers (*Thames*, *Avon*, *Kent*), show that the newcomers learned names from Britons who were still there. Some scholars also argue for Celtic influence on English grammar, though this is unproven. The evidence supports conquest and language shift, not the disappearance of a whole population."
:::
