On a farm, English has *cow*, *pig* (or *swine*), *sheep* and *calf*; on a plate, *beef*, *pork*, *mutton* and *veal*. The farm words are Old English; the table words are French. Since Walter Scott's novel *Ivanhoe* (1819), in which a Saxon jester points out that the animal keeps its English name while a serf looks after it and gets a French one when it is served to the Norman lords, this pair of lists has been the favourite illustration of what the Norman Conquest did to English. It is a good illustration and a slightly misleading one. The French words did not come in 1066, but mostly two or three centuries later; and in Middle English *beef* and *mutton* could still mean the living animals. The real story is more interesting: how a language spoken by a small ruling class for three centuries gave English about a quarter of its vocabulary, a layered system of registers, and some of its spelling.

This chapter explains who spoke French in England, for how long and why; which words English borrowed, when, and from which kind of French; and how the borrowed words fitted into English. It is the historical basis for the register differences described in [[collocation-and-register/register]] — *begin* and *commence*, *ask* and *inquire* — and for many prefixes and suffixes of [[word-formation/prefixes]] and [[word-formation/suffixes]].

## Three languages in one kingdom

The Normans were the descendants of Scandinavian settlers in northern France, who had been granted the land around Rouen in the early tenth century (traditionally in 911) and had adopted the French language and Christian culture within a few generations. In 1066 Duke William of Normandy claimed the English throne, defeated King Harold at Hastings on 14 October, and was crowned in Westminster Abbey on Christmas Day. Within twenty years, almost all the great landholders, bishops and abbots of England were Normans or other French speakers; *Domesday Book* (1086) records how completely the English aristocracy had been replaced. For the social structure that followed — lords, knights and peasants — see the [European History Atlas](/learn/europe/lesson.php?c=christendom&l=lords-and-peasants).

::: definition Anglo-Norman (Anglo-French) {#def-anglo-norman}
**Anglo-Norman**, also called **Anglo-French**, is the French used in England after the Conquest. It began as the Norman dialect of Old French brought by the conquerors; it then developed in England, was learned as a second language by many, and from the thirteenth century was increasingly influenced by the prestigious French of Paris (**Central French**). It was a language of the court, the nobility, the law, administration and much literature until the late fourteenth century, and of some legal records much longer ("Law French").
:::

For about two centuries England was a country of three languages, with different functions:

| language | users | functions |
|---|---|---|
| **French** | the king and court, the nobility, many knights, higher clergy; later merchants and lawyers | speech of the upper classes; law, administration and literature (increasingly from the late twelfth century) |
| **Latin** | the clergy, scholars, royal clerks | the church, learning, most written records |
| **English** | the great majority of the population | everyday speech; some religious and literary writing |

The Normans were never more than a small minority — estimates are uncertain, but at most a few tens of thousands among one and a half to two million people — and English was never in danger of dying out. Children of mixed marriages, stewards who dealt with English peasants, and the many people between the top and the bottom of society must have been bilingual, and by about 1200 many families of Norman descent seem to have spoken English as their first language. A chronicle in English verse attributed to Robert of Gloucester, written around 1300, describes the situation looking back:

> *Vor bote a man conne frenss me telþ of him lute. Ac lowe men holdeþ to engliss & to hor owe speche ȝute.*

‘For unless a man knows French, people think little of him. But low men keep to English and to their own speech still.’ (Translation made for this atlas. *Me* is the indefinite pronoun ‘one, people’; *ȝ* is the letter yogh, here /j/.)

So French was the language of **status**, not of the nation. That is the key to what English borrowed.

::: theorem When French words were borrowed {#rule-timing}
French loans entered English in two main phases:

1. **1066 to about 1250**: relatively few — about 900 by Baugh & Cable's estimate — mostly words that English speakers would meet through contact with the nobility and the church — *castle*, *prison*, *justice*, *service*, *sermon*, *tower* — many from Norman French.
2. **About 1250 to about 1400**: very many, as English **replaced** French among the upper and middle classes and people who had used French brought its vocabulary into their English. Counts from the *Oxford English Dictionary* agree in putting the peak of French borrowing in this period; Jespersen's sample of 1,000 French loans (1905), as reported by Baugh & Cable, dated about two in five of them to 1251–1400.

Borrowing continued after 1400, more slowly and often for more specialised words; and from the fifteenth century it becomes increasingly hard to separate from borrowing from Latin.
:::

::: proof
The pattern looks paradoxical — the most French came into English when French was in decline — but it is what we should expect. A language borrows from another most when bilingual speakers are moving from one to the other: they carry over the words for the things they talk about in the old language — government, law, fashion, food, learning — because those are the words they know. In the early period, the French-speaking elite and the English-speaking majority lived largely separate linguistic lives; English writing was also scarce, so many borrowings may have happened without being recorded. The dates come from first citations in dictionaries, which have a known bias: a word may be spoken long before it is written down, and dictionaries record only surviving texts. Durkin (*Borrowed Words*, 2014) stresses a second problem: for many words, such as *nation*, *question* or *action*, it is impossible to say whether English took them from French or directly from Latin, because both had them and the forms are nearly identical; many are best described as "from French and Latin".
:::

## What English borrowed

Baugh & Cable estimate that over ten thousand French words were adopted in Middle English, of which about three quarters are still in use. They fill whole areas of meaning:

| field | examples |
|---|---|
| government and administration | *government*, *crown*, *state*, *reign*, *royal*, *parliament*, *tax*, *council*, *minister*, *nation* |
| nobility | *noble*, *duke*, *baron*, *prince*, *peer*, *court*, *servant* (but *king*, *queen*, *lord*, *lady*, *earl* remained English) |
| law | *justice*, *judge*, *jury*, *crime*, *plea*, *suit*, *accuse*, *sentence*, *prison*, *evidence*, *property*, *estate* |
| church | *religion*, *sermon*, *prayer*, *saint*, *miracle*, *virgin*, *clergy*, *confess* |
| war | *army*, *navy*, *battle*, *soldier*, *enemy*, *captain*, *siege*, *peace* |
| fashion and household | *dress*, *robe*, *button*, *fashion*, *chair*, *table*, *curtain*, *lamp* |
| food | *dinner*, *supper*, *feast*, *beef*, *pork*, *mutton*, *veal*, *sauce*, *fruit*, *roast*, *boil*, *fry* |
| art, learning, medicine | *art*, *beauty*, *colour*, *music*, *poet*, *study*, *grammar*, *medicine*, *surgeon*, *pain*, *remedy* |
| everyday | *very*, *people*, *place*, *change*, *use*, *easy*, *poor*, *push*, *river*, *uncle*, *aunt*, *cousin*, *face*, *age* |

The last row matters. Some French words became as basic as native ones, and nothing about *very*, *people* or *change* sounds formal today.

::: example Sorting by field and origin {#ex-fields}
Classify each word as native (Old English) or French, and for the French ones name the field: *king*, *royal*, *judge*, *law*, *prayer*, *God*, *soldier*, *fight*, *dinner*, *bread*.
::: solution
- *king* — native (Old English *cyning*); the title stayed English.
- *royal* — French (government and nobility).
- *judge* — French (law).
- *law* — not French but Norse ([[history-of-english/vikings-norse]]): late Old English *lagu*.
- *prayer* — French (church).
- *God* — native.
- *soldier* — French (war).
- *fight* — native (*feohtan*).
- *dinner* — French (food and household).
- *bread* — native.

The pattern is typical: the native words are basic (*God*, *king*, *bread*, *fight*); the French words belong to institutions — the crown, the courts, the church, the army, the noble household. But the fields are not walls: *law*, the most legal of words, is Norse, and *king* and *queen* never gave way to French.
:::

### The farm and the table

The *Ivanhoe* pairs belong here, with their caveats. *Beef* (Old French *boef*), *mutton* (*moton*), *pork* (*porc*), *veal* (*veel*) and *venison* (*venaison*) entered English mainly from the late thirteenth century on, when they could mean either the animal or its meat; Chaucer's contemporaries could speak of living *beeves*. Over the following centuries the French words narrowed to the meat and the English words kept the animal. The specialisation fits the social history — meat on the table of those who could afford it — but it was gradual, and it is incomplete: *chicken*, *fish* and *lamb* serve for both.

## Layers of vocabulary and register

The most lasting result of French borrowing, together with the later Latin borrowing of the Renaissance ([[history-of-english/early-modern-english]]), is that English very often has two or three words for nearly the same idea, from different sources and with different registers.

::: theorem Native, French and Latin layers {#rule-layers}
Where English has near-synonyms from different sources, they tend to differ in register in a regular way:

| native (Old English) | French | Latin (often later) |
|---|---|---|
| *ask* | *question* | *interrogate* |
| *rise* | *mount* | *ascend* |
| *fire* | *flame* | *conflagration* |
| *holy* | *sacred* | *consecrated* |
| *fear* | *terror* | *trepidation* |
| *kingly* | *royal* | *regal* |
| *time* | *age* | *epoch* |
| *begin* | *commence* | *initiate* |
| *help* | *aid* | *assist* (through French) |

1. The **native** word is usually the most general, the most frequent and the most neutral or informal, and often the most emotionally direct (*heart*, *home*, *love*).
2. The **French** word is often more formal, more courtly or more abstract.
3. The **Latin** word is usually the most formal, technical or learned.
:::

::: proof
The tendency has a historical cause: French and Latin were the languages of the institutions — the court, the law, the church, the university — and their words kept the association with formal settings, while the native words remained the vocabulary of home and daily work ([[collocation-and-register/register]]). It is a tendency, not a law, for three reasons. First, some French words became completely ordinary (*very*, *use*, *change*, *pay*), and some native words are rare and literary (*wan*, *betoken*). Second, the meanings differ as well as the registers: *ask* and *question* overlap, but *ask for help* cannot be *question for help*. Third, the categories are not always clear: *assist* came through French from Latin, and many "French" words had Latin forms beside them. The table describes the typical case and is useful for choosing a register in writing; it is not a list of interchangeable words.
:::

::: example Choosing the register {#ex-register}
Rewrite each sentence for the setting given, choosing among native, French and Latin words, and say what you changed.

1. Formal notice: *The meeting will begin at ten, and we ask all members to help.*
2. Message to a friend: *Our journey will commence on Monday; we shall endeavour to arrive by noon.*
::: solution
1. *The meeting will **commence** at ten, and all members are **requested** to **assist**.* — *begin* → *commence* (French), *ask* → *request* (French, through Latin), *help* → *assist* (Latinate). The passive *are requested* also adds formality. In most modern notices, the plain version is in fact better: *will begin* is not too informal for a notice, and plain-language guides recommend it ([[style-and-rhetoric/word-choice]]).
2. *We're **starting** our trip on Monday — we'll **try** to **get there** by twelve.* — *commence* → *start* (native), *journey* (French) → *trip* (a later word, informal), *endeavour* (French) → *try* (French, but neutral!), *arrive* (French) → *get there* (a native phrasal verb). The informal version is largely native and uses a phrasal verb ([[collocation-and-register/phrasal-verbs-register]]).

Notice that *try* is French and *journey* is French: the rule is a tendency of the layers, not a sorting of every word by origin. The test is how each word is used today, which a learner's dictionary marks with labels such as *formal*.
:::
:::

::: warning *Latinate* does not mean *better*
✗ *I shall commence my dinner and subsequently endeavour to purchase a beverage.*
✓ *I'll start dinner and then try to buy a drink.*

Learners who have discovered the formal layer sometimes use it everywhere, and the result sounds pompous or comic. Formal words belong in formal settings — official letters, academic writing, legal texts — and even there the plain word is often preferred. Equally, do not avoid French words in ordinary speech: *very*, *people*, *use* and *easy* are French, and they are entirely neutral.
:::

::: quiz
Which word is the most formal?
- [ ] *kingly*
- [ ] *royal*
- [x] *regal*
- [ ] They are equally formal.
::: solution
*Regal* (from Latin *regalis*) is the most formal and literary of the three. *Royal* (from French, from the same Latin word) is the ordinary word for things connected with a king or queen (*the royal family*), and *kingly* (native) is rarer and describes qualities (*kingly dignity*). The three words come from the same root twice — the Latin *rex* gave *royal* through French and *regal* directly — and from a separate native root (*cyning*) once.
:::
:::

## Two kinds of French

Because Anglo-Norman and Central French developed differently, and English borrowed from both at different times, English sometimes has the same French word twice.

::: theorem Norman and Central French doublets {#rule-norman}
Where Norman French differed in sound from the French of Paris, English sometimes has a doublet: an earlier word from Norman French and a later one from Central French.

1. **Norman *w-*, Central *g(u)-***: *warranty* / *guarantee*, *warden* / *guardian*, *reward* / *regard*, *wage* / *gage* (as in *engage*).
2. **Norman *k-* (*c-*), Central *ch-*** before *a*: *catch* / *chase*, *cattle* / *chattel*.

The Norman form usually came first and has often developed a more concrete or everyday meaning.
:::

::: proof
The doublets follow from known sound differences between the dialects. Germanic *w-* in words borrowed into French was kept in Norman and Picard but became *gu-* in Central French (Germanic *wardōn* → Norman *warder*, Paris *garder*); Latin *ca-* stayed *ka-* in Norman but became *cha-* in Central French (Latin *capere* ‘take’ → Norman *cachier*, Central *chacier*). English borrowed the Norman forms in the early period and the Central forms later, when the French of Paris had become the prestigious model even in England. Pairs like *catch* and *chase* show that two forms can split in meaning once they exist side by side.
:::

::: example Which French? {#ex-which-french}
Compare each English word with its modern French counterpart and decide whether English took the Norman or the Central form: *castle* (French *château*), *garden* (*jardin*), *carry* (*charrier* ‘to cart’), *wardrobe* (*garde-robe*), *chapel* (*chapelle*).
::: solution
1. *castle*: Norman *castel*, with *k-* kept before *a*; Central French had *chastel*, modern *château* (the *s* lost, marked by the circumflex). **Norman.** It is one of the earliest French loans, recorded before 1100.
2. *garden*: Norman *gardin*, with *g-* kept before *a*; Central French *jardin*. **Norman.**
3. *carry*: Norman *carier*; Central *charier*. **Norman.**
4. *wardrobe*: Norman *warderobe*, with Germanic *w-* kept; Central *garderobe*. **Norman.**
5. *chapel*: with *ch-* before *a*. **Central** (or a Norman form that had adopted the Central sound; the Latin is *cappella*).

The first four are household and estate words, borrowed early from the French actually spoken by the Norman lords; *chapel* shows the later prestige of Paris. Modern French can be a guide, but only because its forms continue Central French: always check the dictionary's etymology.
:::
:::

## How French words fitted into English

French words did not stay foreign. They took English endings (*judged*, *dinners*), combined with native elements, and gave English new word-forming material.

- **Hybrids.** French suffixes were added to native bases: *-able* in *readable*, *unbelievable*; *-ment* in *settlement*; *-ess* in *goddess*; *-age* in *shrinkage*. Native suffixes were added to French bases: *beautiful*, *gentleness*, *faithful*, *courtly*. The prefixes *dis-* and *en-* came with French words (*disagree*, *enclose*) and then spread ([[word-formation/prefixes]]).
- **Stress.** French words were stressed near the end, as in French; over the following centuries, many moved their stress forward to follow the Germanic pattern: Chaucer could stress *naTURE* and *coRAGE*, where modern English says *NAture*, *COURage*. The shift is still in progress in some recent loans: *garage* is stressed on the first syllable in GB and usually on the second in GA; *ballet* likewise.
- **Word order.** A few legal and official phrases kept French order, with the adjective after the noun: *attorney general*, *court martial*, *heir apparent*, *body politic*.
- **Spelling.** French-trained scribes changed English spelling: *qu* for Old English *cw* (*cwēn* → *queen*), *ou* for long /uː/ (*hūs* → *hous*, later *house*), *ch* for /tʃ/ (*cirice* → *church*), and *o* for *u* next to the letters *m*, *n*, *v* and *w*, which were written with short vertical strokes that were hard to read next to *u* (*sum* → *some*, *cuman* → *come*, *lufu* → *love*, *munuc* → *monk*). These are some of the irregular spellings explained in [[sounds-of-english/spelling-and-sound]].

::: example Explaining a spelling {#ex-spelling}
Explain from the history of this chapter why these words have the spelling they do: *queen*, *house*, *love*, *son*.
::: solution
1. *queen*: Old English *cwēn*; after the Conquest scribes wrote the French-style *qu* for /kw/ (as in *quarter*, *question*).
2. *house*: Old English *hūs* /huːs/; scribes used the French spelling *ou* for /uː/ (as in French *tout*). The vowel later became /aʊ/ in the Great Vowel Shift ([[history-of-english/great-vowel-shift]]), so the spelling no longer looks like the sound.
3. *love*: Old English *lufu* with short /u/; *u* next to *v* (then written *u* too) was written *o* to make the letters easier to read. The vowel later became /ʌ/. The final *-e* also helped to show that the letter before it was *v*, not the vowel *u*.
4. *son*: Old English *sunu*; *o* for *u* next to *n*, for the same reason. This is why *son* and *sun* (Old English *sunne*) are spelled differently but pronounced the same, /sʌn/.

In each case the spelling records a decision of medieval scribes, not the pronunciation of any period exactly.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese readers will recognise the layering of [[#rule-layers]], because Chinese has a similar contrast between everyday words and formal words drawn from the classical written language. Compare 买 and 购买, 给 and 给予, 做 and 进行, 怕 and 畏惧: the second of each pair is longer, more formal and more typical of written and official Chinese. The source of the formal layer is different — in Chinese it is the classical language of the same tradition, in English two foreign languages — but the social mechanism is the same: words used by institutions keep the flavour of those institutions. A useful habit for translation is to match the layers: 本公司将于明日开始营业 is closer to *The company will commence business tomorrow* than to *The firm's starting tomorrow*. But the matching is not exact, and formal Chinese often needs plainer English than a word-for-word translation suggests ([[translation/english-chinese-compared]]).
:::

::: history
The idea that English vocabulary falls into social layers, with French for the rulers and English for the ruled, owes much to the nineteenth century. Walter Scott's *Ivanhoe* (1819) made the animal–meat pairs famous, and Victorian historians, who saw the Conquest as the oppression of the "Saxons", made the Norman element a symbol. The serious counting began with the *Oxford English Dictionary*, whose dated quotations allowed scholars to date borrowings: Otto Jespersen's sample in *Growth and Structure of the English Language* (1905) showed that the peak came after 1250, not in the generations after 1066. Modern work, much of it based on the *Anglo-Norman Dictionary* and the revised *OED*, has made the picture more complex: many words came from Anglo-French as it was used in England, in business and law, rather than from the speech of the conquerors, and many came from French and Latin together. Philip Durkin's *Borrowed Words* (2014) is the fullest modern account.
:::

## Where this leads

English returned to use as the language of law, government and literature between about 1250 and 1450, and the language that returned was greatly changed: [[history-of-english/middle-english]]. The second great wave of borrowing, from Latin and Greek, came with the Renaissance ([[history-of-english/early-modern-english]]). The register layers of this chapter are used in practice in [[collocation-and-register/register]], [[collocation-and-register/phrasal-verbs-register]] and [[style-and-rhetoric/word-choice]]; false friends between English and French-derived words in other languages are in [[collocation-and-register/false-friends-chinglish]].

::: summary
- After 1066, England had three languages: **French** for the court, nobility and law, **Latin** for the church and records, **English** for most people ([[#def-anglo-norman]]).
- French was a language of **status**; English was never in danger, and by about 1200 many families of Norman descent spoke English.
- Most French words came in **after 1250**, as English replaced French among the upper classes ([[#rule-timing]]); many words came from French and Latin together.
- French gave English the vocabulary of government, law, church, war, fashion, food and learning, but also everyday words such as *very*, *people* and *change*.
- English often has native, French and Latin near-synonyms that differ in register: *ask*, *question*, *interrogate* ([[#rule-layers]]) — a tendency, not a law.
- Norman and Central French gave doublets such as *warranty* / *guarantee* and *catch* / *chase* ([[#rule-norman]]).
- French influence also shaped English spelling (*qu*, *ou*, *ch*, *o* for *u*), word formation (*-able*, *dis-*) and, through stress shift, pronunciation.
:::

::: exercise Evaluate the Ivanhoe story {level=3}
In 120–180 words, explain what is true and what is misleading in the statement: "After 1066 the Normans ate the meat and the Saxons raised the animals, so English got French words for meat."
::: hint

::: solution
A model answer: "The statement captures something real: the French words *beef*, *pork*, *mutton* and *veal* came from the language of the upper classes, who were more likely to eat meat, while the native words *cow*, *swine*, *sheep* and *calf* remained the words of farming. But it is misleading in three ways. First, the date: most of these words entered English from the late thirteenth century onwards, when English was replacing French among the upper classes, not in the years after 1066.
