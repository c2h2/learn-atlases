In Shakespeare's *Hamlet*, the old courtier Polonius finds the prince reading and asks, in the spelling of the First Folio (1623), *What do you read my Lord?* Hamlet answers: *Words, words, words.* A modern reader understands both lines without a note. In the first line of Sonnet 18, printed in 1609 — *Shall I compare thee to a Summers day? / Thou art more louely and more temperate* — only the old pronoun *thee*/*thou*, the verb form *art* and the printer's *u* for *v* in *louely* stand out. And in the King James Bible of 1611, *Our father which art in heauen, hallowed be thy name*, the language is familiar from churches and quotations, but no one speaks like that now.

This is **Early Modern English**, the English of roughly 1500 to 1700: the English of the first printed books, of the Reformation Bibles, of Shakespeare and Milton, of the first English dictionaries and of the scientists of the Royal Society. It is close enough to modern English to be read with a few notes, and different enough that the differences reward attention. This chapter explains how printing helped to fix the written language, how the Renaissance brought thousands of Latin and Greek words into English, how spelling was respelled to look Latin, which grammatical changes separated Shakespeare's English from ours, and what Tyndale, the King James translators and Shakespeare did — and did not do — for the language.

::: definition Early Modern English {#def-emode}
**Early Modern English** (早期现代英语) is the English of about 1500 to about 1700. Its beginning is usually linked to printing (from 1476), the end of the main changes of the Great Vowel Shift's first stage and the London standard of the fifteenth century; its end to the settling of spelling and of most grammatical forms by the late seventeenth century. It is the period of the Renaissance and the Reformation in England, and of the first English settlements overseas ([[history-of-english/spread-of-english]]).
:::

## Printing and the written standard

William Caxton (c. 1422–c. 1491), a merchant who had lived for many years in Bruges, learned printing on the Continent and printed the first book in English there, his own translation of *The Recuyell of the Historyes of Troye* (c. 1473–1474). In 1476 he set up the first press in England, at Westminster, near the court and the royal offices, and in 1477 printed *The Dictes or Sayengis of the Philosophres*, the first dated book printed in England. In about fifteen years he printed around a hundred books, most of them in English, including Chaucer, Gower, Malory's *Morte Darthur* and many of his own translations. For the history of printing in Europe, see the [European History Atlas](/learn/europe/lesson.php?c=crises&l=print).

Caxton worried about the language he had to print. In the prologue to his *Eneydos* (1490), before the story of the eggs and the *eyren* retold in [[history-of-english/middle-english#ex-eggs]], he wrote: *And certaynly our langage now vsed varyeth ferre from that whiche was vsed and spoken whan I was borne.* (‘And certainly our language now in use differs greatly from that which was used and spoken when I was born.’) English was changing, it differed from shire to shire, and there was no authority to decide.

::: theorem How printing affected English {#rule-printing}
1. Printing **multiplied identical copies**, so a single form of a text, in a single spelling, reached readers everywhere. The London-based written English of the fifteenth century, which printers used, became the model for the whole country.
2. Printing **did not fix spelling at once**. Caxton and his successors spelled inconsistently, often varying a word within one page; compositors (many of them from the Low Countries) adjusted spellings to fit lines; and individual printing houses had their own habits. Spelling became largely uniform in printed books only by the middle of the seventeenth century, and private spelling stayed variable much longer.
3. Printing **created a reading public** for English books — religious, practical, literary — which increased the demand for English and the prestige of the standard written form.
:::

::: proof
The evidence is the books themselves. In Caxton's prints a word such as *book* appears as *boke* and *booke*, *been* as *ben*, *bene* and *been*; in the first half of the sixteenth century the variation narrows, and in the seventeenth it is much reduced. The narrowing was not planned by any authority. It came from printers' practice, from spelling books and schoolmasters such as Richard Mulcaster, whose *Elementarie* (1582) argued for spelling by "custom" and listed several thousand words, and from the influence of widely read books such as the Bible. The written standard therefore spread by imitation of print, while speech remained varied. Its spellings, settled during the Great Vowel Shift, record older pronunciations ([[history-of-english/great-vowel-shift#rule-spelling]]).
:::

### Respelling English to look like Latin

During the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, scholars who knew Latin changed the spelling of many words that had come into English from French, to show the Latin word behind them. Some of these changes are why English spelling has silent letters ([[sounds-of-english/spelling-and-sound#rule-silent]]).

| Middle English | respelled | Latin model |
|---|---|---|
| *dette* | *debt* | *debitum* |
| *doute* | *doubt* | *dubitare* |
| *receite* | *receipt* | *recepta* |
| *samoun* | *salmon* | *salmo* |
| *vitailles* | *victuals* | *victualia* |
| *iland* | *island* | Latin *insula* — a false connection |
| *faute* | *fault* | *fallere*, *falsus* |
| *parfit* | *perfect* | *perfectus* |
| *aventure* | *adventure* | *adventura* |

::: theorem Etymological spellings {#rule-etymological}
1. An **etymological spelling** adds or changes letters to show a word's Latin (or Greek) origin, without a change in pronunciation: *debt*, *doubt*, *receipt*, *salmon*.
2. Some such spellings later changed the pronunciation too, because readers pronounced the new letters: *fault* and *perfect* gained their /l/ and /k/, *adventure* its /d/, *author* (Middle English *autour*, respelled with *th* on the model of Greek words) its /θ/. These are **spelling pronunciations**.
3. Some were mistakes: *island* is a native word (Old English *īegland*) that never had an *s*; the *s* came from confusion with the French and Latin *isle*. *Scissors*, *scythe* and *could* (by analogy with *should* and *would*) have letters of the same kind.
:::

::: example Explaining a silent letter {#ex-silent}
Explain the silent letters in *debt*, *island* and *could*, and the pronounced *l* in *fault*.
::: solution
1. *debt*: borrowed from French *dette* in Middle English and spelled without *b*; respelled *debt* in the sixteenth century after Latin *debitum*. The *b* was never pronounced in English ([[#rule-etymological]], point 1).
2. *island*: Old English *īegland*, Middle English *iland*; the *s* was added in the sixteenth century by false association with *isle* (from French, ultimately Latin *insula*). It is a mistaken etymological spelling (point 3).
3. *could*: Middle English *coude*, the past of *can*; the *l* was added by analogy with *should* and *would*, where it had once been pronounced (*sceolde*, *wolde*). It is an analogical spelling, never pronounced.
4. *fault*: Middle English *faute*, from French, pronounced without /l/; respelled after Latin, and in the following centuries the /l/ came to be pronounced from the spelling (point 2). Samuel Johnson noted, still in the eighteenth century, that some speakers pronounced *fault* without the *l*.

Each silent letter has a dated history. The general lesson for learners: silent letters in words of French or Latin origin often show a Latin word that you may know from other English words (*debit*, *dubious*, *reception*).
:::
:::

## The Renaissance vocabulary

The revival of classical learning, the Reformation and the growth of science made Latin and Greek the main sources of new English words in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Writers translating Latin works, or writing on medicine, law, philosophy and mathematics in English for the first time, needed words that English did not have, and took them from Latin — directly now, rather than through French.

- **Latin**: *education*, *exaggerate*, *explain*, *external*, *habitual*, *appropriate*, *contradictory*, *emancipate*, *eradicate*, *investigate*, *tolerate*, *postpone*, *exist*, *expensive*, *temperature*, *genius*, *species*, *specimen*, *vacuum*.
- **Greek** (often through Latin): *anachronism*, *atmosphere*, *catastrophe*, *chaos*, *climax*, *crisis*, *emphasis*, *skeleton*, *system*, *thermometer* (seventeenth century).
- **French, Italian and Spanish**, through trade, travel and the arts: from French *bizarre*, *detail*, *explore*; from Italian *balcony*, *opera*, *sonnet*, *violin*; from Spanish and Portuguese *armada*, *banana*, *mosquito*, and words of the Americas such as *potato* and *tobacco*.

Many Latin verbs of this period are the formal partners of native phrasal verbs, a pattern that still shapes English register ([[collocation-and-register/phrasal-verbs-register]]): *postpone* / *put off*, *investigate* / *look into*, *tolerate* / *put up with*, *eradicate* / *root out*, *extinguish* / *put out*. With the Latin words came their prefixes and suffixes, which then became productive in English: negative *in-* (*il-*, *im-*, *ir-*) beside native *un-* (*inability* beside *unable*, *illegal*, *irregular*), and *dis-*, already borrowed through French, now used freely (*disrespect*, *dislike*) ([[word-formation/prefixes]]). How these Latin and Greek parts are used to build and understand academic words today is the subject of [[word-formation/greek-latin-roots]].

::: definition Inkhorn term {#def-inkhorn}
An **inkhorn term** is a learned word borrowed from Latin or Greek that critics regarded as pretentious or unnecessary — a word that smelled of the scholar's inkhorn (inkpot). The label belongs to a sixteenth-century debate about whether English should enrich itself by borrowing or develop its own resources.
:::

The **inkhorn controversy** had three positions. Enthusiasts such as Sir Thomas Elyot (*The Boke Named the Governour*, 1531) introduced many Latin words deliberately and defended them as necessary. Purists such as Sir John Cheke, in a letter of 1557 printed in Thomas Hoby's translation of *The Courtier* (1561), argued that English should be written "cleane and pure, unmixt and unmangeled with borowing of other tunges", and coined native equivalents instead. Thomas Wilson's *Arte of Rhetorique* (1553) mocked writers who stuffed their English with Latin, with a parody letter full of them. A middle position, represented by Richard Mulcaster, accepted borrowing where needed and defended English against Latin as a language of learning: "I love Rome, but London better, I favor Italie, but England more, I honor the Latin, but I worship the English" (*Elementarie*, 1582).

::: theorem Which Renaissance borrowings survived {#rule-survival}
Of the Latin and Greek words introduced in this period, many failed: they appeared in one or a few writers and were never taken up. Those that survived usually:

1. filled a real gap — a concept or technical term without an English word (*atmosphere*, *skeleton*, *temperature*);
2. were used by many writers in many kinds of text, not by one author;
3. fitted into English morphology, forming families of related words (*educate*, *education*, *educational*).

Words that merely duplicated an existing English or French word, or that were coined for display, mostly disappeared, although some survived as formal synonyms.
:::

::: proof
The pattern can be seen in the historical dictionaries, which record the first and last uses of each word. Words such as *adnichilate* ‘reduce to nothing’ and *eximious* ‘excellent’ are recorded from the period and then disappear; *annihilate* and *excellent* already did their jobs. The contrast between *atmosphere*, needed by natural philosophers and used by many, and *adnichilate*, used by a few, illustrates points 1 and 2. The rule describes tendencies, not certainties: some words survived although they duplicated others (*commence* had *begin*), because the formal register valued them ([[history-of-english/norman-french#rule-layers]]), and some useful-looking words failed by accident. Dating these words also needs care: until recently, dictionaries relied on the texts their readers happened to read, and searchable collections of early printed books have pushed many first dates back.
:::

::: quiz
Which Renaissance borrowing filled a real gap in the vocabulary?
- [x] *skeleton*
- [ ] *eximious* ‘excellent’
- [ ] *adnichilate* ‘annihilate’
- [ ] *obtestate* ‘call to witness, beseech’
::: solution
*Skeleton* (from Greek, through modern Latin, late sixteenth century) named a thing that anatomists needed a word for; it survived. The other three duplicated words that English already had, were used by few writers, and disappeared ([[#rule-survival]]).

:::

## Grammar between Chaucer and now

The grammar of Early Modern English is close to that of today, but several systems were still changing during the period. Shakespeare's plays, written between about 1590 and 1613, show old and new forms side by side.

::: definition *Thou* and *you* {#def-thou}
In Early Modern English, the second-person pronouns had two sets: singular ***thou*** (subject), ***thee*** (object), ***thy***/***thine*** (possessive), and ***ye*** (subject) / ***you*** (object), ***your*** — originally plural, but used, like French *vous*, as a polite **singular** as well. By 1600 *you* was the normal pronoun of the upper and middle classes for one person; *thou* expressed **intimacy** (to a lover, a child, God), **superiority** (to a servant) or **contempt** (to an equal, as an insult). *Ye* as a distinct subject form was going out, replaced by *you*.
:::

A famous example of *thou* as an insult comes in *Twelfth Night* (1601–1602), where Sir Toby Belch advises the timid Sir Andrew how to write a challenge: *if thou thou'st him some thrice, it shall not be amisse.* In the seventeenth century *thou* disappeared from most educated speech, surviving in prayer, in poetry, among Quakers (who used it to everyone on principle) and in some dialects of the north of England.

::: theorem *-eth* and *-s* {#rule-eth}
The third-person singular present ending was ***-eth*** in Chaucer's London (*he singeth*) and ***-s*** in the north ([[history-of-english/middle-english]]). During the sixteenth century *-s* spread in London speech and then in writing:

1. By about 1600, *-s* was the usual form in speech, and Shakespeare uses both — *-eth* for solemnity or metre, *-s* elsewhere; *hath* and *doth* survived longer than other *-eth* forms.
2. The King James Bible (1611) uses only *-eth* (*he maketh me to lie down in green pastures*), a deliberately conservative choice.
3. By the end of the seventeenth century, *-eth* was archaic outside religious and poetic language.
:::

::: theorem The rise of *do* {#rule-do}
In Middle English, questions and negatives were made without *do*: *Know you this man?* *I know not.* During the fifteenth to seventeenth centuries, ***do*** became the regular auxiliary in these sentences when no other auxiliary is present: *Do you know this man?* *I do not know.* In the sixteenth century, *do* was also common in affirmative statements without emphasis (*I do know*), a use that later declined and became emphatic only. Shakespeare uses both patterns: *What do you read?* beside *Know you not …?*
:::

::: proof
The change was charted by Alvar Ellegård (*The Auxiliary Do*, 1953), who counted *do* in thousands of sentences from texts of 1400–1700. His figures show *do* rising in each type of sentence — negative statements, affirmative and negative questions — along an S-shaped curve, slowly at first, then quickly, then slowly again. Anthony Kroch (1989) showed that the curves for the different sentence types rise at the same rate, which suggests a single change in the grammar showing itself in several contexts. The modern rules for questions and negatives with *do* ([[core-grammar/questions-and-negatives]]) are thus the result of a change completed only around 1700; the old pattern survives in a few fixed expressions and in archaic style (*I know not*, *Fear not*), and in the verbs *be* and, in some British usage, *have*.
:::

Real changes look like this because a new form spreads slowly while few people use it, fastest when it is common, and slowly again as the last speakers of the old form remain.

Other changes of the period:

- ***its*** is first recorded in the 1590s, replacing the older neuter possessive *his* (*if the salt haue lost his sauour*, Matthew 5:13 in the King James Bible, which does not use *its*) and the short-lived *it* (*it own*).
- ***who*** and ***which***: *which* could refer to people (*Our father which art in heauen*); *who* as a relative pronoun for people became general during the period.
- **Double comparatives and negatives** were still found in good writing (*the most unkindest cut of all*, *Julius Caesar*); they became stigmatised only in the eighteenth century ([[history-of-english/dictionaries-grammars]]).
- **The progressive** (*is reading*) became more frequent, though still much rarer than today; the passive progressive (*the house is being built*) appears only at the end of the eighteenth century.

::: example Modernising Early Modern English {#ex-modernise}
Rewrite these sentences in modern English and name the Early Modern feature in each.

1. *Thou knowest not what thou sayest.*
2. *He hath a book, but he readeth it not.*
3. *Came he yesterday?*
4. *The tree hath lost his leaves.*
::: solution
1. *You don't know what you're saying.* — *thou* with the verb ending *-est*; negative without *do* ([[#def-thou]], [[#rule-do]]).
2. *He has a book, but he doesn't read it.* — *hath*, *-eth* ([[#rule-eth]]); negative without *do*, with *not* after the verb.
3. *Did he come yesterday?* — a question formed by inverting the main verb, without *do*.
4. *The tree has lost its leaves.* — *his* as the neuter possessive, before *its* became general; *hath*.

Notice that the modern versions are more "analytic": they use the auxiliary *do* and contracted negatives, and only the one second-person form *you*.
:::
:::

## The English Bible: Tyndale and King James

The Reformation made the translation of the Bible into English a central — and dangerous — enterprise. **William Tyndale** (c. 1494–1536) translated the New Testament from Greek and printed it at Worms in 1526, because translation without church approval was illegal in England; copies were smuggled in and burned. He went on to translate the Pentateuch (1530) and other books of the Old Testament from Hebrew, before he was arrested in the Low Countries and executed near Brussels in 1536. Within a few years, English Bibles based largely on his work were officially approved. For the religious and political background, see the [European History Atlas](/learn/europe/lesson.php?c=reformations&l=england).

The **King James Bible** (the "Authorised Version") was commissioned after a conference at Hampton Court in 1604 and published in 1611. About fifty scholars worked in six companies; they were instructed to revise the existing "Bishops' Bible", consulting earlier translations, including Tyndale's. They kept so much of Tyndale that, according to a study by Jon Nielson and Royal Skousen (1998), about 83 per cent of the King James New Testament, and about 76 per cent of the Old Testament books that Tyndale translated, is his wording.

::: example Tyndale and the King James Bible {#ex-bible}
Compare Genesis 1:3 in Tyndale's Pentateuch (1530) and in the King James Bible (1611):

> Tyndale: *Than God sayd: let there be lyghte and there was lyghte.*
> King James: *And God said, Let there be light: and there was light.*

What changed, what stayed, and what does the comparison show about the language of the King James Bible?
::: solution
**What stayed**: the words and the structure — *let there be light and there was light* — are Tyndale's. Tyndale's short, mostly native words (*let*, *there*, *be*, *light*) and his rhythm passed into the King James text unchanged.

**What changed**: spelling (*sayd* → *said*, *lyghte* → *light*, *Than* → *then* in meaning; the King James translators chose *And*, following the Hebrew construction more closely) and punctuation. The spelling of 1611 is more regular than that of 1530 ([[#rule-printing]]).

**What it shows**: the King James Bible is not mainly a new translation in the English of 1611 but a careful revision of sixteenth-century translations, above all Tyndale's. Its language was already somewhat old-fashioned when it appeared — it keeps *thou*, *-eth* and *his* for *its* — and it became more so over the following centuries, which is part of why it sounds solemn to modern ears. Its influence on English idiom is very large: phrases such as *the salt of the earth*, *a law unto themselves* and *the powers that be* are familiar from it, many of them going back to Tyndale.
:::
:::

## Shakespeare

William Shakespeare (1564–1616) wrote his plays and poems between about 1590 and 1613; most of his plays were first collected in the **First Folio** of 1623. His English is that of educated London around 1600, used with unusual range — from the formal verse of kings to the prose of tavern scenes, puns, dialect and nonsense.

::: warning Shakespeare and the "invented words" myth
✗ *Shakespeare invented 1,700 (or 3,000) English words.*
✓ Shakespeare used a very large vocabulary — Marvin Spevack's concordance (1968–1980) counts about 29,000 different word forms in his works, counting inflected forms separately — and many words are first recorded in his plays in the *Oxford English Dictionary*. But "first recorded in Shakespeare" is not "invented by Shakespeare". The dictionary's early readers read Shakespeare far more thoroughly than his contemporaries, and searches of the large digital collections of early printed books have found earlier uses for many words once credited to him. What is clear is that he used the word-formation of his time with great freedom.

Use "first recorded in" when you mean the dictionary's evidence, and keep "coined by" for cases where a writer is known to have made a word.
:::

Shakespeare's freedom is clearest in **conversion** — using a word of one class as another without changing its form ([[word-formation/conversion]]): Cleopatra complains of Caesar, *He words me, girls, he words me* (*Antony and Cleopatra*), and in *Measure for Measure* a character says that *Lord Angelo dukes it well*. Conversion was easy because English had lost most of the endings that once marked word class ([[history-of-english/middle-english#def-levelling]]); it remains one of the most productive processes in English today (*to google*, *to text*).

::: example Reading the 1609 Sonnets {#ex-sonnet}
Here are the first two lines of Sonnet 18 as printed in the Quarto of 1609: *Shall I compare thee to a Summers day? / Thou art more louely and more temperate:*. List every difference from a modern edition and explain it.
::: solution
1. *thee*, *Thou*: the singular pronoun of intimacy ([[#def-thou]]) — the poem addresses a beloved person. A modern edition keeps it, because it is part of the poem, not of the printing.
2. *art*: the verb form agreeing with *thou*.
3. *Summers*: no apostrophe. The possessive apostrophe was not yet regular in 1609 (it became general for the singular in the late seventeenth century); modern editions print *summer's*. The capital *S* reflects the printers' habit of capitalising important nouns, common in the seventeenth century.
4. *louely*: the letter *u* for the sound /v/. Printers then used *v* at the start of a word and *u* elsewhere, whatever the sound (*vnto*, *loue*); the modern rule, *u* for the vowel and *v* for the consonant, was settled during the seventeenth century. The same applies to *i* and *j*.
5. *temperate:* with a colon where a modern editor might put a full stop: punctuation in early printing followed the rhythm of reading aloud more than grammar.

None of these differences is grammatical except *thou*/*art*; the rest is printing convention. That is typical of Early Modern texts: most of the strangeness on the page is spelling and typography, which modernised editions remove.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Two features of this period are easier for Chinese readers than for many others. First, **conversion** — *He words me* — resembles the flexibility of Classical Chinese, where a word could serve as noun or verb according to its position (词类活用: 春风又绿江南岸, where 绿 ‘green’ is used as a verb, ‘makes green’). In both cases, a language with little inflection lets position decide the word class. Second, the **register** of the King James Bible and Shakespeare in modern English is something like that of 文言 phrases in modern Chinese: they are understood, quoted and admired, and they give a passage solemnity, but they should not be used in ordinary speech or writing. *Thou art* and *he goeth* in an essay sound as odd as 之乎者也 in a text message. Learn to recognise them, and to enjoy them in literature ([[reading-literature/drama-shakespeare]]), without imitating them.
:::

::: history
Early Modern English was the first period in which English speakers wrote at length *about* their own language: spelling reformers such as John Hart (*An Orthographie*, 1569) and William Bullokar, who wanted spelling to match speech; schoolmasters such as Mulcaster; the inkhorn debaters; and the first grammarians and lexicographers, whose work is told in [[history-of-english/dictionaries-grammars]]. Their writings are also the main evidence for the pronunciation of the period ([[history-of-english/great-vowel-shift]]). In 1664 the Royal Society set up a committee to improve the English language, and in its *History of the Royal Society* (1667) Thomas Sprat praised the Society's preference for "a close, naked, natural way of speaking", plain prose rather than ornament — an early statement of the plain style still recommended in [[style-and-rhetoric/clarity]].
:::

## Where this leads

The variation of Early Modern English — in spelling, in grammar, in the choice between *thou* and *you* — led eighteenth-century writers to call for rules and dictionaries: [[history-of-english/dictionaries-grammars]]. The first English settlements in North America (1607, 1620) took the English of this period across the Atlantic ([[history-of-english/spread-of-english]]). For Shakespeare's verse and drama see [[reading-literature/drama-shakespeare]] and [[reading-literature/metre-rhythm]]; for the Latin and Greek roots borrowed in this period, [[word-formation/greek-latin-roots]].

::: summary
- **Early Modern English** (c. 1500–1700) is the English of printing, the Renaissance, the Reformation Bibles and Shakespeare ([[#def-emode]]).
- Caxton's press (1476) spread London written English; spelling became uniform gradually, by the mid-seventeenth century, through printers' and schoolmasters' practice ([[#rule-printing]]).
- Scholars respelled words after Latin (*debt*, *doubt*, *receipt*), sometimes wrongly (*island*), and some respellings changed the pronunciation (*fault*, *perfect*) ([[#rule-etymological]]).
- Thousands of Latin and Greek words entered English; the **inkhorn controversy** debated them, and those that filled gaps and were widely used survived ([[#def-inkhorn]], [[#rule-survival]]).
- *Thou* expressed intimacy or contempt beside polite *you* ([[#def-thou]]); *-s* replaced *-eth* ([[#rule-eth]]); *do* became the auxiliary of questions and negatives along an S-curve ([[#rule-do]]); *its* appeared in the 1590s.
- The King James Bible (1611) is largely a revision of Tyndale (1526–1530s) and was conservative in language from the start.
- Shakespeare used a huge vocabulary and free conversion, but "first recorded in Shakespeare" does not mean "invented by him".
:::

In a play of about 1600, which pronoun would you expect in each situation, and why? (a) A lord speaking to his servant. (b) A servant answering the lord. (c) A man praying to God. (d) Two gentlemen meeting politely. (e) One gentleman insulting another.
::: solution
(a) *thou* — superior to inferior. (b) *you* — the polite form to a superior. (c) *thou* — intimacy and reverence in prayer. (d) *you* — the polite form between equals of the upper classes. (e) *thou* — used to an equal, it was an insult (as in Sir Toby's *if thou thou'st him*).
:::

::: exercise Spelling pronunciations {level=2}
*Fault*, *perfect* and *adventure* are now pronounced with the letters that were added to their spelling; *debt*, *doubt* and *salmon* are not. Explain the difference, and say what kind of evidence would show when the pronunciation changed.
::: solution
In all six words, a letter was added to the spelling to show a Latin model ([[#rule-etymological]]). In *fault*, *perfect* and *adventure*, speakers gradually began to pronounce the added letter, because they learned the word from print — a **spelling pronunciation** — and the new pronunciation became standard. In *debt*, *doubt* and *salmon* the older pronunciation held. Evidence for the date of the change would come from the descriptions of orthoepists and lexicographers (Johnson, for example, noted the pronunciation of *fault* without /l/), from rhymes (*fault* rhyming with *ought* or with *salt*) and from the spellings of writers who spelled by ear.
:::

::: exercise Explain the S-curve {level=2}
Using the plot of [[#rule-do]], explain in three or four sentences why a language change often follows an S-shaped curve, and what Kroch's finding about the curves for different sentence types suggests.
::: solution
A new form spreads slowly at first, when few speakers use it and most people rarely hear it; faster in the middle, when it is common enough to be heard and imitated everywhere; and slowly at the end, as the last users of the old form — older speakers, conservative styles, fixed expressions — remain. The result is a logistic or S-shaped curve. Kroch (1989) found that the curves for *do* in different kinds of sentence rose at the same rate, which suggests that they were not separate changes but one change in the grammar — the loss of the main verb's ability to move before the subject or *not* — appearing in several contexts at once.
:::

::: exercise Compare three versions {level=3}
Compare these three versions of the first line of the Lord's Prayer — Old English (c. 1000): *Fæder ure þu þe eart on heofonum*; Wycliffite (1380s): *Oure fadir that art in heuenes*; King James (1611): *Our father which art in heauen*. Describe two changes from each version to the next, and one feature that the King James version keeps from earlier English but modern English has lost.
::: hint
Look at the order of *father* and *our*, the relative pronoun, the ending of *heaven*, and *art*.
:::
::: solution
**Old English → Wycliffite**: the order *fæder ure* becomes *oure fadir* (possessive before the noun); the relative particle *þe* is replaced by *that*; the dative plural *heofonum* becomes *heuenes*, with the preposition *in* and an *-es* plural.

**Wycliffite → King James**: the relative *that* becomes *which*; the plural *heuenes* becomes the singular *heauen* (following the translators' choice and the Bishops' Bible); spelling moves toward the modern (*our*, *father*).

**Kept in the King James version, lost today**: the verb *art* agreeing with the implied *thou*, and *which* used of a person. A modern version (*Our Father in heaven*) avoids both.
:::

::: exercise A short report {level=3}
Write a paragraph of 150–200 words for a class handout answering the question "What did printing do to English?" Use at least two dated facts and say what printing did **not** do.
::: solution
A model answer: "Printing came to England when William Caxton set up a press at Westminster in 1476, after printing the first book in English at Bruges a few years earlier. Its first effect was to multiply identical copies: instead of a scribe in each region writing in his own dialect, a single London printing of a book reached readers everywhere, so the written English of London became the model for the country. Printing also created a larger reading public and a demand for books in English. But printing did not fix English spelling overnight. Caxton himself spelled the same word in different ways, and compositors adjusted spellings to fill their lines; printed spelling became largely uniform only by the middle of the seventeenth century, through the habits of printers and the advice of schoolmasters like Richard Mulcaster (1582). Nor did printing stop the pronunciation from changing: the Great Vowel Shift continued, so the spellings that printers settled on soon recorded an older pronunciation. Printing standardised the written language, not speech."
:::
