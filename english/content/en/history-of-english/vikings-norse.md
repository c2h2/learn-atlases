Read this sentence: *They took the knife and the eggs from the window, and the husband called his fellows to get the skis.* Apart from *the*, *and*, *from*, *to* and *his*, almost every content word in it — *they*, *took*, *knife*, *egg*, *window*, *husband*, *called*, *fellow*, *get*, *ski* — came into English from Old Norse, the language of the Vikings. (*Ski* is a modern borrowing from Norwegian; the rest are medieval.) That is surprising. Languages borrow words for new things — *coffee*, *algebra*, *karaoke* — but they rarely borrow words as ordinary as *take*, *egg* and *window*, and almost never pronouns. English borrowed *they*, *their* and *them*.

This chapter explains how that happened: who the Scandinavian settlers were, where they settled, how their language was related to Old English, and which kinds of evidence show their influence — place names, loanwords, sounds and perhaps grammar. It also sets out a long debate: whether contact with Norse speakers helped English to lose its inflections.

## Raids, armies and settlers

::: definition Old Norse, the Danelaw {#def-norse}
**Old Norse** (古诺尔斯语) is the North Germanic language of Scandinavia in the Viking Age (roughly 800–1050) and after, the ancestor of Icelandic, Faroese, Norwegian, Danish and Swedish ([[history-of-english/english-and-its-relatives#def-germanic]]). The settlers in England came mainly from Denmark (in the east and north-east) and from Norway, often by way of Ireland and Scotland (in the north-west), so their speech was East and West Norse.

The **Danelaw** is the name, first used in the eleventh century, for the part of England in which Danish law and custom prevailed: roughly the land north and east of a line from London to Chester, including Yorkshire, Lincolnshire, East Anglia and the East Midlands.
:::

The Viking Age in England is usually dated from the attack on the monastery of Lindisfarne, off the coast of Northumbria, in 793. The *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*, a year-by-year record in Old English, reports that dreadful omens — whirlwinds, lightning and fiery dragons in the sky — were followed by famine, and then by the raid:

> *… earmlice hæþenra manna hergung adilegode Godes cyrican in Lindisfarnaee þurh reaflac and mansliht.*

A translation made for this atlas: ‘… the harrying of heathen men miserably destroyed God's church on Lindisfarne through plunder and slaughter.’ (The manuscripts date the raid to 8 January; historians usually correct it to 8 June, when ships could sail.)

For seventy years there were raids. In 865 a large army — the *micel here* ‘great army’ of the Chronicle — landed in East Anglia and stayed. Within a few years it had conquered Northumbria, East Anglia and much of Mercia; its leaders shared out land, and their men began to plough it. Only Wessex held out. After King Alfred's victory at Edington in 878, a treaty with the Danish king Guthrum divided England along a line running roughly up the Thames, the Lea and Watling Street. In the tenth century the kings of Wessex reconquered the Danelaw, but the Scandinavian population stayed; and in the early eleventh century England was conquered again, by Swein Forkbeard (1013) and his son **Cnut**, who ruled England, Denmark and for a time Norway, from 1016 to 1035.

So Scandinavian influence came in three phases — raiding, settlement in the Danelaw, and an Anglo-Danish kingdom under Cnut — and it was deepest where the settlement was densest, in the north and east.

## The evidence of names

The clearest evidence of settlement is on the map. Hundreds of English place names contain Norse elements, and they cluster in the old Danelaw:

| element | Norse meaning | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *-by* | ‘farm, village’ | *Derby*, *Grimsby*, *Whitby*, *Rugby* |
| *-thorpe* | ‘secondary settlement, hamlet’ | *Scunthorpe*, *Althorp* |
| *-thwaite* | ‘clearing, meadow’ | *Braithwaite*, *Thornthwaite* (mainly in Cumbria) |
| *-toft* | ‘site of a house’ | *Lowestoft*, *Langtoft* |
| *kirk-* | ‘church’ | *Kirkby*, *Ormskirk* |

Many of these are **hybrids**: a Norse element with an English personal name, or the reverse, as if English and Scandinavian settlers lived side by side. Personal names in Danelaw documents of the tenth to twelfth centuries are often Norse, and Norse words survive in great numbers in the dialects of the north and east: *beck* ‘stream’, *garth* ‘yard’, *laik* ‘play’, *lop* ‘flea’. The street names of York end in *-gate* (*Micklegate*, *Coppergate*), from Norse *gata* ‘street’, not from English *gate*.

::: example Reading a place name {#ex-names}
Analyse the names *Kirkby*, *Grimsby*, *Thornthwaite* and *Ashby*. For each, give the elements, their language and meaning, and say whether the name is purely Norse or a hybrid.
::: solution
1. *Kirkby*: Norse *kirkja* ‘church’ + *bý* ‘village’ — ‘village with a church’. Purely Norse. (Its English equivalent would be something like *Churchton*.)
2. *Grimsby*: the Norse personal name *Grímr*, in the genitive (*-s*), + *bý* — ‘Grímr's village’. Purely Norse.
3. *Thornthwaite*: English *thorn* (Norse had the same word, *þorn*) + Norse *þveit* ‘clearing’ — ‘clearing with thorn bushes’. Either purely Norse or a hybrid: the first element could be from either language, a typical difficulty when the two languages shared a word.
4. *Ashby*: Old English *æsc* ‘ash tree’ or its Norse cognate *askr* + *bý*. In some Ashbys the early spellings have *Ask-*, which points to the Norse form (test 1 of [[#rule-norse-tests]], applied to a name); others have *Ash-* or *Esh-* from the start, which points to an English first element and a hybrid name.

The lesson for evidence: a name can only be analysed from its **earliest recorded spellings** (often in *Domesday Book*, 1086), not from its modern form, which may have been reshaped. That is why the place-name surveys collect every early spelling of every name.
:::
:::

## Two closely related languages

Old English and Old Norse were both Germanic, and they had separated only a few centuries before the Viking Age. A great many words were nearly the same in both:

| Old English | Old Norse | modern English |
|---|---|---|
| *hūs* | *hús* | *house* |
| *mann* | *maðr* (stem *mann-*) | *man* |
| *wīf* | *víf* | *wife* |
| *gōd* | *góðr* | *good* |
| *cuman* | *koma* | *come* |
| *sittan* | *sitja* | *sit* |

The differences lay mostly in the **endings** and in a few regular sound correspondences. This has two consequences. First, English and Norse speakers could probably understand each other to a useful degree, especially with practice; Matthew Townend (*Language and History in Viking Age England*, 2002) argued from the evidence of names and texts that the two languages were mutually intelligible in this pragmatic sense, so that people did not need to be bilingual to communicate. Second, when a Norse word and its English cognate both existed, it is often impossible to tell which one a modern word continues — unless their forms differ in a way we can recognise.

::: theorem Recognising a Norse loanword {#rule-norse-tests}
A modern English word is probably from Old Norse (rather than inherited from Old English) when its form shows a Norse development that Old English did not undergo. The main tests:

1. ***sk* where English had *sh*.** Old English *sc* became /ʃ/ (*scip* → *ship*, *scyrte* → *shirt*); Norse kept /sk/. So *sky*, *skin*, *skill*, *skirt*, *scrape*, *bask* are Norse.
2. **Hard *g* and *k* before front vowels.** Old English softened them (*cirice* → *church*, *giefan* /j/ → Middle English *yeven*); Norse did not. So *get*, *give*, *gift*, *egg*, *keel* and Scots *kirk* show Norse forms.
3. **The diphthong *ei* / *ai*** where Old English had *ā*: Norse *nei* → *nay* beside English *no*; Norse *heill* → *hale* beside English *whole*; Norse *þeir* → *they*.
4. **Distribution and date.** A word first recorded in the north or east, after the Viking Age, with no Old English ancestor, and with a Norse cognate, is a good candidate.

These tests are signs, not proofs, and they apply only to words that have the relevant sounds; many Norse loans pass none of them and are identified from their meaning or their first appearance (*take*, *call*, *want*, *window*, *husband*, *law*).
:::

::: proof
The tests follow from the sound changes described in [[history-of-english/old-english#rule-oe-spelling]]: Old English had palatalised *c* and *g* next to front vowels and turned *sc* into /ʃ/ well before the Viking Age, while North Germanic did not. A word that escaped those changes must have entered English after they were over, from a language that lacked them. The tests have limits. *sk* also appears in later loans from other languages — *school* (Latin), *sketch* (Dutch), *skeleton* (Greek), *ski* (modern Norwegian) — so the first test only works for words recorded in Middle English. And dialect mixture means that some words exist in both forms (see the doublets below), so each word must be checked in a historical dictionary such as the *Oxford English Dictionary* or the *Middle English Dictionary*.
:::

::: warning Not every *sk* is Norse
✗ *School, sketch and skeleton begin with* sk, *so they are Viking words.*
✓ *School* came from Latin *schola* (through Old English *scōl*, later respelled), *sketch* from Dutch in the seventeenth century, *skeleton* from Greek in the sixteenth century. The *sk* test only identifies Norse words among the words that were already in English by the Middle Ages, and even then it needs checking.

The reverse mistake is also common: ✗ *Words without* sk *or hard* g *cannot be Norse.* *Take*, *call*, *window* and *law* pass none of the tests and are certainly Norse.
:::

## What English borrowed

The Norse loanwords in standard English number several hundred; Northern dialects and Scots have many more. What is unusual is not their number but their **kind**. French and Latin gave English the vocabulary of government, law, learning and fashion ([[history-of-english/norman-french]]); Norse gave it everyday words.

- **Nouns**: *sky*, *egg*, *leg*, *skin*, *knife*, *window* (*vindauga* ‘wind-eye’, replacing Old English *ēagþyrel* ‘eye-hole’), *husband* (*húsbóndi* ‘householder’), *fellow* (*félagi* ‘partner, one who lays down money with another’), *sister*, *skill*, *law*, *bank* (of a river), *root*.
- **Verbs**: *take* (replacing Old English *niman*), *get*, *give* (in its modern form), *call*, *want*, *seem*, *raise*, *thrive*, and probably *die* (Old English usually said *steorfan*, which survives, narrowed, as *starve*).
- **Adjectives**: *ill*, *ugly*, *wrong*, *weak*, *loose*, *low*, *happy* (from *hap* ‘luck’), *odd*.
- **Grammatical words**: *they*, *their*, *them*; *both*; *same*; *though*; *till*; and the *fro* of *to and fro*.

Sometimes Norse changed the **meaning** of an English word without replacing it. Old English *drēam* meant ‘joy, music, revelry’; its modern sense ‘dream’ is that of Norse *draumr*. Old English *dwellan* meant ‘lead astray, delay’; the modern sense ‘live in a place’ follows Norse *dvelja* ‘stay’.

::: example Finding the Norse words {#ex-find}
Underline the words of Norse origin in this short text, and for each say how you could know (form, meaning, or only from a dictionary).

*The weak old man felt ill and called his sister. She took a knife, cut some bread, and gave him two eggs. Then they sat by the window and looked at the sky.*
::: solution
- *weak* — dictionary (Norse *veikr*; the native form *wāc* did not survive).
- *ill* — dictionary (Norse *illr*).
- *called* — dictionary (Norse *kalla*).
- *sister* — dictionary; the native form was *sweostor*, which would have given something like *swuster*; Norse *systir* replaced it.
- *took* — dictionary (Norse *taka*; it replaced *niman*).
- *knife* — dictionary (late Old English *cnīf*, from Norse *knífr*).
- *gave* — form: the hard *g* before a front vowel ([[#rule-norse-tests]], test 2).
- *eggs* — form: the hard *g* (test 2).
- *they* — form: the diphthong (test 3) and the *th-* of a Norse pronoun.
- *window* — meaning and form: a Norse compound, *vind* + *auga*.
- *sky* — form: *sk* (test 1). In Old Norse *ský* meant ‘cloud’; it replaced English *heofon* in the physical sense, leaving *heaven* for the religious one.

Eleven words. The rest — *the*, *old*, *man*, *felt*, *his*, *she*, *a*, *cut* (origin uncertain), *some*, *bread*, *him*, *two*, *then*, *sat*, *by*, *looked*, *at* — are native or of unclear origin. Notice how many of the Norse words are among the commonest words of the language: this is why the Danish linguist Otto Jespersen wrote, in *Growth and Structure of the English Language* (1905), that an Englishman "cannot thrive or be ill or die without Scandinavian words".
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which word is most likely to be a Norse loan?
- [ ] *shell*
- [x] *skull*
- [ ] *school*
- [ ] *shall*
::: solution
*Skull* first appears in Middle English with *sk*, and it has Scandinavian cognates (Norwegian *skul*); it is usually traced to a Norse source, although the dictionaries call its origin not quite certain. *Shell* and *shall* have the native development of *sc* to *sh*. *School* has *sk*, but it comes from Latin, not from Norse ([[#rule-norse-tests]] and the warning above).

## *They*, *their*, *them*

The most striking Norse loans are the third-person plural pronouns. Old English had *hīe* ‘they’, *hira* ‘their’ and *him* ‘to them’ ([[history-of-english/old-english]]), which in Middle English often became *he*, *here* and *hem*. These were dangerously close to the singular *he*, *her* and *him*. The Norse forms *þeir*, *þeira*, *þeim* were distinct, and they spread.

::: theorem The *th*-pronouns spread from north to south {#rule-they}
The Norse third-person plural pronouns entered English in the north and east and spread south over several centuries, one form at a time:

1. ***they*** first: it is the normal form in the north by about 1200, in the *Ormulum*, a long verse work written around 1180, probably in Lincolnshire (*þeȝȝ*, with *þeȝȝm* and *þeȝȝre*);
2. ***their*** and ***them*** later: in London English around 1400, Chaucer uses *they* but still the native *hem* ‘them’ and *hir* or *here* ‘their’;
3. by the end of the fifteenth century, *them* and *their* had become usual in London writing too, and the *h*-forms survived only in dialect and in the unstressed *'em* (*Let 'em go*), which continues *hem*, not *them*.
:::

::: proof
The order follows from the evidence of dated and localised texts: northern texts have the *th*-forms first, southern texts last, and in each area the subject form *they* comes before the others. Why *they* first is debated; one suggestion is that subject pronouns are stressed more often than object and possessive ones, and so needed to be distinct more urgently. The pronouns are evidence of something more than trade or conquest. A language does not usually borrow its pronouns from another unless the two communities are in close daily contact, with much intermarriage and many bilingual or semi-bilingual speakers over generations — the situation that the place names suggest for the Danelaw.
:::

::: example Pronouns in Chaucer's London {#ex-chaucer-pronouns}
In the opening of the *Canterbury Tales* (c. 1387), Chaucer writes that people go to Canterbury to seek the martyr *That hem hath holpen, whan that they were seke* (‘who has helped them when they were sick’). In another line he writes of birds: *So priketh hem nature in hir corages* (‘so nature spurs them in their hearts’). Identify the third-person plural pronouns and their origin, and say what the lines show about the spread of the Norse forms.
::: solution
- *they* (subject): Norse *þeir*.
- *hem* (object, twice): native, from Old English *him*/*heom*.
- *hir* (possessive): native, from Old English *hira*.

In late fourteenth-century London, then, the Norse subject pronoun had arrived but the object and possessive forms had not; the system was mixed, exactly as [[#rule-they]] states. *Holpen* is also worth noticing: Chaucer still uses the old strong participle of *help* ([[history-of-english/old-english]]). The Middle English of these lines is read in full in [[history-of-english/middle-english]].
:::
:::

### Other grammatical influence

Several other features of northern English have been linked with Norse:

- the present plural ***are*** of *be* (Old English *sindon* in the south; the northern Old English form was *aron*, and the Norse plural *eru* may have strengthened it);
- the third-person singular ***-s*** (*he sings*), which replaced *-eth* in the north and spread south in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries ([[history-of-english/early-modern-english]]) — although *-s* is already attested in late Old English Northumbrian, so its origin is probably native, with contact perhaps helping it along;
- ***shall*** and ***will*** in northern forms (*sal*), and the infinitive marker *at* in northern dialects (*at do* ‘to do’, as in Norse *at gera*).

## Did the Vikings simplify English?

The largest question is about inflections. Between about 1000 and 1200, English lost most of its case endings, its grammatical gender and many verb endings ([[history-of-english/middle-english]]). The loss began, and went fastest, in the north and east — the old Danelaw.

::: theorem Contact and the loss of inflections: the main positions {#rule-contact}
Scholars agree that inflections were weakened in all of English for internal reasons, and that the process went faster in the north. They disagree about the role of Norse:

1. **Internal change.** Germanic stress on the first syllable ([[history-of-english/english-and-its-relatives#def-germanic]]) weakened unstressed endings everywhere: *-a*, *-e*, *-u* and *-an*, *-on*, *-um* all fell together as *-e*, *-en*, and then often disappeared. The loss would have happened without the Vikings, only more slowly.
2. **Contact acceleration.** In the Danelaw, English and Norse speakers shared many word stems but used different endings (Old English *stān*, *stānas*; Norse *steinn*, *steinar*). In communication between them, the stems carried the meaning and the endings were confusing and redundant, so speakers relied on word order and prepositions and dropped endings faster. This is the view of most handbooks, including Baugh & Cable.
3. **Creolisation and stronger claims.** Some scholars have argued that Middle English arose from a creole or a mixed language (Poussa 1982 argued for an Anglo-Norse creole in the East Midlands), or even, in a much-debated book by Emonds and Faarlund (*English: The Language of the Vikings*, 2014), that Middle English is really a descendant of Norse. Most specialists reject these stronger claims, pointing out that the core vocabulary and grammar of Middle English continue Old English.
:::

::: proof
The evidence for (2) is geographical and chronological: inflectional loss appears earliest in northern texts and latest in southern and Kentish ones, and the areas of fastest loss are those with the densest Norse settlement. Situations of contact between closely related languages are known elsewhere to favour simplification, especially when many adults learn the other language imperfectly. But the evidence is indirect, because very little was written in the Danelaw in the tenth and eleventh centuries, and the north of England had already been weakening its endings in Old English texts. Against (3), linguists note that a creole usually forms in a sharply unequal society, from languages that are not mutually intelligible, and that Middle English keeps far more inherited grammar (strong verbs, the genitive *-s*, the Old English verbal system) than a creole would. The careful conclusion is that contact probably sped up and spread a change already under way, without being its only cause.
:::

::: example Weighing an argument {#ex-weigh}
A student writes: "English lost its case endings because of the Vikings." Rewrite the sentence so that it says only what the evidence supports, and explain each change.
::: solution
A possible rewrite: *English lost most of its case endings between about 1000 and 1200, partly through the general weakening of unstressed syllables, and the loss seems to have been faster in the north and east, where contact with Old Norse speakers was closest and probably accelerated it.*

Changes: (1) the dates are added, so the claim can be checked; (2) "because of" becomes "partly through" plus a second cause, since the internal weakening happened everywhere; (3) the evidence — faster loss in the Danelaw — is named; (4) "probably accelerated" replaces a causal certainty with the strength of claim the evidence allows. This is the hedging of [[modality/hedging-boosting]], used here not for politeness but for accuracy.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Borrowing pronouns is so rare that it is worth comparing with a case Chinese readers know. Spoken Mandarin has one third-person pronoun, *tā*, for ‘he’, ‘she’ and ‘it’. In the 1910s and 1920s, writers translating European literature wanted to distinguish them in writing, and the character 她 was proposed for ‘she’ (the poet and linguist Liu Bannong 刘半农 is usually credited with promoting it, around 1920); 它 and 牠 were used for ‘it’. This was a change in the **written** language, made consciously under the influence of translation, and the spoken language did not change: all three are still *tā*. The English case is the opposite: *they*, *their* and *them* changed the **spoken** language first, without anyone deciding it, through generations of everyday contact between speakers. For a Chinese learner, the practical lesson is about register: Norse loans are not "foreign" in feel at all. *Take*, *get*, *give*, *egg*, *sky* and *they* are among the most basic words of English and belong to every register.
:::

::: history
The study of Scandinavian influence began with place names and dialect words in the nineteenth century, and became systematic with the Swedish scholar Erik Björkman's *Scandinavian Loan-Words in Middle English* (1900–1902), which set out the phonological tests used in [[#rule-norse-tests]]. Otto Jespersen's *Growth and Structure of the English Language* (1905) made the topic popular, with its picture of English and Norse speakers mixing in daily life. The English Place-Name Society, founded in 1923, has since published county-by-county surveys that map Scandinavian names in detail. More recent work has turned to the social history of contact: how much bilingualism there was, how long Norse was spoken in England (probably into the eleventh century in parts of the north, though the evidence is thin), and how the influence can be separated from the internal changes of English.
:::

## Where this leads

Norse words went on entering written English throughout the Middle Ages, as northern texts were written down. The next wave of contact was with French after 1066 ([[history-of-english/norman-french]]), and the results of both — a simpler inflectional system, a mixed vocabulary, dialects that differed sharply between north and south — are the subject of [[history-of-english/middle-english]]. How the *-s* of the third person won against *-eth* is told in [[history-of-english/early-modern-english]]; how sound changes and borrowings spread through a community, in [[linguistics/variation-change]].

::: summary
- Scandinavian influence came through raids (from 793), settlement in the **Danelaw** (from the 870s) and the Anglo-Danish kingdom of Cnut (1016–1035) ([[#def-norse]]).
- Place names in *-by*, *-thorpe*, *-thwaite* and *-toft* show dense settlement in the north and east.
- Old English and Old Norse were closely related and probably mutually intelligible to a useful degree; they differed most in their endings.
- Norse loans can often be recognised by *sk* (*sky*), hard *g* and *k* (*give*, *egg*) and the diphthong *ei* (*they*, *nay*), but the tests need a dictionary check ([[#rule-norse-tests]]).
- Norse gave English everyday words — *take*, *get*, *call*, *egg*, *window*, *husband*, *ill*, *ugly* — and the pronouns *they*, *their* and *them*, which spread south one at a time ([[#rule-they]]).
- Contact probably speeded up the loss of inflections in the north; stronger claims of creolisation or Norse descent are not accepted by most specialists ([[#rule-contact]]).
:::

*skin* — test 1 (*sk*); *get* — test 2 (hard *g* before a front vowel; the Old English verb, *-gietan*, had /j/, and Middle English *yeten* shows the native development); *they* — test 3 (the diphthong); *take* — none: it is known from its first appearance in late Old English and its Norse source *taka*; *window* — none of the sound tests, but its Norse compound structure (*vind* + *auga*) identifies it; *egg* — test 2.
:::

::: exercise Chaucer's pronouns {level=2}
This sentence was written for the atlas in the London English of Chaucer's time (it is not a quotation): *And hem and hir children they ne kepte nat.* (*kepte* ‘looked after’; *ne … nat* ‘not’.) Which pronouns would a London writer of 1500 probably have replaced, and with what?
::: solution
*hem* ‘them’ → *them*; *hir* ‘their’ → *their*. *They* was already Norse in Chaucer. (The double negative *ne … nat* would also have given way to *not* alone during the fifteenth century.)
:::

::: exercise Evaluate a claim {level=3}
Write 150–200 words evaluating the claim: "English is really a Scandinavian language, because it borrowed its pronouns and everyday words from Old Norse."
::: hint
Distinguish borrowing from inheritance ([[history-of-english/english-and-its-relatives#rule-inheritance]]), and consider what has *not* been borrowed.
:::
::: solution
A model answer: "The claim rests on real facts — English did take *they*, *their*, *them*, *take*, *get*, *egg* and hundreds of other everyday words from Old Norse — but it does not follow that English is Scandinavian. Languages are classified by what they inherit, not by what they borrow. Most of the basic vocabulary of English — *I*, *you*, *we*, *he*, *be*, *have*, *go*, *come*, *the*, *and*, the numerals and most body parts — continues Old English, not Norse, and so does its core grammar: the strong verbs, the past in *-ed*, the genitive *-s*, the modal verbs. Where English and Norse words were cognate, they were often nearly identical, which made borrowing easy and makes the influence look larger than it is. The borrowing of pronouns shows unusually intense contact in the Danelaw, and that contact probably sped up the loss of inflections, but most specialists reject the view, argued by Emonds and Faarlund (2014), that Middle English is a descendant of Norse. English is a West Germanic language with a strong North Germanic layer."
:::

::: exercise Research a word {level=3}
Choose one of these words and write a short history (80–120 words) using a historical dictionary with etymologies (the *Oxford English Dictionary*, the *Middle English Dictionary* or an etymological dictionary): *husband*, *fellow*, *law*, *ugly*, *happy*. Give the Norse source, its meaning, the first period in which the word is recorded in English, and how its meaning has changed.
::: solution
A model for *husband*: "*Husband* comes from late Old English *hūsbonda*, borrowed from Old Norse *húsbóndi*, literally ‘house-dweller’, the master of a household (*hús* ‘house’ + *bóndi*, a form of the verb *búa* ‘dwell, farm’). In late Old English it meant the head of a household, and in Middle English it came to mean ‘a man joined to a woman in marriage’, replacing native *wer*. The older meaning survives in the verb *to husband* ‘to use carefully’ and in *husbandry* ‘farming’. The word is therefore both a loanword and an example of how a borrowed word can narrow its meaning." Your answer should give the dictionary you used and its dates; for *fellow*, *law*, *ugly* and *happy*, the Norse sources are *félagi*, *lagu* (plural of *lag* ‘something laid down’), *uggligr* ‘dreadful’ and *happ* ‘luck’ (with an English suffix).
