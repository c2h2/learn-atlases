Li Na is writing to a new pen friend in Ireland.

> *My uncle is a doctor and my cousin is studying in Shanghai.*

Her pen friend writes back: *Is that your mother's brother or your father's brother? And is your cousin a boy or a girl?* In Chinese, Li Na would have said 舅舅 and 表妹, and her friend would have known at once. English *uncle* covers 叔叔, 伯伯, 舅舅, 姑父 and 姨父, and *cousin* covers eight Chinese words. Everyday words are where English and Chinese often divide the world differently.

This chapter brings together the core words for the topics that come up in everyday life: people and family, home, food and drink, work and study, shopping, travel, health and the weather. It cannot list every word — no chapter could — but for each topic it gives a set of very common words, shows where British and American English differ, and points out the places where Chinese speakers often go wrong. It also explains **how** to learn words by topic, because research shows that some ways of grouping words help and others get in the way.

## How to group words for learning

::: definition Thematic set and semantic set {#def-thematic-semantic}
A **thematic set** is a group of words that belong to one situation or story but are different kinds of words: *kitchen*, *cook*, *pan*, *hot*, *delicious*, *wash up*. A **semantic set** is a group of words of the same kind and similar meaning: *apple*, *pear*, *peach*, *plum* (all fruit), or *left*/*right*, *hot*/*cold* (opposites).
:::

Textbooks often present new words in semantic sets: a page of fruit, a page of colours, a page of opposites. It looks logical. But several studies have found that learning **new** words that are very similar at the same time can make learning slower, because the learner mixes them up. Thomas Tinkham (1993) and Rob Waring (1997) found that learners took longer to learn lists of related new words (for example, all fruit) than lists of unrelated words; Tinkham (1997) found that thematic groups did not cause this problem and sometimes helped. Paul Nation (2000) reviewed this research and gave guidelines for teachers.

::: theorem Learn new words in themes, not in sets of near neighbours {#rule-thematic-sets}
When you learn **new** words, group them by situation or theme (going to the doctor: *appointment*, *cough*, *prescription*, *feel better*), not as lists of very similar words or opposites learned at the same time (*left* and *right*, *borrow* and *lend*). Once one word of a pair is well known, the other can be added and contrasted with it.
:::

::: proof
The studies above suggest that similar new words compete in memory: when *left* and *right* are learned together, the learner remembers that the answer is "one of those two" but not which. Words in a theme are different from each other, so they do not compete; and they belong to one scene, so each word helps the learner remember the others. The effect is a tendency found in several experiments, not a law, and later studies have not always found it; Nation (2000) recommends avoiding sets of near-synonyms and opposites for **new** words, and using them for revising words that are already partly known.
:::

| *niece* / *nephew* | a brother's or sister's daughter / son | 侄女、外甥女 / 侄子、外甥 |

| *son* / *daughter* | | *children* (plural of *child*) |
| *brother-in-law* / *sister-in-law* | a husband's or wife's brother/sister, or a sister's/brother's husband/wife | plural *brothers-in-law* |

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese kinship words show the side of the family (父系/母系), whether the person is older or younger, and sometimes whether they are related by blood or by marriage. English usually does not. When the difference matters, English adds words: *my mother's brother*, *my older sister*, *my cousin on my father's side*. *Big brother* and *little sister* are used in informal speech. Note also the order of names: Chinese names put the family name first (*Li Na*: family name *Li*), and many English speakers expect it last. In international settings Chinese speakers often keep their own order and write the family name in capitals (*LI Na*) or explain it: *My family name is Li.*
:::

::: example Saying who someone is {#ex-family}
Translate these family members into natural English. Add words only when the difference is important.

1. 我舅舅（in a sentence about where he lives）
2. 我表妹（in a sentence about her age）
3. 我嫂子
4. 我外公
::: solution
1. *My uncle lives in Guangzhou.* If the side of the family matters: *My mother's brother lives in Guangzhou.*
2. *My cousin is twelve.* — *cousin* says nothing about sex or age, but here the age is in the sentence. If you want to show she is a girl: *My cousin — she's twelve — …* (the pronoun shows it).
3. *My sister-in-law* — or, to be exact, *my brother's wife* (*sister-in-law* can also be a wife's or husband's sister).
4. *My grandfather* — or, to show the side, *my mother's father* / *my grandfather on my mother's side*.

English speakers usually use the general word and add details only when needed.
:::

## Travel and transport

| British English | American English | Chinese |
|---|---|---|
| *underground* / *the Tube* (London) | *subway* | 地铁 (*metro* is used in many cities) |
| *return ticket* | *round-trip ticket* | 往返票 |
| *single ticket* | *one-way ticket* | 单程票 |
| *timetable* | *schedule* | 时刻表 |
| *motorway* | *highway*, *freeway* | 高速公路 |
| *petrol* | *gas* (*gasoline*) | 汽油 |
| *car park* | *parking lot* | 停车场 |
| *pavement* | *sidewalk* | 人行道 |

::: theorem British and American everyday words {#rule-bre-ame}
Where British and American English use different words for the same everyday thing, both words are standard English. In a piece of writing, choose one variety and use it consistently; in speech, use the words you know, and learn to recognise the other variety's words.
:::

::: proof
Most differences between the two varieties are in everyday vocabulary — transport, food, buildings, school — because these words developed after the two communities separated, often for things that did not exist before (*petrol*/*gas*, *motorway*/*freeway*). Grammar and the core vocabulary are almost the same. Both varieties are understood everywhere English is used, so mixing them rarely causes a real problem in speech; in writing, a consistent choice looks more careful. The course *World Englishes* looks at the two varieties in [[world-englishes/british-american]].
:::

Words used in both: *platform*, *station*, *airport*, *flight*, *passport*, *luggage* (uncountable; AmE also *baggage*), *get on*/*get off* a bus or train, *get in*/*get out of* a car or taxi. Asking for and giving directions is practised in [[everyday-english/directions-travel]].

::: quiz
An American friend says *We took the subway and then stood in line for an hour.* What would a British speaker usually say?
- [ ] *We took the motorway and then stood in queue for an hour.*
- [x] *We took the underground and then queued for an hour.*
- [ ] *We took the pavement and then stood in line for an hour.*
- [ ] *We took the tube and then made a line for an hour.*
::: solution
British English: *underground* (or *the Tube* in London) and *queue* (verb: *we queued*; or *we stood in a queue*). *Motorway* is a road for cars and *pavement* is for walking. Both versions are correct English; each is normal in its own country.
:::
:::

| word | note |
|---|---|

| *storm*, *thunder*, *lightning* | 暴风雨、雷、闪电 |

::: warning "Big rain", "eat medicine" and other translations
Many everyday mistakes come from translating Chinese collocations word by word.

- ✗ *There was a big rain yesterday.* ✓ *There was heavy rain yesterday.* ✓ *It rained heavily yesterday.*
- ✗ *I need to eat medicine.* ✓ *I need to take some medicine.*
- ✗ *My body is not comfortable.* ✓ *I don't feel well.* ✓ *I'm not feeling very well.*
- ✗ *Today is very hot.* (understood, but less natural) ✓ *It's very hot today.*
- ✗ *I go to home.* ✓ *I go home.*

These are collocations: the right partner word has to be learned with each word ([[core-vocabulary/phrases-and-chunks]]).
:::

::: example Correcting a message about health and weather {#ex-health-weather}
Correct this message from Zhou Min to her English teacher.

> *Dear Ms Taylor, I'm sorry I can't come to class today. Yesterday there was a big rain and I got wet. Now my body is not comfortable and I have a fever. I will eat medicine and stay in home. Best wishes, Zhou Min*
::: solution
1. *a big rain* → *heavy rain* (*there was heavy rain*, without *a*).
2. *my body is not comfortable* → *I don't feel well*.
3. *I have a fever* is correct (BrE also *I've got a temperature*).
4. *eat medicine* → *take some medicine*.
5. *stay in home* → *stay at home* (BrE) or *stay home* (AmE, also informal BrE).

Corrected:

> *Dear Ms Taylor, I'm sorry I can't come to class today. Yesterday there was heavy rain and I got wet. Now I don't feel well and I have a fever. I will take some medicine and stay at home. Best wishes, Zhou Min*
:::
:::

Put these words into three thematic sets — *at the doctor's*, *in a supermarket*, *at a station* — and add one more word of your own to each set.

*platform, cough, trolley, checkout, appointment, timetable, receipt, prescription, single ticket*
::: solution
- **At the doctor's:** *cough*, *appointment*, *prescription* — add, for example, *temperature* or *sore throat*.
- **In a supermarket:** *trolley*, *checkout*, *receipt* — add, for example, *queue* or *basket*.
- **At a station:** *platform*, *timetable*, *single ticket* — add, for example, *return ticket* or *delayed*.

Each set mixes nouns, verbs and phrases that you need in one situation. That is a thematic set ([[#def-thematic-semantic]]): the words do not compete with each other, and the situation helps you remember them.

Rewrite this British note for an American reader.

::: solution
> *Take the subway to the station, buy a round-trip ticket, and check the schedule. There's a parking lot next to the platform, and you can buy gas at the rest stop on the highway.*

::: application A topic notebook
Keep one page (or one file) for each topic in this chapter. On each page, write the words you meet in that situation, with a short example: *make an appointment*, *Can I try this on?*, *heavy rain*. Add the British or American form if you meet both. When you prepare for a real situation — a visit to the doctor, a train journey — read the page aloud the day before. [[core-vocabulary/learning-and-remembering]] explains how to review such pages.

::: history
Organising a beginner's vocabulary by everyday topics has a long history in language teaching, but it became a standard after the Council of Europe's work on adult language learning in the 1970s. *The Threshold Level* (Jan van Ek, 1975) described what a learner needs to do in English and listed topics such as personal identification, house and home, travel, health and welfare, education, shopping, food and drink, and weather, with the words and phrases needed for each. *Waystage* (1977) did the same for a lower level. The *Common European Framework of Reference for Languages* (2001) continued this approach, describing language use in personal, public, occupational and educational domains.
:::

::: summary
- Learn new words in **thematic sets** (words of one situation), not in sets of very similar words or opposites learned at the same time; research by Tinkham (1993, 1997) and Waring (1997) found that similar new words can interfere with each other.
- Family words: English *uncle*, *aunt* and *cousin* cover many Chinese words; add details (*my mother's brother*) only when they matter.

- Watch countability: *furniture*, *homework*, *work*, *rice*, *bread*, *luggage* are uncountable; *job* and *vegetable* are countable.
- Learn collocations with topic words: *heavy rain*, *strong wind*, *take medicine*, *have breakfast*, *go home*.
- Keep a page for each topic and prepare it before a real situation.
:::
