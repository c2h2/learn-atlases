Zhang Lei has learned that *borrow* means 借. One day he needs a pen, and he asks a classmate:

✗ *Can you borrow me your pen?*

His classmate understands him, but the sentence is wrong. In English, *borrow* means ‘take something and give it back later’. The word for ‘give something for a short time’ is *lend*. The correct question is *Can you lend me your pen?* or *Can I borrow your pen?* Chinese uses one word, 借, for both directions. English uses two.

Zhang Lei knew a translation of *borrow*. He did not know everything about it: which way the pen moves, and which sentence pattern the word takes. This chapter asks a simple question with a long answer: what does it mean to **know a word**? We look at the three parts of word knowledge — form, meaning and use — and at the difference between recognising a word and using it. We also see why one Chinese translation is rarely the whole story.

## Three parts of knowing a word

When you meet a new word, there is a lot to learn about it. Paul Nation, a researcher in New Zealand, put the parts in a simple table in his book *Learning Vocabulary in Another Language* (2001; 2nd edition 2013). Other researchers use similar lists. The table has three main parts.

::: definition Form, meaning and use {#def-form-meaning-use}
Knowing a word has three parts.

1. **Form**: what the word sounds like (its pronunciation and stress), what it looks like (its spelling), and which parts it is made of (for example *un-* + *happy*).
2. **Meaning**: which idea or thing the word stands for, how far that meaning reaches, and which other words it makes you think of.
3. **Use**: the grammar of the word (for example, whether a noun is countable), the words it often goes with (its **collocations**), and when it is suitable — in speech or writing, with friends or in a formal letter.
:::

Each part can be known well, a little, or not at all. You may know how *island* is spelled but not that the *s* is silent: /ˈaɪlənd/. You may know that *heavy* means 重, but not that English says *heavy rain* where Chinese says 大雨. Word knowledge grows step by step. It is not "known" or "unknown".

Here is the table for one word, *advice*:

| part | what you need to know | *advice* |
|---|---|---|
| form — spoken | pronunciation, stress | /ədˈvaɪs/, stress on the second syllable |
| form — written | spelling | *advice* (noun) — not *advise* (verb, /ədˈvaɪz/) |
| form — parts | word parts | *advise*, *adviser*, *advisable* belong to the same family |
| meaning | idea | ‘an opinion about what someone should do’, close to 建议 |
| use — grammar | patterns | uncountable: *some advice*, *a piece of advice*, not ✗ *an advice* |
| use — collocations | common partners | *give advice*, *take someone's advice*, *ask for advice* |
| use — register | where it fits | neutral; common in speech and writing |

That is a lot for one word. Nobody learns all of it at the first meeting. But it shows why a word list with one Chinese meaning per word is only a start.

## Form: sound, spelling and parts

### Spoken form

To understand a word in speech, you must recognise its sound. To use it in speech, you must say it clearly enough. The stress is part of the form. *Hotel* has stress on the second syllable: /həʊˈtel/ in General British (GB), /hoʊˈtel/ in General American (GA). If you put the stress on the first syllable, listeners may not recognise the word. [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics]] explains word stress, and [[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet]] explains the symbols used here.

Some words change their stress when they change their word class:

- *record* (noun) /ˈrekɔːd/ (GB), /ˈrekərd/ (GA): *She broke the world record.*
- *record* (verb) /rɪˈkɔːd/ (GB), /rɪˈkɔːrd/ (GA): *We recorded the song yesterday.*

### Written form

English spelling does not always show the sound. *Knife* has a silent *k*. *Comfortable* is usually said with three syllables, /ˈkʌmftəbl/, not four. Words with the same sound can have different spellings and meanings: *right* and *write*, *their* and *there*. So for each new word, learn the spelling **and** the sound. [[sounds-of-english/spelling-and-sound]] shows the main patterns.

### Word parts

Many words are made of smaller parts. *Unhappy* is *un-* ‘not’ + *happy*. *Teacher* is *teach* + *-er* ‘a person who does this’. If you know the parts, you can often guess a new word. The chapter [[core-vocabulary/word-families]] shows how this works, and the course *Word Formation* goes deeper ([[word-formation/prefixes]], [[word-formation/suffixes]]).

::: quiz
Which of these is part of the **form** of a word?
- [ ] the words it often goes with
- [x] where its stress falls
- [ ] whether it is formal or informal
- [ ] the idea it stands for
::: solution
Stress belongs to the spoken form. Collocations and formality belong to **use**. The idea a word stands for is its **meaning**.
:::
:::

## Meaning: why one translation is not enough

The easiest way to learn a new word is to connect it with a word you already know. For Chinese speakers, that is usually a Chinese word. This is a good first step. Research on vocabulary learning shows that learning a word with its first-language translation is a fast way to start (Nation 2013 reviews these studies). The problem comes later, if you believe that the English word and the Chinese word are the **same**.

::: theorem Words in two languages rarely match exactly {#rule-no-exact-match}
An English word and its usual Chinese translation often share a central meaning, but they usually differ at the edges. One Chinese word may need several English words, and one English word may need several Chinese words, depending on the sentence.
:::

::: proof
Each language divides the world in its own way. Chinese has one verb, 借, for borrowing and lending; English has two. English has one word, *cousin*, for 堂兄、堂妹、表哥、表姐 and the rest; Chinese shows age, sex and the side of the family. Neither language is more exact. Each one makes some differences that the other does not make. The chapter [[core-vocabulary/everyday-topics]] has more examples from family words.
:::

Here are some common verbs where one Chinese word matches several English words:

| Chinese | English | example |
|---|---|---|
| 看 | *look at* (turn your eyes to something) | *Look at this photo.* |
| | *see* (notice with your eyes) | *I can see the sea from my window.* |
| | *watch* (look at something that moves or changes) | *We watched a film last night.* |
| | *read* (look at words) | *She reads the news every morning.* |
| 学 | *learn* (get knowledge or a skill) | *He learned to swim when he was six.* |
| | *study* (spend time learning, often at school) | *She studies medicine at university.* |
| 穿/戴 | *wear* (have clothes, glasses, a watch on your body) | *He wears glasses.* |
| | *put on* (the action of dressing) | *Put on your coat — it's cold.* |
| 说 | *say* (words) | *She said, "Good morning."* |
| | *tell* (a person something) | *Tell me your name.* |
| | *speak* (a language; formally) | *Do you speak French?* |
| | *talk* (have a conversation) | *We talked for an hour.* |

The other direction is just as important. One English word can need different Chinese words. *Play* is 玩 in *The children are playing in the garden*, 踢 in *play football*, 弹 in *play the piano* and 演 in *play a part in a film*. *Open* is 开 in *open the door*, but *turn on* is 开 in *turn on the light*. Many English words have several related senses. This is called **polysemy** (一词多义). A dictionary lists them as numbered senses, and [[core-vocabulary/using-a-dictionary]] shows how to choose the right one.

::: example Choosing among look, see and watch {#ex-look-see-watch}
Choose *look at*, *see* or *watch* for each gap, and say why.

1. *Did you ___ the match on TV last night?*
2. *___ the board, please.*
3. *I can't ___ anything — it's too dark.*
::: solution
1. *watch* — a match moves and changes over time, and we follow it for a while: *Did you watch the match on TV last night?*
2. *look at* — the speaker asks people to turn their eyes to something on purpose: *Look at the board, please.*
3. *see* — the speaker means being able to notice things with the eyes, not choosing to look: *I can't see anything — it's too dark.*

All three are 看 (or 看见) in Chinese. In English, the choice depends on whether the action is on purpose (*look at*, *watch*), whether the thing moves (*watch*), or whether it simply reaches your eyes (*see*).
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese often marks the result of an action with a second verb: 看 is the action, 看见 is the result. English often uses different words instead: *look* (action) and *see* (result); *listen* (action) and *hear* (result); *look for* (action) and *find* (result). So *I looked for my keys but I didn't find them* is normal English, like 我找了钥匙，可是没找到. ✗ *I found my keys for an hour* is wrong, because *find* is the result, not the search.
:::

## Use: grammar, partners and situations

Knowing the form and the meaning of a word is not enough to use it. You also need to know how it fits into a sentence.

### Grammar

Each word has its own grammar. Some examples:

- *Advice*, *information*, *furniture* and *homework* are **uncountable** in English. We say *some information*, not ✗ *an information* or ✗ *informations*. See [[nouns-and-articles/countable-uncountable]].
- *Discuss* takes a direct object: *We discussed the plan.* There is no *about*, even though Chinese says 讨论一下关于……的问题.
- *Suggest* can be followed by *-ing* or by a *that* clause, but not by an object + *to*: *I suggest taking a taxi.* *I suggest that we take a taxi.* ✗ *I suggest you to take a taxi.*
- *Enjoy* needs an object: *I enjoyed the party.* *Did you enjoy yourself?* ✗ *I enjoyed very much.*

### Collocations

Words like to appear with certain other words. These partners are called **collocations**. English says *make a mistake* and *do homework*, *heavy rain* and *strong coffee*, *take a photo* and *catch a cold*. Other combinations are possible to understand, but they sound strange to an expert speaker. [[core-vocabulary/phrases-and-chunks]] shows how to learn words together with their partners, and the course *Collocation and Register* studies them in detail ([[collocation-and-register/what-is-collocation]]).

### Situation and register

Some words fit only certain situations. *Kids* is informal; *children* is neutral. *Purchase* is formal; *buy* is neutral. *Hi* is fine with a friend; *Dear Professor Brown* belongs in an email to a teacher. This is called **register**. At level 1 it is enough to notice labels such as *informal* or *formal* in a learner's dictionary. The later chapter [[collocation-and-register/register]] goes further.

::: warning One word, one pattern
A common mistake is to copy the Chinese pattern into English.

- ✗ *Can you borrow me your pen?* ✓ *Can you lend me your pen?* ✓ *Can I borrow your pen?*
- ✗ *We discussed about the problem.* ✓ *We discussed the problem.* ✓ *We talked about the problem.*
- ✗ *He gave me many advices.* ✓ *He gave me a lot of advice.*

In each case the learner knew the meaning, but not the use. When you learn a word, learn one full, natural example sentence with it.
:::

::: theorem Learn a word together with an example of its use {#rule-learn-with-example}
For words you want to **use**, record at least one natural phrase or sentence that shows the word's grammar and a common partner — for example *lend someone something*, *make a mistake*, *a piece of advice* — not the bare word and its translation.
:::

::: proof
The translation gives you the meaning quickly. A phrase gives you the use, which a translation cannot give. Researchers who study collocation and formulaic language (for example Wray 2002) show that expert speakers store many words in ready-made phrases. A phrase also helps memory: it gives the word a context. For words that you only need to recognise, a translation may be enough at first.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *She gave me an useful advice.*
- [ ] *She gave me a useful advice.*
- [x] *She gave me some useful advice.*
- [ ] *She gave me some useful advices.*
::: solution
*Advice* is uncountable, so it takes no *a* and no plural *-s*. *Some useful advice* is correct. For one item, say *a useful piece of advice*.
:::
:::

## Recognising a word and using it

There are two ways to know a word.

::: definition Receptive and productive knowledge {#def-receptive-productive}
**Receptive** knowledge (接受性词汇) is what you need to understand a word when you hear it or read it. **Productive** knowledge (产出性词汇) is what you need to use the word yourself, in speech or writing. Your **receptive vocabulary** is the set of words you can understand; your **productive vocabulary** is the set of words you can use.
:::

Productive knowledge is harder. To understand *heavy rain*, you only need to know *heavy* and *rain*. To say it, you must remember that English chooses *heavy*, not *big*. To understand *lend*, it is enough to see the context. To use it, you must remember which way the pen moves.

Studies that test the same learners both ways (for example Webb 2008) usually find that receptive vocabulary is larger than productive vocabulary. Most learners can understand many words that they cannot yet use. This is normal, and it is true in your first language too.

::: example Receptive or productive? {#ex-receptive-productive}
Which kind of knowledge does each task need?

1. Reading a story and understanding the word *ancient*.
2. Writing an email and choosing between *borrow* and *lend*.
3. Hearing *Could you open the window?* and opening it.
4. Telling a friend about the weather: *There was heavy rain yesterday.*
::: solution
1. Receptive — reading.
2. Productive — writing, and you must choose the correct word yourself.
3. Receptive — listening; the action shows that the listener understood.
4. Productive — speaking, and you must choose the collocation *heavy rain*.
:::
:::

::: intuition Which words to learn deeply
You do not need productive knowledge of every word. A good plan is: learn the most frequent words well enough to use them, and learn less frequent words well enough to recognise them. The next chapter, [[core-vocabulary/frequency-and-coverage]], explains which words are the most frequent.
:::

## Putting it together: a word in full

Here is how the three parts work together for one useful verb.

::: example A full analysis of suggest {#ex-suggest}
Describe the form, meaning and use of *suggest*, with examples.
::: solution
**Form.** Pronunciation /səˈdʒest/ in both GB and GA (some American speakers also say /səɡˈdʒest/). Stress on the second syllable. Spelling with *gg*. Family: *suggestion* (noun), *suggestive* (adjective, with a different meaning).

**Meaning.** ‘Give an idea for someone to think about or do’, close to 建议. It is softer than *tell* or *order*.

**Use.**
- Grammar: *suggest* + noun (*She suggested a new plan.*), *suggest* + *-ing* (*He suggested meeting at six.*), *suggest* + *that* clause (*I suggest that we leave early.* — *that* can be left out: *I suggest we leave early.*). Not ✗ *suggest someone to do something*.
- Collocations: *strongly suggest*, *make a suggestion*, *have a suggestion*.
- Register: neutral. In speech, *Why don't we …?* and *How about …?* are more common ways to make a suggestion with friends.

A good example sentence to learn: *I suggest that we meet at the station.*（我建议我们在车站见面。）
:::
:::

::: example Correcting a short message {#ex-correct-message}
Wang Fang wrote this message to a classmate. Find the word mistakes and correct them.

> *Hi Tom! Can you borrow me your notes from today's class? I missed it because I was ill. The teacher said us that the test is on Friday. Thanks!*
::: solution
1. *borrow me your notes* → *lend me your notes* (or *Can I borrow your notes*). *Borrow* means taking; Tom gives the notes, so he *lends* them.
2. *said us that* → *told us that*. *Say* is not followed by the listener as an object; *tell* is: *tell someone something*. *The teacher said that …* is also correct.

The rest is correct. *Missed it* is good: *miss a class* means 没去上课. A corrected version:

> *Hi Tom! Can you lend me your notes from today's class? I missed it because I was ill. The teacher told us that the test is on Friday. Thanks!*
:::
:::

::: application Making a word card
When you meet a word you want to use, write a card or a note with: the word and its sound (/ˈ…/ or a mark for the stress), a short meaning (a Chinese translation is fine), the word class, one natural example sentence, and one or two common partners. For *borrow*: /ˈbɒrəʊ/ (GB), /ˈbɑːroʊ/ (GA) · 借（进来） · verb · *Can I borrow your pen?* · *borrow something from someone*. The chapter [[core-vocabulary/learning-and-remembering]] shows how to review such cards.
:::

::: quiz
Zhang Lei can understand *lend* when he reads it, but he still says *borrow* when he means *lend*. What is missing?
- [ ] receptive knowledge of *lend*
- [x] productive knowledge of *lend*
- [ ] knowledge of the spelling of *lend*
- [ ] knowledge of the pronunciation of *lend*
::: solution
He understands the word, so he has receptive knowledge. He cannot yet choose it himself when he speaks, so his productive knowledge is missing.
:::

::: history
The question "what does it mean to know a word?" became a common topic in language teaching after the applied linguist Jack Richards listed the kinds of word knowledge in an article in *TESOL Quarterly* in 1976. Paul Nation built on this in *Teaching and Learning Vocabulary* (1990) and then in *Learning Vocabulary in Another Language* (2001), where he set out the table of form, meaning and use, each known receptively and productively. The second edition (2013) is still a standard reference for teachers and researchers.
:::

The next chapter, [[core-vocabulary/frequency-and-coverage]], asks which words to learn first. [[core-vocabulary/word-families]] shows how the "word parts" side of form helps you learn many words at once. [[core-vocabulary/using-a-dictionary]] shows where to find the form, meaning and use of a word, and [[core-vocabulary/phrases-and-chunks]] shows how to learn words in useful groups. Later courses go deeper: *Word Formation* on word parts ([[word-formation/morphemes]]) and *Collocation and Register* on use ([[collocation-and-register/what-is-collocation]]).

::: summary
- Knowing a word has three parts: **form** (sound, spelling, word parts), **meaning**, and **use** (grammar, collocations, register).
- Word knowledge grows step by step; a word is rarely simply "known" or "unknown".
- A Chinese translation is a good start, but English and Chinese words rarely match exactly: 借 is *borrow* or *lend*; 看 is *look at*, *see*, *watch* or *read*.
- Each word has its own grammar: *advice* is uncountable, *discuss* has no *about*, *suggest* is not followed by *someone to do*.
- **Receptive** knowledge lets you understand a word; **productive** knowledge lets you use it. Receptive vocabulary is usually larger.
- For words you want to use, learn a natural phrase or sentence, not just a translation.
:::

::: exercise Borrow or lend? {level=1}
Complete each sentence with the correct form of *borrow* or *lend*.

1. *Could you ___ me ten yuan? I'll pay you back tomorrow.*
2. *I ___ this book from the library last week.*
3. *My brother never ___ me his bike.*
4. *Can I ___ your phone for a minute?*
::: solution
1. *lend* — *lend someone something*.
2. *borrowed* — *borrow something from somewhere*.
3. *lends* — present simple, third person.
4. *borrow* — *Can I borrow …?* (the speaker takes the phone).
:::
:::

::: exercise Say or tell? {level=1}
Complete with *say*, *said*, *tell* or *told*.

1. *Please ___ me the way to the station.*
2. *She ___ that she was tired.*
3. *He ___ us a funny story.*
4. *What did you ___ ? I didn't hear you.*
::: solution
1. *tell* — *tell someone something*.
2. *said* — *say (that)*; *told* is possible only with a listener: *She told me that she was tired.*
3. *told* — *tell someone a story*.
4. *say* — no listener is named.
:::
:::

::: exercise Learn or study? {level=2}
Choose *learn* or *study* and explain your choice.

1. *I ___ a lot from my grandmother.*
2. *She is ___ing law at university.*
3. *It took me a year to ___ to drive.*
::: solution
1. *learn* (*learned*/*learnt* in the past) — getting knowledge from a person, not formal study: *I learned a lot from my grandmother.*
2. *study* — a subject at school or university, over time: *She is studying law at university.*
3. *learn* — a skill, with *to* + verb: *It took me a year to learn to drive.* (*Learn how to drive* is also correct.)

Note: *learned* and *learnt* are both used in British English; American English mainly uses *learned*.
:::
:::

3. Ahmed reads a menu and sees *grilled*.
4. Sofia asks a shop assistant for a *receipt*.
::: solution

::: exercise Find the mistakes {level=2}
Each sentence has one word mistake. Correct it.

1. *Can you give me some informations about the course?*
2. *We discussed about the trip for an hour.*
3. *My teacher suggested me to read more.*
4. *I watched the photo on my phone.*
::: hint
Think about grammar (countable or uncountable, which pattern) and meaning (moving or still).
:::
::: solution
1. *some information* — *information* is uncountable.
2. *We discussed the trip* (or *We talked about the trip*) — *discuss* takes a direct object.
3. *My teacher suggested that I read more* (or *suggested reading more*, or *told me to read more*).
4. *I looked at the photo* — a photo does not move, and the viewer chooses to look; *watch* is for things that move or change.

- **Meaning.** 决定; ‘choose after thinking’.

::: exercise One Chinese word, several English words {level=3}
The Chinese verb 开 has several English translations. Write one natural English sentence for each meaning: (a) 开门, (b) 开灯, (c) 开车, (d) 开会. Then say in one sentence what this tells you about learning words through translation.

Possible sentences:

- (a) *Could you open the door, please?* — *open*.
- (b) *It's dark — let's turn on the light.* (*switch on* is also common, especially in British English.)
