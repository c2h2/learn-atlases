Look at these four sentences:

- *We need to **decide** today.*
- *It was a difficult **decision**.*
- *She is a **decisive** leader.*
- *I'm still **undecided**.*

If you know *decide*, you can probably understand the other three words, even if you have never seen them before. They share a part, *decide* (or *decis-*), and their endings and beginnings tell you something: *-ion* makes a noun, *-ive* makes an adjective, *un-* means ‘not’. Words that are built on the same base like this form a **word family**.

Families matter for two reasons. First, they make learning cheaper: when you learn one member well, the others cost much less. This is why research on vocabulary size counts word families ([[core-vocabulary/frequency-and-coverage]]). Second, they help you use English correctly: English often needs a different form for a different job in the sentence, where Chinese can use the same form. 决定 is a verb in 我们决定了 and a noun in 这个决定, but English needs *decide* and *decision*. This chapter shows how families are built, which endings show which word class, and how to use families to learn faster.

## What is a word family?

::: definition Word family {#def-word-family}
A **word family** (词族) is a base word together with its **inflected forms** and its common **derived forms**. For example, the family of *care* includes *cares*, *cared*, *caring* (inflected forms) and *careful*, *carefully*, *careless*, *carelessly* (derived forms).
:::

The two kinds of family member are made in different ways.

::: definition Inflection and derivation {#def-inflection-derivation}
**Inflection** (屈折变化) adds an ending that shows a grammatical meaning, such as plural, past tense or comparison, without making a new word: *book* → *books*, *walk* → *walked*, *tall* → *taller*. **Derivation** (派生) adds a **prefix** (前缀) or a **suffix** (后缀) to make a new word, often with a new meaning or a new word class: *happy* → *unhappy*, *happy* → *happiness*.
:::

English has only eight regular inflectional endings, and you meet them in [[core-grammar/word-classes]]:

| ending | job | example |
|---|---|---|
| *-s* | plural noun | *book* → *books* |
| *-'s* | possessive | *Tom* → *Tom's* |
| *-s* | verb, third person singular present | *work* → *works* |
| *-ed* | past tense | *work* → *worked* |
| *-ed* / *-en* | past participle | *work* → *worked*; *take* → *taken* |
| *-ing* | *-ing* form | *work* → *working* |
| *-er* | comparative | *fast* → *faster* |
| *-est* | superlative | *fast* → *fastest* |

Derivational affixes are many more. Linguists Laurie Bauer and Paul Nation (1993) arranged them in seven levels, from the most frequent and regular (such as *un-*, *-er*, *-ness*, *-less* and *-ly*) to the rare and irregular. The chapter does not need the full list; the most useful affixes are in the tables below. The course *Word Formation* covers them in detail: [[word-formation/prefixes]] and [[word-formation/suffixes]].

## Endings that show word class

The most useful thing to learn about suffixes is which word class they usually make. When you meet a new word, its ending often tells you whether it is a noun, an adjective, a verb or an adverb — and that tells you how it fits in the sentence.

::: theorem Common suffixes and the word class they make {#rule-suffix-class}
These suffixes usually make the word class shown.

| word class | suffixes | examples |
|---|---|---|
| noun | *-tion*, *-sion*, *-ment*, *-ness*, *-ity*, *-ance*, *-ence*, *-er*, *-or*, *-ist* | *information*, *decision*, *payment*, *kindness*, *ability*, *distance*, *difference*, *teacher*, *actor*, *artist* |
| adjective | *-ful*, *-less*, *-ous*, *-al*, *-ive*, *-able*, *-ible*, *-y*, *-ic* | *useful*, *careless*, *dangerous*, *national*, *active*, *comfortable*, *possible*, *sunny*, *economic* |
| verb | *-ize* (often *-ise* in British English), *-en*, *-ify* | *organize*/*organise*, *widen*, *simplify* |
| adverb | *-ly* (added to an adjective) | *quickly*, *carefully*, *happily* |

This is a strong tendency, not an absolute rule: *-ly* also makes some adjectives (*friendly*, *lovely*, *lonely*), and *-al* also makes some nouns (*arrival*, *refusal*).
:::

::: proof
These endings came into English with the words that carry them — many of them from French and Latin (*-tion*, *-ity*, *-ous*, *-able*), others from Old English (*-ness*, *-ful*, *-less*, *-ly*). Because each ending appeared in many words of the same class, it became a signal of that class. Exceptions exist because words have their own histories. *Friendly* comes from an old adjective ending meaning ‘like’, which is related to the adverb ending *-ly*, so *-ly* marks adjectives in some words and adverbs in others. A learner's dictionary always shows the word class ([[core-vocabulary/using-a-dictionary]]).
:::

::: quiz
Which word ending in *-ly* is an **adjective**, not an adverb?
- [ ] *quickly*
- [x] *friendly*
- [ ] *happily*
- [ ] *carefully*
::: solution
*Friendly* is an adjective: *a friendly dog*, *She is very friendly.* It has no adverb form of its own; we say *in a friendly way*. The other three are adverbs made from the adjectives *quick*, *happy* and *careful*. Other *-ly* adjectives are *lovely*, *lonely*, *ugly* and *elderly*.
:::
:::

::: example Which form fits the gap? {#ex-which-form}
Complete each sentence with the correct member of the family *success* / *succeed* / *successful* / *successfully*, and explain how you chose.

1. *She is a very ___ businesswoman.*
2. *Hard work is the key to ___.*
3. *If you practise, you will ___.*
4. *The team ___ finished the project.*
::: solution
Look at the job of the gap in the sentence.

1. Between *very* and the noun *businesswoman*: an adjective is needed. → *successful* (*-ful* makes adjectives).
2. After the preposition *to* and *the key to …*: a noun is needed. → *success*.
3. After *will*: a base form of a verb is needed. → *succeed*.
4. Between the subject *The team* and the verb *finished*: an adverb is needed to describe how. → *successfully* (*-ly* added to the adjective).

Full answers: *She is a very successful businesswoman. Hard work is the key to success. If you practise, you will succeed. The team successfully finished the project.*
:::
:::

::: warning Using the wrong member of the family
Chinese learners often know the right word family but choose the wrong member, because the Chinese word does not change.

- ✗ *I am very success.* ✓ *I am very successful.*
- ✗ *We must protection the environment.* ✓ *We must protect the environment.*
- ✗ *He speaks English very fluent.* ✓ *He speaks English very fluently.*
- ✗ *It is important to make a decide.* ✓ *It is important to make a decision.*

The listener usually understands, but the sentence sounds wrong. Before you say or write the word, ask: does this place in the sentence need a noun, a verb, an adjective or an adverb?
:::

## Prefixes that change meaning

Prefixes usually do not change the word class. They change the meaning. A small number of prefixes are very common and worth learning early.

| prefix | meaning | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *un-* | not; reverse an action | *unhappy*, *unusual*; *unlock*, *undo* |
| *in-*, *im-*, *il-*, *ir-* | not | *incorrect*, *impossible*, *illegal*, *irregular* |
| *dis-* | not; opposite | *dislike*, *disagree*, *dishonest* |
| *re-* | again | *rewrite*, *rebuild*, *reopen* |
| *mis-* | wrongly | *misunderstand*, *misspell* |
| *pre-* | before | *preview*, *prepay* |
| *over-* | too much | *oversleep*, *overcooked* |

The forms *im-*, *il-* and *ir-* are versions of *in-* used before certain letters: *im-* before *p*, *b* and *m* (*impossible*, *imbalance*, *immature*), *il-* before *l* (*illegal*), *ir-* before *r* (*irregular*). This is not a rule you can use to make new words freely — *un-* is far more common for new adjectives — but it helps you recognise them.

::: example Guessing a new word from its parts {#ex-guess-parts}
Li Wei reads: *The story was so unbelievable that nobody trusted him.* He does not know *unbelievable*. How can he work out its meaning?
::: solution
He splits the word into parts:

- *un-* = ‘not’
- *believe* = 相信 (a word he knows)
- *-able* = ‘can be …’ (makes an adjective: *washable* ‘can be washed’)

So *unbelievable* = ‘not able to be believed’, 难以置信的. The context agrees: *nobody trusted him*. Note the spelling change: the final *e* of *believe* is dropped before *-able*. [[word-formation/spelling-changes]] explains these changes.
:::
:::

::: quiz
What does *rewrite* mean?
- [ ] write badly
- [x] write again
- [ ] not write
- [ ] write before
::: solution
*Re-* means ‘again’, so *rewrite* means ‘write again’ (often to make better). ‘Write badly’ would need *mis-*; ‘write before’ would need *pre-*.
:::
:::

## Sound and spelling inside a family

Family members do not always sound the same. When a suffix is added, the stress can move, and the vowels can change. This is one of the hardest parts of word families for listeners.

| word | GB | GA | stress |
|---|---|---|---|
| *photograph* | /ˈfəʊtəɡrɑːf/ | /ˈfoʊtəɡræf/ | first syllable |
| *photography* | /fəˈtɒɡrəfi/ | /fəˈtɑːɡrəfi/ | second syllable |
| *photographic* | /ˌfəʊtəˈɡræfɪk/ | /ˌfoʊtəˈɡræfɪk/ | third syllable |
| *economy* | /ɪˈkɒnəmi/ | /ɪˈkɑːnəmi/ | second syllable |
| *economic* | /ˌiːkəˈnɒmɪk/ | /ˌiːkəˈnɑːmɪk/ | third syllable |

Two useful tendencies: words ending in *-ic* and *-tion*/*-sion* usually have their stress on the syllable just before the ending (*eCOnomic*, *phoTOgraphic*, *deCIsion*, *inforMAtion*), and words ending in *-graphy* and *-logy* put it on the syllable just before *-graphy* or *-logy* (*phoTOgraphy*, *geOgraphy*, *biOlogy*). [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics]] explains how to hear and say stress, and the course *Connected Speech* returns to it in [[connected-speech/word-stress-patterns]].

Spelling can change too: *happy* → *happiness* (*y* becomes *i*), *decide* → *decision* (*d* becomes *s*), *begin* → *beginning* (the consonant doubles). See [[word-formation/spelling-changes]].

::: example Building a family table {#ex-family-table}
Make a table of the family of *employ* with nouns, adjectives and a verb, and write one example sentence for each.
::: solution
| word | class | meaning | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *employ* | verb | give someone a paid job | *The factory employs 300 people.* |
| *employer* | noun | the person or company that gives the job | *My employer pays me every month.* |
| *employee* | noun | the person who has the job | *She is an employee of the bank.* |
| *employment* | noun | having a paid job | *Employment in the city has grown.* |
| *unemployed* | adjective | without a job | *He has been unemployed since March.* |
| *unemployment* | noun | the state of having no job; the number of people without jobs | *Unemployment fell last year.* |

Notice the pair *employer* / *employee*: *-er* is the person who does the action, *-ee* the person to whom it is done (also *trainer* / *trainee*, *interviewer* / *interviewee*).
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
In Chinese, one form often works as a noun, a verb or an adjective: 决定, 发展, 幸福, 成功. The word class is shown by the position in the sentence and by small words such as 的 and 地. English also lets some words change class without a change of form — *work*, *love*, *answer* and *change* can each be a noun or a verb. This is called **conversion** (转类), and [[word-formation/conversion]] looks at it. But for a large number of English words the class is shown by an ending: *develop* / *development*, *happy* / *happiness* / *happily*, *succeed* / *success* / *successful*. So when you learn an English word, learn its family too, and notice which ending marks which class. In Chinese, 地 can turn an adjective into an adverbial (快乐地); in English, *-ly* does a similar job (*happily*).
:::

::: warning False family members
Some words look like members of a family but have a different meaning. Learn them separately.

- *hard* ‘difficult; with effort’ — *hardly* ‘almost not’: *He hardly worked* means he did very little work, not that he worked hard. ✓ *He worked hard.*
- *late* — *lately* ‘recently’: *I've been tired lately.* ✗ *I arrived lately.* ✓ *I arrived late.*
- *near* — *nearly* ‘almost’: *I nearly missed the bus.*
- *high* — *highly* ‘very; very well’: *highly recommended*, *highly skilled*. ✓ *The bird flew high.*
:::

## Learning with families

::: theorem Learn the family, not just the word {#rule-learn-family}
When you learn a high-frequency word, also learn the most common members of its family — usually one or two forms in other word classes — and note how the stress and spelling change. When you meet an unknown word, look for a base and affixes you already know before you use the dictionary.
:::

::: proof
Research on reading shows that learners can often recognise a new family member if they know the base and the affix, so families make vocabulary growth faster ([[core-vocabulary/frequency-and-coverage]] counts word families for this reason). But recognition and use are different ([[core-vocabulary/knowing-a-word]]): learners often understand *employment* but still say ✗ *I am looking for an employ*. So for productive use, the family members must be learned and practised, not only guessed. And guessing has limits: false family members (*hardly*, *lately*) and words whose parts do not add up (*understand*) must be checked in context or in a dictionary.
:::

::: example Correcting word-class mistakes in a short text {#ex-correct-text}
Find and correct the five word-class mistakes in this paragraph.

> *My city has changed a lot. Ten years ago, the streets were not very safety. Now there are many beauty parks, and people walk there happy in the evening. The government has done a lot of develop, and the air is clean. I think the change is very success.*
::: solution
1. *not very safety* → *not very safe* — after *very* we need an adjective; *safety* is a noun.
2. *many beauty parks* → *many beautiful parks* — before a noun we need an adjective; *beauty* is a noun.
3. *walk there happy* → *walk there happily* — to describe how people walk we need an adverb.
4. *a lot of develop* → *a lot of development* (or *a lot of work on development*) — after *a lot of* we need a noun.
5. *very success* → *very successful* — after *very* we need an adjective.

*The air is clean* is correct: after *is*, the adjective *clean* is right.
:::
:::

::: history
The word family as a unit for counting and teaching vocabulary goes back to the early word lists. Michael West's *General Service List* (1953) gave each headword together with its common derived forms. Laurie Bauer and Paul Nation's article "Word families" (1993) gave the idea a clear form: seven levels of affixes, ordered by how frequent, regular and productive they are, so that researchers could say exactly which forms count as one family. Nation's later frequency lists, used in studies such as Nation (2006), are lists of word families built on this scheme.
:::

The course *Word Formation* goes deeper into every part of this chapter: [[word-formation/morphemes]] (the smallest parts of words), [[word-formation/prefixes]], [[word-formation/suffixes]], [[word-formation/conversion]] and [[word-formation/spelling-changes]]. ::: summary
- A **word family** is a base word with its inflected forms (*decides*, *decided*) and common derived forms (*decision*, *decisive*, *undecided*).
- **Inflection** adds a grammatical ending and keeps the same word; **derivation** adds a prefix or suffix and makes a new word.
- Suffixes usually show word class: *-tion*, *-ment*, *-ness*, *-ity* (nouns); *-ful*, *-less*, *-ous*, *-al*, *-ive*, *-able* (adjectives); *-ize*/*-ise*, *-en*, *-ify* (verbs); *-ly* (adverbs). This is a strong tendency, with exceptions such as *friendly*.
- Prefixes usually change meaning, not class: *un-*, *in-*/*im-*/*il-*/*ir-*, *dis-* ‘not’; *re-* ‘again’; *mis-* ‘wrongly’.
- Stress and spelling can change inside a family: *PHOtograph*, *phoTOgraphy*, *photoGRAphic*.
- Chinese often uses one form for several word classes; English often needs a different family member. Ask what the sentence needs before choosing the form.
- Beware of false family members such as *hardly* and *lately*.
:::
