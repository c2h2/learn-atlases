Chen Jing is telling a colleague from Canada about her home town.

**Chen Jing:** *My city has about eight hundred ten thousand people.*

**Colleague:** *Sorry — eight hundred … ten thousand?*

Chen Jing means 八十万, which is 800,000: *eight hundred thousand*. But she translated 万 as *ten thousand* and kept the Chinese grouping, and the number fell apart. Numbers seem easy — every learner knows *one, two, three* — but large numbers, fractions, dates, times and prices each have their own rules in English, and some of these rules are very different from Chinese.

This chapter teaches the **words and written forms** for numbers, dates, times and money: how to say and write them, where British and American English differ, and how to move between 万 and 亿 and *thousand* and *million*. Using these words fast in real conversation — hearing *fifteen* or *fifty*, checking a phone number, paying in a shop — is practised in [[everyday-english/numbers-in-conversation]].

## Numbers from 1 to 100

Most number words are regular, but a few have spellings that learners often get wrong.

| | | | |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 *one* | 11 *eleven* | 21 *twenty-one* | 40 ***forty*** (not *fourty*) |
| 2 *two* | 12 *twelve* | 22 *twenty-two* | 50 *fifty* |
| 3 *three* | 13 *thirteen* | 30 *thirty* | 60 *sixty* |
| 4 *four* | 14 *fourteen* | 33 *thirty-three* | 70 *seventy* |
| 5 *five* | 15 ***fifteen*** (not *fiveteen*) | 38 *thirty-eight* | 80 ***eighty*** (one *t*) |
| 10 *ten* | 18 ***eighteen*** (one *t*) | 39 *thirty-nine* | 100 *a hundred* / *one hundred* |

Numbers from 21 to 99 are written with a **hyphen** when they are spelled out: *twenty-one*, *forty-five*, *ninety-nine*.

The pairs *thirteen*/*thirty*, *fourteen*/*forty* and so on are easy to confuse in speech. The *-teen* numbers usually have stress on *-teen*: /ˌθɜːˈtiːn/ (GB), /ˌθɜːrˈtiːn/ (GA); the *-ty* numbers have stress on the first syllable: /ˈθɜːti/ (GB), /ˈθɜːrti/ (GA). The details, and how to avoid misunderstandings, are in [[everyday-english/numbers-in-conversation]].

## Big numbers

::: definition Cardinal numbers and place names {#def-cardinal}
**Cardinal numbers** (基数词) say how many: *one*, *twelve*, *three hundred*. English names large numbers in steps of a thousand: ***thousand*** (1,000 = 10³), ***million*** (1,000,000 = 10⁶), ***billion*** (1,000,000,000 = 10⁹). In writing, a comma (or, in some styles, a thin space) separates each group of three digits from the right: *1,000*, *25,000*, *3,400,000*.
:::

::: theorem Saying a large number {#rule-big-number}
To say a large number in English:

1. Write it in figures and put a comma after every three digits, counting from the right.
2. Read each group of up to three digits as a number from 1 to 999.
3. After each group, say the word for its comma: *billion*, *million*, *thousand*.
4. In British English, say *and* before the tens and units of each group (*three hundred **and** twenty*); American English often leaves it out (*three hundred twenty*).

*Hundred*, *thousand*, *million* and *billion* have no plural *-s* after a number: *five hundred*, *two million*. With *of* they do take *-s* and mean ‘a very large number’: *hundreds of people*, *millions of stars*.
:::

::: proof
English and many European languages group digits in threes, so the commas in the written form match the words. Chinese groups digits in fours: 万 (10⁴) and 亿 (10⁸). That is why a Chinese speaker must "move the boundary" when changing a number between the two languages. The safe method is to go through the figures, as in step 1, and never translate 万 word for word. The rule about plural *-s* follows from the fact that after a number, *hundred* and *thousand* work like a unit, not like a noun: compare *two dozen eggs*. Swan (*Practical English Usage*) and the learner's dictionaries describe both the *and* difference and the plural rule in the same way.
:::

Some examples:

- *345* — *three hundred and forty-five* (BrE) / *three hundred forty-five* (AmE)
- *7,008* — *seven thousand and eight* (BrE) / *seven thousand eight* (AmE)
- *62,500* — *sixty-two thousand five hundred*
- *1,250,000* — *one million two hundred and fifty thousand*, or, more often, *one point two five million*

::: example From 亿 to million {#ex-yi-to-million}
A news report says that a city has 1.2亿 visitors a year, and a company earned 3,600万 yuan. How do you say these numbers in English?
::: solution
**1.2亿.** 1亿 = 100,000,000. So 1.2亿 = 120,000,000. Put in the commas: 120,000,000. Read the groups: *a hundred and twenty* **million**. So: *a hundred and twenty million visitors* (AmE also *one hundred twenty million*).

**3,600万.** 1万 = 10,000. So 3,600万 = 3,600 × 10,000 = 36,000,000. Commas: 36,000,000. Read: *thirty-six* **million**. So: *thirty-six million yuan*.

A quick check: one 亿 = a hundred million, and ten 亿 = a billion. One 万 = ten thousand, and a hundred 万 (百万) = a million.
:::
:::

::: warning Plural -s after a number
- ✗ *The city has two millions people.* ✓ *The city has two million people.*
- ✗ *Five hundreds students came.* ✓ *Five hundred students came.*
- ✓ *Thousands of people visit the museum every year.* (*of* + plural, no number before it)
:::

## Ordinal numbers

::: definition Ordinal numbers {#def-ordinal}
**Ordinal numbers** (序数词) show position in a series: *first*, *second*, *third*, *fourth*. In figures they are written with the last two letters of the word: *1st*, *2nd*, *3rd*, *4th*, *21st*, *32nd*, *103rd*.
:::

Most ordinals add *-th* to the cardinal number: *sixth*, *seventh*, *tenth*, *hundredth*. The irregular ones need attention:

| cardinal | ordinal | note |
|---|---|---|
| *one* | *first* (*1st*) | irregular |
| *two* | *second* (*2nd*) | irregular |
| *three* | *third* (*3rd*) | irregular |
| *five* | *fifth* (*5th*) | *ve* → *f* |
| *eight* | *eighth* (*8th*) | only one *t*: /eɪtθ/ |
| *nine* | *ninth* (*9th*) | no *e* |
| *twelve* | *twelfth* (*12th*) | *ve* → *f* |
| *twenty* | *twentieth* (*20th*) | *y* → *ie* |
| *twenty-one* | *twenty-first* (*21st*) | only the last part changes |

Ordinals are used for dates (*the third of May*), floors of a building, birthdays (*her twentieth birthday*), centuries (*the twenty-first century*) and order (*the first time*). In fractions, they name the parts: *a third*, *three fifths*.

::: note English and Chinese
Floors are numbered differently in Britain and America. In British English the floor at street level is the ***ground floor***, and the one above it is the ***first floor***. In American English the floor at street level is the ***first floor***. Chinese 一楼 is therefore *the ground floor* in British English and *the first floor* in American English. Chinese also uses 第 to make ordinals (第一, 第二), while English changes the word itself (*one* → *first*). And Chinese 两 and 二 are both *two* in English: *two books*, *the second book*.
:::

::: example Which floor? {#ex-floor}
Li Ming works on 三楼 of an office building in Shanghai. How should he tell (a) a British visitor and (b) an American visitor where his office is?
::: solution
Count the floors. In China, 一楼 is at street level, so 三楼 is two floors above the street.

(a) **British English.** Street level is the *ground floor*; one floor up is the *first floor*; two floors up is the *second floor*. So: *My office is on the second floor.* In a British lift, the button for street level is often marked *G*.

(b) **American English.** Street level is the *first floor*, as in Chinese. So: *My office is on the third floor.*

If he is not sure which system the visitor uses, he can avoid the problem: *My office is two floors up from the entrance.* Note the preposition: *on* the second floor.
:::
:::

## Fractions, decimals and percentages

| written | said |
|---|---|
| ½ | *a half* / *one half* |
| ⅓ | *a third* / *one third* |
| ¼ | *a quarter* (AmE also *a fourth*) |
| ¾ | *three quarters* (AmE also *three fourths*) |
| ⅖ | *two fifths* |
| 1½ | *one and a half* |
| 0.5 | *nought point five* (BrE) / *zero point five* / *point five* |
| 3.14 | *three point one four* |
| 2.05 | *two point oh five* / *two point zero five* |
| 25% | *twenty-five per cent* (BrE) / *twenty-five percent* (AmE) |

::: theorem Fractions, decimals and percentages {#rule-fractions}
- A fraction is said as a cardinal number (the top) and an ordinal number (the bottom): *two fifths*, *three eighths*. When the top is more than one, the ordinal takes *-s*. The exceptions are *half* (*a half*, *two halves*) and *quarter*.
- A decimal is said with ***point***, and each digit after the point is said separately: *3.75* is *three point seven five*, not ✗ *three point seventy-five*.
- English writes the decimal point as a full stop (*3.5*) and groups thousands with a comma (*3,500*). Many European languages do the opposite, so *3,5* there means three and a half.
- *Per cent* is written as two words in most British texts and as one word, *percent*, in American English.
:::

::: proof
In a fraction, the ordinal names the size of each part (a *fifth* is one of five equal parts) and the cardinal says how many of those parts there are (*two* fifths). Saying the digits after the point one by one keeps a decimal exact: *0.705* is *nought point seven oh five*, and nobody can mistake it for *0.75*, *nought point seven five*. The spelling of *per cent*/*percent* is a matter of style in each variety; both are correct.
:::

Chinese reads fractions from the bottom up: 三分之二 is ‘of three parts, two’. English goes from the top: *two thirds*. So the order of the numbers is the opposite.

::: example A shop discount {#ex-discount}
A shop in China shows a sign 八折, and a jacket costs ¥400. Wang Hao wants to explain the offer in English to a visitor. What does he say, and what is the new price?
::: solution
八折 means you pay 80% of the price, so the discount is 20%. In English, a discount is described by how much you **save**, not by how much you pay:

- ✓ *Everything is twenty per cent off.* / *There's a twenty per cent discount.*
- Not *eighty per cent off*, which would mean you pay only 20%.

New price: 80% of ¥400 = 0.8 × 400 = ¥320. Said: *three hundred and twenty yuan* (AmE also *three hundred twenty yuan*). *Yuan* has no plural *-s*.

So: *The jacket is twenty per cent off, so it's three hundred and twenty yuan instead of four hundred.*
:::
:::

## Years, centuries and decades

| written | said |
|---|---|
| 1066 | *ten sixty-six* |
| 1905 | *nineteen oh five* |
| 1999 | *nineteen ninety-nine* |
| 2000 | *two thousand* |
| 2008 | *two thousand and eight* (BrE) / *two thousand eight* (AmE) |
| 2026 | *twenty twenty-six* or *two thousand and twenty-six* (AmE *two thousand twenty-six*) |
| the 1990s | *the nineteen nineties*, or *the nineties* |
| the 21st century | *the twenty-first century* |

Years are usually said in two pairs of digits, not as a whole number and never digit by digit (✗ *two zero two six*). A **decade** is written with *-s*: *the 1990s* (no apostrophe is needed: *1990s*, not *1990's*, in most modern styles). A **century** is counted from year 1, so *the 21st century* is the years 2001–2100, as 二十一世纪 is in Chinese; in everyday speech people also use it for "the 2000s".

## Dates

| | British English | American English |
|---|---|---|
| written in full | *3 May 2026* | *May 3, 2026* |
| said | *the third of May, twenty twenty-six* / *May the third* | *May third, twenty twenty-six* |
| in figures | *03/05/2026* (day/month/year) | *05/03/2026* (month/day/year) |

::: theorem Writing and saying dates {#rule-dates}
In a date, the day is **said** as an ordinal number (*the third*, *May third*), even when it is **written** as a cardinal figure (*3 May*, *May 3*). In British English the order is day–month–year; in American English month–day–year. When a date in figures might be misread, write the month as a word.
:::

::: proof
Both orders are standard, each in its own country. The danger is only between them: *03/05* is 3 May in Britain and March 5 in the United States. The international standard ISO 8601 uses year–month–day (*2026-05-03*), the same order as Chinese (2026年5月3日), and it cannot be misread. The rule that the day is spoken as an ordinal is shown in all the major usage guides (for example Swan's *Practical English Usage*); older written forms such as *3rd May* and *May 3rd* show the ordinal in writing too, and are still common.
:::

::: example Writing a Chinese date in English {#ex-date}
Write and say 2026年10月1日 in British and American English, and explain how to avoid confusion in an email.
::: solution
**British English.** Written: *1 October 2026* (or *1st October 2026*). Said: *the first of October, twenty twenty-six* or *October the first*.

**American English.** Written: *October 1, 2026*. Said: *October first, twenty twenty-six*.

**In figures.** British *01/10/2026*, American *10/01/2026*. A reader from the other country could understand *01/10* as 10 January. In an email to people from different countries, write the month as a word (*1 October 2026*) or use the international form *2026-10-01*.

The names of months and days always begin with a capital letter in English: *October*, *Monday*.
:::
:::

Prepositions with dates — *on 3 May*, *in May*, *in 2026* — are taught in [[core-grammar/prepositions-time-place]].

## Time words

The ways of saying clock times (*a quarter past seven*, *seven fifteen*) are set out with examples in [[everyday-english/numbers-in-conversation]]. Here are the words that go with them:

- *a.m.* ‘before noon’ and *p.m.* ‘after noon’: *9 a.m.*, *6.30 p.m.* British English often writes the time with a full stop (*6.30*), American English with a colon (*6:30*).
- *o'clock* only with exact hours: *seven o'clock*, not ✗ *seven thirty o'clock*.
- *half an hour* (30 minutes), *a quarter of an hour* (15 minutes), *an hour and a half* (90 minutes).
- *a fortnight* ‘two weeks’ is common in British English and rare in American English.
- *noon* or *midday* (12.00), *midnight* (00.00).

## Money

| written | said | words to know |
|---|---|---|
| £5.99 | *five pounds ninety-nine* / *five ninety-nine* | *pound* (£), *penny*, plural *pence* (*p*) |
| \$12.50 | *twelve dollars fifty* / *twelve fifty* | *dollar* (\$), *cent* |
| €20 | *twenty euros* | *euro*, *cent* |
| ¥88 | *eighty-eight yuan* | *yuan* (plural *yuan*), also *renminbi* (RMB) |

Useful words: *cash* ‘coins and notes’; *a coin*; *a note* (BrE) / *a bill* (AmE) for paper money; *change* ‘money you get back’ and also ‘coins’ (*Have you got any change?*); *a receipt* /rɪˈsiːt/ (the *p* is silent); *the price*; *cheap* and *expensive*; *a discount*; *pay by card* / *pay in cash*.

The verbs *cost*, *pay* and *spend* have different patterns:

- *The book **cost** (me) twenty pounds.* — the thing is the subject.
- *I **paid** twenty pounds **for** the book.* — the person is the subject; *for* + the thing.
- *I **spent** twenty pounds **on** books.* — *on* + the thing; *spend* is also used for time: *I spent two hours on my homework.*

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *I paid twenty pounds to this book.*
- [ ] *This book spent me twenty pounds.*
- [x] *I spent twenty pounds on this book.*
- [ ] *I cost twenty pounds for this book.*
::: solution
*Spend* + money + *on* + thing is correct. The other patterns would be *I paid twenty pounds for this book* and *This book cost me twenty pounds*.
:::
:::

::: quiz
How do you say *2.07*?
- [ ] *two point seven*
- [ ] *two point zero seventy*
- [x] *two point oh seven*
- [ ] *two and seven*
::: solution
After the point, each digit is said separately: *oh* (or *zero*), then *seven*. *Two point seven* would be 2.7, a different number.
:::
:::

::: quiz
What is 五万 in English?
- [ ] *five ten thousands*
- [ ] *five thousand*
- [x] *fifty thousand*
- [ ] *five hundred thousand*
::: solution
五万 = 5 × 10,000 = 50,000. With the comma after three digits from the right, it is read *fifty* + *thousand*.
:::

## Phone numbers

Phone numbers are said **one digit at a time**, in the groups in which they are written: *0161 496 0582* is *oh one six one, four nine six, oh five eight two*. British speakers often say *double* for two identical digits (*double four*). Saying the groups clearly and checking the number are practised in [[everyday-english/numbers-in-conversation]].

::: history
The figures 0–9 that English uses came to Europe from India through the Arabic-speaking world, which is why they are called Hindu–Arabic numerals. Leonardo of Pisa (Fibonacci) did much to make them known in Europe in his book *Liber Abaci* (1202). The word *million* came into English from French in the Middle Ages. For a long time *billion* meant different things: a million million (10¹²) in traditional British usage and a thousand million (10⁹) in American usage. In 1974 the British government announced that it would use *billion* in the American sense, and today 10⁹ is the normal meaning everywhere in English.
:::

## Where this leads

Numbers in fast speech — telling *fifteen* from *fifty*, checking a phone number, prices in a shop — are the subject of [[everyday-english/numbers-in-conversation]]. Prepositions with times and dates are in [[core-grammar/prepositions-time-place]]. Reading numbers in charts and reports comes in [[reading-skills/numbers-and-charts]], and writing about data in [[essay-writing/describing-data]].

::: summary
- English names large numbers in steps of a thousand (*thousand*, *million*, *billion*); Chinese in steps of ten thousand (万, 亿). Change through the figures: 万 = *ten thousand*, 百万 = *a million*, 亿 = *a hundred million*, 十亿 = *a billion*.
- No plural *-s* after a number (*two million people*), but *hundreds of*, *millions of*.
- British English says *and* before the tens (*three hundred and twenty*); American English often does not.
- Ordinals: *first*, *second*, *third*, then mostly *-th*; watch *fifth*, *eighth*, *ninth*, *twelfth*, *twentieth*. Dates are said with ordinals.
- Fractions: cardinal + ordinal (*two thirds*); decimals with *point* and single digits (*three point one four*).
- Dates: BrE *3 May 2026*, day/month/year; AmE *May 3, 2026*, month/day/year. Write the month as a word when the order could be misread.
- Money: *cost*, *pay for*, *spend on*; *note* (BrE) / *bill* (AmE); *yuan* has no plural *-s*. 八折 is *twenty per cent off*.
:::
