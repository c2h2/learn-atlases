The food in Wang Fang's university canteen is often cold, and she wants to tell the manager. She looks up 意见 in a small Chinese–English dictionary and finds: *opinion; view; idea; objection; complaint*. She takes the first word and writes:

✗ *I have an opinion about the food.*

The manager reads it as ‘I have a view about the food’ and waits to hear what it is. Wang Fang meant ‘I have a complaint’. The dictionary was not wrong — 意见 can mean all of those things — but it did not tell her which English word fits which situation. A good learner's dictionary, used well, would have shown her that *complaint* is the word for 意见 in the sense of 不满, with examples like *make a complaint about the food*.

A dictionary is the most useful single tool for learning vocabulary, but only if you know what is in it and how to read it. This chapter shows the kinds of dictionary, what a learner's dictionary entry tells you about the form, meaning and use of a word ([[core-vocabulary/knowing-a-word]]), how to find the right sense, and when to use a bilingual or an English–English dictionary.

## Kinds of dictionary

::: definition Learner's dictionary {#def-learners-dictionary}
A **learner's dictionary** (学习型词典) is a dictionary written for people learning a language rather than for native speakers. An English learner's dictionary explains words in simple English, using a limited **defining vocabulary** of a few thousand common words; it gives pronunciation, grammar patterns, collocations and many example sentences, usually chosen from a corpus of real English.
:::

| kind | what it is | good for | limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| bilingual (English–Chinese) | English words with Chinese translations | quick meaning of a word you meet while reading | one translation may hide differences in meaning and use |
| bilingual (Chinese–English) | Chinese words with English translations | finding candidate English words for writing | gives several words with no guide to choose; often few examples |
| monolingual learner's (English–English) | definitions in simple English, examples, grammar | learning the use of a word; checking a word before you use it | slower at first; needs a vocabulary of about 2,000 words to read the definitions |
| bilingualised (英汉双解) | a learner's dictionary entry with Chinese translations added | the best of both for learners at levels 1–2 | the Chinese can tempt you to skip the English examples |
| general (for native speakers) | very many words and senses, often with history | rare words, technical words, etymology | definitions can be harder than the word itself |

Most major learner's dictionaries are published by British and American publishers, in print and online, and online versions usually let you hear recorded GB and GA pronunciations. This chapter describes what such dictionaries contain in general; it does not recommend any one product.

## What an entry shows

Here is an entry written for this chapter in the style of a learner's dictionary.

> **borrow** /ˈbɒrəʊ/ (GB), /ˈbɑːroʊ/ (GA) *verb* [T]
>
> **1** to take and use something that belongs to someone else, and give it back later: *Can I borrow your pen?* · *She borrowed three books from the library.* → compare **lend**
>
> **2** to take money from a bank or a person and pay it back later: *We had to borrow money to buy the flat.* · *borrow (something) from a bank*
>
> **3** to take a word or an idea from another language or person and use it: *English has borrowed many words from French.*
>
> GRAMMAR: **borrow something from somebody/something** — not ✗ *borrow somebody something*
>
> ▸ **borrower** *noun* [C]

::: theorem The parts of a learner's dictionary entry {#rule-entry-parts}
A typical learner's dictionary entry shows:

1. **the headword** in its base form (the lemma), with its spelling, and often its syllables;
2. **pronunciation** in phonemic symbols, usually GB and GA, with the stress mark ˈ;
3. **word class** (*noun*, *verb*, *adjective* …);
4. **grammar codes and patterns**: for nouns [C] countable or [U] uncountable; for verbs [T] transitive (needs an object) or [I] intransitive; patterns such as *borrow sth from sb*, *suggest doing sth*;
5. **numbered senses**, in most modern learner's dictionaries with the most frequent sense first, each with a definition;
6. **example sentences** in italics, showing natural use and common **collocations**;
7. **labels** for variety and register: *BrE*, *AmE*, *formal*, *informal*, *old-fashioned*, *offensive*;
8. **extras**: notes on synonyms and common mistakes, related words and family members, phrasal verbs and idioms under the main word, and in many dictionaries a mark for the most frequent words.
:::

::: proof
Each part answers a question from the form–meaning–use table of [[core-vocabulary/knowing-a-word]]: the headword and pronunciation give the **form**; the senses and definitions the **meaning**; the grammar codes, examples, collocations and labels the **use**. Learner's dictionaries add more about use than dictionaries for native speakers, because native speakers already know the grammar of the words. Ordering senses by frequency became possible when dictionaries began to be based on large corpora (the first such learner's dictionary was *COBUILD*, 1987), so that the sense a learner is most likely to need comes first. The details differ between dictionaries — each has its own codes and labels, explained in its front pages — but the parts are much the same.
:::

::: example Reading an entry {#ex-read-entry}
Use the entry for *borrow* above to answer these questions.

1. Where is the stress?
2. Can you say *Borrow me your bike*? Why or why not?
3. Which sense is used in *Japanese has borrowed many words from English*?
4. Which word should you compare it with?
::: solution
1. On the first syllable: /ˈbɒrəʊ/ — the mark ˈ comes before the stressed syllable.
2. No. The grammar note says *borrow something from somebody*, not *borrow somebody something*. To ask for the bike: *Can I borrow your bike?* or *Can you lend me your bike?*
3. Sense 3, ‘take a word or idea from another language’ — the thing borrowed is words, not objects or money.
4. *Lend* — the arrow → *compare* sends you to the word for the opposite direction.
:::
:::

::: quiz
In a learner's dictionary, what does [U] after a noun mean?
- [ ] the noun is unusual
- [x] the noun is uncountable
- [ ] the noun is used only in the United States
- [ ] the noun has an irregular plural
::: solution
[U] means uncountable: no *a*/*an* and no plural (*advice*, *information*). [C] means countable. American use is shown by a label such as *AmE* or *US*.
:::
:::

::: example Using the labels {#ex-labels}
Li Wei is writing an email to a professor he has never met. He found these words in a dictionary. Which labels should make him stop and think, and what should he write instead?

1. *Hi! I'm a kid from China who wants to study with you.* (*kid*: *informal*)
2. *I'd like to commence my studies in September.* (*commence*: *formal*)
3. *Could you send me some info about the course?* (*info*: *informal*)
::: solution
1. *Kid* is labelled *informal*, and a university applicant is not a child. Write: *Dear Professor Brown, I am a student from China, and I would like to study with you.* (*Hi!* is also too informal for a first email.)
2. *Commence* is labelled *formal*. It is not wrong in a formal email, but *start* or *begin* is more natural: *I would like to start my studies in September.*
3. *Info* is labelled *informal*. Write *some information about the course*.

Labels do not mean "wrong": *kid* and *info* are fine in a message to a friend. They tell you **where** a word fits ([[core-vocabulary/knowing-a-word]], "Situation and register").
:::
:::

## Finding the right sense

Many common words have several senses. Choosing the wrong one is the most common problem when learners use a dictionary.

::: theorem How to find the right sense {#rule-find-sense}
To find the meaning of a word in a sentence:

1. **Decide the word class** in your sentence: noun, verb, adjective …? (*Fine* is a noun in *pay a fine* and an adjective in *I'm fine.*)
2. **Find the base form** (lemma): *went* → *go*; *studies* → *study*; *better* → *good* or *well*. Phrasal verbs and idioms are usually listed under their first main word: *give up* under *give*.
3. **Read all the senses** for that word class, not only the first, and look at the examples.
4. **Test the meaning** by putting the definition into your sentence. If it does not make sense, try the next sense — or a phrasal verb or idiom.
:::

::: proof
Step 1 matters because many English words belong to more than one class ([[word-formation/conversion]]), and dictionaries list each class separately. Step 2 matters because dictionaries list lemmas, not every form, and irregular forms (*went*, *left*, *lay*) can lead you to the wrong word: *left* is the past of *leave* as well as the opposite of *right*. Step 3 matters because the first sense is only the most frequent in general, not in your text. Step 4 is a check: the right sense makes the sentence make sense. These steps are the standard advice in the guides at the front of learner's dictionaries and in vocabulary textbooks such as Nation (2013).
:::

::: example Which sense of fine? {#ex-fine}
A learner's dictionary gives these senses of *fine*:

- adjective **1** good enough, acceptable; also: well, healthy **2** of high quality **3** very thin or made of very small pieces
- noun **1** money that you must pay as a punishment
- verb **1** to make someone pay money as a punishment

Which sense is used in each sentence?

1. *I was fined £60 for parking there.*
2. *How are you? — I'm fine, thanks.*
3. *The beach was covered in fine white sand.*
4. *If you return the book late, you'll have to pay a fine.*
::: solution
1. Verb, sense 1 — *was fined* is a passive verb form (*fine* + *-d*). 我被罚了60英镑.
2. Adjective, sense 1 — ‘well’.
3. Adjective, sense 3 — ‘made of very small pieces’ (细沙).
4. Noun, sense 1 — after *a* we need a noun: ‘money paid as a punishment’ (罚款).

The word class in the sentence chose the right group of senses each time; the context chose the sense within the group.
:::
:::

::: warning Irregular forms that look like other words
When a form is irregular, the dictionary lists it under its base form, and it may look like a different word.

- *She was lying on the beach.* — *lying* is from *lie* ‘be in a flat position’ (or from *lie* ‘say something untrue’ — the context decides), not from *lay*.
- *He left at six.* — *left* is the past of *leave*, not the opposite of *right*.
- *I found it.* — *found* is the past of *find*; there is also a different verb *found* ‘start an organisation’ (*found a company*), with its own past *founded*.
- *The wind was strong.* — the noun *wind* /wɪnd/ ‘moving air’ is a different word from the verb *wind* /waɪnd/ ‘turn’.

Most learner's dictionaries list common irregular forms as headwords too, with a note sending you to the base form (*left* → *leave*).
:::

## From Chinese to English: check both ways

Wang Fang's problem at the start of this chapter came from going from Chinese to English. A Chinese–English dictionary gives several English words for one Chinese word, but cannot always tell you which one fits.

::: theorem Check every English candidate before you use it {#rule-check-both-ways}
When you find an English word through a Chinese–English dictionary (or a translation tool), look the English word up in an English–English or bilingualised learner's dictionary before you use it. Check its meaning, its grammar pattern, its collocations and its register, and compare your sentence with the dictionary's examples.
:::

::: proof
A Chinese–English dictionary lists English equivalents of the Chinese word in all its senses, so its list mixes words that are not interchangeable (*opinion*, *objection*, *complaint* for 意见). The English–English entry shows what each English word means and how it is used — *complaint* is [C], used in *make a complaint about something* — which is exactly the information needed to choose ([[core-vocabulary/knowing-a-word#rule-no-exact-match]] explains why the lists differ). Translation tools have the same weakness for single words and short phrases without context; the course *Translation* discusses them in [[translation/machine-translation]].
:::

::: example Choosing an English word for 意见 {#ex-yijian}
Choose the best English word for 意见 in each sentence, and check it with an English–English dictionary.

1. 你对这个计划有什么意见？
2. 很多学生对食堂有意见。
3. 谢谢你的宝贵意见，我会修改文章。
::: solution
1. 意见 = ‘what you think’: *What's your opinion of the plan?* or *What do you think of the plan?* (*opinion of/about*, countable or uncountable).
2. 意见 = 不满: *Many students have complaints about the canteen.* or *Many students are unhappy with the canteen.* (*complaint about*, [C]; *make a complaint* is the usual verb + noun pattern.)
3. 意见 = ideas for improving something: *Thank you for your useful comments/suggestions — I will revise the essay.* (*comments on*, *suggestions for*; *feedback* is also common, and is [U]: *some feedback*, not ✗ *a feedback*.)

In each case the Chinese–English dictionary listed the word, but only the English–English entries — the definition, the [C]/[U] code and the examples — showed which one fits.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese dictionaries are arranged by pinyin or by radicals and strokes; English dictionaries are alphabetical, letter by letter, so *bear* comes before *beard*, and *beard* before *beautiful*. Compound words and phrases may be listed in different places: *ice cream* may be a headword of its own, while *give up* is usually under *give*. Chinese dictionaries show pronunciation with pinyin and tones; English dictionaries use phonemic symbols and the stress mark. If your school taught the "DJ" or "KK" symbols, see the comparison table in [[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet]]. Finally, a Chinese dictionary explains 字 and 词; an English dictionary explains words and phrases, and its examples are often more useful than its definitions.
:::

::: quiz
You meet *She gave up smoking last year.* Where in a learner's dictionary should you look first?
- [ ] under *up*
- [ ] under *smoking*
- [x] under *give*, in the phrasal verbs
- [ ] under *gave* as a separate word
::: solution
Phrasal verbs are usually listed under their verb, so look for *give up* in or after the entry for *give*. *Gave* is the past of *give*; some dictionaries list it as a headword with a cross-reference, but the meaning is under *give*.
:::
:::

::: application A dictionary routine
For a word you want to **use**: (1) find the right sense; (2) note the pronunciation and stress; (3) note the grammar code and pattern; (4) copy one example sentence, or write your own on the same model; (5) note one collocation and any label (*formal*, *BrE*). Put the result on a word card ([[core-vocabulary/learning-and-remembering]]). For a word you only need to **understand** in a text, steps (1) and perhaps (2) are enough — do not let the dictionary stop your reading for every unknown word ([[reading-skills/words-from-context]]).
:::

::: history
The first dictionaries for learners of English were made by teachers who worked abroad. Michael West and James Endicott's *New Method English Dictionary* (1935) explained its words using a small defining vocabulary of about 1,500 words. A. S. Hornby, E. V. Gatenby and H. Wakefield, teaching in Japan, compiled the *Idiomatic and Syntactic English Dictionary* (Tokyo, 1942), with verb patterns and many examples; Oxford University Press republished it in 1948, and it became the first of the *Oxford Advanced Learner's Dictionary* editions. The *Longman Dictionary of Contemporary English* (1978) defined its words with a controlled vocabulary of about 2,000 words. The *Collins COBUILD English Language Dictionary* (1987), edited by John Sinclair, was the first learner's dictionary based on a large computer corpus, with full-sentence definitions and examples from real texts; corpus evidence is now standard in all of them.
:::

::: summary
- A **learner's dictionary** explains words in simple English with a limited **defining vocabulary**, and gives pronunciation, grammar, collocations and corpus-based examples.
- Bilingual dictionaries give quick meanings; English–English and bilingualised learner's dictionaries show **use**. Use each for what it does well.
- An entry shows the headword (lemma), pronunciation (GB/GA, stress), word class, grammar codes ([C]/[U], [T]/[I]) and patterns, numbered senses (most frequent first), examples, collocations, labels (*BrE*, *formal* …) and related words.

- When you go from Chinese to English, **check every English candidate** in an English–English entry before using it (意见 → *opinion*, *complaint*, *comments* …).
- For words you want to use, copy the pattern, a collocation and an example; for words you only need to understand, take the meaning and keep reading.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Countable or uncountable? {level=1}
Use a learner's dictionary to find whether each noun is [C], [U] or both. Write one correct phrase for each.

*advice*, *suggestion*, *equipment*, *paper*, *news*, *idea*
::: solution
- *advice* [U] — *some advice*, *a piece of advice*
- *suggestion* [C] — *a good suggestion*, *two suggestions*
- *equipment* [U] — *some equipment*, *a piece of equipment*
- *paper* — [U] ‘material for writing’ (*some paper*, *a sheet of paper*); [C] ‘a newspaper’ or ‘an academic article’ (*a paper*, *two papers*)
- *news* [U] — *some news*, *a piece of news* (*news* ends in *-s* but takes a singular verb: *The news is good.*)
- *idea* [C] — *an idea*, *some ideas*
:::
:::

::: exercise Find the base form {level=1}
Write the form you would look up in a dictionary.

1. *children*
2. *went*
3. *studies*
4. *better* (in *She feels better.*)
5. *mice*
6. *thought* (in *I thought so.*)
::: solution
1. *child*
2. *go*
3. *study*
4. *well* (the comparative of *well* ‘healthy’; *better* is also the comparative of *good*)
5. *mouse*
6. *think* (there is also a noun *thought*, ‘an idea’; here it is the past of *think*)
:::
:::

::: exercise Transitive or intransitive? {level=1}
A dictionary marks verbs [T] (needs an object) or [I] (no object). Correct the sentences that are wrong.

1. *We arrived the hotel at ten.* ([I])
2. *Please explain me this word.* ([T]: *explain sth to sb*)
3. *She discussed about the problem.* ([T])
4. *The baby is sleeping.* ([I])
::: solution
1. *We arrived at the hotel at ten.* — *arrive* is [I], so it needs *at* (or *in* for a city or country).
2. *Please explain this word to me.* — the pattern is *explain something to somebody*.
3. *She discussed the problem.* — *discuss* is [T]: the object follows directly.
4. Correct.
:::
:::

::: exercise Which sense? {level=2}
The verb *run* has many senses. Match each sentence with a sense: (a) move fast on foot, (b) manage a business, (c) (of a machine) work, (d) (of a bus or train) travel on a regular route.

1. *Her parents run a small restaurant.*
2. *The buses run every ten minutes.*
3. *I run in the park every morning.*
4. *Don't touch the engine while it's running.*
::: solution
1. (b) manage a business
2. (d) travel on a regular route
3. (a) move fast on foot
4. (c) work (of a machine)
:::
:::

::: exercise Labels {level=2}
A dictionary labels these words. Say what each label tells you and give a neutral or other-variety word.

1. *kid* — *informal*
2. *commence* — *formal*
3. *lorry* — *BrE*
4. *sidewalk* — *AmE*
::: solution
1. *Informal*: used with friends and family, not in formal writing. Neutral: *child*.
2. *Formal*: used in official or formal writing and speech. Neutral: *begin* or *start*.
3. *BrE*: British English. American English: *truck*.
4. *AmE*: American English. British English: *pavement*.
:::
:::

::: exercise From Chinese to English {level=2}
A Chinese–English dictionary gives *learn* and *study* for 学习. Use an English–English dictionary and choose the better word for each sentence.

1. 她在大学学习经济学。
2. 我从这次错误中学到了很多。
3. 你应该每天学习两个小时。
::: solution
1. *She is studying economics at university.* — *study* a subject at a school or university.
2. *I learned a lot from this mistake.* — *learn* is getting knowledge or a lesson from experience; *study* is not used this way.
3. *You should study for two hours every day.* — spending time on learning, often with books. (*learn* would need an object: *learn ten new words every day*.)
:::
:::

::: exercise Write a dictionary entry {level=3}
Write a short learner's-dictionary entry, in your own words, for *lend*, following the model of *borrow* in this chapter: pronunciation (GB and GA), word class and grammar code, at least two senses with definitions in simple English and an example each, a grammar note, and a cross-reference.
::: hint
Look up *lend* in a learner's dictionary first, but write the definitions and examples yourself.
:::
::: solution
A model:

> **lend** /lend/ *verb* [T] (past tense and past participle **lent** /lent/)
>
> **1** to give something to someone for a short time, expecting to get it back: *Could you lend me your umbrella?* · *I lent my bike to Tom.* → compare **borrow**
>
> **2** to give money to someone that they must pay back, often with extra money (interest): *The bank lent them the money for the house.*
>
> GRAMMAR: **lend somebody something** or **lend something to somebody**
>
> ▸ **lender** *noun* [C]

The pronunciation is the same in GB and GA. Check: the two grammar patterns, the irregular past *lent*, and the cross-reference to *borrow*.
:::
:::

::: exercise Correct with a dictionary {level=3}
Each sentence has one word that is not quite right, although the meaning is clear. Use a learner's dictionary — the examples and grammar patterns — to correct it, and say what in the entry helped you.

1. *I want to improve my English level.*
2. *Can you give me some advices?*
3. *He is very good in making friends.*
4. *Please open the light.*
::: solution
1. *I want to improve my English.* — the entry for *improve* gives examples like *improve your English*, *improve your skills*. (*Improve my level of English* is possible but less common; *raise my level* is a translation of 提高水平.)
2. *Can you give me some advice?* — *advice* is marked [U].
3. *He is very good at making friends.* — the entry for *good* shows the pattern *good at (doing) something*.
4. *Please turn on the light.* (or *switch on*) — the entry for *open* has no sense for lights; the entry for *light* gives the collocations *turn on* / *switch on* / *put on the light*.
:::
