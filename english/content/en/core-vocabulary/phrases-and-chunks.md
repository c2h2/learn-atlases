Sun Mei is a new student in Manchester. Her flatmate Daniel knocks on her door.

**Daniel:** *Hi, Sun Mei. Have you got a minute?*

**Sun Mei:** *Sure. What's up?*

**Daniel:** *A few of us are going out for dinner tonight. Would you like to come?*

**Sun Mei:** *I'd love to, but I'm afraid I've got a lot of homework. Maybe next time?*

**Daniel:** *No problem. By the way, the heating's broken again — I've already told the landlord.*

**Sun Mei:** *Oh no. Thanks for letting me know.*

Sun Mei speaks easily, and she sounds natural. That is not because she builds every sentence word by word from grammar rules. Much of what she says comes in ready-made pieces: *Have you got a minute?*, *What's up?*, *Would you like to …?*, *I'd love to, but …*, *I'm afraid …*, *Maybe next time?*, *No problem*, *By the way*, *Thanks for letting me know*. Expert speakers use thousands of such pieces. They are called **chunks**, and learning them is one of the fastest ways to speak more fluently and more naturally. This chapter explains what chunks are, the main kinds, the research behind them, and how to learn them.

## What is a chunk?

::: definition Chunk (formulaic sequence) {#def-chunk}
A **chunk** is a group of words that speakers store in memory and use as a single unit, instead of building it word by word each time: *by the way*, *make a decision*, *I'm afraid (that) …*, *Nice to meet you*. Researchers also call them **formulaic sequences**, **multi-word units** or **prefabricated** ("prefab") phrases.
:::

Chunks have three common features, although not every chunk has all three:

1. **They are frequent.** Speakers meet and use them again and again.
2. **They are fixed or partly fixed.** You cannot freely change the words: *by the way*, not ✗ *by a way* or ✗ *on the way* (which means something else: 在路上).
3. **Their meaning or use may not follow from the words.** *By the way* means ‘I am changing the topic’, and *I'm afraid* in *I'm afraid I can't come* means ‘I'm sorry to say’, not ‘I'm frightened’.

| kind | examples | note |
|---|---|---|
| fixed phrases (polywords) | *by the way*, *of course*, *at least*, *as well*, *in fact*, *on the other hand* | work like one word |
| collocations | *make a decision*, *heavy rain*, *take a photo*, *strong coffee* | words that often go together ([[collocation-and-register/what-is-collocation]]) |
| phrasal verbs | *look after*, *give up*, *find out*, *get on with* | a verb + a small word, often with a new meaning ([[verb-patterns/phrasal-verbs]]) |
| sentence frames | *I'm afraid (that) …*, *Would you like to …?*, *The thing is, …*, *It depends on …* | a fixed beginning with a slot to fill |
| social formulas | *Nice to meet you*, *See you later*, *Never mind*, *Thanks for …ing* | fixed ways to greet, thank, apologise, end a conversation |
| idioms | *a piece of cake* (very easy), *break the ice* | the meaning cannot be guessed from the words ([[collocation-and-register/idioms]]) |

::: quiz
What does *by the way* do in *By the way, the heating's broken again*?
- [ ] It shows how something happened.
- [x] It introduces a new topic, often one that has just come to mind.
- [ ] It means ‘on the road’.
- [ ] It ends the conversation.
::: solution
*By the way* signals a change of topic: Daniel moves from dinner to the heating. ‘On the road’ is *on the way*. The chunk must be learned as a whole, because its meaning does not come from *by*, *the* and *way*.
:::
:::

## Why chunks matter

::: theorem Chunks make speech fluent and natural {#rule-chunks-fluency}
Expert speakers rely on a large store of memorised chunks. Using chunks makes speech **faster** (less has to be built while speaking) and more **natural** (the listener hears the combinations they expect). A learner who knows the chunk *make a decision* says it quickly and correctly; a learner who builds it from *decide* + a noun + a verb may say ✗ *do a decision*.
:::

::: proof
The linguists Andrew Pawley and Frances Syder (1983) pointed out two puzzles about native speakers. First, they speak fluently, faster than they could plan sentence by sentence. Second, out of the many grammatical ways to say something, they choose the few that sound natural: *I want to marry you* rather than grammatical but strange alternatives such as ? *I wish to be wedded to you*. Their explanation was that speakers store many thousands of "lexicalised sentence stems" — chunks — and put them together. The corpus linguist John Sinclair (1991) called this the **idiom principle**: speakers choose ready-made combinations whenever they can, and build freely (the "open-choice principle") only when they must. Britt Erman and Beatrice Warren (2000) counted the prefabricated combinations in samples of English speech and writing and estimated that they made up a little over half of the texts (about 55%). Alison Wray's *Formulaic Language and the Lexicon* (2002) brought this research together.
:::

The practical message is simple: when you learn words, learn the chunks they live in. [[core-vocabulary/knowing-a-word]] recommended learning a word with an example of its use; [[core-vocabulary/function-words]] recommended learning prepositions in phrases. Chunks are the same idea on a larger scale.

::: example Finding the chunks {#ex-find-chunks}
Find the chunks in this short conversation in a café, and say what kind each one is.

**Assistant:** *Hi there, what can I get you?*

**Ahmed:** *Can I have a large coffee, please? To take away.*

**Assistant:** *Sure. Anything else?*

**Ahmed:** *No, that's all, thanks.*

**Assistant:** *That's three twenty, please.*

**Ahmed:** *Here you are. Keep the change.*
::: solution
- *Hi there* — social formula (a friendly greeting).
- *What can I get you?* — sentence frame used by people serving food and drink.
- *Can I have …, please?* — sentence frame for asking for something.
- *To take away* — fixed phrase (BrE; AmE *to go*).
- *Anything else?* — fixed phrase (short for *Would you like anything else?*).
- *That's all, thanks* — social formula for ending an order.
- *That's …, please* — sentence frame for saying the price.
- *Here you are* — social formula used when giving something.
- *Keep the change* — fixed phrase (‘you can keep the money I would get back’).

Almost every turn is a chunk. That is typical of short service conversations, which repeat the same patterns many times a day ([[everyday-english/shopping-eating-out]]).
:::
:::

## Collocations: make or do?

The commonest chunks for Chinese learners to get wrong are collocations — especially with very common verbs such as *make*, *do*, *take* and *have*, which are hard to translate because their meanings are so general.

::: widget graph
nodes: make@-2,0; do@2,0; a decision@-4.5,2; a mistake@-5,0.5; friends@-5,-1; a phone call@-4,-2.5; the bed@-2,-3; dinner@-2,2.8; homework@4.5,2; the shopping@5,0.5; your best@5,-1; exercise@4,-2.5; the washing-up@2,-3; business@2,2.8
edges: make-a decision; make-a mistake; make-friends; make-a phone call; make-the bed; make-dinner; do-homework; do-the shopping; do-your best; do-exercise; do-the washing-up; do-business
title: Make or do?
caption: Common collocations of *make* (left) and *do* (right). A rough tendency: *make* is often used for creating or producing something (*make dinner*, *make a phone call*), and *do* for activities, work and tasks (*do homework*, *do the shopping*). Many collocations do not follow the tendency (*make the bed*, *make a mistake*, *do business*), so each must be learned as a chunk. *Do the washing-up* is British; American English says *do the dishes*.
:::

::: theorem Learn the whole chunk, including the small words {#rule-whole-chunk}
Learn a chunk exactly, with its function words, its word order and its slots: *make a decision* (not *do*), *at the end of the day*, *I'm looking forward to seeing you* (*to* + *-ing*). When a chunk has a slot, learn what can go in it: *Would you like **to** + verb?*, *Would you like + noun?*, *Thanks for + noun / -ing*.
:::

::: proof
A chunk is useful because it is stored ready-made; if a learner stores only its content words, the function words have to be rebuilt each time and are easily wrong (✗ *look forward to see you*, ✗ *in the other hand*). Many chunks follow no general rule — *on the other hand* but *in other words*; *at the end* (of something) but *in the end* (finally) — so there is nothing to work out, only something to remember. Knowing the slot lets the learner use one frame many times: *Thanks for coming*, *Thanks for the present*, *Thanks for letting me know*.
:::

::: example Make, do, take or have? {#ex-make-do}
Correct these sentences. Each comes from a Chinese collocation translated word by word.

1. *I need to do a decision soon.* (做决定)
2. *Can you make me a favour?* (帮我个忙)
3. *We opened a meeting this morning.* (开会)
4. *Let's take a rest.* (休息一下)
5. *She learned a lot of knowledge at university.* (学到了很多知识)
::: solution
1. *I need to **make** a decision soon.* — *make a decision* is fixed.
2. *Can you **do** me a favour?* — the chunk is *do someone a favour* (BrE spelling *favour*, AmE *favor*).
3. *We **had** a meeting this morning.* (or *held*, more formal) — *open a meeting* means only ‘start it officially’.
4. *Let's **have** a rest.* or *Let's **take** a break.* — *take a rest* is heard, but *have a rest* (BrE) and *take a break* are the usual chunks.
5. *She **gained** a lot of knowledge at university.* or, more naturally, *She learned a lot at university.* — *learn knowledge* is not a natural collocation in English.
:::
:::

## Sentence frames for speaking

Sentence frames are especially useful for beginners, because one frame gives many sentences. Here are some of the most useful for everyday conversation, grouped by what they do.

| function | frames |
|---|---|
| inviting | *Would you like to …?* · *Do you fancy …ing?* (BrE, informal) · *How about …ing?* |
| accepting and refusing | *I'd love to.* · *That sounds great.* · *I'd love to, but …* · *Maybe next time.* |
| giving bad news politely | *I'm afraid (that) …* · *Unfortunately, …* · *I'm sorry, but …* |
| giving an opinion | *I think …* · *In my opinion, …* · *To be honest, …* |
| asking for help | *Could you …, please?* · *Would you mind …ing?* · *Do you know where …?* |
| gaining time | *Let me think.* · *That's a good question.* · *The thing is, …* |
| changing topic | *By the way, …* · *Anyway, …* · *Speaking of …, …* |

::: example Completing frames {#ex-frames}
Complete each frame to suit the situation.

1. A colleague invites you to a film tonight, but you have to work late. *I'd love to, but …*
2. You want a stranger to watch your bag for a moment. *Would you mind …?*
3. You must tell a customer that the shop has no more of a product. *I'm afraid …*
::: solution
Possible answers:

1. *I'd love to, but I have to work late tonight. Maybe at the weekend?*
2. *Would you mind watching my bag for a moment?* — *mind* is followed by an *-ing* form. (The polite answer meaning ‘yes, I'll do it’ is *No, not at all* — because the question is "do you mind?".)
3. *I'm afraid we've sold out of that one.* or *I'm afraid we don't have any more.*

Each answer keeps the frame exactly and fills the slot with words that fit its grammar.
:::
:::

::: warning Nearly-right chunks
A chunk with one word changed often sounds wrong or means something else.

- ✗ *in the other hand* ✓ *on the other hand*
- ✗ *I'm looking forward to see you.* ✓ *I'm looking forward to seeing you.*
- ✗ *by the way of* ✓ *by the way*
- ✗ *At the end, we decided to stay.* ✓ *In the end, we decided to stay.* (*at the end of the film* is correct: *at the end of* + noun)
- ✗ *as soon as possibly* ✓ *as soon as possible*
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese is full of chunks too: 不好意思, 没关系, 辛苦了, 慢走, and the four-character 成语. The trap is to translate a Chinese chunk word by word, because the English chunk for the same situation is usually different:

- 不好意思 (to get past someone) → *Excuse me.*; (apologising for a small thing) → *Sorry.*
- 没关系 → *That's OK.* / *No problem.* / *Never mind.* / *Don't worry about it.*
- 辛苦了 → no single chunk; *Thanks for all your hard work.* or *You must be tired.*
- 慢走 → *Take care.* / *See you.* / *Get home safely.*
- 吃了吗？(as a greeting) → *How are you?* / *How's it going?* — in English, asking whether someone has eaten is a real question or an invitation, not a greeting.

The question to ask is not "how do I say these words?" but "what do English speakers say in this situation?" Differences in such social habits are discussed in [[pragmatics/small-talk-norms]].
:::

::: theorem The meaning of a chunk is the meaning of the whole {#rule-chunk-meaning}
Many chunks have a meaning or a use that cannot be worked out from their words: *I'm afraid* (‘I'm sorry to say’), *by the way* (‘a new topic’), *break the ice* (‘make people feel relaxed’), *Would you mind …?* (a polite request). Learn the meaning and the situation of the whole chunk.
:::

::: proof
Chunks become fixed through frequent use in particular situations, and over time their meaning shifts away from the literal meaning of their parts — *I'm afraid* has softened from a statement of fear to a polite way of giving bad news. A learner who analyses the words will misunderstand them (*Would you mind …? — No* means ‘yes, I'll do it’). That is why a learner's dictionary lists such chunks as phrases in their own right ([[core-vocabulary/using-a-dictionary]]), and why idioms, the least predictable chunks, are taught separately in [[collocation-and-register/idioms]].
:::

::: quiz
Your neighbour asks, *Would you mind turning the music down a bit?* You agree. What do you say?
- [ ] *Yes, I mind.*
- [x] *No, not at all — sorry!*
- [ ] *Yes, I would.*
- [ ] *I'm afraid.*
::: solution
*Would you mind …?* literally asks whether you object. To agree to the request, say *No* (‘I don't mind’) — *No, not at all*, or *Of course not*, often with *sorry*. *Yes, I would* would mean you refuse.
:::
:::

::: example Making a dialogue more natural {#ex-natural-dialogue}
Wang Hao wrote this dialogue for a speaking test. Every sentence is grammatical, but it sounds stiff. Rewrite it with chunks from this chapter.

**Wang Hao:** *I want to ask you a question. Do you have time now?*

**Teacher:** *Yes.*

**Wang Hao:** *I cannot come to the class tomorrow. The reason is that I have to go to the hospital. I am sorry.*

**Teacher:** *It is not a problem.*
::: solution
A more natural version:

**Wang Hao:** *Excuse me, have you got a minute?* (AmE *do you have a minute?*)

**Teacher:** *Sure. What's up?*

**Wang Hao:** *I'm afraid I can't come to class tomorrow — I've got a hospital appointment. Sorry about that.*

**Teacher:** *That's OK. Thanks for letting me know.*

Chunks used: *Excuse me*, *have you got a minute?*, *Sure*, *What's up?*, *I'm afraid …*, *Sorry about that*, *That's OK*, *Thanks for letting me know*. Note also *come to class* (no *the* in this chunk) and the contractions (*can't*, *I've*), which are normal in speech.
:::
:::

::: application Keeping a chunk notebook
When you read or listen, notice groups of words that come together — especially at the beginning of sentences and in short replies. Write the whole chunk with the situation: *Thanks for letting me know* — said when someone tells you useful news. Mark slots with three dots: *Would you mind …ing?* Practise them as cards ([[core-vocabulary/learning-and-remembering]]) — chunks work well on cards, because the card can show the situation on one side and the chunk on the other. Then use each new chunk at least once in speech or writing in the same week.
:::

::: history
Teachers noticed the importance of word combinations long before corpora existed. Harold Palmer, working in Tokyo, published a *Second Interim Report on English Collocations* in 1933. Andrew Pawley and Frances Syder's paper on "native-like selection and native-like fluency" (1983) argued that fluent speech depends on memorised sequences. John Sinclair's *Corpus, Concordance, Collocation* (1991) used corpus evidence to describe the "idiom principle". In language teaching, James Nattinger and Jeanette DeCarrico's *Lexical Phrases and Language Teaching* (1992) and Michael Lewis's *The Lexical Approach* (1993) put chunks at the centre of the syllabus, and Alison Wray's *Formulaic Language and the Lexicon* (2002) brought together the research from linguistics and psychology.
:::

::: summary
- A **chunk** (formulaic sequence) is a group of words stored and used as a unit: fixed phrases, collocations, phrasal verbs, sentence frames, social formulas and idioms.
- Chunks make speech **faster** and more **natural**; research suggests that a large part of ordinary English — about half in Erman and Warren's (2000) estimate — is made of prefabricated combinations (Pawley & Syder 1983; Sinclair 1991; Wray 2002).
- Learn chunks **exactly**, with their function words and word order, and learn what can fill their slots: *Would you like to + verb?*, *Thanks for + -ing*.
- Common verbs such as *make*, *do*, *take* and *have* form many collocations that must be learned one by one: *make a decision*, *do homework*, *have a meeting*.
- Do not translate Chinese chunks word by word; ask what English speakers say in the same situation (不好意思 → *Excuse me* / *Sorry*).
- The meaning of a chunk is the meaning of the whole: *I'm afraid …*, *by the way*, *Would you mind …? — No, not at all.*
:::
