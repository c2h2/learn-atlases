Liu Yang has written the first lines of an essay about his home city:

> *In the last twenty years, the develop of my city has been very rapidly. The government has made many improves, and people's lives are more convenience than before.*

His teacher understands every word and still marks four mistakes. *Develop*, *rapidly*, *improves* and *convenience* all belong to the right families, but each is the wrong member: the sentence needs *development*, *rapid*, *improvements* and *convenient*. In English, the end of a word usually tells you its word class, and the sentence decides which class it needs. In Chinese, 发展 can be a noun or a verb and 方便 an adjective or a noun, with no change of form, so a Chinese writer gets no warning from the word itself.

This chapter is about the endings that make these differences: **suffixes**. A first table of common suffixes is in [[core-vocabulary/word-families#rule-suffix-class]]. Here we go further: which suffix to choose when several are possible, what meanings suffixes add besides word class, which ones move the stress, and which ones are still used to make new words.

## What suffixes do

::: definition Suffix {#def-suffix}
A **suffix** (后缀) is an affix placed after a base. A derivational suffix usually does two things at once: it gives the new word a **word class**, and it adds a **meaning**. *-ness* makes a noun meaning ‘the state or quality of being …’ (*kind* → *kindness*); *-less* makes an adjective meaning ‘without …’ (*care* → *careless*); *-ify* makes a verb meaning ‘make …’ (*simple* → *simplify*).
:::

::: theorem The last suffix decides the word class {#rule-last-suffix}
When a word has several derivational suffixes, its word class is the class made by the **last** one: *nation* (noun) → *national* (adjective) → *nationalise* (verb) → *nationalisation* (noun). Prefixes do not normally affect the class ([[word-formation/prefixes#rule-prefix-class]]). Inflections, which come after all derivational suffixes, do not change it either: *nationalisations* is still a noun.
:::

## Making nouns

### Nouns from verbs

| suffix | meaning | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *-tion*, *-ation*, *-sion* | the act or result of | *inform* → *information*, *decide* → *decision*, *organise* → *organisation* |
| *-ment* | the act or result of | *pay* → *payment*, *develop* → *development*, *agree* → *agreement* |
| *-al* | the act of | *arrive* → *arrival*, *refuse* → *refusal*, *approve* → *approval* |
| *-ance*, *-ence* | the act or state of | *perform* → *performance*, *exist* → *existence*, *prefer* → *preference* |
| *-ing* | the act of; something made | *build* → *building*, *meet* → *meeting*, *paint* → *painting* |

::: theorem Choosing a noun suffix for a verb {#rule-verb-noun}
For most verbs only one noun form is in normal use, and it must be learned with the verb: *decide* → *decision* (not ✗ *decidement*), *arrive* → *arrival* (not ✗ *arrivation*), *improve* → *improvement* (not ✗ *improvation*). Two tendencies help:

1. Verbs ending in *-ise*/*-ize* and *-ify* take *-ation*: *organise* → *organisation*, *realise* → *realisation*, *classify* → *classification*, *simplify* → *simplification*.
2. Verbs ending in *-ate* take *-ion*: *educate* → *education*, *create* → *creation*, *translate* → *translation*.

Where two noun forms exist, they usually differ in meaning: *a building* (the house) / *the construction* (the process); *an arrival* (one person or event arriving) / *the arrivals hall*.
:::

::: proof
These suffixes came into English with whole words, mostly from French and Latin (*-tion*, *-ment*, *-ance*, *-al*), and each English verb has usually kept the noun that came with it or that was first made from it. Once one noun is established, a second one with the same meaning is rarely needed, so it does not become common — linguists call this **blocking**: the existence of *decision* blocks ✗ *decidement*. The two tendencies are regular because *-ise*, *-ify* and *-ate* are themselves Latin-type suffixes, and *-ation*/*-ion* is the Latin-type noun ending that goes with them. *-ing* is different: it can make a noun from almost any verb (*the swimming*, *the cooking*), but such nouns usually name the activity, not a result.
:::

::: example Which noun? {#ex-noun}
Complete each sentence with the noun form of the verb in brackets.

1. *We are waiting for the ___ (arrive) of the train.*
2. *The ___ (govern) has announced new rules.*
3. *She made an important ___ (discover).*
4. *I'd like some ___ (inform) about the course.*
5. *His ___ (perform) in the final was excellent.*
6. *The ___ (organise) of the event took months.*
::: solution
1. *arrival* (*-al*). 2. *government* (*-ment*). 3. *discovery* (*-y*; less common, but the established form). 4. *information* (*-ation*). 5. *performance* (*-ance*). 6. *organisation* (AmE *organization*; *-ise*/*-ize* → *-ation*, [[#rule-verb-noun]]).

Sentence 3 shows that the list of suffixes is not closed: *-y* makes a few nouns from verbs (*discover* → *discovery*, *deliver* → *delivery*, *recover* → *recovery*). This is why the noun form must be learned with the verb.
:::
:::

### Nouns from adjectives: *-ness*, *-ity* and others

| suffix | examples |
|---|---|
| *-ness* | *kind* → *kindness*, *dark* → *darkness*, *happy* → *happiness*, *aware* → *awareness* |
| *-ity* | *able* → *ability*, *possible* → *possibility*, *real* → *reality*, *popular* → *popularity* |
| *-ance*, *-ence*, *-cy* | *important* → *importance*, *different* → *difference*, *accurate* → *accuracy* |
| *-th* | *wide* → *width*, *long* → *length*, *deep* → *depth*, *strong* → *strength*, *warm* → *warmth*, *true* → *truth* |

::: theorem *-ness* and *-ity* {#rule-ness-ity}
1. ***-ness*** can be added to almost any adjective, and it is the usual choice for new nouns: *politeness*, *readiness*, *user-friendliness*. It never changes the stress or the vowels of the base.
2. ***-ity*** is added mainly to adjectives of Latin or French origin, especially those ending in *-able*/*-ible*, *-al*, *-ar* and *-ic*: *ability*, *visibility*, *reality*, *popularity*, *electricity*. It moves the stress to the syllable before it and often changes vowels.
3. Adjectives in *-ant* and *-ent* take *-ance* and *-ence*: *important* → *importance*, *independent* → *independence*.
:::

::: proof
Studies of English word-formation agree that *-ness* is the most productive noun-making suffix in English (Plag 2018; Bauer, Lieber & Plag 2013): it is native, it attaches to nearly any adjective, and the result is always transparent. *-ity* came with Latin and French words and kept a preference for bases of the same origin; it is still used to make new nouns from such bases (*walkability* and *scalability* are recent), but rarely from native ones. Some adjectives have both nouns, usually with a difference of use: *sensitivity* is the normal word in science and medicine (*light sensitivity*), while *sensitiveness* is rare. The *-th* nouns are old native formations; no new ones are made, so they must simply be learned.
:::

### Nouns for people

| suffix | meaning | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *-er* | a person or thing that does … | *teacher*, *driver*, *player*, *printer*, *dishwasher* |
| *-or* | the same, mostly in Latin-type words | *actor*, *visitor*, *editor*, *director*, *translator* |
| *-ist* | a person who practises or plays … | *pianist*, *scientist*, *journalist*, *dentist* |
| *-ian* | a person skilled in or belonging to … | *musician*, *librarian*, *historian*, *Canadian* |
| *-ant*, *-ent* | a person who does … | *assistant*, *applicant*, *student*, *resident* |
| *-ee* | a person to whom something is done | *employee*, *trainee*, *interviewee*, *payee* |

*-er* is by far the most productive: it can be added to almost any verb, so new words such as *blogger*, *podcaster* and *streamer* are understood at once. It also names machines and tools (*printer*, *charger*, *opener*). *-or* and *-er* sound the same (/ə/ in GB, /ər/ in GA), so the spelling has to be learned: *visitor* but *writer*, *actor* but *speaker*.

*-ee* usually names the person who receives the action — the *employer* employs the *employee* — but in a few words it names someone who simply does something: an *escapee* has escaped, an *attendee* attends a meeting. It is stressed: *emplo**YEE***, *trai**NEE***.

::: quiz
Which word means ‘a person who is being trained’?
- [ ] *trainer*
- [x] *trainee*
- [ ] *training*
- [ ] *trainor*
::: solution
*-ee* names the person to whom the action is done: the *trainee* is trained by the *trainer*. *Training* is the activity, and *trainor* does not exist.
:::
:::

## Making adjectives

| suffix | meaning | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *-able*, *-ible* | can be …-ed; having … | *washable*, *readable*, *comfortable*, *visible*, *possible* |
| *-ful* | full of, having | *careful*, *useful*, *helpful*, *beautiful* |
| *-less* | without | *careless*, *useless*, *homeless*, *endless* |
| *-ous* | having the quality of | *dangerous*, *famous*, *nervous*, *various* |
| *-al* | relating to | *national*, *cultural*, *musical*, *personal* |
| *-ive* | tending to, having the quality of | *active*, *creative*, *expensive*, *attractive* |
| *-ic*, *-ical* | relating to | *economic*, *scientific*, *historical*, *political* |
| *-y* | having, like | *sunny*, *rainy*, *salty*, *sleepy* |
| *-ish* | rather; about; of a nation | *reddish*, *tallish*, *sixish*, *British*, *Turkish* |
| *-ly* | having the qualities of | *friendly*, *lovely*, *costly*, *weekly* |

*-able* is the most productive adjective suffix: it can be added to almost any transitive verb to mean ‘can be …-ed’ (*downloadable*, *recyclable*, *shareable*). *-ible* is not used for new words; the choice between the two is part of [[word-formation/spelling-changes]].

*-ful* and *-less* often make pairs (*careful* / *careless*, *useful* / *useless*, *helpful* / *helpless*), but not always: there is *beautiful* but no ✗ *beautiless*, *homeless* but no ✗ *homeful*. And a pair is not always opposite in the way you might expect: *helpless* means ‘unable to help yourself’, not ‘not helpful’ (that is *unhelpful*).

::: example Choosing an adjective suffix {#ex-adjective}
Make an adjective from the word in brackets to complete each sentence, and explain the meaning the suffix adds.

1. *Thanks — that was really ___ (help).*
2. *Don't worry, these glasses are ___ (wash) — put them in the dishwasher.*
3. *The film was so long and ___ (end) that I fell asleep.*
4. *Be careful on the roads; it's ___ (ice) this morning.*
5. *She's very ___ (create) — she paints, writes and designs clothes.*
6. *Without the key I was ___ (help): I couldn't get in.*
::: solution
1. *helpful* — *-ful* ‘having, giving’: it gave help.
2. *washable* — *-able* ‘can be …-ed’: they can be washed.
3. *endless* — *-less* ‘without’: it seemed to have no end.
4. *icy* — *-y* ‘having, covered with’ (the *e* of *ice* is dropped before *-y*).
5. *creative* — *-ive* ‘tending to, good at’ (the *e* of *create* is dropped before *-ive*).
6. *helpless* — *-less* ‘without’: unable to do anything. Compare 1 and 6: *helpful* and *helpless* are not simple opposites. The opposite of *helpful* is *unhelpful*.
:::
:::

*-ish* is very common in informal speech, where it can mean ‘approximately’: *Let's meet at sixish* (around six). *She's thirtyish.* Speakers even use *ish* alone as an informal answer: *Are you ready? — Ish.* (‘More or less.’)

::: warning *-ic* or *-ical*?
Some adjectives have two forms with different meanings. Learner's dictionaries mark the difference; the most common pairs are:

- *economic* ‘relating to the economy’ — *economical* ‘not wasting money or fuel’: *economic growth*; *an economical car*.
- *historic* ‘important in history’ — *historical* ‘about or from the past’: *a historic victory*; *historical documents*.
- *electric* ‘working by electricity’ — *electrical* ‘relating to electricity in general’: *an electric car*; *electrical engineering*.
- *classic* ‘excellent, typical, of lasting value’ — *classical* ‘of ancient Greece and Rome; of serious music’: *a classic film*; *classical music*.

✗ *This car is very economic.* ✓ *This car is very economical.* ✗ *I like reading historic novels.* ✓ *I like reading historical novels.* Many other words have only one form (*scientific*, *political*, *basic*), and some have two with the same meaning (*geographic*, *geographical*).
:::

## Making verbs

| suffix | meaning | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *-ise*, *-ize* | make, become; put into a state | *modernise*, *organise*, *realise*, *summarise*, *globalise* |
| *-ify* | make | *simplify*, *classify*, *identify*, *purify* |
| *-en* | make or become | *widen*, *shorten*, *darken*, *soften*, *strengthen*, *lengthen* |
| *-ate* | (in Latin-type words) make, act | *activate*, *motivate*, *hyphenate* |

*-ise*/*-ize* and *-ify* are productive, especially in technical and business English (*digitise*, *prioritise*, *gamify*). *-en* is added to short adjectives (*wide*, *short*, *dark*, *soft*) and to a few nouns (*strength*, *length*); no new verbs are made with it.

::: theorem *-ise* or *-ize*? {#rule-ise-ize}
1. **American English** uses *-ize*: *organize*, *realize*, *modernize*.
2. **British English** accepts both. *-ise* is more common in British newspapers and everyday writing; *-ize* is used by Oxford University Press (the "Oxford spelling") and by some other publishers and international organisations. Whichever you choose, use it consistently.
3. Some verbs are **always** spelled *-ise*, in both varieties, because their *-ise* is not this suffix but part of a root: *advertise*, *advise*, *comprise*, *compromise*, *despise*, *exercise*, *improvise*, *revise*, *supervise*, *surprise*, *televise*. And *analyse*, *paralyse* are spelled *-yse* in British English and *-yze* in American English.
4. The nouns follow the verbs: *organisation*/*organization*.
:::

## Making adverbs

The main adverb suffix is *-ly*, added to adjectives: *quick* → *quickly*, *careful* → *carefully*, *happy* → *happily*, *basic* → *basically* (adjectives in *-ic* take *-ally*; *publicly* is the one common exception). Two smaller groups:

- *-ward(s)* ‘in the direction of’: *backward(s)*, *forward(s)*, *upward(s)*, *homeward(s)*. British English prefers *-wards* for adverbs (*walk backwards*); American English often uses *-ward*.
- *-wise* ‘in the manner of; as far as … is concerned’: *clockwise*, *likewise*, and informally *money-wise*, *weather-wise* (*Weather-wise, it was a good week*). The informal use is common in speech but avoided in formal writing.

## Suffixes and stress

Some suffixes change the pronunciation of the base; others leave it unchanged. The rules are set out in [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics#rule-suffix]] and [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics#rule-neutral]]: in short, *-ion*, *-ic*, *-ical*, *-ity*, *-ial* and *-ious* put the stress on the syllable just before them; *-ee* and *-eer* take the stress themselves; *-ness*, *-ment*, *-ful*, *-less*, *-ly*, *-er* and *-ish* do not move it.

What that chapter mentions only briefly is that a stress-moving suffix often changes the **vowel** of the base as well:

| base | derived word | vowel change |
|---|---|---|
| *nation* /ˈneɪʃn/ | *national* /ˈnæʃnəl/ | /eɪ/ → /æ/ |
| *sane* /seɪn/ | *sanity* /ˈsænəti/ | /eɪ/ → /æ/ |
| *divine* /dɪˈvaɪn/ | *divinity* /dɪˈvɪnəti/ | /aɪ/ → /ɪ/ |
| *serene* /səˈriːn/ | *serenity* /səˈrenəti/ | /iː/ → /e/ |
| *photograph* /ˈfəʊtəɡrɑːf/ (GB) | *photography* /fəˈtɒɡrəfi/ (GB) | /əʊ/ → /ə/, /ə/ → /ɒ/ |

The spelling stays the same, so a reader recognises the family at once, but a listener may not: *sanity* does not sound like *sane*. This is one more reason to learn the pronunciation of each family member, not only of the base ([[connected-speech/word-stress-patterns]]).

::: example Stress and vowels in a family {#ex-stress}
Mark the stressed syllable in each word and say which suffix rule explains it.

1. *produce* (verb), *production*, *productive*, *productivity*
2. *person*, *personal*, *personality*
3. *employ*, *employment*, *employee*
::: solution
1. pro**DUCE** — base. pro**DUC**tion — *-ion*: stress just before. pro**DUC**tive — *-ive* here keeps the stress of the base. produc**TIV**ity /ˌprɒdʌkˈtɪvəti/ (GB), /ˌproʊdʌkˈtɪvəti/ (GA) — *-ity*: stress just before, so it moves one syllable to the right.
2. **PER**son — base. **PER**sonal — *-al* does not move it here. perso**NAL**ity /ˌpɜːsəˈnæləti/ (GB), /ˌpɜːrsəˈnæləti/ (GA) — *-ity*: stress on the syllable before, and the weak /ə/ of *personal* becomes a full /æ/.
3. em**PLOY** — base. em**PLOY**ment — *-ment* is neutral. emplo**YEE** — *-ee* takes the stress (some speakers also say em**PLOY**ee).
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which word is stressed on the **third** syllable?
- [ ] *possible*
- [x] *possibility*
- [ ] *impossible*
- [ ] *possibly*
::: solution
*-ity* puts the stress just before it: pos-si-**BIL**-i-ty /ˌpɒsəˈbɪləti/ (GB), /ˌpɑːsəˈbɪləti/ (GA). *Possible* and *possibly* are stressed on the first syllable, *impossible* on the second (the prefix *im-* does not take the stress).
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese has few true suffixes. The commonest are 子 (桌子, 椅子), 儿 (花儿, mainly in northern Mandarin), 头 (石头, 木头) and the plural 们, which is used mainly for people. Besides these, a group of characters work like suffixes in many words: 者 (作者, 读者 — close to *-er*), 家 (科学家, 钢琴家 — close to *-ist*), 员 (服务员, 运动员), 性 (可能性, 重要性 — close to *-ity* and *-ness*) and 化 (现代化, 全球化 — close to *-ise* and *-isation*). Chinese grammarians call them 类后缀 (quasi-suffixes). Several of them, especially 性 and 化, became much more common in the twentieth century, when many words were translated from European languages.

The real difference is not the number of suffixes but what they are used for. In Chinese a word like 发展, 方便 or 成功 can be a noun, a verb or an adjective without any change; its job is shown by its place in the sentence and by words such as 的 and 地. In English, word class is usually shown **in the word**, by the suffix, and the grammar of the sentence demands the right class. So the question a Chinese writer has to learn to ask, every time, is: *Does this place in the sentence need a noun, a verb, an adjective or an adverb?* — and then to choose the suffix that makes it.
:::

::: example Correcting an essay {#ex-essay}
Correct the four word-class mistakes in Liu Yang's sentences at the start of the chapter, and explain each correction.

> *In the last twenty years, the develop of my city has been very rapidly. The government has made many improves, and people's lives are more convenience than before.*
::: solution
1. *the develop* → *the development*. After *the* and before *of* we need a noun; *-ment* makes the noun from the verb *develop*.
2. *has been very rapidly* → *has been very rapid*. After *be* we need an adjective, not an adverb. (Alternatively: *my city has developed very rapidly*, where the adverb describes the verb.)
3. *many improves* → *many improvements*. After *many* we need a plural noun; *-ment* makes it, and *-s* makes it plural.
4. *more convenience* → *more convenient*. After *are more* we need an adjective; *convenience* is the noun.

Corrected: *In the last twenty years, the development of my city has been very rapid. The government has made many improvements, and people's lives are more convenient than before.*
:::
:::

::: history
The suffix *-ize* goes back to the Greek verb ending *-izein*, which passed into Latin as *-izare* and into French as *-iser*. English verbs in *-ize* were at first spelled with *z*, following Greek and Latin; the spelling *-ise* spread in Britain later, under the influence of French, where the ending is spelled with *s*. Noah Webster's *American Dictionary of the English Language* (1828) helped fix *-ize* in American spelling, while British usage remained divided. Oxford University Press kept *-ize* as its house style, which is why the British spelling with *z* is called "Oxford spelling". Both are correct in British English today.
:::

## Where this leads

Some suffixes come with spelling changes — *happy* + *-ness* = *happiness*, *hope* + *-ful* = *hopeful* but *hope* + *-ing* = *hoping* — and [[word-formation/spelling-changes]] sets out the rules, including the choice between *-able* and *-ible*. A word can also change class with **no** suffix at all (*a text*, *to text*); that is [[word-formation/conversion]]. In academic writing, nouns made with *-tion*, *-ment* and *-ity* are especially frequent; [[collocation-and-register/academic-vocabulary]] looks at them, and the stress patterns are practised in [[connected-speech/word-stress-patterns]].

::: summary
- A **suffix** follows the base; a derivational suffix gives the word its **word class** and adds a meaning. The **last** derivational suffix decides the class.
- Nouns from verbs: *-tion*, *-ment*, *-al*, *-ance*/*-ence*, *-ing*. The noun form must be learned with each verb (*decision*, *arrival*, *improvement*); *-ise*/*-ify* verbs take *-ation*.
- Nouns from adjectives: *-ness* (any adjective, the usual choice for new words) and *-ity* (Latin-type bases; moves the stress); *-th* in old words (*width*, *strength*).
- People: *-er* (most productive), *-or*, *-ist*, *-ian*, *-ant*/*-ent*; *-ee* for the person who receives the action.
- Adjectives: *-able* (productive), *-ful*, *-less*, *-ous*, *-al*, *-ive*, *-ic*/*-ical* (*economic* ≠ *economical*), *-y*, *-ish*.
- Verbs: *-ise*/*-ize* (AmE *-ize*; BrE both), *-ify*, *-en*. Adverbs: *-ly*.
- *-ion*, *-ic*, *-ity* move the stress and can change vowels (*sane* → *sanity*); *-ness*, *-ment*, *-ful*, *-less*, *-ly* do not.
:::

*-ity* likes Latin-type bases, especially those ending in *-ar*, *-ible*/*-able* and *-ic*; *-ness* goes with almost anything.
:::
::: solution
- **DARK**ness, **REA**diness, po**LITE**ness, a**WARE**ness — *-ness* does not move the stress.
- popu**LAR**ity, possi**BIL**ity, flexi**BIL**ity, elec**TRIC**ity — *-ity* puts the stress just before it. (*Electric* has *c* /k/, but *electricity* has /s/: /ɪˌlekˈtrɪsəti/.)
:::
:::

::: exercise Correct the paragraph {level=3}
Find and correct the six word-class mistakes in this paragraph.

> *Online shopping is very convenience, and it saves a lot of time. But it also has some disadvantage. Deliveries create a lot of pollute, and some products are not safety. I think we should be more care when we buy things online, and the government should strong the rules.*
::: solution
1. *very convenience* → *very convenient* — an adjective after *very*.
2. *some disadvantage* → *some disadvantages* — *some* with a count noun needs the plural noun.
3. *a lot of pollute* → *a lot of pollution* — a noun after *a lot of*.
4. *not safety* → *not safe* — an adjective after *are not*.
5. *more care* → *more careful* — an adjective after *be more*.
6. *should strong the rules* → *should strengthen the rules* — a verb after *should*; *-en* makes the verb from *strength*.

Mistake 2 is an inflection (the plural *-s*), not a derivational suffix, but it is still a mistake of form that a Chinese writer can easily miss.

Corrected: *Online shopping is very convenient, and it saves a lot of time. But it also has some disadvantages. Deliveries create a lot of pollution, and some products are not safe. I think we should be more careful when we buy things online, and the government should strengthen the rules.*
:::
