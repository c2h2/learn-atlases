Read these messages between two friends, Sun Hao and Emma:

**Sun Hao:** *Did you get my **text**?*

**Emma:** *Sorry, no. Can you **text** me again? Or **email** the details?*

**Sun Hao:** *OK. I'll **email** you tonight. The concert is a **must**, by the way.*

**Emma:** *Great. I'll **google** the band.*

*Text* is a noun in the first line and a verb in the second. *Email* is a verb, though it began as a noun; *must* is usually a modal verb but here a noun (‘something you must not miss’); and *google* is a verb made from the name of a company. None of these words has a suffix to show its new class. The word stays the same, and only its place in the sentence tells you what it is.

This way of making words is called **conversion**. It is one of the most productive processes in modern English, and one of the hardest for learners to notice, precisely because nothing changes in the form. This chapter shows the main kinds of conversion, what the new words mean, how to tell which class a word belongs to in a sentence, and where conversion is not possible.

## What conversion is

::: definition Conversion {#def-conversion}
**Conversion** (转类) is the use of a word in a new word class **without any change of form**: *a text* (noun) → *to text* (verb); *to walk* (verb) → *a walk* (noun); *empty* (adjective) → *to empty* (verb). Because no affix is added, conversion is also called **zero derivation**.
:::

The new word behaves completely like a member of its new class. A noun made by conversion takes articles and the plural: *a walk*, *two walks*. A verb made by conversion takes all the verb endings: *texts*, *texted*, *texting*.

The main kinds are:

| from | to | examples |
|---|---|---|
| noun | verb | *email*, *text*, *phone*, *bottle*, *butter*, *microwave*, *chair* (a meeting), *host* |
| verb | noun | *a walk*, *a run*, *a look*, *a swim*, *a guess*, *a call*, *a must* |
| adjective | verb | *empty*, *clean*, *dry*, *calm (down)*, *warm (up)*, *slow (down)*, *open* |
| other classes | noun or verb | *the ups and downs*, *no ifs or buts*; informal *to up the price*, *to down a drink* |

Noun → verb and verb → noun are by far the most frequent. Adjective → verb is common with short adjectives that describe a change of state: *The streets emptied. Dry your hands. Calm down!*

## Nouns that become verbs

When a noun becomes a verb, the verb's meaning is built from the noun in a few regular ways.

::: theorem Meanings of verbs made from nouns {#rule-noun-verb}
A verb made from a noun usually means one of these:

1. **use the thing as an instrument**: *to hammer* (hit with a hammer), *to email*, *to phone*, *to microwave*, *to brush*.
2. **put something into or onto the thing**: *to bottle* (put into bottles), *to pocket*, *to file*, *to box*.
3. **give or add the thing**: *to butter* (put butter on), *to water*, *to paint*, *to salt*.
4. **take the thing away**: *to peel* (an apple), *to skin*, *to bone* (a fish), *to dust* (the shelves).
5. **act like or as the person**: *to host*, *to chair*, *to nurse*, *to parrot* (repeat without understanding).
6. **go or send by**: *to ship*, *to bus*, *to text*.

The context — and above all the object — shows which meaning is meant.
:::

::: proof
These meanings are not arbitrary. A noun names a thing; a verb made from it names an action in which that thing plays a typical part — as the tool, the place, the material, the thing removed or the role. Because the role is the most typical one for that thing, the listener can usually work out the meaning: you *bottle* wine because bottles hold things, you *hammer* a nail because hammers hit. The same noun can occasionally have two opposite meanings. *Dust* means ‘remove dust from’ in *dust the shelves*, but ‘put a fine powder on’ in *dust the cake with sugar*: dust is both something we remove and something we spread. Dictionaries list both.
:::

::: example What does the verb mean? {#ex-noun-verb}
Explain the meaning of each verb in bold, and say which meaning of [[#rule-noun-verb]] it has.

1. *Could you **butter** the bread?*
2. *She **chaired** the meeting very well.*
3. *Don't just **parrot** the textbook — explain it in your own words.*
4. *First **peel** the potatoes, then cut them.*
5. *I'll **bike** to work tomorrow.*
6. *He **pocketed** the money and walked away.*
::: solution
1. ‘put butter on’ — giving or adding (3).
2. ‘acted as the chair (the person in charge) of’ — acting as a person (5).
3. ‘repeat, like a parrot, without understanding’ — acting like (5).
4. ‘take the peel (skin) off’ — removing (4).
5. ‘go by bike’ — going by (6). *Cycle* is also common.
6. ‘put into his pocket’ — putting into (2); here it also suggests he took money that was not his.
:::
:::

## Verbs that become nouns

A verb turned into a noun usually names **one act** of doing the verb: *a walk* is one occasion of walking, *a look* one act of looking, *a guess* one attempt at guessing. These nouns are very common with a small group of verbs that carry little meaning of their own — *have*, *take*, *give*, *make* — in expressions that are often more natural than the simple verb:

- *have a look*, *have a rest*, *have a swim*, *have a try*, *have a chat*
- *take a walk*, *take a break*, *take a look* (especially AmE)
- *give someone a call*, *give a shout*, *give it a go*
- *make a guess*, *make a call*

*Let's have a swim* sounds more relaxed and informal than *Let's swim*; *Have a look at this* is softer than *Look at this*. British and American English differ in some of these: *have a shower* is more British, *take a shower* more American, but both are understood everywhere. Chunks like these are learned as wholes ([[core-vocabulary/phrases-and-chunks]]).

Some verb-to-noun conversions have a special meaning: *a must* ‘something you should not miss’, *a good buy* ‘something worth buying for its price’, *a hit* ‘a successful song or film’, *a find* ‘a valuable discovery’.

::: quiz
Which sentence uses *look* as a **noun**?
- [ ] *Look at the board, please.*
- [ ] *You look tired.*
- [x] *Can I have a look at your notes?*
- [ ] *We are looking for a flat.*
::: solution
In *have a look*, *look* follows the article *a*: it is a noun meaning ‘one act of looking’. In the other sentences it is a verb: an imperative, a linking verb (*look tired*) and an *-ing* form.
:::
:::

## How to tell the word class in a sentence

Because the form does not help, the sentence must. Position and the small words around a word show its class.

::: theorem Clues to word class {#rule-clues}
A word is probably a **noun** if it:

- follows a determiner: *a*, *the*, *my*, *this*, *some*, *every* (*a **text***, *my **guess***);
- follows an adjective: *a long **walk***;
- has a plural or possessive ending: *two **walks***;
- is the subject or object of a verb, or follows a preposition: *in **reply***.

It is probably a **verb** if it:

- follows a subject and agrees with it, or has a tense ending: *She **texts** me every day; I **emailed** him*;
- follows a modal or *to*: *can **text***, *to **email***;
- is followed by an object: ***bottle** the wine*;
- begins an instruction: ***Water** the plants!*
:::

::: example Noun, verb or adjective? {#ex-class}
Give the word class of each word in bold and the clue that shows it.

1. *Can you **water** the plants while I'm away?*
2. *I need a glass of **water**.*
3. *The kids **empty** the dishwasher every evening.*
4. *The room was **empty**.*
5. *She has two **hits** in the charts this month.*
6. *He **hits** the ball very hard.*
::: solution
1. Verb: after *can you*, followed by an object (*the plants*).
2. Noun: after *of*, the head of *a glass of water*.
3. Verb: after the subject *The kids*, followed by an object; it means ‘make empty’.
4. Adjective: after *was*, describing the room.
5. Noun: after *two*, with the plural *-s* (‘successful songs’).
6. Verb: after the subject *He*, with the third person *-s*, followed by an object.

Sentences 5 and 6 show that the same ending *-s* can be a plural (noun) or a third person singular (verb): only the position decides.
:::
:::

Newspaper headlines leave out most of the small words, so conversion can make them hard to read. *Council plans housing boost* could be read in several ways; a reader has to decide that *plans* is the verb and *housing boost* the object. When a headline is confusing, try each word as a noun and as a verb.

::: example Reading headlines {#ex-headlines}
Find the main verb in each headline and explain what it means. All the headlines were written for this lesson.

1. *Ministers back school meals plan*
2. *Heavy rain floods city centre*
3. *Students face exam changes*
4. *Local team tops league*
::: solution
1. *back* — usually a noun or adverb, here a verb meaning ‘support’: ministers support the plan for school meals. *School meals plan* is a compound noun (the object).
2. *floods* — the noun *flood* used as a verb, ‘covers with water’: heavy rain has covered the city centre with water. The *-s* is the third person singular, not a plural.
3. *face* — the noun *face* used as a verb, ‘have to deal with’: students will have to deal with changes to their exams.
4. *tops* — the noun *top* used as a verb, ‘is at the top of’: the local team is first in the league.

In each case the clue is the same: the word comes after the subject (*Ministers*, *Heavy rain*, *Students*, *Local team*) and before an object, which is the position of a verb ([[#rule-clues]]).
:::
:::

## Conversion with a change of sound or spelling

Some noun–verb pairs of Latin origin are spelled the same but differ in stress: the noun is stressed on the first syllable and the verb on the second.

| noun | verb |
|---|---|
| a **RE**cord | to re**CORD** |
| a **PRE**sent | to pre**SENT** |
| an **IN**crease | to in**CREASE** |
| an **EX**port, an **IM**port | to ex**PORT**, to im**PORT** |
| a **PRO**test | to pro**TEST** |
| a **SUS**pect | to sus**PECT** |

Linguists usually treat these as conversion with a change of stress. The pattern is described in [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics#rule-two]] and practised in [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics#ex-noun-verb]]; it is a tendency, not a law (*a re**PORT***, *to re**PORT*** are stressed the same).

A second small group differ in their final consonant, and sometimes in spelling:

- *use* /juːs/ (noun) — *use* /juːz/ (verb); *house* /haʊs/ (noun) — *house* /haʊz/ (verb); *close* /kləʊs/ (GB), /kloʊs/ (GA) (adjective ‘near’) — *close* /kləʊz/ (GB), /kloʊz/ (GA) (verb ‘shut’).
- *advice* (noun) — *advise* (verb); *belief* — *believe*; *proof* — *prove*; *half* — *halve*.
- British English: *practice* (noun) — *practise* (verb); *licence* (noun) — *license* (verb). American English uses *practice* for both and *license* for both.

These are old pairs, not modern conversions, but they need the same care: the sentence decides the class, and the class decides the pronunciation or spelling.

::: warning *Advice* or *advise*?
✗ *Can you advice me?* ✓ *Can you advise me?* (verb, with *s* and /z/: /ədˈvaɪz/) — ✗ *She gave me a good advise.* ✓ *She gave me some good advice.* (noun, with *c* and /s/: /ədˈvaɪs/; also uncountable, so not *an advice*). In British English the same pattern holds for *practice*/*practise*: ✓ *I need more practice. I practise every day.*
:::

## Where conversion is not possible

Conversion is productive, but it is not free. Three things limit it.

::: theorem Limits of conversion {#rule-limits}
1. **An existing word blocks it.** If there is already a word with the meaning, conversion is usually not used: ✗ *to decision* (we have *decide*), ✗ *a decide* (we have *decision*), ✗ *to arrival* (*arrive*). So learners cannot convert a word just because Chinese uses one form for both classes.
2. **Words with derivational suffixes rarely convert.** *Kindness*, *happiness*, *development* do not become verbs, and *-ise*/*-ify* verbs do not become nouns. Conversion works mostly on short, simple words.
3. **New conversions may be informal or disliked.** Verbs such as *to impact* (‘to affect’), *to action* (‘to deal with’) and *to task* are common in business English, and nouns such as *an ask* (‘a request’) and *a spend* (‘an amount of money spent’) are spreading, but many readers find them jargon. Dictionaries record them; in formal writing, use them only if they are already established in your field.
:::

::: proof
Blocking works for conversion just as for suffixes ([[word-formation/suffixes#rule-verb-noun]]): speakers prefer the established word, so a second word with exactly the same meaning has no use. When a conversion does exist beside a related word, it usually means something different: *to author* a book is not quite the same as *to write* it (it suggests being the official author), and *to host* an event is more than *to receive guests*. Short, simple words convert easily because English has lost almost all the endings that once marked the class of such words (see the history note below); longer derived words carry their class in their suffix, and changing it without changing the suffix would send a contradictory signal.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is **not** acceptable English?
- [ ] *Can you microwave the soup?*
- [ ] *We bused the children to the museum.*
- [x] *The company decisioned to close the factory.*
- [ ] *She's hosting a party on Saturday.*
::: solution
*Decision* cannot be used as a verb: the verb *decide* already exists, so the conversion is blocked. ✓ *The company decided to close the factory.* *Microwave*, *bus* and *host* are established verbs made by conversion. (*Bused* is also spelled *bussed*.)
:::
:::

::: example Rewriting with conversion {#ex-rewrite}
Rewrite each sentence so that the word in bold changes class by conversion. Keep the meaning.

1. *I sent her a **text** last night.* (→ verb)
2. *Let's **walk** in the park after lunch.* (→ noun)
3. *Please make the bin **empty** before you leave.* (→ verb)
4. *I'll send the file by **email**.* (→ verb)
5. *Can you **look** at my essay?* (→ noun)
::: solution
1. *I texted her last night.*
2. *Let's have* (or *take*, *go for*) *a walk in the park after lunch.*
3. *Please empty the bin before you leave.*
4. *I'll email the file* (or *email you the file*).
5. *Can you have* (or *take*) *a look at my essay?*

The converted verbs take normal verb endings (*texted*); the converted nouns need a determiner and usually a light verb (*have a walk*, *have a look*).
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese speakers know the idea behind conversion well. Many Chinese words belong to more than one class — Chinese grammarians call them 兼类词 — and words such as 学习, 研究, 发展 and 决定 work as nouns or verbs with no change. Classical Chinese went further and could use almost any word as a verb: in 王安石's line 春风又绿江南岸, the adjective 绿 ‘green’ is used as a verb, ‘makes green’ — exactly the kind of use English has in *The spring wind greens the riverbank*.

So conversion itself is not strange. What causes mistakes is the difference in scope. In Chinese a multi-class word is the normal case; in English most words still have a separate form for each class (*decide*/*decision*, *succeed*/*success*), and conversion is possible only where English usage allows it. The Chinese habit therefore leads to ✗ *We must decision quickly* or ✗ *the develop of the city*. A second difference is that an English converted word takes all the endings of its new class: ✓ *She emailed me*, ✓ *two walks*, while the Chinese word never changes. When you translate, the opposite move is also common: an English noun often becomes a Chinese verb (*He is a good swimmer* → 他游泳游得很好), which translators call 词类转换 ([[translation/structural-shifts]]).
:::

::: history
Conversion became easy in English when the language lost most of its endings. In Old English, nouns and verbs usually looked different: the verb ‘to love’ was *lufian*, with the infinitive ending *-ian*, while the noun ‘love’ was *lufu*. As these endings weakened and disappeared in Middle English, many nouns and verbs ended up with the same form (*love*, *love*), and from then on it was natural to use any noun as a verb and the reverse. By Shakespeare's time conversion was a favourite tool of writers: in *Richard II* (c. 1595) the Duke of York answers a young man who calls him ‘gracious uncle’ with *Grace me no grace, nor uncle me no uncle*, turning two nouns into verbs. The process continues today. *Google*, the name of a company founded in 1998, was added to the *Oxford English Dictionary* as a verb in 2006.
:::

## Where this leads

Conversion changes class without a suffix; [[word-formation/suffixes]] shows the much larger group of words that change class with one, and [[word-formation/compounds]] shows how phrasal verbs become nouns (*to set up* → *a setup*), often with a change of stress. The use of *have a look*, *take a break* and similar chunks is part of [[collocation-and-register/verb-noun-adjective-noun]]. For the stress of noun–verb pairs, see [[connected-speech/word-stress-patterns]]; for word-class changes in translation, see [[translation/structural-shifts]].

::: summary
- **Conversion** uses a word in a new class with **no change of form**: *a text* → *to text*, *to walk* → *a walk*, *empty* → *to empty*.
- Noun → verb and verb → noun are the commonest. A converted word takes all the endings of its new class: *texted*, *two walks*.
- Verbs from nouns mean ‘use as a tool’ (*hammer*), ‘put into’ (*bottle*), ‘add’ (*butter*), ‘remove’ (*peel*), ‘act as’ (*host*) or ‘go by’ (*bus*).
- Nouns from verbs usually name one act (*a walk*, *a look*) and often follow *have*, *take*, *give*: *have a look*, *take a break*.
- The class is shown by position and the words around it: determiners before nouns, subjects, modals and objects around verbs.
- Some pairs differ in stress (*a **RE**cord* / *to re**CORD***), sound (*use* /s/ ~ /z/) or spelling (*advice* / *advise*; BrE *practice* / *practise*).
- Conversion is blocked by an existing word (✗ *to decision*), is rare with suffixed words, and some new conversions (*to action*, *an ask*) are informal jargon.
:::
