Zhou Min's flatmate has left a note on the fridge:

> *Got flu, sorry! Can't come to the lab today. Could you tell Prof. Ellis I'll email the stats ASAP? Also, brunch on Sat is still on — the café by the gym. BTW, the new sitcom on the BBC is great!*

Zhou Min knows all the words in their full form, but here nearly half of them are shortened. *Flu* is *influenza*; *lab* is *laboratory*; *fridge* is *refrigerator*; *stats* is *statistics*; *gym* is *gymnasium*; *Prof.* is *Professor*; *Sat* is *Saturday*. *Brunch* is made from *breakfast* and *lunch*, and *sitcom* from *situation comedy*. *ASAP*, *BTW* and *BBC* are made from the first letters of *as soon as possible*, *by the way* and *British Broadcasting Corporation*.

English shortens words in three main ways: by cutting off part of a word (**clipping**), by joining parts of two words (**blending**), and by using the first letters of several words (**acronyms** and **initialisms**). Shortened words are everywhere in conversation, messages, news and science, and many have become ordinary words that speakers no longer feel as short forms at all: *bus*, *photo*, *exam*, *laser*. This chapter explains the three processes, how the short forms are pronounced and written, which ones are informal, and which articles go before them.

## Clipping

::: definition Clipping {#def-clipping}
**Clipping** (截短) makes a shorter word by cutting off part of a longer one, without changing its meaning or word class: *laboratory* → *lab*, *examination* → *exam*, *telephone* → *phone*, *influenza* → *flu*. The new word is a **clipping** (截短词) or a **clipped form**.
:::

English clips words in three places. The end is by far the most common.

| what is cut | examples |
|---|---|
| the **end** (back clipping) | *lab*(*oratory*), *exam*(*ination*), *gym*(*nasium*), *photo*(*graph*), *ad*(*vertisement*), *info*(*rmation*), *memo*(*randum*), *uni*(*versity*) (BrE), *maths*/*math* (*mathematics*) |
| the **beginning** (front clipping) | (*tele*)*phone*, (*omni*)*bus*, (*aero*)*plane* / (*air*)*plane* |
| **both ends** | (*in*)*flu*(*enza*), (*re*)*frig*(*erator*) → *fridge* |

Some clippings are made from a phrase or compound: *mobile* (*phone*), *pub* (*public house*), *sitcom* (*situation comedy*), *sci-fi* (*science fiction*). And some change their spelling to fit the pronunciation: *bike* (from *bicycle*), *fridge* (with *dg* for the sound /dʒ/), *mic* or *mike* (from *microphone*). British and Australian English also add *-y* or *-ie* to a clipped word, which makes it sound friendly and informal: *telly* (*television*), *brekkie* (*breakfast*), *barbie* (*barbecue*, especially Australian).

::: theorem Clippings and register {#rule-clip-register}
1. Most clippings begin in informal speech. Many stay **informal**: *uni*, *telly*, *info*, *ad*, *fridge*, *stats*, *mic*. In formal writing, use the full word: *university*, *television*, *information*, *advertisement*, *refrigerator*, *statistics*.
2. Some clippings have become **neutral** and are used in all registers, while the full form sounds old-fashioned or technical: *bus* (not *omnibus*), *phone*, *photo*, *exam*, *flu*, *plane*, *pub*, *gym*. Some full forms are used only in special senses: *influenza* in medicine, *examination* in formal or medical contexts (*a medical examination*).
3. British and American English differ in some: BrE *maths*, AmE *math*; BrE *uni* (no American equivalent; AmE says *college* or *school*).

A learner's dictionary labels clippings *informal* where they are.
:::

::: proof
A shorter word is easier to say, so frequently used words tend to get shorter, and the shortening usually starts in the groups that use a word most — students (*exam*, *lab*, *uni*), doctors and nurses, people who work in a trade. Once a clipping is used by everyone and in every situation, the long form starts to sound marked, and the clipping becomes the normal word. This happened long ago to *bus* (from *omnibus*, early nineteenth century), *cab* (from *cabriolet*) and *pram* (from *perambulator*), and more recently to *phone* and *photo*. Whether a clipping is still informal is therefore a fact about each word at a given time, which is why dictionaries label it.
:::

::: quiz
Which clipping would be **most** out of place in a formal university essay?
- [ ] *bus*
- [ ] *exam*
- [x] *info*
- [ ] *phone*
::: solution
*Info* is still informal: in an essay, write *information*. *Bus*, *exam* and *phone* are now neutral words used in all registers. (*Exam* is fine in most academic writing; *examination* is more formal still.)
:::
:::

::: example Which part was cut? {#ex-clipping}
For each clipping, give the full word, say which part was cut (end, beginning or both), and say whether the clipping is informal or neutral.

1. *I left my umbrella on the **plane**.*
2. *There's some milk in the **fridge**.*
3. *My sister is in her second year at **uni**.*
4. *Let's take the **bus**.*
5. *I'll send you the **memo**.*
::: solution
1. *plane* ← *aeroplane* (BrE) or *airplane* (AmE): the beginning was cut. Neutral.
2. *fridge* ← *refrigerator*: both ends were cut, and the spelling changed (*dg* for /dʒ/). Informal to neutral; in technical writing, *refrigerator*.
3. *uni* ← *university*: the end was cut. Informal, and mainly British and Australian.
4. *bus* ← *omnibus*: the beginning was cut. Neutral — *omnibus* is no longer used for the vehicle.
5. *memo* ← *memorandum*: the end was cut. Neutral in offices; *memorandum* is formal or legal.
:::
:::

## Blends

::: definition Blend {#def-blend}
A **blend** (拼缀词) is a word made by joining parts of two words, usually the beginning of the first and the end of the second: *br*(*eakfast*) + (*l*)*unch* → *brunch*; *sm*(*oke*) + (*f*)*og* → *smog*; *mo*(*tor*) + (*ho*)*tel* → *motel*. The meaning combines the meanings of both words.
:::

A blend differs from a compound because parts of the words are cut away (a compound keeps whole words: *bus stop*), and from a clipping because two words are combined (a clipping shortens one word). Common blends:

| blend | from | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *brunch* | *breakfast* + *lunch* | a late-morning meal |
| *smog* | *smoke* + *fog* | polluted air in a city |
| *motel* | *motor* + *hotel* | a hotel for people travelling by car |
| *emoticon* | *emotion* + *icon* | a face made of keyboard signs, such as :-) |
| *podcast* | *iPod* + *broadcast* | a recorded programme to download |
| *vlog* | *video* + *blog* | a blog made of videos |
| *Chinglish* | *Chinese* + *English* | English shaped by transfer from Chinese |
| *Brexit* | *Britain* + *exit* | the United Kingdom's leaving the European Union |

Blends are popular in advertising, journalism and technology, because they are short and catchy. Some (*brunch*, *smog*, *motel*) have become ordinary words; many others are made for one occasion and soon forgotten. A few blends have produced new word parts: from *alcoholic*, the ending *-aholic* (‘a person who cannot stop doing or having something’) appears in *workaholic* and *chocaholic*; from *Watergate*, the ending *-gate* is used in journalism for any scandal.

::: example How is the blend made? {#ex-blend}
Show how each blend is made, and give its meaning.

1. *motel*
2. *Spanglish*
3. *infotainment*
4. *emoticon*
::: solution
1. *mo*(*tor*) + (*h*)*otel* — a hotel for people travelling by car, with parking near the rooms. The two words overlap in *-otel*.
2. *Span*(*ish*) + (*E*)*nglish* — a mixture of Spanish and English, used by some bilingual speakers in the United States. Compare *Chinglish*.
3. *info*(*rmation*) + (*enter*)*tainment* — television programmes that present news or facts in an entertaining way.
4. *emoti*(*on*) + *icon* — a picture of a face made from keyboard characters, such as :-). Today's small pictures of faces and objects are called *emoji*, a word borrowed from Japanese, not a blend of *emotion*, although many English speakers connect the two.

In each blend, the reader can recover the two source words only if they know them: blends depend on the shared knowledge of writer and reader, which is why many blends do not last.
:::
:::

## Acronyms and initialisms

::: definition Initialism and acronym {#def-acronym}
Both are made from the first letters of a phrase.

- An **initialism** (首字母缩略词) is pronounced **letter by letter**: *BBC* /ˌbiː biː ˈsiː/ (*British Broadcasting Corporation*), *USA*, *EU*, *CEO*, *DIY* (*do it yourself*), *ATM*.
- An **acronym** (首字母拼音词) is pronounced **as a word**: *NASA* /ˈnæsə/ (*National Aeronautics and Space Administration*), *NATO* /ˈneɪtəʊ/ (GB), /ˈneɪtoʊ/ (GA), *AIDS*, *PIN* (*personal identification number*).

Many people use *acronym* for both kinds; dictionaries do too. The distinction matters for pronunciation and for the choice of *a* or *an*.
:::

Some acronyms have become so ordinary that they are written in small letters, and most speakers do not know they were ever abbreviations: *laser* (*light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation*), *radar* (*radio detection and ranging*), *scuba* (*self-contained underwater breathing apparatus*). Some abbreviations can be said both ways: *FAQ* (*frequently asked questions*) as /ˌef eɪ ˈkjuː/ or /fæk/, *ASAP* as /ˌeɪ es eɪ ˈpiː/ or /ˈeɪsæp/.

::: theorem Saying and writing initialisms {#rule-initialism}
1. **Stress**: an initialism is stressed on its **last** letter, with weaker stresses on the others: *B B **C***, *U **K***, *C E **O***, *D I **Y***.
2. **Letter names**: say the English names of the letters. The letter *Z* is *zed* /zed/ in British English and *zee* /ziː/ in American English; *H* is /eɪtʃ/ (some speakers say /heɪtʃ/).
3. **Plurals**: add a small *-s*, usually without an apostrophe: *CDs*, *MPs*, *PhDs*, *the 1990s*.
4. **Full stops**: modern British usage writes *BBC*, *UK*, *PhD*, *eg*/*e.g.*, *Dr*, *Mr* with few or no full stops; American usage more often writes *U.S.*, *Ph.D.*, *Dr.*, *Mr.* Follow one style consistently.
5. **First use**: in writing for a general reader, give the full form the first time, with the short form in brackets: *the World Health Organization (WHO)*. After that the short form alone is enough.
:::

::: theorem *A* or *an*; *the* or no article {#rule-article}
1. The choice of *a* or *an* depends on the **first sound**, not the first letter. Many letter names begin with a vowel sound: *F* /ef/, *H* /eɪtʃ/, *L* /el/, *M* /em/, *N* /en/, *S* /es/, *X* /eks/, and of course *A*, *E*, *I*, *O*. So: *an FBI agent*, *an MP*, *an NHS hospital*, *an SUV*, *an X-ray*. But *U* begins with /j/: *a UN report*, *a UFO*. And an acronym said as a word follows its own first sound: *a NATO country*, *a NASA scientist*.
2. **Initialisms** that name organisations, countries and institutions usually take ***the***: *the BBC*, *the EU*, *the UN*, *the UK*, *the USA*. **Acronyms** said as words usually do not: *NASA*, *NATO*, *UNESCO*. Some initialisms are used without *the* because they are names of things, not of institutions: *DIY*, *CPR*, *TV* (*on TV*).
:::

::: quiz
Which is correct?
- [ ] *She works for a NHS hospital.*
- [x] *She works for an NHS hospital.*
- [ ] *She works for a hospital of NHS.*
- [ ] *She works for an the NHS hospital.*
::: solution
*NHS* (*National Health Service*, the British public health system) is an initialism said /ˌen eɪtʃ ˈes/. It begins with the vowel sound /e/, so it takes *an*: *an NHS hospital*. Without the following noun, we use *the*: *She works for the NHS.*
:::
:::

::: example Pronounce and choose the article {#ex-article}
Say each abbreviation aloud, decide whether it is an initialism or an acronym, and put *a* or *an* before it.

1. ___ *FAQ* page
2. ___ *UFO*
3. ___ *NASA* mission
4. ___ *MBA* student
5. ___ *USB* cable
6. ___ *HR* manager
::: solution
1. *an FAQ page* if said as letters /ˌef eɪ ˈkjuː/ (initialism); *a FAQ page* if said as the word /fæk/ (acronym). Both are found.
2. *a UFO* — usually said as letters, /ˌjuː ef ˈəʊ/ (GB), /ˌjuː ef ˈoʊ/ (GA); *U* begins with /j/, so *a*. (Some speakers say /ˈjuːfəʊ/, also with *a*.)
3. *a NASA mission* — said as a word, /ˈnæsə/, beginning with /n/.
4. *an MBA student* (*Master of Business Administration*) — *M* is /em/.
5. *a USB cable* — *U* is /juː/.
6. *an HR manager* (*human resources*) — *H* is /eɪtʃ/. (Speakers who say /heɪtʃ/ write *a HR manager*; *an* is the usual written form.)
:::
:::

## Shortened forms in messages

Text messages, chats and social media use many abbreviations: *BTW* (*by the way*), *FYI* (*for your information*), *IMO* (*in my opinion*), *LOL* (*laughing out loud*), *TBH* (*to be honest*), *ASAP*, and short spellings such as *u* (*you*), *thx* (*thanks*) and *pls* (*please*). They are useful between friends, and *ASAP* and *FYI* are common in workplace emails. But they are informal, and they can confuse readers who do not know them, so they do not belong in essays, formal emails or job applications.

::: warning Short forms in the wrong register
- ✗ *Dear Professor Smith, I'd like some info about the uni's MA programmes ASAP. Thx!*
- ✓ *Dear Professor Smith, I would be grateful for some information about the university's MA programmes. Thank you in advance.*

*MA* (*Master of Arts*) is fine: it is a standard initialism. *Info*, *uni*, *ASAP* and *Thx* are informal. Shortened forms also need care with an audience from another country: *uni* is unusual in American English, and initialisms of local institutions (*NHS*, *DMV*, *HSK*) should be written out in full for readers who may not know them.
:::

::: example From message to formal email {#ex-register}
Rewrite this message to a colleague as a short formal email to a manager. Replace every informal shortened form.

> *Hi! FYI the stats from the lab are ready. Can u check the info b4 the exam board mtg on Fri? Thx, Ana*
::: solution
A model:

> *Dear Ms Wang,*
>
> *I am writing to let you know that the statistics from the laboratory are now ready. Could you please check the information before the examination board meeting on Friday?*
>
> *Thank you.*
>
> *Best regards,*
> *Ana Costa*

Changes: *FYI* → *I am writing to let you know*; *stats* → *statistics*; *lab* → *laboratory* (in many workplaces *lab* would be acceptable); *u* → *you*; *info* → *information*; *b4* → *before*; *exam* → *examination* (or keep *exam*, which is neutral); *mtg* → *meeting*; *Fri* → *Friday*; *Thx* → *Thank you*. The greeting and closing also change to the formal pattern ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/emails-messages]]).
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese shortens names and phrases constantly, but usually in a different way from English. A Chinese 缩略语 or 简称 is normally made by taking **one character from each word**: 北京大学 → 北大, 高速铁路 → 高铁, 春节运输 → 春运, 环境保护 → 环保. Because each character is a syllable with a meaning, the short form is still a meaningful word. The nearest English equivalents are clipped compounds and blends such as *sitcom* and *sci-fi*; English initialisms like *BBC* have no Chinese counterpart made from Chinese characters.

Chinese does use many English initialisms directly — *GDP*, *CEO*, *VIP*, *ATM*, *Wi-Fi*, *CT* — and some made from Pinyin, such as *RMB* (人民币) and *HSK* (汉语水平考试). Two points need attention when you use initialisms in English. First, say them with English letter names and stress on the last letter: *G D **P***, *C E **O***; the letter *Z* is *zed* in British and *zee* in American English. Second, Chinese short forms translated literally do not work in English: an English reader cannot recover 高铁 from a made-up initialism. Write the full English name (*high-speed rail*) and add a short form only if it is already established.
:::

::: history
Clipped words have been used in English for centuries, and they have often been criticised. *Mob* was clipped in the 1680s from the Latin phrase *mobile vulgus* ‘the fickle common people’, and in 1710 Jonathan Swift complained in *The Tatler* about fashionable clippings such as *mob* and *rep* (for *reputation*), which he thought were spoiling the language. *Mob* survived; *rep* is still used informally. Blends got their popular name from Lewis Carroll: in *Through the Looking-Glass* (1871), Humpty Dumpty explains that *slithy* means ‘lithe and slimy’ — ‘it's like a portmanteau — there are two meanings packed up into one word’. (A portmanteau was a large travelling bag that opened into two halves.) Acronyms became common in the twentieth century, with the growth of large organisations, armies and technology; *radar* was coined in the United States Navy during the Second World War, and *laser* was coined in the late 1950s, shortly before the first laser was built in 1960.
:::

## Where this leads

Shortened words are a large part of informal vocabulary, and choosing between the full and the short form is a question of register: see [[collocation-and-register/register]] and, for messages and emails, [[sentences-and-paragraphs/emails-messages]]. Back-formations such as *babysit* (from *babysitter*), which shorten a word by removing what looks like a suffix, are discussed in [[word-formation/learning-with-word-formation]]. Many clippings come from Greek and Latin words (*photo*, *bus*, *lab*); their roots are the subject of [[word-formation/greek-latin-roots]].

::: summary
- **Clipping** cuts part of a word: usually the end (*lab*, *exam*, *photo*), sometimes the beginning (*phone*, *bus*) or both ends (*flu*, *fridge*). The meaning and word class do not change.
- Many clippings are **informal** (*uni*, *info*, *telly*, *stats*); others are now neutral (*bus*, *phone*, *exam*, *flu*). Check the dictionary label.
- A **blend** joins parts of two words: *brunch*, *smog*, *motel*, *Chinglish*. Some last; many do not.
- An **initialism** is said letter by letter (*BBC*, *CEO*); an **acronym** is said as a word (*NASA*, *laser*). Initialisms are stressed on the last letter.
- *A* or *an* depends on the first **sound**: *an MP*, *an FBI agent*, *a UN report*, *a NASA scientist*. Initialisms for institutions usually take *the* (*the BBC*, *the EU*); acronyms said as words usually do not (*NASA*).
- Write the full form the first time for a general reader; keep message abbreviations (*BTW*, *thx*) out of formal writing.
:::
