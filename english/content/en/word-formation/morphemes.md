Wang Fang is reading a news story and meets a word she has never seen: *unaffordable*. She does not reach for her phone. She sees three parts she already knows — *un-* ‘not’, *afford* ‘have enough money for’, and *-able* ‘can be …’ — and puts them together: ‘that people do not have enough money for’. The sentence is *Housing in the city centre has become unaffordable for young families*, and her guess fits perfectly.

Chinese readers do this all the time without thinking. If you meet 电动车 for the first time, you know 电, 动 and 车, so you understand it at once. English words are built from parts too, but the parts are harder to see: they are not separate characters, they do not always match syllables, and their spelling can change when they are joined. This chapter shows what the parts of English words are, gives them names, and explains how to split a word into its parts safely. The rest of the course builds on it.

The core idea of word families — inflection and derivation, and the common endings for each word class — was introduced in [[core-vocabulary/word-families]]. Here we look more closely at the parts themselves.

## The smallest parts with meaning

::: definition Morpheme {#def-morpheme}
A **morpheme** (语素) is the smallest part of a word that has a meaning or a grammatical job. It cannot be divided into smaller parts that still have meaning.

- *cat* is one morpheme.
- *cats* is two: *cat* + *-s* (‘more than one’).
- *unkind* is two: *un-* (‘not’) + *kind*.
- *unkindness* is three: *un-* + *kind* + *-ness* (makes a noun).
- *teachers* is three: *teach* + *-er* (‘a person who …’) + *-s* (plural).
:::

The test is meaning. *Kind* cannot be divided further: *k* and *ind* mean nothing. And *-ness* is a morpheme because it does the same job in hundreds of words: *kindness*, *darkness*, *sadness*, *happiness*, *illness*.

Morphemes are not the same as syllables. A **syllable** (音节) is a unit of sound; a morpheme is a unit of meaning. The two often do not match:

| word | syllables | morphemes |
|---|---|---|
| *elephant* | 3 (*el-e-phant*) | 1 |
| *cats* | 1 | 2 (*cat* + *-s*) |
| *walked* /wɔːkt/ | 1 | 2 (*walk* + *-ed*) |
| *helpful* | 2 (*help-ful*) | 2 (*help* + *-ful*) |
| *banana* | 3 | 1 |
| *unhappiness* | 4 | 3 (*un-* + *happy* + *-ness*) |

*Elephant* and *banana* are long words with no parts: no piece of them means anything by itself. *Walked* is a short word with two parts: the ending *-ed* adds no syllable here, but it adds a meaning, ‘in the past’.

::: quiz
How many morphemes are there in *rewritten*?
- [ ] 1
- [ ] 2
- [x] 3
- [ ] 4
::: solution
Three: *re-* (‘again’) + *write* + *-en* (the past participle ending, as in *taken*, *eaten*). The final *e* of *write* is dropped and the *t* is doubled when *-en* is added, but the spelling change does not add or remove a morpheme. The word has three syllables too (*re-writ-ten*), but that is a coincidence: count meanings, not syllables.
:::
:::

## Free and bound morphemes

Some morphemes can stand alone as words. Others only appear joined to something else.

::: definition Free and bound morphemes {#def-free-bound}
A **free morpheme** (自由语素) can be used as a word by itself: *kind*, *book*, *help*, *happy*, *the*. A **bound morpheme** (粘着语素) cannot stand alone; it must be attached to another morpheme: *un-*, *re-*, *-ness*, *-ful*, *-s*, *-ed*.
:::

Most prefixes and suffixes are bound. But some **roots** are bound too. Look at these words:

- *receive*, *perceive*, *deceive*, *conceive*
- *reception*, *perception*, *deception*, *conception*

They clearly share a part, *-ceive* (*-cept-* before *-ion*), and the prefixes *re-*, *per-*, *de-* and *con-* are familiar. But *ceive* is not an English word. It comes from the Latin verb *capere* ‘take’, and it only appears inside longer words. It is a **bound root**. The chapter [[word-formation/greek-latin-roots]] is about roots like this.

::: definition Root, affix, prefix, suffix and base {#def-root-affix}
- The **root** (词根) is the core of a word, the morpheme that carries its main meaning: *kind* in *unkindness*, *help* in *unhelpful*, *-ceive* in *receive*.
- An **affix** (词缀) is a bound morpheme added to a root or to a larger word. An affix before the root is a **prefix** (前缀): *un-*, *re-*, *dis-*. An affix after it is a **suffix** (后缀): *-ness*, *-ful*, *-ly*.
- The **base** is whatever an affix is added to. It can be a root (*kind* in *kindness*) or a word that already has affixes (*unkind* in *unkindness*; *helpful* in *helpfulness*). Some books call the base a **stem** (词干).
:::

We write prefixes with a hyphen after them (*un-*) and suffixes with a hyphen before them (*-ness*), to show which side they attach to. The hyphen is not part of the spelling: *unkind*, not *un-kind*.

Most English words have one root. A **compound** has two or more: *bookshop* is *book* + *shop*, and *bookshops* is *book* + *shop* + *-s*. Compounds are the subject of [[word-formation/compounds]].

::: quiz
Which word contains a **bound root** — a root that is not an English word by itself?
- [ ] *careless*
- [ ] *unhappy*
- [x] *inspect*
- [ ] *rebuild*
::: solution
*Inspect* is *in-* ‘into’ + *spect* ‘look’ (from Latin *specere*, as in *spectator*, *respect*, *prospect*). *Spect* is not a word on its own, so it is a bound root. The roots of the others — *care*, *happy*, *build* — are all free.
:::
:::

## Inflection and derivation

Bound morphemes at the end of a word do two very different jobs. You met the difference in [[core-vocabulary/word-families#def-inflection-derivation]]: an **inflection** (屈折变化) marks grammar and keeps the same word, while **derivation** (派生) makes a new word. English has only eight inflectional suffixes:

| inflection | job | example |
|---|---|---|
| *-s* | plural of nouns | *books* |
| *-'s* | possessive | *Anna's* |
| *-s* | third person singular present of verbs | *works* |
| *-ed* | past tense | *worked* |
| *-ed* / *-en* | past participle | *worked*, *taken* |
| *-ing* | *-ing* form of verbs | *working* |
| *-er* | comparative | *faster* |
| *-est* | superlative | *fastest* |

Every other affix is derivational. That includes all English prefixes. How do you tell the two kinds apart when a word is new to you? Four tests help.

::: theorem Four tests for inflection and derivation {#rule-four-tests}
1. **Word class.** Derivation can change the word class (*kind* adj. → *kindness* noun; *teach* verb → *teacher* noun). Inflection never does: *books* is still a noun, *worked* is still a verb.
2. **Range.** An inflection can be added to almost every word of its class: nearly every count noun has a plural, nearly every verb has a past tense. A derivational affix is choosy: we say *kindness* and *sadness*, but not ✗ *goodity* or ✗ *happity*.
3. **Order.** Inflections come last. In *teachers* the plural *-s* follows the derivational *-er*; ✗ *teachser* is impossible. Derivational affixes are inside, nearer the root.
4. **Grammar.** Inflections are often required by the sentence: *She work___ here* must be *works*. Derivational affixes are a choice of word, not a requirement of grammar.
:::

::: proof
The tests follow from what the two kinds of affix are for. An inflection gives a grammatical form of a word that already exists, so it must be available for every word that may need that form, and it cannot change what kind of word it is. A derivational affix makes a new word, which enters the dictionary as a word in its own right; whether a given new word exists is partly a matter of history and use, so the range is uneven. The tests are reliable for English because English has so few inflections. They are tendencies elsewhere: a few derivational affixes, such as *-ly* added to adjectives, are almost as regular as inflections, and some grammarians therefore discuss *-ly* together with inflection. Learner's dictionaries still list *quickly* as a separate word from *quick*.
:::

Two endings belong to both kinds. You have to look at the job, not the form.

- *-er*: in *taller*, *faster* it is the comparative (inflection); in *teacher*, *driver*, *printer* it makes a noun (derivation).
- *-ing*: in *She is building a house* it is the *-ing* form of the verb (inflection); in *a tall building* it is part of a noun, *building* ‘a house, office, etc.’, which can even have its own plural, *buildings* (derivation).

::: example Inflection or derivation? {#ex-which-ending}
Decide whether each ending in bold is an inflection or a derivational suffix, and give your reason.

1. *The new print**er** is faster than the old one.*
2. *This road is wid**er** than ours.*
3. *She has three sister**s**.*
4. *We had a long meet**ing** this morning.*
5. *They are meet**ing** us at the station.*
::: solution
1. *printer*: derivation. *-er* turns the verb *print* into a noun ‘a machine that prints’ (test 1: the class changes).
2. *wider*: inflection. *-er* is the comparative of the adjective *wide*; *wider* is still an adjective (test 1), and almost every short adjective has this form (test 2). *Faster* in sentence 1 is also an inflection.
3. *sisters*: inflection, the plural of the noun.
4. *meeting*: derivation. *A long meeting* is a noun (it follows *a long*, and has a plural, *meetings*).
5. *meeting*: inflection. *Are meeting* is the present progressive of the verb *meet*.
:::
:::

## One morpheme, several forms

The plural ending is written *-s* or *-es*, but it is pronounced in three different ways.

::: definition Allomorph {#def-allomorph}
An **allomorph** (语素变体) is one of the different forms of the same morpheme. The plural morpheme has three spoken allomorphs: /s/ in *cats* /kæts/, /z/ in *bags* /bæɡz/ and /ɪz/ in *buses* /ˈbʌsɪz/. The prefix *in-* ‘not’ has the written allomorphs *in-*, *im-*, *il-* and *ir-*: *incorrect*, *impossible*, *illegal*, *irregular*.
:::

The choice of allomorph is not random. It depends on the sound before the ending.

::: theorem Pronouncing *-s* and *-ed* {#rule-s-ed}
The endings *-s* (plural, possessive and third person singular) and *-ed* (past tense and past participle) are pronounced according to the last sound of the base:

| last sound of the base | *-s* | *-ed* |
|---|---|---|
| /s/, /z/, /ʃ/, /ʒ/, /tʃ/, /dʒ/ | /ɪz/: *buses*, *wishes*, *watches*, *judges* | as in the rows below: *wished* /t/, *judged* /d/ |
| /t/, /d/ | as in the rows below: *cats* /s/, *beds* /z/ | /ɪd/: *wanted*, *needed* |
| another voiceless sound (/p/, /k/, /f/, /θ/ …) | /s/: *cups*, *books*, *laughs* | /t/: *helped*, *looked*, *laughed* |
| another voiced sound (/b/, /ɡ/, /m/, /l/, a vowel …) | /z/: *jobs*, *bags*, *days*, *pens* | /d/: *robbed*, *played*, *called* |
:::

::: proof
The extra vowel /ɪ/ is needed when the ending would be hard to hear: after a hissing sound, a second /s/ or /z/ would melt into it (*bus* + /z/), and after /t/ or /d/ the /t/ or /d/ of the past would disappear. Elsewhere the ending simply agrees in voicing with the sound before it — voiceless after voiceless, voiced after voiced — which is easier to say than a mixture. The spelling does not show any of this: *-s* and *-ed* are written the same way for all three allomorphs. GB and GA follow the same rule; some speakers, especially in America, say /əz/ and /əd/ instead of /ɪz/ and /ɪd/. [[sounds-of-english/syllables-clusters]] practises the consonant clusters these endings make.
:::

::: warning Adding a syllable to *-ed*
Many learners say every *-ed* as a separate syllable: ✗ /ˈwɔːkɪd/ for *walked*, ✗ /ˈplʌɡɪd/ for *plugged*. ✓ *walked* /wɔːkt/ and *plugged* /plʌɡd/ have one syllable each. The extra syllable is only for verbs ending in /t/ or /d/: *wanted* /ˈwɒntɪd/ (GB), /ˈwɑːntɪd/ (GA), *needed* /ˈniːdɪd/. The same goes for *-s*: *likes* is /laɪks/, one syllable, not ✗ /ˈlaɪkɪz/.
:::

## Splitting a word into morphemes

To split a long word into its morphemes, work from the outside in, and check every piece.

::: theorem How to split a word {#rule-split}
1. **Take off any inflection** at the end (*-s*, *-ed*, *-ing*, *-er*, *-est*). It is always the last morpheme.
2. **Take off suffixes one at a time, from the end**, asking each time whether what is left is a word or a root you know: *carelessly* → *careless* + *-ly* → *care* + *-less* + *-ly*.
3. **Take off prefixes from the front**: *unhelpful* → *un-* + *helpful*.
4. **Undo spelling changes** before you check the base: *happiness* → *happi* + *-ness*, and *happi* is *happy* with *y* changed to *i*; *hoping* → *hop(e)* + *-ing*.
5. **Check the meaning.** Every piece must have a meaning that contributes to the meaning of the whole word. If a piece means nothing, or the meanings do not add up, it is not a morpheme here.
:::

Step 5 is the most important, and the one learners most often forget. *Carpet* contains the letters of *car* and *pet*, but neither has anything to do with the meaning of *carpet*: it is one morpheme. The same is true of *butterfly* in a different way: *butter* and *fly* are real morphemes and real words, but nobody can work out ‘a beautiful insect with large wings’ from them. Linguists still count *butterfly* as a compound of two morphemes, because it is built from two words; but for a learner it must be learned as a whole. The chapter [[word-formation/learning-with-word-formation]] returns to traps like this.

The figure shows the structure of a word with three morphemes. Each arrow means ‘is made of’.

::: widget graph
nodes: unhappiness@0,4; unhappy@-2,2; -ness@2,2; un-@-3,0; happy@-1,0
edges: unhappiness>unhappy; unhappiness>-ness; unhappy>un-; unhappy>happy
caption: The structure of *unhappiness*. *Un-* is first added to the adjective *happy*, giving the adjective *unhappy*; then *-ness* turns that adjective into a noun. The word is built in layers, not in one step. You can drag the words to rearrange the tree.
:::

Why that order, and not *un-* + *happiness*? Because *un-* is added mainly to adjectives (*unkind*, *unusual*, *unfair*) and to verbs (*untie*, *unlock*), and only to a few nouns. *Unhappy* is a word; *-ness* then makes the noun from it. The order of layers can change the meaning of a word, as the next example shows.

::: example One word, two structures {#ex-unlockable}
What does *unlockable* mean? Split it in two different ways and explain the two meanings.
::: solution
The morphemes are *un-* + *lock* + *-able*. They can be put together in two orders.

1. First *lock* + *-able* = *lockable* ‘can be locked’; then *un-* + *lockable* = ‘**not** able to be locked’. Here *un-* means ‘not’, as with adjectives: *This old door is unlockable — the lock is broken.*
2. First *un-* + *lock* = the verb *unlock* ‘open with a key’; then *unlock* + *-able* = ‘able to be **unlocked**’. Here *un-* means ‘reverse the action’, as with verbs: *The phone is unlockable with your fingerprint.*

So the same three morphemes give opposite meanings, depending on which layer comes first. In real texts the context decides, and a careful writer avoids the word when the context does not make the meaning clear.
:::
:::

::: example Counting morphemes {#ex-count}
Split each word into morphemes, name each one (root, prefix, derivational suffix or inflection), and give the total.

1. *disagreements*
2. *carelessly*
3. *rebuilt*
4. *nationalities*
::: solution
1. *dis-* (prefix, ‘not’) + *agree* (root) + *-ment* (derivational suffix, makes a noun) + *-s* (inflection, plural) = **4**.
2. *care* (root) + *-less* (derivational, ‘without’) + *-ly* (derivational, makes an adverb) = **3**.
3. *re-* (prefix, ‘again’) + *built* = **2** at the level of word parts. *Built* is the past form of *build*, but the past is shown by changing *d* to *t*, not by adding a separate piece; we count it as *build* + past, so a linguist would say *re-* + *build* + past tense = 3 morphemes. Both answers are acceptable if you explain them.
4. *nation* (root) + *-al* (derivational, makes an adjective) + *-ity* (derivational, makes a noun) + *-s* (inflection, plural; *y* becomes *ie* before *-s*) = **4**.

Answer 3 shows that a morpheme does not always have its own piece of spelling. The irregular past forms *took*, *ran* and *went* also contain two morphemes each — a verb and ‘past’ — without an ending you can cut off.
:::
:::

::: example Spelling changes inside a word {#ex-spelling-inside}
Split these words and undo the spelling changes: *happiness*, *making*, *stopped*, *possibility*.
::: solution
- *happiness* = *happy* + *-ness*: *y* changes to *i* before the suffix.
- *making* = *make* + *-ing*: the final *e* is dropped before a vowel.
- *stopped* = *stop* + *-ed*: the final consonant *p* is doubled after a short stressed vowel.
- *possibility* = *possible* + *-ity*: *-ble* becomes *-bil-* before *-ity* (also *able* → *ability*, *visible* → *visibility*).

These changes follow rules, which [[word-formation/spelling-changes]] explains. When you split a word, expect them, and do not take *happi* or *mak* for new roots.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese readers have a head start with morphemes, because Chinese writing shows most of them. In most Chinese words each character (字) is one syllable and one morpheme: 电脑 is 电 + 脑, 火车 is 火 + 车, 学生 is 学 + 生. There are exceptions — 葡萄, 蝴蝶, 咖啡 and 沙发 are two characters but only one morpheme, because neither character means anything by itself in these words — but the general match is strong. Chinese linguists call the morpheme 语素 (some older books say 词素).

In English the match does not exist. A morpheme may be part of a syllable (the *-s* of *cats*), one syllable (*un-*), or several (*elephant*), and nothing in the writing shows where one ends and the next begins. So the habit of looking for parts is useful, but the parts must be found by meaning, not by letters or syllables. The other big difference is inflection: Chinese has almost none (们 is limited to people and is often left out, and 了, 着 and 过 are separate particles, not parts of the verb), so the small English endings *-s* and *-ed* are easy to forget. They are morphemes like any other, and they carry meaning: *book* and *books* mean different things to the listener.
:::

::: warning Splitting by letters instead of by meaning
Do not split a word into pieces just because the pieces look like words or affixes.

- *carpet* is not *car* + *pet*; *season* is not *sea* + *son*; *discuss* has no *dis-* ‘not’.
- *deliver* has nothing to do with the *liver*, and *pineapple* is not a kind of apple.
- *island* is one morpheme: its *s* was added to the spelling in the sixteenth century because people connected the word with *isle*, a different word with which it shares no origin.

If the meanings of the pieces do not add up to the meaning of the word, the pieces are not morphemes in that word.
:::

::: history
The idea that words are built from meaningful parts is very old: the Sanskrit grammarian Pāṇini analysed words into roots and affixes more than two thousand years ago. The term **morpheme** was introduced in the late nineteenth century by the Polish linguist Jan Baudouin de Courtenay. It became central to the study of English when the American linguist Leonard Bloomfield, in his book *Language* (1933), defined the morpheme as a form that has no partial resemblance in sound and meaning to any other form — that is, a form that cannot be divided further into parts with meaning. Modern descriptions of English word structure, such as Bauer, Lieber and Plag's *Oxford Reference Guide to English Morphology* (2013), still start from this unit, while discussing hard cases such as *went* and *cranberry*, whose *cran-* appears in no other English word.
:::

::: summary
- A **morpheme** is the smallest part of a word with a meaning or a grammatical job: *un-* + *kind* + *-ness*.
- Morphemes are units of meaning, not of sound: *elephant* has three syllables and one morpheme; *cats* has one syllable and two.
- **Free** morphemes can be words by themselves (*kind*); **bound** morphemes cannot (*un-*, *-ness*, and bound roots such as *-ceive*).
- A word has a **root**, and may have **prefixes** before it and **suffixes** after it; an affix is added to a **base**, which may already contain affixes.
- **Inflections** (only eight in English) mark grammar, never change word class and come last; **derivational** affixes make new words.
- One morpheme can have several forms (**allomorphs**): plural /s/, /z/, /ɪz/; *in-*, *im-*, *il-*, *ir-*.
- Split a word from the outside in, undo spelling changes, and check that every piece adds meaning; *carpet* is not *car* + *pet*.
:::

- *mis-* (P) + *understood* (R + I: *understand* + past participle; *understood* has no separable ending). Many learners also see *under* + *stand* inside *understand*, but its meaning cannot be built from them, so for the learner it is one root.
- *employ* (R) + *-er* (D) + *-s* (I).
- *un-* (P) + *friend* (R) + *-ly* (D, makes the adjective *friendly*) + *-ness* (D; *y* → *i*). Four morphemes.
- *re-* (P) + *think* (R) + *-ing* (I).
:::
:::

::: exercise Real parts or false parts? {level=3}
For each word, say whether the pieces in brackets are its real morphemes. Explain your answer, and give the real analysis where there is one.

1. *rebuild* (*re-* + *build*)
2. *report* (*re-* + *port*)
3. *carpet* (*car* + *pet*)
4. *teacher* (*teach* + *-er*)
5. *corner* (*corn* + *-er*)
6. *uncover* (*un-* + *cover*)
::: hint
Ask two questions: does each piece have a meaning elsewhere? And do the meanings add up to the meaning of the whole word for a speaker of English today?
:::
::: solution
1. Yes: *re-* ‘again’ + *build*; *rebuild* means ‘build again’.
2. Historically yes — Latin *re-* ‘back’ + *portare* ‘carry’; a report ‘carries back’ news — and *port* appears in *transport*, *export*, *import*. But *re-* here does not mean ‘again’, and the meaning cannot be worked out from the parts. Linguists disagree about whether to count two morphemes in such words; for a learner, *report* is best learned as one word, with the root *port* noticed as a help for memory ([[word-formation/greek-latin-roots]]).
3. No: *carpet* is one morpheme. Neither *car* nor *pet* contributes to its meaning.
4. Yes: *teach* + *-er* ‘a person who teaches’.
5. No: a *corner* is not ‘a person or thing that corns’. *Corner* is one morpheme. The letters *-er* do not always make the suffix *-er*: compare *water*, *paper*, *never*.
6. Yes: *un-* ‘reverse the action’ + *cover*; *uncover* means ‘take the cover off’, and so ‘discover something hidden’.
:::
:::

::: exercise Explain a word to a classmate {level=3}
Choose two of these words: *unbreakable*, *disagreement*, *rewritable*, *carelessness*. For each one, write three or four sentences that explain to a classmate how the word is built and what it means. Use the terms *root*, *prefix*, *suffix* and *base*, show the layers in the right order, and end with an example sentence of your own.
::: solution
Model answers (yours may be worded differently):

- *unbreakable*: The root is the verb *break*. First the suffix *-able* is added, giving the adjective *breakable* ‘can be broken’. Then the prefix *un-* ‘not’ is added to this base, giving *unbreakable* ‘cannot be broken’. (*Un-* + *break* would not work, because *unbreak* is not a word.) Example: *These plates are unbreakable, so they are good for children.*
- *disagreement*: The root is the verb *agree*. The prefix *dis-* ‘not’ makes the verb *disagree*, and the suffix *-ment* turns that verb into a noun. Example: *There was a small disagreement about the price.*
- *rewritable*: The root is *write*. *Re-* ‘again’ gives *rewrite*, and *-able* gives *rewritable* ‘can be written again’; the *e* of *write* is dropped before *-able*. Example: *This disc is rewritable.*
- *carelessness*: The root is *care*. *-less* ‘without’ makes the adjective *careless*, and *-ness* makes the noun. Example: *The accident was caused by carelessness.*

Check your own answer: the layers must each be real words (*breakable*, *disagree*, *rewrite*, *careless*), and each suffix must make the word class that the next layer needs.
