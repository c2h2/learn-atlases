Zhou Hao is reading a news article and meets three words he has never seen: *unaffordable*, *carpet* and *deregulation*. He tries the method of this course on all three. *Un-afford-able* works at once: ‘not able to be afforded’, too expensive, and the sentence (*Rents in the city centre have become unaffordable*) confirms it. *De-regul-ation* takes longer, but *regular* and *regulate* help: ‘the removal of rules’, which fits an article about banks. *Carpet* is a disaster. *Car* + *pet* gives ‘a pet for the car’, which makes no sense in *We bought a new carpet for the living room*. He looks it up: *carpet* has no parts at all in English.

This is the whole story of this chapter. Word parts are one of the most useful tools a learner has: they turn thousands of words into a few hundred parts and patterns. But they must be used with two checks — the context and, for important words, the dictionary — because some words only look as if they have parts. This chapter brings the course together into a method: how to guess from parts, how to check the guess, how to recognise the traps, and how to use word families to learn words in groups rather than one at a time.

## Why word parts help

There are three reasons, and they are different.

1. **Guessing.** When you meet a new word, its parts often tell you its general meaning: *re-* + *write* = ‘write again’; *-less* = ‘without’; *bio-* = ‘life’. You can keep reading without stopping.
2. **Remembering.** A new word connected to words you know is easier to remember than an isolated one. *Contradict* is easier if you know *predict* and *dictionary*; *transparent* is easier if you know *apparent*.
3. **Producing.** If you know *-ness* makes nouns from adjectives and *-ise* (*-ize*) makes verbs, you can make the forms you need when you write — and check them (*kindness*, *modernise*), rather than leaving out a word because you only know its adjective.

Nation (2013) lists learning word parts as one of the main strategies for vocabulary growth, alongside guessing from context, using dictionaries and learning from word cards, because a small number of frequent affixes appears in a very large number of words. The prefixes and suffixes of [[word-formation/prefixes]] and [[word-formation/suffixes]] are exactly those frequent affixes.

## The word-part method

::: theorem The word-part method {#rule-method}
When you meet an unknown word in a text:

1. **Split** the word into parts you recognise ([[word-formation/morphemes#rule-split]]), undoing any spelling changes at the joins (*happi-ness* → *happy* + *-ness*; [[word-formation/spelling-changes]]).
2. **Find the word class** from the last suffix ([[word-formation/suffixes#rule-last-suffix]]) and from the position in the sentence ([[word-formation/conversion#rule-clues]]).
3. **Combine the meanings** of the parts into a first guess.
4. **Check the guess against the context.** Does it fit the sentence and the paragraph? If not, try another split, or treat the word as having no useful parts.
5. **Check important words in a dictionary**, especially words you want to use yourself; note the exact meaning, the pronunciation and a typical example.
:::

::: proof
Step 4 is the step learners most often skip, and it is the one that makes the method safe. Studies of learners' guessing, summarised by Nation (2013), show that learners who analyse a word into parts and do not check the result against the context make confident mistakes. Steps 1–3 give a hypothesis; step 4 tests it; step 5 confirms it and adds what parts can never give — the exact modern meaning, the collocations and the register. The method is therefore not ‘work out the meaning from the parts’ but ‘make a guess from the parts and test it’.
:::

::: example Using the method {#ex-method}
Use the word-part method on the word in bold.

> *Many students find the new rules **counterproductive**: they were meant to save time, but in fact they make everything slower.*
::: solution
1. Split: *counter-* + *product* + *-ive*. *Product* is related to *produce* (*pro-* ‘forward’ + *duc* ‘lead’; [[word-formation/greek-latin-roots]]).
2. Word class: *-ive* makes adjectives; it comes after *find the new rules* (*find something + adjective*), so it is an adjective.
3. Combine: *counter-* ‘against’ + *productive* ‘producing good results’ → ‘working against good results’.
4. Context: the rules were meant to save time but make things slower — they have the opposite effect to the one intended. The guess fits.
5. Dictionary: *counterproductive* ‘having the opposite effect to the one intended’. The guess was right, and the dictionary makes it precise.
:::
:::

## The traps: false analysis

::: definition False analysis {#def-false-analysis}
**False analysis** is splitting a word into parts that look like morphemes but are not. The letters happen to match a prefix, a suffix or a word, but the word was never made from them, and the "meaning of the parts" has nothing to do with the meaning of the word.
:::

Some common words that invite false analysis:

| word | false analysis | what it really is |
|---|---|---|
| *carpet* | *car* + *pet* | one morpheme; borrowed from Old French or Italian, ultimately from Latin |
| *butterfly* | *butter* + *fly* | a real compound in Old English, but nobody knows for sure why the insect was named after butter; the parts do not explain the meaning |
| *discuss* | *dis-* + *cuss* | from Latin *discutere* ‘shake apart, examine’; there is no English base *cuss* in it |
| *deliver* | *de-* + *liver* | from French, ultimately Latin *de-* + *liberare* ‘set free’ (as in *liberate*); nothing to do with the organ |
| *island* | *is* + *land* | Old English *īegland*; the *s* was added in the sixteenth century under the influence of the unrelated word *isle* |
| *female* | *fe-* + *male* | from Latin *femella* ‘young woman’ through French; it was reshaped to look like *male*, but the two words are not related |
| *pineapple* | *pine* + *apple* | a real compound, but the meaning has moved: it first meant a pine cone, and was later used for the tropical fruit |
| *understand* | *under* + *stand* | a real compound from Old English, but its meaning cannot be worked out from ‘stand under’ |

The last three rows show two different kinds of trap. In *carpet*, *discuss*, *deliver*, *island* and *female*, the parts are not there at all. In *butterfly*, *pineapple* and *understand*, the parts are real but the meaning is not compositional — it cannot be worked out by combining them. The context check catches both.

::: warning Too many "roots"
Some popular memory books split almost every English word into "roots", including words that have none: *pear* as *p* + *ear*, *ambition* as *am* + *bit* + *ion*. As memory tricks they may work for a while, but they teach the wrong habit: to see parts where there are none, and to trust the parts more than the context. Real word parts can be checked: the dictionary's etymology, or a group of other words that really share the part (*inspect*, *spectator*, *respect* all share *spect*; *pear* and *ear* share nothing). If you cannot find at least two other words with the same part and a related meaning, treat the "part" with suspicion.
:::

::: quiz
Which word **can** be usefully split into parts that explain its meaning?
- [ ] *carpet*
- [ ] *discuss*
- [x] *disagree*
- [ ] *deliver*
::: solution
*Disagree* is *dis-* ‘not, the opposite of’ + *agree*: ‘not agree’ — fully compositional. *Carpet*, *discuss* and *deliver* only look as if they contain parts (*car*, *dis-*, *de-*); none of them was formed from those parts in English.
:::
:::

::: example True or false parts? {#ex-false}
Decide whether each word can be usefully split, and explain.

1. *rewrite*
2. *report*
3. *restaurant*
4. *reread*
5. *recommend*
::: solution
1. Yes: *re-* ‘again’ + *write*. Fully compositional.
2. Partly: *re-* ‘back’ + *port* ‘carry’ — ‘carry back’ news ([[word-formation/greek-latin-roots]]). Useful as a memory aid, but *re-* here does not mean ‘again’, and there is no English verb *port* in this sense.
3. No: *restaurant* comes from French (‘a place that restores you’); splitting off *re-* and *-ant* gives nothing useful. Learn it as a whole.
4. Yes: *re-* ‘again’ + *read*: ‘read again’. Note the pronunciation: /ˌriːˈriːd/.
5. Historically *re-* + *commend* ‘praise, entrust’, but for a learner the parts do not lead to the meaning ‘suggest that something is good’. Learn it as a whole; note the spelling (one *c*, two *m*'s).

The letters *re* at the start of a word are a prefix only when a real base follows (*write*, *read*, *build*, *open*) and the meaning is ‘again’ or ‘back’.
:::
:::

## When speakers re-analyse words

False analysis is not only a learner's mistake. Native speakers do it too, and when many people do it, it can change the language.

::: definition Back-formation {#def-back-formation}
**Back-formation** is making a new, shorter word by removing what looks like an affix from an existing longer word. The longer word came **first**: *editor* → *edit*, *burglar* → *burgle*, *babysitter* → *babysit*, *donation* → *donate*, *television* → *televise*. Speakers treated *-or*, *-ar*, *-er* and *-ion* as suffixes and "recovered" a verb that had never existed.
:::

In a back-formation the analysis was false when it was first made — *burglar* was never *burgle* + *-ar* — but once the new word is accepted, the family is regular: *burgle*/*burglar* looks exactly like *teach*/*teacher*. For a learner, this is good news: the pairs behave like normal families, whatever their history. Only very recent or jokey back-formations may still sound informal (*enthuse* from *enthusiasm* is criticised by some writers).

::: definition Folk etymology {#def-folk-etymology}
**Folk etymology** is the reshaping of an unfamiliar word or part of a word so that it looks like familiar words. Speakers who did not understand a part replaced it with something that seemed to make sense:

- *cockroach* from Spanish *cucaracha*, reshaped as if it contained *cock* and *roach*;
- *bridegroom* from Old English *brȳdguma* (*guma* ‘man’), reshaped with *groom* after *guma* fell out of use;
- *female*, reshaped to look like *male* (see the table above);
- *hamburger*, originally ‘from Hamburg’ (*Hamburg* + *-er*), re-analysed as *ham* + *burger*, which produced *burger*, *cheeseburger* and *veggie burger* — although there is no ham in a hamburger.
:::

Folk etymology also gives rise to false stories about where words come from. A famous one says that *sincere* comes from Latin *sine cera* ‘without wax’, because honest sculptors did not hide cracks with wax. The story is attractive and untrue: the word comes from Latin *sincerus* ‘clean, pure’. Like the false roots above, such stories can be checked in a good etymological dictionary, and should be.

::: example Which came first? {#ex-back-formation}
In each pair, which word is older? Use a dictionary with etymologies if you are not sure, and say whether the younger word is a back-formation.

1. *teach* — *teacher*
2. *edit* — *editor*
3. *donate* — *donation*
4. *write* — *writer*
5. *sightseeing* — *sightsee*
::: solution
1. *teach* is older; *teacher* is a normal derivation with *-er*.
2. *editor* is older; *edit* is a back-formation.
3. *donation* is older; *donate* is a back-formation.
4. *write* is older; *writer* is a normal derivation.
5. *sightseeing* is older; *sightsee* is a back-formation.

Notice that the pairs look the same today: from the modern language alone you cannot tell which word came first. That is why the dates must come from a dictionary, not from the parts — and why, for using the words, it does not matter.

## Learning with word families

Knowing how words are built changes how you can learn them: not one at a time, but in families. The idea of the word family and the table of family members are introduced in [[core-vocabulary/word-families]]; here we look at how to use word formation to grow those families.

::: theorem Growing a word family {#rule-grow}
When you learn a new word, spend one minute growing its family:

1. **Inflections**: the forms that are always regular for you to make (*decide*, *decides*, *decided*, *deciding*).
2. **Frequent, regular derivatives**: the ones made with the common affixes of this course (*decision*, *decisive*, *indecisive*, *decisively*, *undecided*).
3. **Pronunciation changes** in the family, especially stress and vowel changes (*de**CIDE*** /dɪˈsaɪd/, *de**CI**sion* /dɪˈsɪʒn/; [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics#rule-suffix]]).
4. **Gaps and traps**: forms that do not exist or do not mean what the parts suggest (✗ *decidement*; *decided* as an adjective, ‘clear, definite’: *a decided advantage*).

Then check the family in a learner's dictionary, which lists derived forms at the end of an entry or close to it.
:::

Bauer and Nation (1993) arranged English affixes in **levels** for exactly this purpose — to decide which family members a learner can be expected to recognise. At the lowest levels a family contains only the inflected forms (*decide*, *decides*, *decided*); the next levels add the most frequent and regular affixes, such as *-er*, *-ness*, *-less*, *-ly*, *un-*, *-ment* and *-ful*; the highest levels add affixes that are less frequent or change the base in less regular ways, and finally the classical roots of [[word-formation/greek-latin-roots]]. The levels are a guide to order: learn the regular, frequent patterns first, because they pay back soonest.

::: example A family notebook entry {#ex-family}
Zhou Hao has just learned *afford*. Grow its family, with notes.
::: solution
| form | class | parts | notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| *afford*, *affords*, *afforded*, *affording* | verb | — | usually with *can*/*could*/*be able to*: *We can't afford a car.* |
| *affordable* | adjective | *afford* + *-able* | ‘not too expensive’: *affordable housing* (a frequent collocation) |
| *unaffordable* | adjective | *un-* + *affordable* | the word from his article |
| *affordability* | noun | *affordable* + *-ity* | formal, used in reports and news; *-able* → *-abil-ity* ([[word-formation/suffixes#rule-ness-ity]]) |

Traps: no ✗ *affordment*; the noun meaning ‘the ability to pay’ is *affordability*, not ✗ *affordance* (*affordance* exists, but as a technical term in design and psychology with a different meaning).

Pronunciation: the stress stays on *-ford-* in every form: *af**FORD***, *af**FORD**able*, *unaf**FORD**able*, *afforda**BIL**ity*, where *-ity* moves the main stress.

One minute of work, and one new word has become five forms Zhou Hao can recognise and four he can use.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese learners already learn vocabulary in families — but Chinese families are built on shared characters. Knowing 学, a learner can recognise 学生, 学校, 大学, 学习, 数学, and Chinese teaching makes much use of this. English families work in a similar way, but with two differences that matter.

First, English parts are not visually separate. A character is always a unit; an English morpheme has to be found inside a string of letters, and the boundary may be hidden by spelling changes (*happi-ness*) or by assimilation (*im-possible*, *col-lect*). That is why the splitting skill and the spelling rules of this course are needed before the family method works well.

Second, an English family usually stays in one area of meaning but changes **word class** (*decide*, *decision*, *decisive*), and the learner must choose the right class in each sentence. Chinese compounds made with 学 belong to different areas of meaning (student, school, university, mathematics) and the word class is not marked on the word. So the typical Chinese learner's error in English is not misunderstanding the family, but choosing the wrong member: ✗ *It is important to success*, ✗ *I am very interesting in music*. Growing families with their word classes, as in [[#rule-grow]], is aimed directly at that error.
:::

::: history
Teaching English vocabulary through word parts is not new: the American psychologist Edward Thorndike published *The Teaching of English Suffixes* in 1941, with counts of how often suffixes occur. Interest in it as a strategy for second-language learners grew from the 1980s and 1990s, when researchers began to ask how many words learners need and how word families should be counted. Bauer and Nation's levels of affixes (1993) gave a principled answer to the counting question, and became the basis of many later word-family lists used in research and teaching. Research since then has continued to show that knowledge of affixes grows with general vocabulary size, and that it can be taught.
:::

## Where this leads

The method of this chapter is one of several ways of working out new words; guessing from context in general is the subject of [[reading-skills/words-from-context]], and using a dictionary to check guesses is taught in [[core-vocabulary/using-a-dictionary]]. How to review new words so that they stay in memory is covered in [[core-vocabulary/learning-and-remembering]]. For the academic words where word parts help most, see [[collocation-and-register/academic-vocabulary]].

::: summary
- Word parts help in three ways: **guessing** meanings, **remembering** new words, and **producing** the forms you need.
- The **word-part method**: split the word, find the word class, combine the meanings, **check the guess against the context**, and check important words in a dictionary.
- **False analysis** splits a word into parts it does not have (*carpet*, *discuss*, *deliver*, *island*, *female*); some real compounds are not compositional (*butterfly*, *understand*).
- Test a "part" by finding other words that really share it; distrust memory tricks that invent roots.
- **Back-formation** removes an apparent affix to make a new word (*editor* → *edit*, *burglar* → *burgle*); **folk etymology** reshapes unfamiliar words to look familiar (*cockroach*, *hamburger* → *burger*). Stories like *sincere* = *sine cera* are false.
- **Grow word families**: inflections, frequent derivatives, pronunciation changes, gaps and traps — in the order suggested by Bauer and Nation's levels.
- For Chinese learners, the hard part is less understanding the family than **choosing the right member** for the sentence.
:::

::: exercise Check a story {level=3}
A classmate tells you two stories about English words. Explain which is true, which is false, and how you could check.

1. "*Sincere* comes from Latin *sine cera* ‘without wax’, because Roman sculptors who did not hide cracks with wax were honest."
2. "*Hamburger* has nothing to do with ham; it comes from the city of Hamburg, and *burger* was made from it later."
::: solution
1. False. *Sincere* comes from Latin *sincerus* ‘clean, pure, whole’; the *sine cera* story is a well-known invention. Check: a dictionary with etymologies (such as the OED or an online etymological dictionary) gives the real source and often mentions the false story.
2. True. *Hamburger* is *Hamburg* + *-er* ‘from Hamburg’. Speakers re-analysed it as *ham* + *burger*, and *burger* then became a word and an element in *cheeseburger* and *veggie burger*. Check: the same dictionaries give the origin and the dates of *burger*.

The general lesson: a good story is not evidence. Check etymologies, as you check guesses from parts.

::: exercise Grow a family {level=3}
Choose a verb you learned recently (or use *produce*, *create* or *employ*). Grow its family following [[#rule-grow]]: inflections, at least four derived forms with their word classes, the stress of each form, one example sentence for two of the forms, and at least one gap or trap. Then check every form in a learner's dictionary.
::: solution
A model for *employ*:

- inflections: *employ*, *employs*, *employed*, *employing*
- derived forms:
  - *employer* (noun, the person or company that employs): em**PLOY**er
  - *employee* (noun, the person employed): em**PLOY**ee or employ**EE** (both are heard)
  - *employment* (noun): em**PLOY**ment
  - *unemployed* (adjective): unem**PLOYED**
  - *unemployment* (noun): unem**PLOY**ment
  - *employable* (adjective): em**PLOY**able
- sentences: *The factory employs 300 people. — Unemployment has fallen this year.*
- traps: *-er* is the one who gives work and *-ee* the one who receives it (*employer*/*employee*, like *interviewer*/*interviewee*); there is no ✗ *employation*.

Your family should contain only forms you have checked, with the stress marked from the dictionary, not from guessing.
