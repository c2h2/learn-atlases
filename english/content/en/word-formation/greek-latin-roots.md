On her first day at an international school, Li Xiaoyu has a timetable with *biology*, *geography* and *physics*, a reading list that includes an *autobiography*, and a science lab with a *microscope*, a *thermometer* and a *telescope* on the roof. Nearly all of these words are new to her in English, but they are not as many new things to learn as they seem. *Biology* and *biography* share *bio-* ‘life’; *biography*, *geography* and *autobiography* share *-graph-* ‘write’; *microscope* and *telescope* share *-scope* ‘look at’; *thermometer* is ‘heat’ + ‘measure’. A dozen word parts from Greek explain all of them.

The academic and scientific vocabulary of English is built largely from Greek and Latin parts. These parts usually cannot stand alone as English words — there is no English word *bio* or *spect* — but they appear in hundreds of words, with stable meanings. A learner who knows the commonest ones can often work out the general meaning of a new academic word, and remembers new words better because they are connected to words already known. This chapter presents the most useful Greek and Latin roots, shows how they combine with each other and with prefixes and suffixes, and shows where they mislead.

How these words came into English — mostly in the Renaissance and in the scientific centuries that followed — is told in [[history-of-english/early-modern-english]]; here we look only at how they are built.

## Combining forms and bound roots

::: definition Combining form {#def-combining-form}
A **combining form** (构词成分) is a bound element, usually from Greek or Latin, that combines with another such element (or with a word) to make a new word: *bio-* + *-logy* → *biology*, *tele-* + *-phone* → *telephone*, *micro-* + *-scope* → *microscope*. Words made in this way are called **neoclassical compounds**: compounds, because they join two or more elements with full meanings, and *neoclassical*, because their parts come from the classical languages but most of the words were made in modern times.
:::

Combining forms are like roots in having full meanings (‘life’, ‘far’, ‘small’), but like affixes in being bound. Many can stand at the front or the back of a word: *graph* is first in *graphic* and last in *photograph*; *phone* is last in *telephone* and first in *phonetics*. Between two Greek elements English usually puts the vowel *o* (*therm-o-meter*, *bi-o-logy*, *dem-o-cracy*); between Latin ones it often puts *i* (*agr-i-culture*).

Latin has given English many **bound roots** that are not combining forms but the root of a verb, usually with a Latin prefix in front of them: *in-spect*, *trans-port*, *pre-dict*, *intro-duce*. You met one in [[word-formation/morphemes#def-free-bound]]: *-ceive* in *receive*, *perceive*, *deceive*.

Some of these combine with English words or Latin roots as well as with each other: *microwave*, *headphones*, *television* (Greek *tele-* + Latin-based *vision*). Purists once disliked such mixed words; today nobody notices.

::: theorem Stress in Greek-type words {#rule-greek-stress}
1. Words ending in ***-logy***, ***-graphy***, ***-logist*** and ***-grapher*** are stressed on the syllable just before the ending: *bi**OL**ogy*, *ge**OG**raphy*, *psych**OL**ogist*, *pho**TOG**rapher*.
2. Words ending in ***-meter*** for an instrument are usually stressed on the syllable before *-meter*: *ther**MOM**eter*, *ba**ROM**eter*, *spee**DOM**eter*. (Units are different: ***KIL**ometre* in British English and often *ki**LOM**eter* in American English.)
3. Words ending in ***-ic*** follow the *-ic* rule: *bio**LOG**ical*, *photo**GRAPH**ic*, *demo**CRAT**ic* ([[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics#rule-suffix]]).
:::

So the stress, and with it the vowels, changes across a family: **PHO**tograph, pho**TOG**raphy, photo**GRAPH**ic. A learner who knows the rule can say the word correctly the first time ([[word-formation/suffixes#ex-stress]]).

::: quiz
What does *chronological* order mean?
- [ ] order of size
- [x] order of time, from earliest to latest
- [ ] order of importance
- [ ] alphabetical order
::: solution
*Chron-* means ‘time’ and *-logical* ‘relating to the study or logic of’: *chronological order* is the order in which events happened. Compare *chronic* (lasting a long time: *a chronic illness*) and *synchronise* (make happen at the same time).

::: example Working out a scientific word {#ex-greek}
Work out the meaning of each word from its parts, then check it against the sentence.

1. *Most plastic bags are not **biodegradable**.*
2. *She was **dehydrated** after the long run.*
3. *His **autobiography** was published last year.*
4. *The doctor used a **stethoscope**.*
::: solution
1. *bio-* ‘life’ + *de-* ‘down, apart’ + *grade* ‘step’ + *-able* ‘can be’: ‘able to be broken down by living things’ (bacteria). The sentence fits: plastic bags do not rot away.
2. *de-* ‘remove’ + *hydr-* ‘water’ + *-ate* (verb) + *-ed*: ‘having lost too much water from the body’. Fits: after a long run without drinking.
3. *auto-* ‘self’ + *bio-* ‘life’ + *-graphy* ‘writing’: ‘the story of a person's life, written by that person’. Compare *biography*, written by someone else.
4. *stetho-* + *-scope* ‘instrument for looking at’. *Stetho-* is not in the table; it comes from the Greek for ‘chest’. The parts give ‘an instrument for examining the chest’, which is right — but a doctor *listens* through it rather than looks. The roots give the general idea; the context and the dictionary give the exact meaning.
:::
:::

| *struct* | build | *construct*, *structure*, *instruction*, *destroy* (*struct* changed) |
| *ject* | throw | *inject*, *project*, *reject*, *object* |
| *rupt* | break | *interrupt*, *erupt*, *bankrupt*, *disrupt* |
| *tract* | pull, draw | *attract*, *extract*, *tractor*, *distract* |
| *ven*, *vent* | come | *invent*, *event*, *convenient*, *prevent* |

A root often appears with several prefixes, and the prefix gives the direction or the kind of action. Look at *port* ‘carry’:

- *ex-* ‘out’ → *export* ‘carry goods out of a country’
- *im-* (= *in-*) ‘in’ → *import* ‘carry goods into a country’
- *trans-* ‘across’ → *transport* ‘carry from one place to another’
- *re-* ‘back’ → *report* ‘carry news back’
- *sup-* (= *sub-*) ‘under’ → *support* ‘carry from below’

The Latin prefixes change their last consonant before some roots, as *in-* does in *impossible* ([[word-formation/prefixes#rule-negative]]): *con-* ‘together’ appears as *com-* (*compose*), *col-* (*collect*) and *cor-* (*correct*); *ad-* ‘to’ as *ac-* (*accept*), *af-* (*affect*), *ap-* (*approve*), *at-* (*attract*); *sub-* ‘under’ as *sup-* (*support*), *suf-* (*suffer*). Recognising these spellings helps you find the root.

::: example Undoing the prefix change {#ex-assimilation}
Each word begins with a Latin prefix whose last letter has changed. Give the original prefix, find where the root begins, and say why the spelling has a double consonant.

1. *collect*
2. *accept*
3. *support*
4. *correct*
5. *affect*
::: solution
1. *col-* = *con-* ‘together’ + *lect* (Latin *legere* ‘gather’): ‘gather together’. The *n* became *l* before *l*, giving *ll*.
2. *ac-* = *ad-* ‘to’ + *cept* (Latin *capere* ‘take’, the root of *receive* and *reception*): ‘take to yourself’. The *d* became *c* before *c*.
3. *sup-* = *sub-* ‘under’ + *port* ‘carry’: ‘carry from below’. The *b* became *p* before *p*.
4. *cor-* = *con-* + *rect* (Latin *regere* ‘guide, rule’, as in *direct*): ‘make straight’. The *n* became *r* before *r*.
5. *af-* = *ad-* + *fect* (Latin *facere* ‘do, make’, as in *effect* and *factory*): ‘act on, do something to’. The *d* became *f* before *f*.

The double consonant is a useful signal: when an academic word begins with *acc-*, *aff-*, *app-*, *att-*, *coll-*, *comm-*, *corr-*, *supp-* or *suff-*, there is very often a Latin prefix + root behind it. The same change explains the double letters in *illegal* and *irregular* ([[word-formation/prefixes#rule-negative]]) — and the spelling mistakes that come from forgetting them (✗ *acomodation*, ✓ *accommodation*).
:::

::: example One root, many words {#ex-latin}
Use the root *dict* ‘say’ and the prefixes and suffixes you know to explain each word in bold.

1. *Can you **predict** the result?*
2. *The two reports **contradict** each other.*
3. *Look it up in a **dictionary**.*
4. *The teacher **dictated** a short text to the class.*
::: solution
1. *pre-* ‘before’ + *dict* ‘say’: ‘say before it happens’ — 预测.
2. *contra-* ‘against’ + *dict*: ‘say the opposite of’ — 相互矛盾.
3. *dict* + *-ion* (noun) + *-ary* (a place or collection): ‘a collection of words (sayings)’ — 词典.
4. *dict* + *-ate* (verb): ‘say words aloud for someone to write down’ — 口授, 听写. The same root gives *dictator*, a ruler whose word is law.

The four meanings are different, but each keeps the idea of ‘saying’, which makes the words easier to remember together.
:::

## Using roots to guess meanings

::: theorem Guessing from roots {#rule-guess}
To work out the meaning of an unknown word with Greek or Latin parts:

1. **Split it**: find any prefix, the root or combining forms, and any suffix (*in-spect-ion*).
2. **Give each part its basic meaning** and put them together in the order *suffix* (word class), *prefix* + *root* (meaning): *inspection* = a noun, ‘looking into’.
3. **Test the guess in the context.** If it does not fit, the word may have moved away from its parts, or you may have split it wrongly.
4. **Check important words in a dictionary.** Roots give the general area of meaning; the dictionary gives the exact, modern one.
:::

::: proof
Roots work because many academic words were made from these parts, often in modern times and on purpose, by scientists and scholars who knew Latin and Greek and wanted transparent names (*thermometer*, *photosynthesis*, *biodegradable*). For such words the parts add up well. But most Latin-root words were borrowed already made, and their meanings had been changing for centuries in Latin, French and English before a learner meets them. *Prevent* is *pre-* ‘before’ + *vent* ‘come’ — once ‘come before, act first’, now ‘stop something from happening’. *Suspect* is *sub-* ‘under’ + *spect* ‘look’ — ‘look at from below, look at secretly’. The historical meaning is a useful memory aid, but it is not the definition. Research on learners' guessing supports this balance: Nation (2013) recommends learning the most frequent affixes and roots as a strategy, while warning that guesses from word parts must always be checked against the context.
:::

::: warning Roots that mislead
- The root is not always where it seems: *pedestrian* contains Latin *ped-* ‘foot’, but *pediatrician* (BrE *paediatrician*) contains Greek *paed-* ‘child’. Both have *ped*.
- The historical meaning may be far from the modern one: *prevent* (‘come before’) now means ‘stop’; *candidate* comes from Latin *candidus* ‘white’ (Roman candidates wore white togas), which tells you nothing about elections today.
- Some words only look as if they contain a root. *Audit* really does contain *aud* ‘hear’ (accounts were once read aloud for checking), but *portion* has nothing to do with *port* ‘carry’: it comes from the Latin for ‘share’.
- *Phil-* ‘love’ and *-phobia* ‘fear’ are easily confused because they look similar at a glance: *philosophy* ‘love of wisdom’, *claustrophobia* ‘fear of closed spaces’.

When the meaning of the parts and the context disagree, trust the context, and check.
:::

::: quiz
A friend says that *transparent* must mean ‘across the parents’. What is the best answer?
- [ ] That is right: *trans-* means ‘across’.
- [x] *Trans-* is right, but *parent* is not the English word *parent*: the root means ‘appear, be seen’, so *transparent* is ‘seen through’.
- [ ] *Transparent* has no parts; it must be learned as a whole.
- [ ] The prefix is *tran-*, not *trans-*.
::: solution
*Transparent* is *trans-* ‘through’ + a Latin root *par-* ‘appear’ (as in *appear* and *apparent*) + *-ent* (adjective): ‘that can be seen through’. The letters *parent* are a coincidence. This is a reminder to check each part against words that really share it — *apparent*, *appear* — and against the context: *transparent glass*, *a transparent process*.
:::
:::

::: example Reading a science paragraph {#ex-paragraph}
Use roots, prefixes and suffixes to explain the words in bold. Then say which one the roots explain least well.

> *In **photosynthesis**, plants use the energy of sunlight to make sugar. Scientists **measure** the process with special **instruments**, and a **microscope** shows the structures inside the leaf. The results are **visible** within hours, and they **contradict** an older **hypothesis**.*
::: solution
- *photosynthesis*: *photo-* ‘light’ + *synthesis* ‘putting together’ (Greek *syn-* ‘together’ + *thesis* ‘placing’): ‘putting things together using light’ — making sugar with the energy of light.
- *measure*: not a Greek or Latin compound for an English learner; a basic word to learn as a whole (it is related to *-meter*, but distantly).
- *instruments*: *in-* + *struct* ‘build’ + *-ment* + *-s* — historically ‘things built in, equipment’; the modern meaning ‘tools for measuring or for making music’ is far from the parts. This is the word the roots explain least well.
- *microscope*: *micro-* ‘small’ + *-scope* ‘instrument for looking’.
- *visible*: *vis* ‘see’ + *-ible* ‘can be’: ‘can be seen’.
- *contradict*: *contra-* ‘against’ + *dict* ‘say’: ‘say the opposite’.
- *hypothesis*: Greek *hypo-* ‘under’ + *thesis* ‘placing’: ‘what is placed under’ — an idea put forward as a basis for testing. The parts help, but the scientific meaning must be learned.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese scientific and academic terms are built very much like English neoclassical compounds: from meaningful parts, each written with a character. Many of them can be matched part for part with the Greek or Latin elements. *Telescope* is 望远镜 — 望 ‘look’, 远 ‘far’, 镜 ‘lens’ — almost exactly *tele-* ‘far’ + *-scope* ‘instrument for looking’. *Microscope* is 显微镜, ‘instrument that shows the small’; *thermometer* is 温度计, ‘temperature measurer’; *biology* is 生物学, ‘study of living things’, with 学 doing the job of *-logy*; *geography* is 地理. Many such Chinese terms were created in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries to translate European science, some of them first in Japan.

This gives Chinese learners a real advantage: the habit of reading a technical term part by part, which Chinese teaches, is exactly what English academic words need. Chinese vocabulary books often teach English **词根** (roots) and **词缀** (affixes) for this reason. Two cautions. First, the English parts are not visible as characters, and their spelling changes (*con-*, *com-*, *col-*); you need to know them well enough to recognise them. Second, some popular memory methods invent "roots" that are not real (splitting *pear* into *p* + *ear*); they may help memory for a while, but they teach false analysis. Use real roots, which you can check in a dictionary's etymology.
:::

::: history
English borrowed Latin and Greek words in great numbers during the Renaissance, and the borrowing was controversial. Critics called the new learned words **inkhorn terms** — words that smelled of the scholar's inkpot — and Thomas Wilson's *Arte of Rhetorique* (1553) mocked writers who filled their English with them. Many of the words survived anyway (*education*, *conspicuous*, *exaggerate*), while others disappeared. From the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries onwards, scientists used Greek and Latin parts on purpose to name new things and fields, and new words of this kind are still made every year. Some were made with great care: in 1834 the scholar William Whewell suggested the word *scientist*, on the model of *artist*, for people who study the natural world.
:::

::: summary
- Much academic and scientific English is built from Greek and Latin parts that are **bound**: they are not English words by themselves.
- **Greek combining forms** join each other in **neoclassical compounds**: *bio-* ‘life’, *geo-* ‘earth’, *tele-* ‘far’, *micro-* ‘small’, *auto-* ‘self’, *-graph* ‘write’, *-logy* ‘study of’, *-scope* ‘look at’, *-meter* ‘measure’.
- **Latin roots** usually take a prefix: *port* ‘carry’, *spect* ‘look’, *dict* ‘say’, *duct* ‘lead’, *script* ‘write’, *miss* ‘send’, *vis* ‘see’, *struct* ‘build’, *ject* ‘throw’, *tract* ‘pull’. Prefixes change their last letter before some roots: *com-*, *col-*, *cor-*; *ac-*, *af-*, *ap-*.
- *-logy*, *-graphy* and *-meter* put the stress on the syllable before them: *bi**OL**ogy*, *ther**MOM**eter*.
- To guess: split the word, give each part its basic meaning, test the guess in context, check important words in a dictionary.
- Root meanings are historical; modern meanings may have moved (*prevent*, *candidate*), and some letters only look like roots (*transparent* has no *parent*).
- Chinese technical terms (望远镜, 生物学) are built part by part in the same way — a real help, as long as the roots you use are real.
:::

::: exercise Explain an academic paragraph {level=3}
Read the paragraph. Choose six words built from Greek or Latin parts, split each into its parts, give the meaning of the parts, and say whether the parts lead to the modern meaning.

> *The researchers interviewed two hundred students and transcribed the recordings. Their analysis suggests that students who receive regular feedback produce more coherent arguments. However, the authors admit that the sample was small, and they recommend a larger, international study.*
::: solution
A model answer (other choices are possible):

- *interviewed*: *inter-* ‘between’ + *view* (from Latin *videre* ‘see’, through French) + *-ed* — ‘seeing each other’; the modern meaning ‘asking someone questions’ is related but has moved.
- *transcribed*: *trans-* ‘across’ + *scrib* ‘write’ + *-ed* — ‘written across’ from one form to another: speech written down as text. The parts lead well to the meaning.
- *receive*: *re-* ‘back’ + *-ceive* ‘take’ — ‘take back’; today simply ‘get’. Partly helpful.
- *produce*: *pro-* ‘forward’ + *duc* ‘lead’ — ‘lead forward, bring out’; the modern meaning ‘make’ is close.
- *admit*: *ad-* ‘to’ + *mit* ‘send’ — ‘let in’; *admit* that something is true is ‘let it in’ to the argument. Helpful as a memory aid.
- *international*: *inter-* ‘between’ + *nation* + *-al* — ‘between nations’. Transparent.

Your choice may include *analysis* (Greek *ana-* ‘up, throughout’ + *lysis* ‘loosening’ — ‘breaking up into parts’) or *coherent* (*co-* ‘together’ + *her* ‘stick’ — ‘sticking together’, logically connected).
