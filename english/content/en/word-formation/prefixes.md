Zhang Lei is helping a friend move flat, and he picks up a can of paint thinner with a large red label: *HIGHLY INFLAMMABLE*. He knows *in-* means ‘not’ — *incorrect*, *incomplete*, *invisible* — so he decides the can is safe to keep next to the cooker. He is wrong, dangerously so. *Inflammable* means ‘burns easily’, exactly the same as *flammable*. Its *in-* is a different prefix, meaning ‘in, into’: something inflammable goes *into flames*.

Prefixes are among the most useful word parts a learner can know. A small number of them appear in thousands of words, and their meanings are usually clear and stable. But, as Zhang Lei found, the same spelling can hide two different prefixes, and a prefix that looks familiar is not always doing its usual job. This chapter sets out the common prefixes by meaning — negation, reversal, wrong action, degree, time, number and place — shows how to choose between the negative prefixes, and points out the traps.

The basic terms (*morpheme*, *prefix*, *base*, *bound*) are explained in [[word-formation/morphemes]]. A first short list of prefixes is in [[core-vocabulary/word-families]].

## What prefixes do

::: definition Prefix {#def-prefix}
A **prefix** (前缀) is an affix placed before a base. It adds a meaning to the base — ‘not’, ‘again’, ‘before’, ‘too much’ — and makes a new word: *happy* → *unhappy*, *write* → *rewrite*, *war* → *pre-war*, *eat* → *overeat*.
:::

::: theorem Prefixes usually keep the word class {#rule-prefix-class}
In English, a prefix usually changes the **meaning** of a word but not its **word class**: *happy* and *unhappy* are both adjectives, *write* and *rewrite* are both verbs, *market* and *supermarket* are both nouns. The suffix, at the end of the word, is what normally decides the class ([[word-formation/suffixes]]).

A few prefixes are exceptions. *En-* (*em-* before *b* and *p*) and *be-* make verbs: *large* → *enlarge*, *danger* → *endanger*, *power* → *empower*, *friend* → *befriend*. *Out-* usually keeps the class but changes how the verb is used (see [[#ex-out]]).
:::

So when you meet a word with a prefix, the question to ask is ‘what meaning does this add?’ Most prefixes are pronounced with little or no stress — *un**HAP**py*, *re**WRITE***, *mis**LEAD*** — or with a secondary stress (*ˌoverˈeat*). The stress stays on the base, which is one reason prefixed words are easy to understand when you hear them: the familiar part is the strong part. (For stress, see [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics]].)

## Negative prefixes: *un-*, *in-*, *dis-*, *non-*

Four prefixes mean ‘not’ or ‘the opposite of’. Together they make thousands of words.

| prefix | joins mainly to | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *un-* | adjectives (including those in *-able*, *-ed*, *-ing*), some nouns | *unhappy*, *unfair*, *unusual*, *unable*, *unexpected*, *unwilling*, *unrest* |
| *in-* (*im-*, *il-*, *ir-*) | adjectives of Latin or French origin | *incorrect*, *invisible*, *impossible*, *illegal*, *irregular* |
| *dis-* | verbs, nouns and adjectives | *disagree*, *dislike*, *disorder*, *dishonest*, *disloyal* |
| *non-* | nouns and adjectives | *non-smoker*, *non-fiction*, *non-stop*, *non-urgent* |

::: theorem Choosing a negative prefix {#rule-negative}
1. **Learn each negative word as it is.** The choice is fixed by usage, not by a rule you can always apply: we say *unhappy* but *impossible*, *dishonest* but *unkind*. A learner's dictionary shows the opposite of each adjective.
2. **When you need a new word, *un-* is the safest choice** for adjectives, and *non-* for nouns and for ‘not belonging to this group’: *unaffordable*, *unshareable*, *non-member*, *non-native*.
3. ***In-* changes its form** before certain consonants: *im-* before *p*, *b* and *m* (*impatient*, *imbalance*, *immature*), *il-* before *l* (*illegal*, *illogical*), *ir-* before *r* (*irregular*, *irresponsible*), and *in-* elsewhere (*inactive*, *incorrect*, *informal*). *In-* is now hardly ever used to make new words.
4. ***Un-* and *non-* can differ in meaning.** *Non-* is neutral: ‘not of this kind’. *Un-* often judges: ‘not as it should be’. A *non-scientific* book is about something other than science; an *unscientific* argument is bad science. A *non-professional* is someone who is not a professional; *unprofessional* behaviour is behaviour that falls below professional standards.
:::

::: proof
The pattern comes from history. *Un-* is a native English prefix: it was already in Old English and has been used to make new words ever since. *In-* came with words borrowed from Latin and French, mostly in the Middle and Early Modern English periods, and it stayed attached to such words: the *im-*, *il-*, *ir-* forms are the Latin spellings, in which the *n* had become like the following consonant. Studies of word-formation in dictionaries and corpora (Bauer 1983; Plag 2018; Bauer, Lieber & Plag 2013) agree that *un-* and *non-* are productive — they are still used to make new words — while *in-* is at most marginally productive: new negative adjectives in modern English almost always take *un-* or *non-*. *Dis-* came from Latin through French and is still used, mostly with verbs. Because the four prefixes came into English at different times with different words, no single rule decides between them for existing words.
:::

::: quiz
Which word is **not** a real English word?
- [ ] *unhappy*
- [ ] *impolite*
- [x] *inhappy*
- [ ] *dishonest*
::: solution
*Inhappy* does not exist. The opposite of *happy* is *unhappy*: *happy* is a word of Germanic origin (from *hap* ‘luck’, a word borrowed from Old Norse), and it takes the native prefix *un-*. *Impolite* (*in-* before *p* becomes *im-*) and *dishonest* are standard.
:::
:::

::: example Choosing the negative prefix {#ex-negative}
Complete each sentence with the opposite of the word in brackets, using *un-*, *in-* (*im-*, *il-*, *ir-*), *dis-* or *non-*. Explain your choice.

1. *Parking here is ___ (legal); you may get a fine.*
2. *I'm afraid I ___ (agree) with you.*
3. *The fruit is still green — it's ___ (ripe).*
4. *This is a ___ (smoking) hotel.*
5. *His handwriting is completely ___ (readable).*
6. *She was very ___ (patient) with the slow waiter.*
::: solution
1. *illegal* — *legal* is a Latin-origin adjective that takes *in-*, which becomes *il-* before *l*.
2. *disagree* — *dis-* is the usual negative prefix with verbs.
3. *unripe* — *ripe* is a native adjective; *un-* is the normal choice.
4. *non-smoking* — *non-* means ‘not of this kind’: a hotel where smoking is not allowed. (*Unsmoking* does not exist.)
5. *unreadable* — adjectives in *-able* made from English verbs almost always take *un-* (*unbreakable*, *unbelievable*). Note: *illegible* is also correct for handwriting, but it is a different word, from Latin *legibilis*.
6. *impatient* — *patient* takes *in-*, which becomes *im-* before *p*.
:::
:::

## Reversing and removing: *un-*, *de-*, *dis-*

With verbs, *un-* has a different meaning. It does not mean ‘not’ but ‘do the opposite of’ or ‘undo’.

- *tie* → *untie*, *lock* → *unlock*, *pack* → *unpack*, *do* → *undo*, *zip* → *unzip*, *fold* → *unfold*
- *To unpack a suitcase* is not ‘not to pack it’; it is to take things out of it after packing.

*De-* and *dis-* can also reverse or remove:

- *de-*: *defrost* (remove the ice), *decode* (change a code back into plain words), *deforest* (remove the forests), *devalue*
- *dis-*: *disconnect*, *disappear*, *dislike*, *disembark* (leave a ship or plane)

::: quiz
What does *unfold* mean in *She unfolded the letter and read it*?
- [ ] She did not fold the letter.
- [x] She opened the letter out from its folded shape.
- [ ] She folded the letter again.
- [ ] She folded it badly.
::: solution
*Fold* is a verb, so *un-* means ‘reverse the action’: the letter was folded, and she opened it out. ‘Not fold’ would be *didn't fold*; ‘fold again’ would be *refold*; ‘fold badly’ would need *mis-*, though *misfold* is rare outside science.

## Wrongly and badly: *mis-*, *mal-*

- *mis-* ‘wrongly’: *misunderstand*, *mispronounce*, *misspell*, *mislead*, *misprint*, *misuse*.
- *mal-* ‘badly’: *malfunction*, *malnutrition*, *malpractice*. *Mal-* is much less common and mostly formal or technical.

Notice the spelling of *misspell* and *misspelling*: *mis-* + *spell* gives two *s*'s. In the same way *un-* + *natural* gives *unnatural*, and *dis-* + *satisfied* gives *dissatisfied*. Nothing is dropped when a prefix is added ([[word-formation/spelling-changes]]).

## Degree and size: *over-*, *under-*, *out-*, *super-*, *sub-*

These prefixes say how much, or where something is in relation to something else. Several of them have both a meaning of degree and a meaning of place.

| prefix | meaning | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *over-* | too much | *overeat*, *oversleep*, *overcrowded*, *overpriced* |
| | above, across | *overhead*, *overcoat*, *overseas* |
| *under-* | too little | *underpaid*, *undercooked*, *underestimate* |
| | below | *underground*, *underline*, *underwater* |
| *out-* | more or better than | *outrun*, *outnumber*, *outgrow*, *outlive* |
| *super-* | above, more than normal | *superhuman*, *superstar*, *supermarket* |
| *sub-* | under, less than, part of | *subway*, *subtitle*, *substandard*, *subgroup* |

*Over-* and *under-* are opposites in their degree meaning: *overpaid* / *underpaid*, *overestimate* / *underestimate*, *overcooked* / *undercooked*. They are very productive in this meaning: you can understand *overthink*, *overshare* or *underuse* the first time you see them.

::: example Degree or place? {#ex-over-under}
Does *over-* or *under-* show degree (‘too much’, ‘too little’) or place (‘above’, ‘below’) in each sentence? Give the meaning of the word.

1. *The bus was so overcrowded that we waited for the next one.*
2. *Put on your overcoat — it's cold.*
3. *Nurses here are underpaid.*
4. *We took the underground to the museum.*
5. *I think you underestimate how long the journey takes.*
::: solution
1. Degree: *overcrowded* ‘with too many people’.
2. Place: *overcoat* ‘a coat worn over other clothes’.
3. Degree: *underpaid* ‘paid too little’.
4. Place: *underground* ‘the railway under the city’ (BrE; AmE usually *subway*).
5. Degree: *underestimate* ‘think something is smaller, shorter or less than it really is’.

A quick test: if you can add ‘too’ (*too crowded*, *paid too little*), the prefix shows degree. The degree meaning usually goes with adjectives, participles and verbs; the place meaning often appears in nouns (*overcoat*, *underground*, *underwear*).
:::
:::

::: example *Out-* and the object {#ex-out}
Compare the sentences. What does *out-* add, and what happens to the grammar of the verb?

1. *Mia runs fast.* → *Mia outran all the other girls.*
2. *Tom lived to 90.* → *Tom outlived his brothers.*
3. *The students number about 300.* → *The students outnumber the teachers ten to one.*
::: solution
*Out-* means ‘more, further or better than someone else’. The comparison needs a second person or thing — the one who is beaten — and that person becomes the **object** of the new verb:

1. *Run* has no object in *Mia runs fast*; *outrun* has one: *all the other girls*. *Mia outran them* = she ran faster than they did.
2. *Outlive his brothers* = live longer than his brothers.
3. *Outnumber the teachers* = be more in number than the teachers.

So *out-* is an unusual prefix: it keeps the word class (a verb stays a verb), but it turns a verb without an object into one that needs an object. You cannot say ✗ *Mia outran.*
:::
:::

## Time and order: *pre-*, *post-*, *re-*, *ex-*, *fore-*

| prefix | meaning | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *pre-* | before | *pre-war*, *preview*, *prepay*, *pre-school* |
| *post-* | after | *post-war*, *postgraduate*, *postpone* |
| *re-* | again; back | *rewrite*, *rebuild*, *reopen*, *recycle*, *repay* |
| *ex-* | former | *ex-wife*, *ex-president*, *ex-colleague* |
| *fore-* | before, in front | *forecast*, *foresee*, *forehead* |

*Re-* is one of the most productive prefixes in English: it can be added to almost any verb of action whose result can be repeated (*rename*, *reheat*, *restart*, *reload*). It is much harder to use with verbs of state: ✗ *reknow*, ✗ *relike*.

::: warning *Re-* with and without a hyphen
A few verbs with *re-* have an old meaning that cannot be guessed from the parts, and a new one that can. The hyphen keeps them apart:

- *recover* ‘get better after an illness’ — *re-cover* ‘put a new cover on’: *We had the old sofa re-covered.*
- *reform* ‘improve a system’ — *re-form* ‘form again’: *The band re-formed after ten years.*
- *resign* ‘leave a job’ — *re-sign* ‘sign again’: *The player re-signed with his old club.*
- *recount* ‘tell a story’ — *re-count* ‘count again’: *The votes were re-counted.*

✗ *The singer recovered the old song* (meaning ‘recorded a new version’) is confusing; ✓ *The singer covered the old song* or *made a new recording of it*. When *re-* means simply ‘again’ and there is no confusion, no hyphen is needed: *rewrite*, *reopen*. Before a base beginning with *e*, both are found: *re-enter* (usual in BrE), *reenter* (common in AmE).
:::

*Ex-* ‘former’ is always written with a hyphen and joins mainly nouns for people and roles: *my ex-boss*, *an ex-soldier*. It comes from the same Latin *ex* ‘out of’ as the *ex-* of *export* and *exit* — someone who is ‘out of’ a role — but in English the two work differently: *ex-* ‘former’ is added freely to English nouns, while the *ex-* ‘out’ of *export* is part of words borrowed from Latin, which you will meet in [[word-formation/greek-latin-roots]]. In informal English *ex* is also a noun by itself: *She's still friends with her ex.*

## Number: *uni-*, *mono-*, *bi-*, *tri-*, *multi-*, *semi-*

| prefix | meaning | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *uni-*, *mono-* | one | *uniform*, *unicycle*, *monolingual*, *monotone* |
| *bi-* | two | *bicycle*, *bilingual*, *bilateral* |
| *tri-* | three | *triangle*, *tricycle*, *trilingual* |
| *multi-* | many | *multinational*, *multicultural*, *multi-storey* (BrE car park) |
| *semi-* | half, partly | *semicircle*, *semi-final*, *semi-skilled* |

*Uni-*, *bi-* and *multi-* are from Latin, *mono-* from Greek, and *tri-* is found in both. In scientific words, Greek and Latin number prefixes are used in long series (*monoxide*, *dioxide*, *trioxide*), which [[word-formation/greek-latin-roots]] returns to.

::: warning *Biweekly* and *bimonthly*
*Bi-* means ‘two’, but in time words it is ambiguous. *A biweekly meeting* can mean ‘every two weeks’ or ‘twice a week’, and *bimonthly* can mean ‘every two months’ or ‘twice a month’; dictionaries such as Merriam-Webster list both meanings. Careful writers avoid the problem: ✓ *every two weeks*, *twice a week*, *every other month*. *Biannual* (twice a year) and *biennial* (every two years) are also easily confused. British English also has *fortnightly* ‘every two weeks’.
:::

## Place and relation: *inter-*, *trans-*, *co-*, *anti-*

- *inter-* ‘between, among’: *international*, *interview*, *interact*, *intercity*
- *trans-* ‘across, through’: *transatlantic*, *transport*, *translate*, *transform*
- *co-* ‘together, jointly’: *co-worker*, *co-author*, *cooperate*, *co-founder*
- *anti-* ‘against’: *anti-war*, *antisocial*, *anti-virus*, *antibiotic*

*Co-* is often hyphenated before a vowel (*co-operate* in some British writing, *cooperate* in American and much British writing) and in new words for people (*co-host*, *co-pilot*). Both are correct; follow your dictionary or your organisation's style guide.

::: quiz
What is a *co-founder*?
- [ ] someone who founded a company a second time
- [x] one of two or more people who founded something together
- [ ] someone who works against the founder
- [ ] a former founder
::: solution
*Co-* means ‘together with others’: *She is the co-founder of a small design company* — she started it with one or more other people. ‘Against’ would be *anti-*; ‘former’ would be *ex-*; ‘again’ would be *re-*.
:::
:::

::: example Prefixes in a short text {#ex-text}
Read the text and explain the meaning of each word in bold by its prefix and base.

> *The old cinema in our town was **rebuilt** last year. It now shows **subtitled** films from many countries, and the **pre-show** talks are popular. Tickets are not **overpriced**, and the staff are never **impolite**. Some people say the café is **undersized**, but I **disagree**.*
::: solution
- *rebuilt*: *re-* ‘again’ + *built* — built again.
- *subtitled*: *sub-* ‘under’ + *title* + *-ed* — with a translation written under the picture.
- *pre-show*: *pre-* ‘before’ + *show* — happening before the film.
- *overpriced*: *over-* ‘too much’ + *priced* — costing more than they should.
- *impolite*: *im-* (= *in-* before *p*) ‘not’ + *polite* — rude.
- *undersized*: *under-* ‘too little’ + *sized* — too small.
- *disagree*: *dis-* ‘not, the opposite of’ + *agree* — have a different opinion.

Every prefix here keeps the word class of its base: *rebuilt* and *disagree* are verbs like *built* and *agree*; *impolite* is an adjective like *polite*.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Many English prefixes have close Chinese equivalents, and some modern Chinese words were made as translations of English ones: *supermarket* is 超级市场 or 超市, *ex-wife* is 前妻, *semi-final* is 半决赛, *bilingual* is 双语, *anti-war* is 反战, *misunderstand* is 误解. Chinese grammarians call characters such as 超, 前, 半, 反, 非, 不, 多 and 可 **类前缀** (quasi-prefixes), a term from 吕叔湘's 《汉语语法分析问题》 (1979): they behave like prefixes in many words but also keep their meaning as ordinary morphemes, so Chinese has few true affixes. This similarity makes English prefixes fairly easy for Chinese learners.

Two points need care. First, Chinese often uses a whole word where English uses a prefix, and the word order can differ: *pre-war* is 战前 and *post-war* 战后, with 前 and 后 after the noun, while English puts *pre-* and *post-* before it. Second, 不 covers the meanings of *un-*, *in-*, *dis-* and *non-*, so Chinese gives no help in choosing between them: 不可能 is *impossible*, 不诚实 is *dishonest*, 不高兴 is *unhappy*, and 不吸烟的 is *non-smoking*. The English choice has to be learned word by word ([[#rule-negative]]).
:::

::: warning Prefixes that are not what they seem
- *inflammable* = *flammable* ‘burns easily’. The *in-* means ‘into’ (Latin *inflammare* ‘set on fire’). The opposite is *non-flammable*.
- *invaluable* = ‘extremely valuable’, not ‘without value’. *Priceless* is similar. The word for ‘without value’ is *worthless*.
- *unloosen* = *loosen*, and *ravel* and *unravel* can both mean ‘come apart’ (of threads): here *un-* adds little or nothing.
- *disgruntled* ‘annoyed’ has no positive *gruntled* in ordinary use; the novelist P. G. Wodehouse used *gruntled* as a joke.

Check any prefixed word that seems to contradict its context in a dictionary.
:::

::: history
The two main negative prefixes of English show its double history. *Un-* comes from Old English and is found in the other Germanic languages too (German *un-*, as in *unmöglich* ‘impossible’). *In-* came into English with thousands of Latin and French words, especially between the fourteenth and seventeenth centuries. The result is pairs that combine both systems, such as *unable* and *inability*, or *unjust* and *injustice*. The confusion over *inflammable* comes from the same history: Latin had both a negative *in-* and an *in-* meaning ‘in, into’. In the twentieth century, fire-safety organisations in English-speaking countries began to prefer *flammable* on warning labels, because *inflammable* could be misread as ‘not flammable’; both words are still correct.
:::

## Where this leads

Prefixes rarely change word class; suffixes usually do, and they are the subject of [[word-formation/suffixes]]. Many prefixes of Latin and Greek origin — *ex-* ‘out’, *pro-* ‘forward’, *tele-* ‘far’ — combine with bound roots in academic words; see [[word-formation/greek-latin-roots]]. The spelling of words like *misspell* and *unnatural* is part of [[word-formation/spelling-changes]], and [[word-formation/learning-with-word-formation]] shows how to use prefixes to guess new words safely. For the words that English borrowed with *in-* and *dis-*, see [[history-of-english/norman-french]] and [[history-of-english/early-modern-english]].

::: summary
- A **prefix** goes before a base and usually changes the **meaning**, not the word class; *en-*/*em-* and *be-*, which make verbs, are exceptions.
- **Negative prefixes**: *un-* (the most common, still used for new words), *in-*/*im-*/*il-*/*ir-* (Latin-origin adjectives; hardly used for new words), *dis-* (often with verbs), *non-* (neutral ‘not of this kind’). Learn the negative of each word as it is.
- With verbs, *un-*, *de-* and *dis-* mean ‘reverse’ or ‘remove’: *untie*, *defrost*, *disconnect*.
- *mis-* ‘wrongly’; *over-* ‘too much’ and *under-* ‘too little’; *out-* ‘more than’ (and it adds an object: *outrun someone*).
- Time: *pre-*, *post-*, *re-* (again), *ex-* (former, with a hyphen). Number: *uni-*, *mono-*, *bi-*, *tri-*, *multi-*, *semi-*. Place: *inter-*, *trans-*, *co-*, *anti-*, *sub-*, *super-*.
- Hyphens separate *re-cover* from *recover*, *re-sign* from *resign*.
- Beware of *inflammable* (= *flammable*), *invaluable* (= very valuable) and ambiguous *biweekly*.
:::

1. *I'm sorry, I unagree with you.*

1. ✗ *unagree* → ✓ *I'm sorry, I disagree with you.* (*Dis-* with this verb.)
2. The prefix has been misread: *inflammable* means ‘burns easily’. ✓ *Keep the gas away from fire — it's highly inflammable* (or *flammable*), *so it's dangerous.*
3. *Biweekly* is ambiguous, and ‘next Tuesday and the Tuesday after’ means *weekly*. ✓ *The meeting is weekly* (or, to mean every two weeks: *The meeting is every two weeks*).
4. ✗ *inlegal* → ✓ *illegal*: *in-* becomes *il-* before *l*.
:::

::: exercise Explain a notice {level=3}
A student hall has this notice. Explain the meaning of every prefixed word, and rewrite the notice in simpler English without any of the prefixes.

> *Non-residents must sign in at reception. Do not overload the washing machines. Misuse of the fire alarm is illegal. Rooms must be vacated and unlocked by 10 a.m. on departure day; rooms left untidy will be recleaned at the resident's expense.*
::: hint
Some words have two morphemes before the root (*recleaned* = *re-* + *clean* + *-ed*). For *unlocked*, ask whether *un-* is ‘not’ or ‘reverse’.
:::
::: solution
- *non-residents*: people who do not live in the hall.
- *overload*: put too much in.
- *misuse*: using something wrongly (here, setting off the alarm when there is no fire).
- *illegal*: *il-* (= *in-*) ‘not’ + *legal* — against the law.
- *unlocked*: ‘not locked’ — the past participle used like an adjective, describing the state the room must be left in. (That state is the result of the action of unlocking, so here the two meanings of *un-* lead to the same instruction: leave the door open.)
- *untidy*: not tidy.
- *recleaned*: cleaned again.

A simpler version: *People who do not live here must sign in at reception. Do not put too many clothes in the washing machines. Using the fire alarm when there is no fire is against the law. On the day you leave, you must be out of your room by 10 a.m. and leave the door open. If your room is not tidy, it will be cleaned again and you will pay for it.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Your own new words {level=3}
English speakers make new prefixed words all the time, and listeners understand them at once. Make four new words of your own with *re-*, *over-*, *un-* and *non-*, each in a sentence about your daily life, and explain why a listener would understand them even if they are not in a dictionary. Then say why you could **not** make a new word with *in-* in the same way.
::: solution
Model answers:

- *re-*: *My phone needed a restart, and then I had to re-download all my apps.* (*Re-* + any repeatable action verb is understood at once.)
- *over-*: *I overplanned the trip — there was no time to relax.* (*Over-* ‘too much’ with a verb of action.)
- *un-*: *Before you leave, please unmute your microphone and say goodbye.* (*Un-* ‘reverse’ with a verb.) Or with an adjective: *This hotel is unaffordable in summer.*
- *non-*: *The office has a non-coffee drinker corner.* (*Non-* ‘not of this group’ with a noun phrase.)

Listeners understand such words because *re-*, *over-*, *un-* and *non-* are **productive**: they are regularly used to make new words, and their meanings are stable. *In-* is hardly productive in modern English: it survives mainly in words borrowed long ago, so a new word such as ✗ *inaffordable* sounds wrong, and the existing words must be learned one by one.
:::
