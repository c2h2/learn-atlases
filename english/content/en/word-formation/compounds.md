Chen Jie's English friend sends her a photo from his garden with the message *Look at my new greenhouse!* She expects a house painted green. The photo shows a small building made of glass, full of tomato plants. A **greenhouse** is not a green house: it is one word, with its own meaning, and in speech it sounds different — **GREEN**house, with the stress on the first part, against a green **HOUSE**.

English makes many of its words this way, by joining two words that already exist: *bus stop*, *bedroom*, *toothbrush*, *mobile phone*, *website*, *well-known*. Such words are called **compounds**, and Chinese speakers know the idea well, because most Chinese words are made in the same way: 火车, 黑板, 电脑. This chapter shows how English compounds are built and what they mean, how to tell a compound from an ordinary phrase, how they are written (*bus stop*, *website* or *part-time*?), and how they make their plurals.

The basic stress pattern of compounds was introduced in [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics]]; here we use it as one of several tests.

## What a compound is

::: definition Compound {#def-compound}
A **compound** (复合词) is a word made of two or more words (or roots) joined together: *bed* + *room* → *bedroom*, *bus* + *stop* → *bus stop*, *well* + *known* → *well-known*. The parts may be written together, with a hyphen, or with a space between them, but the compound works in the sentence as **one word**, with one meaning.
:::

How do you know that *greenhouse* is one word and *green house* is two? Several tests point the same way.

::: theorem Tests for a compound {#rule-compound-tests}
A sequence of two words is probably a compound if:

1. **Stress**: the first part is stressed (**BUS** stop, **GREEN**house, **TOOTH**brush), while in a phrase the second part usually is (a green **HOUSE**, a new **BUS**).
2. **Meaning**: the meaning is more specific than the parts suggest. A *greenhouse* is a glass building for plants; a *blackboard* is a board for writing on, and it can be green.
3. **No separate modification**: the first part cannot be modified by itself. *A very green house* is possible; ✗ *a very greenhouse* is not. *A greener house* compares colour; ✗ *a greenerhouse* does not exist.
4. **Fixed order and form**: the parts cannot be separated or reordered: ✗ *a house that is green-* has nothing to do with a greenhouse.

No single test is perfect: some compounds are stressed on the second part (*apple **PIE***, *town **HALL***), and some are written as two words. But when two or more tests agree, the sequence is a compound.
:::

::: proof
The tests reflect what a compound is: a name for one kind of thing, stored in memory as a unit. Because the name refers to a kind, the first part narrows the meaning of the second (a *bus stop* is a kind of stop) instead of describing one particular thing, and it cannot be graded or compared like an adjective in a phrase. The stress pattern follows the same logic: the first part carries the information that distinguishes this kind of thing from others, so it receives the main stress. The exceptions — mainly compounds whose first part names a material (*apple pie*, *cotton shirt*) or a place (*town hall*, *kitchen table*) — are discussed in [[connected-speech/word-stress-patterns]]; their stress is like that of phrases, but they still pass the meaning test.
:::

## The head of a compound

::: definition Head {#def-head}
The **head** (中心成分) of a compound is the part that gives its basic meaning and its word class. In English compounds the head is the **last** part: a *bookshop* is a kind of shop, a *toothbrush* is a kind of brush, a *racehorse* is a kind of horse. The other part, the **modifier**, says which kind.
:::

::: theorem The head is on the right {#rule-head}
1. The **last** element of an English compound is normally its head. It decides the word class: *sugar-free* is an adjective because *free* is; *babysit* is a verb because *sit* is.
2. Changing the order changes the head, and so the meaning: a *horse race* is a race (for horses); a *racehorse* is a horse (for racing). A *flower garden* is a garden; a *garden flower* is a flower.
3. Inflections go on the head: *bookshops*, *toothbrushes*, *bus stops*.
:::

::: example Which is which? {#ex-head}
Explain the difference in meaning between the compounds in each pair by finding the head.

1. *a house boat* — *a boat house*
2. *a dog house* — *a house dog*
3. *a fruit tree* — *tree fruit*
4. *a coffee cup* — *a cup of coffee*
::: solution
1. *Houseboat*: the head is *boat* — a boat that people live in. *Boathouse*: the head is *house* — a building where boats are kept.
2. *Doghouse* (AmE; BrE *kennel*): the head is *house* — a small house for a dog. *House dog*: the head is *dog* — a dog that lives in a house.
3. *Fruit tree*: the head is *tree* — a tree that produces fruit. *Tree fruit*: the head is *fruit* — fruit that grows on trees (as opposed to berries on bushes).
4. *A coffee cup* is a compound: a kind of cup, made for coffee, and it may be empty. *A cup of coffee* is a phrase with *of*: a cup with coffee in it. The phrase is about the coffee, the compound about the cup.

In every case the second part names the kind of thing, and the first part narrows it. The written form (one word or two) does not change this.
:::
:::

Some compounds have no head inside them: a *pickpocket* is not a pocket but a person who steals from pockets; a *redhead* is not a head but a person with red hair; a *paperback* is a book. These are called **exocentric** compounds. They are few, and they are learned as whole words.

::: quiz
What is a *wine glass*?
- [x] a glass made for drinking wine
- [ ] a glass with wine in it
- [ ] a kind of wine
- [ ] glass made from wine bottles
::: solution
The head is *glass*, so a *wine glass* is a kind of glass — the kind made for wine. It can be empty. *A glass of wine* is a phrase: a glass with wine in it. Compare *coffee cup* / *cup of coffee*, *teaspoon* / *spoonful of tea*.
:::
:::

## Kinds of compound

### Compound nouns

Most English compounds are nouns. The first part can be:

- a **noun**: *bus stop*, *bookshop*, *football*, *toothbrush*, *website*, *password*
- an **adjective**: *blackboard*, *greenhouse*, *software*, *mobile phone*, *high school*
- a **verb** or **-ing form**: *swimming pool*, *washing machine*, *driving licence* (AmE *driver's license*), *playground*
- a **preposition** or **adverb**: *input*, *outcome*, *bystander*, *underwear*

A very large group are compounds whose head is an *-er* noun and whose first part is the object of the verb: *taxi driver* ‘someone who drives a taxi’, *dishwasher* ‘a machine that washes dishes’, *English teacher* ‘someone who teaches English’, *bookseller*, *coffee maker*. Notice the order: the verb phrase *drive a taxi* becomes the compound *taxi driver*, with the object **first**. These compounds are very productive: *dog walker*, *phone charger*, *job seeker*.

### Compound adjectives

Compound adjectives are common in descriptions, adverts and news. Some common patterns:

| pattern | examples |
|---|---|
| noun + adjective | *sugar-free*, *world-famous*, *waterproof*, *user-friendly* |
| adjective/adverb + participle | *well-known*, *good-looking*, *old-fashioned*, *long-lasting*, *home-made* |
| noun + *-ing* participle | *English-speaking*, *time-consuming*, *energy-saving* |
| number + noun (before a noun) | *a ten-minute walk*, *a three-day trip*, *a five-year-old child* |
| adjective + noun + *-ed* | *blue-eyed*, *left-handed*, *short-sighted*, *kind-hearted* |

### Compound verbs

True compound verbs are less common. Many were made by taking the ending off a compound noun: *babysit* from *babysitter*, *sightsee* from *sightseeing*, *proofread* from *proofreader*. This process — making a shorter word by removing what looks like a suffix — is called **back-formation**; [[word-formation/learning-with-word-formation]] returns to it. Other compound verbs have an adverb or preposition first: *download*, *update*, *overtake*, *outrun* (see [[word-formation/prefixes#ex-out]]).

## Compounds and stress

The stress rule of [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics]] is the most useful single test, and it matters for understanding speech, because the same two words can form a compound or a phrase with a different meaning.

::: example Compound or phrase? {#ex-stress}
Each phrase in bold can be said in two ways. Give both stress patterns and explain the two meanings.

1. *She's a **dancing teacher**.*
2. *We need a **moving van**.*
3. *Look at that **French teacher**.*
::: solution
1. **DAN**cing teacher (compound): a teacher of dancing; the head is *teacher*, and *dancing* says what she teaches. dancing **TEA**cher (phrase): a teacher who is dancing at the moment.
2. **MO**ving van (compound): a van used for moving furniture to a new home (BrE also *removal van*). moving **VAN** (phrase): a van that is moving, not parked.
3. **FRENCH** teacher (compound): a teacher of French (the language). French **TEA**cher (phrase): a teacher who is French (the nationality) — who may teach maths.

In writing, the context usually shows which meaning is meant. In speech, the stress does it; a listener who hears **DAN**cing teacher does not think of a teacher who is dancing.
:::
:::

Compound adjectives behave differently: they usually have two stresses, and the second is the stronger when the adjective stands alone: *She is well-**KNOWN***, *a world-**FA**mous singer*. Before a stressed noun, the main stress often moves to the first part: *a **WELL**-known **WRI**ter*. This shift is described in [[connected-speech/word-stress-patterns]].

## Writing compounds: one word, a hyphen or a space?

::: theorem How compounds are written {#rule-spelling}
English compounds are written in three ways:

- **closed** (one word): *bedroom*, *football*, *website*, *toothbrush*, *greenhouse*
- **hyphenated**: *mother-in-law*, *well-known*, *part-time*, *ten-minute*
- **open** (two words): *bus stop*, *mobile phone*, *credit card*, *swimming pool*

There is no complete rule; the dictionary decides. But there are strong tendencies:

1. Compounds that are short, old and frequent tend to be closed (*bedroom*, *football*, *airport*).
2. Newer and longer noun compounds tend to be open (*bus stop*, *credit card*, *climate change*).
3. Compound adjectives **before a noun** are usually hyphenated: *a well-known writer*, *a part-time job*, *a ten-minute walk*. After a verb, many are written without a hyphen: *The writer is well known. She works part time.*
4. British English uses hyphens more than American English, which tends to close compounds or leave them open: BrE *e-book*, *co-operate* (in some styles), AmE *ebook*, *cooperate*.
:::

::: proof
Spelling follows usage over time. A new combination starts as two words; as it becomes familiar and is felt as one unit, writers begin to join it, first often with a hyphen, later without one. *Web site* became *website*, *e-mail* is now usually *email*, and *on-line* is now *online*. Style guides record these changes: the Associated Press Stylebook, for example, changed *Web site* to *website* in 2010 and *e-mail* to *email* in 2011. The hyphen before a noun has a clear job: it shows that the two words belong together as one modifier. *A small-business owner* owns a small business; *a small business owner* could be a business owner who is small.
:::

::: example Hyphen or no hyphen? {#ex-hyphen}
Decide whether each compound in brackets needs a hyphen in the sentence.

1. *She has a (full time) job in a bank.*
2. *She works (full time) in a bank.*
3. *It was a (two hour) meeting.*
4. *The meeting lasted (two hours).*
5. *He is a (well known) actor.*
6. *The actor is (well known) in China.*
::: solution
1. *a full-time job* — before a noun: hyphen.
2. *works full time* — after the verb, as an adverb: no hyphen (some writers and dictionaries also hyphenate it here).
3. *a two-hour meeting* — before a noun: hyphen, and the noun is singular (*hour*, not *hours*; see [[#rule-plural]]).
4. *two hours* — an ordinary noun phrase: no hyphen, plural noun.
5. *a well-known actor* — before a noun: hyphen.
6. *is well known* — after *be*: usually no hyphen in British and American style guides, though *well-known* is also common.
:::
:::

## Plurals and numbers in compounds

::: theorem Plurals in compounds {#rule-plural}
1. The plural goes on the **head**, at the end: *bus stops*, *toothbrushes*, *taxi drivers*.
2. The first noun is usually **singular**, even when the meaning is plural: a *toothbrush* cleans many teeth; a *shoe shop* sells many shoes; a *bookshop* sells many books.
3. In a compound adjective with a number, the noun is **singular**: *a ten-minute walk*, *a three-day trip*, *a five-year-old girl*, *a 20-euro note*. ✗ *a five-years-old girl* is a common mistake.
4. A few compounds keep a plural first part because the singular has a different meaning or does not exist: *sports car*, *clothes shop*, *savings account*, *customs officer*.
5. In a few old compounds the head comes **first**, and so does the plural: *passers-by*, *mothers-in-law*, *runners-up*. In informal speech *mother-in-laws* is also heard.
:::

::: quiz
Which is correct?
- [ ] *We went on a three-days trip to Xi'an.*
- [x] *We went on a three-day trip to Xi'an.*
- [ ] *We went on a three-day's trip to Xi'an.*
- [ ] *We went on a three days-trip to Xi'an.*
::: solution
In a compound adjective before a noun, the number is joined to a **singular** noun with a hyphen: *a three-day trip*. Compare the phrase without a following noun: *The trip lasted three days.* (A different, correct pattern uses the possessive without a hyphen: *a three days' trip* — older and less common than *a three-day trip*.)
:::
:::

::: example Making compound adjectives {#ex-modifier}
Rewrite the words in italics as a compound adjective before the noun.

1. *a walk that takes ten minutes*
2. *a child who is six years old*
3. *a country where people speak English*
4. *bread that was made at home*
5. *a job that takes a lot of time*
::: solution
1. *a ten-minute walk* (singular *minute*).
2. *a six-year-old child* (singular *year*, two hyphens).
3. *an English-speaking country* (noun + *-ing* participle; note *an* before the vowel sound).
4. *home-made bread* (BrE; AmE often *homemade*).
5. *a time-consuming job*.

Compound adjectives like these let English pack a whole clause into a modifier before the noun — which is also the position where Chinese puts its modifiers (一个需要十分钟的路程), so the structure is less strange than it looks.
:::
:::

## Compounds from phrasal verbs

Many nouns are made from phrasal verbs: *take off* → *takeoff*, *break down* → *breakdown*, *set up* → *setup*, *check in* → *check-in*, *log in* → *login*, *work out* → *workout*. The noun and the verb differ in three ways:

| | verb | noun |
|---|---|---|
| stress | take **OFF**, break **DOWN** | **TAKE**off, **BREAK**down |
| spelling | two words: *log in*, *set up* | one word or hyphenated: *login*, *setup*, *check-in* |
| grammar | can take an object: *set up a company* | takes articles and plurals: *a setup*, *two breakdowns* |

::: warning Verb or noun?
- ✗ *Please login with your password.* ✓ *Please log in with your password.* (verb: two words) — *Your login is your email address.* (noun: one word)
- ✗ *We need to setup the meeting room.* ✓ *We need to set up the meeting room.* — *The setup took an hour.*
- ✗ *I workout every morning.* ✓ *I work out every morning.* — *I do a short workout every morning.*

In informal writing and on screens, the one-word forms are often used as verbs (*login*, *setup*), but in careful writing the verb is two words.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Compounding is the main way of making words in Chinese, and most Chinese words of two or more syllables are compounds. Chinese grammars classify them by the relation between the parts, with names such as 偏正式 (modifier + head: 火车, 黑板), 联合式 or 并列式 (two parts of equal rank: 父母, 买卖), 动宾式 (verb + object: 司机, 担心), 主谓式 (subject + predicate: 地震, 心疼) and 补充式 (verb + result or direction: 提高, 打开).

English has mainly the first type. English compound nouns, like Chinese 偏正式 words, put the modifier first and the head last: *fire engine* and 消防车 are built the same way. Two differences need care. First, English has very few compounds of equal parts (*bittersweet*, *singer-songwriter*), so pairs like 父母 and 买卖 are translated by phrases or single words: *parents*, *business*. Second, when a verb and its object form a compound, English puts the **object first**: *taxi driver*, *dishwasher*, *English teacher* — the opposite order of the verb phrase *drive a taxi* and of Chinese 动宾式 words, where the verb comes first. Chinese 补充式 verbs such as 打开 and 提高 have no compound equivalent in English; English uses a single verb (*open*, *raise*) or a phrasal verb (*turn on*).
:::

::: history
Compounding is the oldest way of making new words in English. Old English used it far more than modern English does, and its poets were especially fond of compounds called **kennings**, which describe a thing by an image: in *Beowulf* the sea is *hronrād* ‘whale-road’ and the body *bānhūs* ‘bone-house’. After the Norman Conquest, English borrowed huge numbers of French and Latin words, and many old compounds were replaced by borrowings. But compounding never stopped: it is still one of the most productive processes in English, and the vocabulary of computers and the internet is full of new compounds (*website*, *password*, *smartphone*, *download*). Their spelling keeps changing as they become familiar; the Associated Press Stylebook, a guide used by many American journalists, accepted *website* in 2010 and *email* in 2011.
:::

## Where this leads

Some compounds are shortened in use — *mobile phone* → *mobile*, *television set* → *TV* — and some new words are blends of two words, such as *brunch*: see [[word-formation/shortening]]. Compounds made from Greek and Latin roots, such as *biology* and *telephone*, are the subject of [[word-formation/greek-latin-roots]]. The stress of compounds and of phrases is practised further in [[connected-speech/word-stress-patterns]], and noun + noun combinations in academic writing are discussed in [[nouns-and-articles/noun-phrases]]. For phrasal verbs as verbs, see [[verb-patterns/phrasal-verbs]].

::: summary
- A **compound** is a word made of two or more words: *bus stop*, *greenhouse*, *well-known*. It works as one word with one meaning.
- Tests: stress on the first part (**GREEN**house), a specific meaning, no separate modification of the first part, fixed order.
- The **head** is the last part; it gives the word class and the basic meaning (*racehorse* is a horse, *horse race* is a race). Plurals go on the head.
- In verb + object compounds the object comes first: *taxi driver*, *dishwasher*.
- Compounds are written closed (*website*), hyphenated (*part-time*) or open (*bus stop*); check the dictionary. Compound adjectives before a noun are hyphenated: *a well-known writer*.
- The first noun is usually singular: *a toothbrush*, *a ten-minute walk*, *a five-year-old girl* (not ✗ *five-years-old*).
- Nouns from phrasal verbs: *a setup*, *a login* (one word, stress on the first part); the verbs are two words: *set up*, *log in*.
:::
