Ma Lin has written a short email to her English teacher. The spellchecker is switched off, and she has made six spelling mistakes:

> *I am writting to say that I am hopeing to join the begining class. I have been studing English for a year, and I think it is a responsable choice. I am sure it will bring me a lot of happyness.*

Every one of these mistakes happens at the same place: the point where a suffix is added to a word. *Write* + *-ing* is *writing* (one *t*), *hope* + *-ing* is *hoping* (no *e*), *begin* + *-ing* is *beginning* (two *n*'s), *study* + *-ing* is *studying* (keep the *y*), *happy* + *-ness* is *happiness* (*y* → *i*), and *responsible* ends in *-ible*. English spelling looks irregular, but the changes at the joins between morphemes follow a small number of rules, and most of them have a reason: they keep the pronunciation of the base word visible in the spelling.

This chapter sets out those rules: dropping a silent *e*, doubling a final consonant, changing *y* to *i*, a few smaller rules, and the two choices that rules alone cannot decide, *-able*/*-ible* and *-ance*/*-ence*. The relation between English spelling and sounds in general is the subject of [[sounds-of-english/spelling-and-sound]].

## Vowel suffixes and consonant suffixes

The rules depend on the first letter of the suffix.

::: definition Vowel suffix and consonant suffix {#def-vowel-suffix}
A **vowel suffix** begins with a vowel letter: *-ing*, *-ed*, *-er*, *-est*, *-able*, *-ous*, *-ive*, *-ity*, *-al*, *-ence*. For these rules, *-y* (as in *icy*, *sunny*) also counts as a vowel suffix. A **consonant suffix** begins with a consonant letter: *-ment*, *-ness*, *-ful*, *-less*, *-ly*, *-s*.
:::

## Silent *e*

::: theorem Dropping silent *e* {#rule-drop-e}
When a word ends in a silent *e*:

1. **drop the *e* before a vowel suffix**: *hope* → *hoping*, *make* → *making*, *write* → *writer*, *use* → *usable*, *create* → *creative*, *ice* → *icy*, *fame* → *famous*.
2. **keep the *e* before a consonant suffix**: *hope* → *hopeful*, *care* → *careful*, *move* → *movement*, *love* → *lovely*, *safe* → *safely*, *late* → *lateness*.
:::

::: proof
The silent *e* has a job: it shows that the vowel before it is "long" (it says its letter name): *hop* /hɒp/ (GB), /hɑːp/ (GA) but *hope* /həʊp/ (GB), /hoʊp/ (GA); *cut* but *cute*. A vowel suffix can do the same job, because a single consonant followed by a vowel letter also signals a long vowel: *hoping*, *cuter*. So before a vowel suffix the *e* is not needed and is dropped. Before a consonant suffix it is still needed, so it stays: without it, *hopful* would suggest the vowel of *hop*. Historically, this *e* was pronounced in Middle English; after it fell silent, writers kept it as a marker of the long vowel, and the spelling rules grew around that use.
:::

The exceptions also follow the logic of the rule — the *e* stays when it still has a job:

- **after soft *c* and *g* before *a* or *o***: *notice* → *noticeable*, *change* → *changeable*, *manage* → *manageable*, *courage* → *courageous*. Without the *e*, *c* and *g* before *a* or *o* would be "hard" (/k/, /ɡ/) as in *cable* and *go*.
- **to keep two words apart**: *dye* → *dyeing* (colouring) but *die* → *dying*; *singe* → *singeing* (burning slightly) but *sing* → *singing*.
- **after *ee*, *oe* and *ye***: *see* → *seeing*, *agree* → *agreeing*, *canoe* → *canoeing*, *eye* → *eyeing* (also *eying*).

A few common words drop the *e* before a consonant suffix: *true* → *truly*, *argue* → *argument*, *whole* → *wholly*, *nine* → *ninth*, *awe* → *awful*. And some words have two accepted spellings, one of them more British: *judgement* (BrE, general use) / *judgment* (AmE, and BrE in law); *ageing* (BrE) / *aging* (AmE).

::: quiz
Which spelling is correct?

- [x] *noticeable*
- [ ] *noticible*
- [ ] *notisable*
::: solution
*Notice* + *-able* keeps the *e*: without it, the *c* before *a* would be read as /k/. So *noticeable* /ˈnəʊtɪsəbl/ (GB), /ˈnoʊtɪsəbl/ (GA). The same happens in *serviceable*, *peaceable* and *changeable*.
:::
:::

## Doubling the final consonant

::: theorem Doubling a final consonant {#rule-double}
Double the final consonant of a word before a vowel suffix when **all three** of these are true:

1. the word ends in **one vowel letter + one consonant letter** (*stop*, *big*, *begin*, *prefer*);
2. the **final syllable is stressed** (every one-syllable word counts as stressed);
3. the consonant is not *w*, *x* or *y*.

So: *stop* → *stopped*, *stopping*; *big* → *bigger*, *biggest*; *run* → *running*, *runner*; *begin* → *beginning*, *beginner*; *prefer* → *preferred*; *admit* → *admitted*; *occur* → *occurred*, *occurrence*.

But no doubling when:

- there are **two vowel letters** before the consonant: *rain* → *raining*, *cook* → *cooked*, *beat* → *beaten*;
- the word ends in **two consonants**: *help* → *helped*, *start* → *starting*, *fast* → *faster*;
- the final syllable is **not stressed**: *open* → *opening*, *visit* → *visited*, *happen* → *happened*, *offer* → *offering*, *budget* → *budgeted*;
- the consonant is *w*, *x* or *y*: *snow* → *snowing*, *fix* → *fixed*, *play* → *playing*;
- the suffix is a **consonant suffix**: *ship* → *shipment*, *sad* → *sadness*, *glad* → *gladly*.
:::

::: proof
Doubling does the opposite job to the silent *e*: it shows that the vowel stays **short**. A single consonant followed by a vowel letter normally signals a long vowel (*hoping*, *diner*, *later*); two consonants signal a short one (*hopping*, *dinner*, *latter*). So *hop* + *-ing* needs two *p*'s to keep the short vowel of *hop*. The stress condition comes from the same logic: the rule protects the pronunciation of a stressed vowel, and unstressed vowels are usually reduced to /ə/ or /ɪ/ anyway, so there is nothing to protect in *opening* or *visited*. When a suffix moves the stress away from the last syllable, the doubling stops too: *preFER* → *preferred* (stress kept) but *PREFerence* (stress moved, single *r*); *reFER* → *referring* but *REFerence*.
:::

::: example Double or not? {#ex-double}
Add the suffix and explain your decision.

1. *plan* + *-ed*
2. *visit* + *-ing*
3. *forget* + *-ing*
4. *wait* + *-ed*
5. *wet* + *-er*
6. *listen* + *-er*
7. *fix* + *-ing*
8. *commit* + *-ment*
::: solution
1. *planned* — one vowel + one consonant, one syllable (stressed): double.
2. *visiting* — the final syllable (*-it*) is not stressed: **VI**sit.
3. *forgetting* — for**GET**: the final syllable is stressed, one vowel + one consonant: double.
4. *waited* — two vowel letters (*ai*): no doubling.
5. *wetter* — one syllable, one vowel + one consonant: double.
6. *listener* — **LIS**ten: final syllable not stressed.
7. *fixing* — *x* is never doubled.
8. *commitment* — *-ment* is a consonant suffix: no doubling (compare *committed*, *committee*, with vowel suffixes).
:::
:::

### British and American *l*

British English doubles a final *l* after a single vowel letter even when the syllable is not stressed; American English usually follows the general rule and does not:

| British English | American English |
|---|---|
| *travel* → *travelled*, *travelling*, *traveller* | *traveled*, *traveling*, *traveler* |
| *cancel* → *cancelled*, *cancelling* | *canceled*, *canceling* (but *cancellation* in both) |
| *model* → *modelling* | *modeling* |
| *label* → *labelled* | *labeled* |

Both are correct in their own variety; keep to one in a text.

### Words ending in *-c*

Words ending in *-ic* add *k* before *-ed*, *-ing* and *-er*, so that the *c* stays /k/: *panic* → *panicked*, *panicking*; *picnic* → *picnicked*, *picnicker*; *traffic* → *trafficking*.

## Changing *y* to *i*

::: theorem *y* after a consonant {#rule-y-i}
1. When a word ends in **consonant + *y***, change the *y* to *i* before any suffix **except** one beginning with *i*: *happy* → *happiness*, *happily*, *happier*; *carry* → *carried*, *carries*, *carrier*; *study* → *studies*, *studied*; *beauty* → *beautiful*; *rely* → *reliable*.
2. Keep the *y* before ***-ing*** (and *-ish*), so as not to write two *i*'s: *study* → *studying*, *carry* → *carrying*, *baby* → *babyish*.
3. When a word ends in **vowel + *y***, keep the *y*: *play* → *played*, *player*; *enjoy* → *enjoyment*, *enjoyable*; *grey* → *greyer* (AmE *gray* → *grayer*). Four common exceptions: *day* → *daily*, *pay* → *paid*, *say* → *said*, *lay* → *laid*.
4. Words ending in ***-ie*** change it to *y* before *-ing*: *die* → *dying*, *lie* → *lying*, *tie* → *tying*.
:::

A few one-syllable adjectives keep the *y* before *-ly* and *-ness*: *shy* → *shyly*, *shyness*; *dry* → *dryness* (and *dryly*, also *drily*); *sly* → *slyly*. And *busy* + *-ness* became *business*, with its own pronunciation /ˈbɪznəs/ and its own meaning; ‘the state of being busy’ is *busyness*.

::: quiz
Which is correct?
- [ ] *She studys every evening.*
- [ ] *She is studing for an exam.*
- [x] *She studied hard and is still studying.*
- [ ] *She studyed hard and is still studiing.*
::: solution
*Study* ends in consonant + *y*: *y* → *i* before *-es* and *-ed* (*studies*, *studied*), but the *y* stays before *-ing* (*studying*), which would otherwise have two *i*'s.
:::
:::

::: example Adding endings to words in *-y* {#ex-y}
Add the endings and explain.

1. *easy* + *-ly*
2. *enjoy* + *-able*
3. *try* + *-ing*, *try* + *-ed*
4. *company* + *-s*
5. *say* + *-ed*
6. *lie* + *-ing*
::: solution
1. *easily* — consonant + *y*: *y* → *i*.
2. *enjoyable* — vowel + *y*: keep the *y*.
3. *trying* (keep *y* before *-ing*), *tried* (*y* → *i*).
4. *companies* — consonant + *y*: *y* → *i*, and *-s* becomes *-es*.
5. *said* — one of the four exceptions (*day*, *pay*, *say*, *lay*).
6. *lying* — *-ie* becomes *y* before *-ing*.
:::
:::

## Smaller rules: prefixes, *-ful*, *all-*, *-ly*

- **Prefixes never change the spelling of the base**, and the base never changes the prefix (except the Latin assimilations such as *il-*, *im-*: [[word-formation/prefixes#rule-negative]]). So a letter can appear twice at the join: *mis-* + *spell* → *misspell*; *un-* + *necessary* → *unnecessary*; *dis-* + *satisfied* → *dissatisfied*; *over-* + *rate* → *overrated*; *im-* + *mature* → *immature*.
- **The suffix *-ful* has one *l***: *careful*, *beautiful*, *spoonful*, even though the word *full* has two. Adverbs add *-ly* normally: *carefully*, *beautifully*.
- ***All* becomes *al-*** at the start of a few words: *always*, *already*, *almost*, *although*, *altogether*. But *all right* is two words in careful writing (*alright* is common in informal writing), and *already* (‘before now’) is different from *all ready* (‘all prepared’).
- **Adverbs in *-ly***: adjectives ending in consonant + *-le* change *-le* to *-ly*: *simple* → *simply*, *gentle* → *gently*, *possible* → *possibly*. Adjectives in *-ll* add only *-y*: *full* → *fully*, *dull* → *dully*. Adjectives in *-ic* add *-ally*: *basic* → *basically*, *tragic* → *tragically* (except *publicly*).

## *-able* or *-ible*?

Both endings mean ‘can be …’ and sound the same (/əbl/), so the spelling has to be chosen.

::: theorem *-able* or *-ible* {#rule-able-ible}
1. Use ***-able*** when the base is a **complete English word**: *accept* → *acceptable*, *read* → *readable*, *wash* → *washable*, *suit* → *suitable*, *comfort* → *comfortable*, *rely* → *reliable*, *notice* → *noticeable*. Every new word takes *-able*: *downloadable*, *recyclable*.
2. ***-ible*** usually follows a Latin root that is **not** an English word by itself: *visible*, *possible*, *edible*, *terrible*, *horrible*, *incredible*, *plausible*.
3. This is a strong tendency with exceptions, which must be learned: *responsible*, *sensible*, *flexible*, *accessible*, *digestible*, *convertible* take *-ible* although *response*, *sense*, *flex*, *access*, *digest* and *convert* are words. Several of them have a related noun in *-ion* (*convert* → *conversion*, *convertible*; *digest* → *digestion*, *digestible*; *flex* → *flexion*, *flexible*), which is a useful hint: when the base also forms a noun in *-sion* or *-tion*, *-ible* is likely.
:::

::: proof
The two endings come from Latin *-abilis* and *-ibilis*, which belonged to different classes of Latin verbs; English borrowed many words with both, already formed. When English began to make its own new adjectives, it used *-able*, partly because *-able* was also the usual form in French, from which much of this vocabulary came. That is why *-able* is productive — it attaches to any English verb — and *-ible* survives only in borrowed words. The hint about *-ion* nouns works because *-ible* words and *-sion*/*-tion* nouns often come from the same Latin stem (*vis-ible*, *vis-ion*; *digest-ible*, *digest-ion*). It is a hint, not a rule: *collectable* and *collectible* are both found.
:::

::: example Choosing the ending {#ex-able}
Add *-able* or *-ible* and explain the choice. Watch for the other spelling rules at the join.

1. *rely*
2. *vis-* (see)
3. *love*
4. *manage*
5. *forget*
6. *digest*
::: solution
1. *reliable* — *rely* is a complete word: *-able*; consonant + *y* → *i* ([[#rule-y-i]]).
2. *visible* — *vis-* is a Latin root, not an English word: *-ible*.
3. *lovable* — a complete word: *-able*; drop the silent *e* before a vowel suffix ([[#rule-drop-e]]). (*Loveable* is also found, but *lovable* is the usual spelling.)
4. *manageable* — *-able*, and the *e* stays after soft *g* before *a*.
5. *forgettable* (most often met in *unforgettable*) — *forget* + *-able*, with the *t* doubled because the final syllable of for**GET** is stressed ([[#rule-double]]).
6. *digestible* — an exception to learn: *digest* is a word, but the adjective takes *-ible*, matching the noun *digestion*.
:::
:::

## *-ance*, *-ence*, *-ant*, *-ent*

There is no simple rule for these endings, because they too go back to different Latin verb classes. But three facts help.

1. **Pairs match.** If you know the adjective, you know the noun: *important* → *importance*, *relevant* → *relevance*, *different* → *difference*, *independent* → *independence*, *patient* → *patience*.
2. **Soft and hard *c* and *g*.** After a "soft" *c* (/s/) or *g* (/dʒ/), the ending is *-ence*/*-ent*, because *c* and *g* are soft before *e*: *innocence*, *intelligent*, *emergency*, *diligent*. After a "hard" *c* (/k/) or *g* (/ɡ/), it is *-ance*/*-ant*: *significant*, *elegance*, *extravagant*.
3. **Common mistakes are few.** A short list covers most of them: *independent* (not ✗ *independant*), *existence* (not ✗ *existance*), *appearance* (not ✗ *appearence*), *occurrence* (double *r* and *-ence*), *maintenance* (from *maintain*, but with *-ten-*), *relevant*, *permanent*.

In British English, *dependant* is a noun (‘a person who depends on someone financially’) and *dependent* the adjective; American English uses *dependent* for both.

::: warning Trusting the spellchecker
A spellchecker catches ✗ *happyness* and ✗ *begining*, but not real words in the wrong place: *dying* for *dyeing*, *later* for *latter*, *hoping* for *hopping*, *diner* for *dinner*, *already* for *all ready*. Check that your spellchecker is set to the variety you are writing in — British English (*travelled*, *organise*, *colour*) or American (*traveled*, *organize*, *color*) — or it will mark correct words as mistakes and miss the mixing of varieties in one text.
:::

::: example Correcting Ma Lin's email {#ex-email}
Correct the six spelling mistakes in Ma Lin's email from the beginning of the chapter, and name the rule for each.

> *I am writting to say that I am hopeing to join the begining class. I have been studing English for a year, and I think it is a responsable choice. I am sure it will bring me a lot of happyness.*
::: solution
1. *writting* → *writing*. *Write* ends in silent *e*: drop it before *-ing* ([[#rule-drop-e]]). The vowel of *write* is long (/aɪ/), so there is no doubling; *written* has two *t*'s because its vowel is short (/ɪ/).
2. *hopeing* → *hoping*: drop the silent *e* before *-ing*.
3. *begining* → *beginning*: be**GIN** has a stressed final syllable ending in one vowel + one consonant: double the *n* ([[#rule-double]]).
4. *studing* → *studying*: keep the *y* before *-ing* ([[#rule-y-i]]).
5. *responsable* → *responsible*: one of the *-ible* words to learn ([[#rule-able-ible]]).
6. *happyness* → *happiness*: consonant + *y* → *i* before *-ness*.

Corrected: *I am writing to say that I am hoping to join the beginning class. I have been studying English for a year, and I think it is a responsible choice. I am sure it will bring me a lot of happiness.* (A teacher might also suggest *the beginners' class*, the usual name for such a class.)
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Nothing in Chinese writing corresponds to these rules. A Chinese character never changes its form when it combines with another (学 is the same in 学生, 大学 and 学习), and Pinyin, which might show changes of sound, is not used to spell words in ordinary writing. So the idea that a word changes its spelling when a suffix is added — *happy*, *happi-ness* — is new to Chinese learners, and the errors it causes (✗ *happyness*, ✗ *studing*, ✗ *begining*) are common at every level.

Two things help. First, the rules make sense once you see what they protect: the long and short vowels of the base (*hoping* /həʊp-/ against *hopping* /hɒp-/ in GB). Reading the word aloud, with the right vowel, often tells you whether a letter should be doubled or dropped. Second, the changes are at the **join** between morphemes, so when you check your writing, look at the ends of words that have suffixes. The splitting skill from [[word-formation/morphemes#rule-split]] works in reverse: if you can see *happi* + *ness*, you can also write it.
:::

::: history
English spelling was largely fixed in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, by printers and then by dictionaries, above all Samuel Johnson's *Dictionary of the English Language* (1755), whose spellings became the standard in Britain. Most of the rules in this chapter were already the usual practice by then. Noah Webster, in his *American Dictionary of the English Language* (1828), made a number of changes for American English, among them a single *l* in unstressed syllables (*traveler*, *canceled*) — which is why British and American English differ on the doubling of *l* but agree on the other rules. Proposals for simpler spelling have been made many times since, but the rules described here have stayed stable for over two hundred years.
:::

::: summary
- The rules depend on the suffix: **vowel suffixes** (*-ing*, *-ed*, *-er*, *-able*, *-ous*, *-y* …) and **consonant suffixes** (*-ment*, *-ness*, *-ful*, *-ly*, *-s*).
- **Silent *e*** is dropped before a vowel suffix (*hoping*, *creative*) and kept before a consonant suffix (*hopeful*, *movement*); it stays after soft *c*/*g* before *a*/*o* (*noticeable*, *courageous*).
- **Double** a final single consonant after a single vowel letter when the last syllable is stressed (*stopped*, *beginning*, *preferred*), but not after an unstressed syllable (*opening*, *visited*) or for *w*, *x*, *y*. British English also doubles *l* (*travelled*); American English does not (*traveled*).
- **Consonant + *y*** → *i* before suffixes (*happiness*, *carried*), except before *-ing* (*carrying*); vowel + *y* keeps the *y* (*played*), except *daily*, *paid*, *said*, *laid*.
- Prefixes do not change the base (*misspell*, *unnecessary*); *-ful* has one *l*; *all* → *al-* in *always*, *already*.
- ***-able*** after complete words and in all new words; ***-ible*** mostly after Latin roots (*visible*), with exceptions to learn (*responsible*).
- ***-ance*/*-ence***: learn them in pairs with *-ant*/*-ent*; soft *c*/*g* takes *-ence*/*-ent*.
:::

::: exercise Stress and doubling {level=2}
Explain the difference in spelling in each pair.

1. pre**FER**red: stress on the final syllable of *prefer* is kept, so *r* is doubled. **PREF**erence: *-ence* moves the stress to the first syllable, so no doubling.
2. ad**MIT**: stressed final syllable, doubled *t*. **VIS**it: unstressed final syllable, single *t*.
3. *hoping* is from *hope* (long vowel; *e* dropped); *hopping* is from *hop* (short vowel; *p* doubled to keep it short). The spelling shows which word it is.
4. British English doubles a final *l* after a single vowel even in an unstressed syllable; American English follows the general rule and does not.
:::
:::

::: exercise Proofread a paragraph {level=3}
Correct the spelling mistakes in this paragraph. There are eight. Say which rule each one breaks.

> *Last summer I started a new hobby: cycleing. At the begining I was very nervus, and I fell off twice. My freinds were very helpfull, and they gave me usefull advice. Now I ride every day, and I am planing a longer trip. It is the most enjoiable thing I have done this year.*
::: solution
1. *cycleing* → *cycling*: drop the silent *e* before *-ing*.
2. *begining* → *beginning*: stressed final syllable (be**GIN**), one vowel + one consonant: double.
3. *nervus* → *nervous*: the suffix is *-ous* (*nerve* + *-ous*, *e* dropped).
4. *freinds* → *friends*: not a morpheme rule, just the spelling of the word (*i* before *e*).
5. *helpfull* → *helpful*: the suffix *-ful* has one *l*.
6. *usefull* → *useful*: the same.
7. *planing* → *planning*: *plan* has one vowel + one consonant: double (*planing* is from *plane* ‘make smooth’).
8. *enjoiable* → *enjoyable*: vowel + *y* keeps the *y*.

Corrected: *Last summer I started a new hobby: cycling. At the beginning I was very nervous, and I fell off twice. My friends were very helpful, and they gave me useful advice. Now I ride every day, and I am planning a longer trip. It is the most enjoyable thing I have done this year.*
:::

::: exercise Write and check {level=3}
Write 80–100 words about something you learned recently. Use at least ten words with suffixes, including at least two that drop an *e*, two that double a consonant and two that change *y* to *i*. Then underline those words and, for each, write the base, the suffix and the rule.
::: solution
A model:

> *Last month I tried **baking** bread for the first time. My first loaf was a **disappointing** failure: I had been **hurrying**, and I **stopped** the kneading too early. My sister, who is a **surprisingly** good cook, gave me some **useful** tips. The **beginning** is the hardest part, she said, and **patience** is everything. My second loaf was **tastier** and **easier** to slice, and the third was **completely** different — light and **lovely**. Now I am **planning** to try rye bread, which is **apparently** more difficult.*

- drop *e*: *baking* (*bake* + *-ing*), *surprisingly* (*surprise* + *-ing* + *-ly*), *tastier* (*taste* + *-y*, then *-er*: the *e* is dropped before *-y*)
- double: *stopped* (*stop* + *-ed*), *beginning* (*begin* + *-ing*), *planning* (*plan* + *-ing*)
- *y* → *i*: *tastier* (*tasty* + *-er*), *easier* (*easy* + *-er*)
- keep *y* before *-ing*: *hurrying* (*hurry* + *-ing*)
- keep *e* before a consonant suffix: *completely* (*complete* + *-ly*), *lovely* (*love* + *-ly*), *useful* (*use* + *-ful*)
- no change: *disappointing* (*dis-* + *appoint* + *-ing*: the two *p*'s belong to the base, not to doubling), *apparently* (*apparent* + *-ly*), *patience*

Check your own text in the same way: write the base and suffix next to each underlined word, and the change (if any) will be clear.
