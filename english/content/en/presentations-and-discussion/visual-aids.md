Huang Jie and Sofia Lindqvist are presenting the results of the same survey — how students at their university travel to campus — to two different seminar groups. Here is Huang Jie's main slide:

> **Survey results**
> - The survey was conducted in March with 412 respondents from all faculties
> - 38% of respondents travel by bus, 27% walk, 19% cycle, 11% drive and 5% use other modes
> - Cycling has increased compared with the previous survey in 2022, when it was 12%
> - Many respondents commented that the cycle lanes on the main road are unsafe
> - Driving has decreased slightly
> - Recommendation: improve cycle lanes

Sofia's slide shows the same results:

> **Cycling has grown fastest — but riders say the main road is unsafe**
> *(a bar chart of the five ways of travelling, 2022 and this year side by side; the two cycling bars are in a darker colour, with "12%" and "19%" written above them)*
> *Survey of 412 students, March*

Huang Jie reads his six bullet points aloud, one by one, while the audience reads ahead of him. Sofia says: *Here's how people get to campus. The light bars are 2022, the dark ones this year. Most of you will expect the bus to be first, and it still is. But look at cycling — it's gone from twelve per cent to nineteen. That's the fastest growth of any mode. And when we asked cyclists what would help, the same answer came up again and again: the main road.* Her listeners look at the chart while she talks, and they know from the title what they are supposed to see.

The data are identical. The difference is in how the slide shares the work with the speaker. This chapter is about that division of labour: what to put on slides, what to leave for the voice, and how to talk an audience through a chart or an image. It builds on the plan and signposts of the earlier chapters; the vocabulary of trends and quantities is taught for writing in [[essay-writing/describing-data]], and reading research figures in [[academic-reading/reading-data]]. Here the focus is on doing it aloud.

## What slides are for

::: definition Visual aid {#def-visual-aid}
A **visual aid** is anything the audience looks at to help them understand or remember a talk: projected slides, a chart, a photograph, a diagram, an object, a demonstration, a handout. A visual aid supports the speaker; it is not a copy of what the speaker says, and it is not a document to be read instead of listening.
:::

Many talks have two documents hidden in one file: a set of slides for the audience to look at during the talk, and a report for people to read afterwards. They need different designs. A report is read at the reader's own speed and must make sense without the author; slides are seen for a minute each while someone is talking, and must make sense *with* the speaker. Huang Jie's slide is really a page of a report.

::: theorem Slides show; the speaker explains {#rule-slides}
Design each slide to show **one main idea** that the audience can take in within a few seconds — a picture, a chart, a diagram, or a few words — and give the explanation, the detail and the connections **in speech**. If the audience would need to read a slide for more than a few seconds, they are not listening to you; put the detail in a handout or in your notes.
:::

::: proof
Listeners cannot read and listen to different words at the same time with full attention: both compete for the same language-processing resources, so the audience either reads or listens, and usually reads, because reading is faster. A speaker who shows a dense slide and talks over it is competing with herself. A picture or chart, by contrast, is processed partly through a different channel, so the audience can look at it while hearing the explanation — which is the central idea of the research described in the next section. There is also a practical benefit: a speaker whose slides carry only the main idea must know the detail herself, which makes the talk sound prepared rather than read.
:::

## Mayer's principles

The best-tested advice on combining words and pictures comes from research on **multimedia learning** by Richard E. Mayer and colleagues, collected in *Multimedia Learning* (3rd edition, 2020). Mayer's experiments mostly used short, narrated animations and lessons rather than live talks, so applying them to slides is an extension — but a widely accepted one, and the principles fit what experienced speakers advise.

::: definition Five multimedia principles {#def-mayer}
In Mayer's terms (2020), people learn better:

1. **Multimedia principle**: from words and pictures than from words alone.
2. **Coherence principle**: when extraneous material — interesting but irrelevant pictures, decorative backgrounds, background music, unnecessary detail — is **excluded**.
3. **Signalling principle**: when cues **highlight** the essential material and its organisation — headings, arrows, a contrasting colour, a spoken *the key thing here is…*.
4. **Redundancy principle**: from graphics and spoken words than from graphics, spoken words **and** the same words printed on the screen.
5. **Spatial contiguity principle**: when related words and pictures are placed **near each other** — labels on the parts of a diagram rather than in a key.

Mayer states other principles too, among them **temporal contiguity** (words and the matching pictures presented at the same time, not one after the other) and **segmenting** (a complex lesson given in parts that the learner can follow one at a time).
:::

::: theorem Applying the principles to slides {#rule-mayer}
1. **Use a picture or chart** for anything that has a shape — a trend, a comparison, a process, a place — and words for what does not.
2. **Cut** decoration, clip art, background images and any detail you will not talk about (coherence).
3. **Signal** the one thing to notice: a headline that states it, a darker bar, an arrow, a circle (signalling).
4. **Do not print what you say** next to a picture: if the slide shows a chart and you explain it aloud, do not add the same explanation as bullet points (redundancy).
5. **Label on the picture**, not in a key or a separate box (spatial contiguity).
6. **Build complex slides in steps**, revealing one part as you talk about it (temporal contiguity, segmenting).
:::

::: proof
Mayer explains these effects with a cognitive theory of multimedia learning built on three assumptions: people process words and pictures in partly separate channels (the dual-channel assumption, after Paivio's dual coding theory); each channel can hold only a little at a time (limited capacity, as in Sweller's cognitive load theory); and learning means actively selecting, organising and integrating the material. Words and pictures together use both channels, which is why the multimedia principle helps. Decoration and irrelevant detail consume limited capacity without helping understanding (coherence). Cues tell the learner where to spend it (signalling). Printed words that duplicate spoken words make the eyes split their attention between the text and the picture, and make the learner compare two versions of the same words (redundancy). A label far from its object makes the eyes travel back and forth (spatial contiguity).

The principles have **boundary conditions**, which Mayer discusses: the redundancy effect is weaker or absent when the printed words are few — a short label or a key term placed next to the relevant part of the picture — and when there is no picture at all, and, as with most of the principles, the effects tend to be strongest for learners who know little about the topic. For second-language audiences in particular, writing a **difficult term, a name or a number** on the slide is usually helpful, because these are exactly the items a listener may fail to catch; what the redundancy principle warns against is printing whole sentences that the speaker then reads out.
:::

::: warning Reading your slides aloud
✗ A slide of six full sentences, which the speaker reads out word for word.
✓ A slide with a headline, a chart and two or three labels, which the speaker explains in her own words.

Reading slides aloud breaks the redundancy principle in its strongest form — the same words on screen and in speech, with no picture — and it signals to the audience that they could have read the slides at home. If you need full sentences to remember what to say, put them in the speaker notes, not on the screen.
:::

### Headlines that make a point

A slide title such as *Survey results* or *Methodology* names a topic. A title such as *Cycling has grown fastest — but riders say the main road is unsafe* states the point the slide makes, so that the audience knows what to look for before the speaker explains it. This **assertion–evidence** design — a one-sentence headline stating a claim, with visual evidence beneath it and no bullet list — was developed by the engineering educator Michael Alley (*The Craft of Scientific Presentations*, 2nd edition, 2013), and it applies the signalling principle to the whole slide.

::: example Rebuilding a slide {#ex-rebuild}
Here is a slide from Ibrahim Diallo's talk on reducing paper use in his department. Rebuild it using [[#rule-mayer]] and an assertion headline.

> **Paper use**
> *(a photograph of a forest in the background)*
> - In 2023 the department printed about 310,000 pages
> - In 2024, after double-sided printing became the default, it printed about 240,000 pages
> - This is a reduction of about 23%
> - Most printing is lecture handouts
> - Staff were surveyed and most would accept online handouts
::: solution
**Diagnosis.** The title names a topic. The forest photograph is decoration (coherence) and makes the text harder to read. The five bullets contain two different ideas: the effect of double-sided printing (a comparison with a shape) and the next step (handouts online), which deserve separate slides. Everything is in full sentences, which Ibrahim would be tempted to read (redundancy).

**Slide 1.** Headline: *Double-sided printing cut our paper use by almost a quarter.* Evidence: two bars, 2023 and 2024, labelled *310,000 pages* and *240,000 pages* directly on the bars (spatial contiguity). Plain background.

**Slide 2.** Headline: *Lecture handouts are now our biggest use of paper.* Evidence: a single bar or pie divided by type of printing, with handouts in a darker colour (signalling) and the other types in grey.

**Slide 3.** Headline: *Most staff would accept handouts online.* Evidence: the survey result as one large number, with the question asked in small type below.

What Ibrahim **says**: the background, the exact percentage (*about 23 per cent*, which he can also add as a label), what the survey asked, and his recommendation. The slides show; he explains ([[#rule-slides]]).
:::
:::

::: example Which words stay on the slide? {#ex-words}
Kim Min-jun is explaining how a heat pump works to a class of first-year students, many of whom are second-language listeners. His slide shows a diagram of the cycle, and he has written underneath it: *The refrigerant absorbs heat from the outside air in the evaporator, is compressed so that its temperature rises, releases the heat into the house in the condenser, and then passes through an expansion valve.* Which words should stay on the slide?
::: solution
1. **The sentence goes.** Kim will say it aloud while pointing to the diagram; printing it as well makes the audience read instead of looking (redundancy, [[#rule-mayer]]).
2. **The technical terms stay, as labels on the diagram**: *evaporator*, *compressor*, *condenser*, *expansion valve*, each placed next to its part (spatial contiguity). These are the words a second-language listener is most likely to miss or misspell in notes, and they are short — the boundary condition under which on-screen words help rather than compete.
3. **One signal is added**: arrows round the cycle, perhaps blue on the cold side and red on the warm side, with *outside* and *inside* written on the two halves so that the colour is not the only cue.
4. **The order is built in steps**: Kim reveals each stage as he describes it (segmenting), so that the audience's eyes are where his words are.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which change to a chart slide is best supported by the **coherence** principle?
- [ ] Adding the speaker's explanation as bullet points under the chart.
- [x] Removing a decorative photograph from behind the chart.
- [ ] Moving the labels from a key into the bars.
- [ ] Making the most important bar a darker colour.
::: solution
Coherence means excluding extraneous material, such as decoration. The first change would break the redundancy principle; the third applies spatial contiguity; the fourth applies signalling.
:::
:::

## Readable slides

A slide that cannot be read from the back of the room does not exist for the people sitting there. A few design rules follow from the size of rooms and the eyesight of audiences, not from fashion:

- **Large text**: headlines large enough to read from the back row, and nothing in the main text smaller than a size you could read from there. Test it: stand at the back of the room, or step a few metres back from your laptop screen.
- **Strong contrast**: dark text on a light background or light on dark, never text over a photograph.
- **Not colour alone**: a noticeable minority of people, more often men, have difficulty distinguishing some colours, most often red and green. Use colour together with position, labels, patterns or darkness, so that the point survives in greyscale.
- **Few words**: a headline and a handful of labels. If a slide must show a table, show only the rows and columns you will talk about, and highlight the cell that matters.
- **One idea per slide**: more slides with less on each are easier to follow than fewer, crowded ones. The number of slides matters less than the time each idea needs.

::: warning Tiny numbers in a copied table
✗ Pasting a full table from a report — twelve columns, thirty rows, small type — and saying *I know you can't read this, but…*.
✓ Showing the three numbers that matter, large, perhaps as a simple chart, with the source in small type and the full table in a handout.

*I know you can't read this* tells the audience that the slide is useless and that the speaker knew it. Any slide that needs that apology should be redesigned or removed.
:::

## Describing a chart aloud

When a chart is on the screen, the audience needs to be told how to read it before they can follow what it shows. A good spoken description follows the order in which a listener needs the information.

::: theorem Four steps for describing a chart {#rule-chart}
1. **Say what the chart shows** — its subject, and where the data come from: *This chart shows library visits by hour, from the entry gates, for one week in October.*
2. **Explain how to read it** — the axes, the units, the colours or lines: *Along the bottom you've got the time of day, from eight in the morning to eleven at night; up the side, the number of people coming in each hour.*
3. **State the main trend or comparison** — the one thing you want the audience to see: *What jumps out is the evening: the busiest hours are between seven and ten.*
4. **Point out one notable detail** and say what it means: *There's a smaller peak just before lunch, and notice how sharply it drops at ten — that's when we close. So we're shutting the doors at the busiest time of day.*
:::

::: proof
Steps 1 and 2 give the listener a frame; without them, step 3 is a claim about a picture they cannot yet read. Step 3 is the reason the chart is there, and it should match the headline of the slide; step 4 connects the chart to the argument of the talk. The order also suits the eye: people look first at the title and axes and then search for the pattern, so the speaker is guiding the search rather than racing it. Describing every bar or data point is a common mistake: the chart already shows them all, and the audience needs the speaker to say which ones matter. Mayer's signalling principle applies here in speech: *what jumps out is…* and *notice…* are spoken cues to the essential part.
:::

### The language of spoken chart description

Many expressions are the same as in writing ([[essay-writing/describing-data]]); speech prefers the shorter, more personal ones and the present tense for what is on the screen.

| job | spoken expressions |
|---|---|
| introducing the chart | *This chart shows…* / *Here you can see…* / *Let's have a look at…* / *What I've got here is…* |
| explaining the axes | *Along the bottom / On the horizontal axis we have…* / *Up the side / On the vertical axis…* / *Each line is…* / *The dark bars are…* |
| directing attention | *As you can see, …* / *If you look at the left-hand side…* / *Notice that…* / *What's interesting here is…* / *What jumps out is…* |
| describing change | *It rises steadily / sharply / slightly…* / *It peaks at…* / *It levels off…* / *It drops / falls back…* / *It stays more or less flat…* |
| comparing | *…is about twice as high as…* / *…is well ahead of…* / *…is roughly the same as…* |
| interpreting | *What this tells us is…* / *So the point is…* / *This suggests that…* |

::: theorem Say numbers so they can be heard {#rule-numbers}
In speech, **round** numbers to what the listener needs (*just under a quarter*, *about two hundred and forty thousand*), give **one or two** numbers per point rather than many, and **put the key number on the slide** as well as saying it. Say percentages and changes carefully: *from twelve to nineteen per cent* is a rise of **seven percentage points**, which is an increase of more than half in the share of cyclists.
:::

::: proof
A listener can hold only a few numbers at once, and numbers are among the items second-language listeners most often mishear (*fifteen* and *fifty*, *thirteen* and *thirty*, which differ mainly in stress and the final syllable — [[listening-and-conversation/gist-and-detail#rule-numbers]]). Rounding keeps the shape of the information while removing digits the listener would forget; showing the number on the slide is the one kind of on-screen text that the redundancy principle does not argue against. The difference between percentage points and per cent is a common source of misunderstanding in both writing and speech ([[essay-writing/describing-data#rule-percentage-points]]).
:::

::: example Describing a chart in four steps {#ex-chart}
Using the library chart above, write what Ana Ribeiro might say.
::: solution
1. **What it shows.** *This is what the entry gates recorded for one week in October — how many people came into the library each hour.*
2. **How to read it.** *Time of day runs along the bottom, from eight in the morning to ten at night, when we close. Up the side is the number of people coming in.*
3. **Main trend.** *And as you can see, the evening is by far the busiest time — it climbs from about six and peaks around eight.*
4. **Notable detail and meaning.** *There's a smaller bump before lunch — that's people between morning classes. But look at the right-hand edge: at ten o'clock, when the numbers are still high, we close. So we're turning people away at one of our busiest times.*

Notice what she does not do: read out the value for every hour. She gives approximate times rather than exact counts, because the shape is the point. The headline of her slide would be the main trend: *The library is busiest in the evening — just before we close.*
:::
:::

::: quiz
A speaker says: *Bus use went from 40 per cent of journeys to 30 per cent.* Which description is accurate?
- [ ] Bus use fell by 10 per cent.
- [x] Bus use fell by 10 percentage points, which is a quarter of its earlier share.
- [ ] Bus use fell by 25 percentage points.
- [ ] Bus use fell by 30 per cent.
::: solution
The share fell from 40 to 30: a drop of 10 **percentage points**. Relative to the starting value, 10 is a quarter of 40, so it is a 25 per cent fall in the share. *Fell by 10 per cent* would wrongly suggest a fall to 36 per cent.
:::
:::

## Images, diagrams and objects

Not every visual is a chart. For a photograph, a map, a diagram or a physical object, the same principle holds — say what it is, guide the eye, and say why it matters — but the language of **place** replaces the language of trends.

- *In the top left-hand corner…* / *At the bottom of the picture…* / *In the background / foreground…*
- *On the left you can see the old building; on the right, the extension.*
- *This arrow shows where the water comes in.* / *Follow the line from here to here.*
- *This part here — the darker part — is the root.*
- For a process: *It starts here… then it goes through… and it ends up here.* (See [[essay-writing/describing-data#rule-process]].)

::: example Talking through a diagram {#ex-diagram}
Nadia Rahman is showing a cross-section of a green roof: from the top, plants, a growing layer of soil, a filter sheet, a drainage layer, a root barrier, a waterproof membrane and the roof deck. Write four or five sentences she might say, guiding the eye from top to bottom and saying why the order matters.
::: solution
*This is what a green roof looks like if you cut through it. Let's start at the top, with the plants — usually low, tough plants that don't need much water. Under them is the growing layer, a special light soil, and then this thin grey line is a filter sheet, which stops the soil washing down. Below that is the drainage layer — the part that holds some rainwater and lets the rest run off. And right at the bottom, above the roof itself, there are two layers that protect the building: a root barrier and a waterproof membrane. So the order matters: everything above the membrane is there to hold water, and everything below it is there to keep water out.*

She uses place language (*at the top*, *under them*, *below that*, *at the bottom*), points to what she names (*this thin grey line*), and ends with the point of the diagram rather than with the last layer. On the slide, each layer is labelled directly (spatial contiguity), and she might reveal the layers one at a time (segmenting).
:::
:::

## Working with slides during the talk

::: application Presenting with slides
1. **Know your slides** well enough to talk about each one without reading it. Use the presenter view, if the software has one, for your notes and the next slide.
2. **Introduce before you show** — or as you show: *Let's look at how this has changed.* Then click. An audience that has been told what is coming reads the slide faster.
3. **Give the audience a moment** after a new slide appears, especially a chart: a second or two of silence lets them look before you speak.
4. **Point and turn back**: point at the part you mean, then face the audience again before you explain it ([[presentations-and-discussion/delivery#rule-body]]).
5. **Blank the screen** when you want full attention — for a story, a discussion, the conclusion. Most slide software can do this with a single key; a plain black slide also works.
6. **Do not apologise for your slides.** If a slide needs an apology, change or remove it before the talk.
7. **Have a plan for failure.** If the projector fails, can you give the talk without slides? A one-page handout or a whiteboard sketch of the main chart is a good insurance.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Slides as reports.** In many workplaces and universities, in China and elsewhere, a slide file often doubles as a written report (汇报材料) that is circulated and read afterwards, which pushes slides towards full sentences and dense tables. When that is the expectation, make two versions: a lean one to present and a full one to send. If only one is allowed, put the detail in the speaker notes or an appendix at the end of the file.
- **Titles.** English slide titles in sentence case (*Cycling has grown fastest*) are common and easy to read; Title Case For Every Word is also used. Either is fine if you are consistent. A full-sentence headline is not too long for a slide.
- **如图所示 and *as you can see*.** The written formula *As shown in the figure* sounds stiff in a talk; *As you can see here* or *If you look at this line* is natural speech.
- **Numbers.** Chinese groups large numbers in ten-thousands (万) and hundred-millions (亿), English in thousands and millions, so 二十四万 is *two hundred and forty thousand* (BrE; AmE often omits *and*). Convert before the talk and write the English form in your notes, rather than converting while speaking ([[everyday-english/numbers-in-conversation]]).
- **Percentages.** *Per cent* is written as two words in British English and usually as one, *percent*, in American English; both are pronounced the same. The stress in *percentage* is on the second syllable: per**cen**tage.
:::

::: history
Speakers have used visual aids for as long as there have been maps and blackboards, but two inventions shaped the modern slide. William Playfair's *The Commercial and Political Atlas* (1786) introduced the line graph and the bar chart, making it possible to show change and comparison at a glance. The magic lantern, a seventeenth-century invention, became a common lecture tool in the nineteenth century; the twentieth brought photographic slides and, from the 1960s, the overhead projector, on which lecturers wrote or laid printed transparencies. Software for making and showing slides on a personal computer appeared in the late 1980s and spread rapidly in the 1990s; with it came the bullet-point slide and, soon, its critics, among them the statistician and designer Edward Tufte. Richard Mayer's *Multimedia Learning* (2001; 3rd edition, 2020) gave the debate an experimental basis.
:::

::: summary
- A visual aid supports the speaker; slides show **one main idea**, and the speaker explains ([[#def-visual-aid]], [[#rule-slides]]).
- Mayer's principles: words with pictures; **cut** decoration (coherence); **highlight** what matters (signalling); **don't print what you say** (redundancy); label **on** the picture (spatial contiguity); **build** complex slides in steps ([[#def-mayer]], [[#rule-mayer]]).

- Describe a chart in four steps: what it shows, how to read it, the main trend, one notable detail and its meaning ([[#rule-chart]]).

::: exercise Analyse a slide outline {level=3}
Here is the slide outline of a twelve-minute talk on a proposed four-day school week. For each slide, say what you would keep, cut, combine or redesign, and why.

1. *Title slide with the talk title, the speaker's name and a decorative border of clocks.*
2. *Agenda: Introduction, Background, Literature review, Methodology, Results, Discussion, Conclusion, References.*
3. *Twelve bullet points summarising five studies.*
4. *A table of attendance figures for twenty schools over six years, in small type.*
5. *A line chart of average test scores for schools with four- and five-day weeks, with a legend in the corner.*
6. *Conclusion: "Thank you for listening! Any questions?"*
::: hint
Think about the audience's few seconds per slide, the talk's central idea, and each of Mayer's principles.
:::
::: solution
1. **Keep, simplify**: title and name, without the decorative border (coherence). A title that states the topic or the question (*Would a four-day week help our schools?*) is better than a generic one.
2. **Redesign**: this is the structure of a written paper, not a twelve-minute talk. Replace with a preview of two to four main points in the talk's own terms — *attendance, results, family costs* — and say it aloud.
3. **Cut or split**: twelve bullets cannot be read and heard at once (redundancy). Choose the one or two findings that matter and show them as a chart or one large number per slide, with the sources in small type.
4. **Redesign**: show only the comparison you will talk about — perhaps average attendance before and after the change — as a simple chart; put the full table in a handout (readability, coherence).
5. **Keep, improve**: label the two lines directly instead of using a legend (spatial contiguity), make the comparison of interest stand out (signalling), and give the slide a headline that states the finding.
6. **Redesign**: end with the central idea and the two or three main points, as a summary slide; *Thank you* and the invitation to ask questions are said aloud. A slide with the main chart or the key message can stay on screen during the questions.
:::
:::

::: exercise Speaking: a one-chart talk {level=3}
Find or draw a simple chart from your own field or everyday life (your monthly spending, the temperature in your city over a year, steps walked per day). Give a ninety-second talk about it: an assertion headline, the four steps of [[#rule-chart]], and a final sentence that says what the chart means for your audience. Record it and check that you did not read out every value.
::: solution
Answers vary. Check the recording for: a clear statement of what the chart shows and its source; a short explanation of the axes and colours; one main trend stated in the words of the headline; one notable detail with its meaning; rounded numbers, no more than three or four; place and change language (*on the left*, *it climbs steadily*, *it peaks*); and a pause after the chart first appears.
:::
:::
