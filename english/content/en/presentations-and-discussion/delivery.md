Chen Yuxuan, a second-year engineering student, has prepared a ten-minute talk on how her city treats its drinking water. Her slides are clear, her plan follows [[presentations-and-discussion/planning-a-talk]] and her signposts follow [[presentations-and-discussion/signposting-a-talk]]. At the first rehearsal she reads her script from the laptop screen. It takes seven minutes instead of ten. Her voice stays on almost one pitch, the pauses fall only at full stops, and she looks up twice. When she finishes, her friend Daniel Osei, who is listening, says: *It was all correct — but I lost you after the second slide.*

At the second rehearsal, a week later, she speaks from six cards of keywords. The talk takes nine and a half minutes. She hesitates a few times and once says *sorry — the second stage, I mean*. But she pauses before each new section, her voice rises on the words that matter, and she looks at Daniel while she talks. This time he can repeat her three main points afterwards.

Nothing in the content changed. What changed is **delivery**: how the words reach the listener through the voice, the eyes and the body. This chapter covers speaking from notes, pace, pausing, stress and pitch, eye contact and gesture, and the nervousness that affects all of them. It does not teach intonation again — the tones, the nucleus and pitch range are in [[connected-speech/intonation-basics]], [[connected-speech/sentence-stress]] and [[connected-speech/intonation-meaning]] — but applies them to ten minutes of speech in front of an audience.

## Four ways to deliver a talk

::: definition Methods of delivery {#def-methods}
Public-speaking textbooks usually distinguish four **methods of delivery** (Lucas and Stob, *The Art of Public Speaking*, 13th edition, 2020):

1. **Reading from a manuscript** — the whole text is written out and read aloud.
2. **Reciting from memory** — the whole text is written out and learned by heart.
3. **Speaking impromptu** — speaking with little or no preparation, as when you are asked for an opinion in a meeting.
4. **Speaking extemporaneously** — the talk is carefully prepared and rehearsed, but delivered from brief notes, so that the exact sentences are made at the moment of speaking.
:::

A manuscript is right when every word matters legally, as in an official statement; memorising suits a very short, set text such as a toast; impromptu speaking is the daily business of seminars and meetings. For a presentation, nearly all advice points the same way.

::: theorem Speak from notes, not from a script {#rule-notes}
For a presentation of more than a minute or two, prepare thoroughly and then **speak extemporaneously from notes**: keywords for each point, the transitions, every number and name written exactly, and the first and last sentences in full. Rehearse aloud from the notes, not from the draft, until the order of ideas is secure — but do not try to fix the wording.
:::

::: proof
A written script contains written grammar — long noun phrases, embedded clauses, passives — which is harder to follow by ear than the shorter, looser clauses of speech (Biber et al., *Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English*, 1999). Reading aloud also tends to put pauses at punctuation rather than at divisions of meaning, to narrow the pitch range, and to keep the eyes on the page. A memorised talk avoids the page, but one forgotten sentence leaves the speaker with no route to the next. Speaking from notes makes the speaker build each sentence as it is needed, which produces spoken grammar and natural pausing almost automatically. The price — some hesitation, the occasional self-correction — is one that audiences readily accept.
:::

::: warning Memorising every word in a second language
✗ Writing a full script, memorising it word for word, and repeating it until it is fluent.
✓ Memorising the opening and the closing, learning the order of the points and the key phrases, and speaking the rest from keywords.

Learners often choose full memorisation because it feels safe: the grammar has been checked and nothing can go wrong. In practice, one forgotten word can stop the whole talk, and the recited delivery — fast, even, looking at the ceiling — is easy for an audience to recognise. The safety you want comes from knowing the **content** well, not from knowing the **sentences**.
:::

## Pace

How fast should you speak? There is no correct number, but there is useful evidence about what is normal. Tauroza and Allison (1990, *Applied Linguistics* 11:1) measured the speech rate of British English in several kinds of recorded speech and found that rates differed by genre: conversation was the fastest of their categories, at around 210 words per minute on average, radio monologues around 160, and lectures given to non-native-speaker audiences around 140. Most presentation textbooks suggest something in the region of 120–160 words per minute for a talk, slower for an audience of second-language listeners or for difficult content. These are averages over whole talks; within a talk, the rate should change.

::: theorem Vary the pace with the content {#rule-pace}
Speak at a rate your particular audience can follow, and **vary it**:

1. **Slow down** for the central idea, for transitions, for numbers, names and technical terms, and for anything the listener must remember.
2. **Speed up a little** for familiar material, for asides, and for examples that illustrate a point already made.
3. **Plan the length in words**, not in slides: at about 130 words per minute, a ten-minute talk contains roughly 1,300 words of content.
:::

::: proof
A number, a name or a new term takes more effort to decode and store than a familiar phrase, so slowing down there gives the listener time to catch up, while speeding up through predictable material keeps the talk lively. Nervous speakers often speed up evenly, which removes this signal. Planning in words is arithmetic: time = words ÷ rate. Chen Yuxuan's script had about 900 words; at her nervous rate of roughly 130 words per minute it took just under seven minutes.
:::

::: example Timing a talk {#ex-timing}
Kwame Mensah is asked to give an eight-minute update. His first draft has 1,600 words. In rehearsal, his natural rate is about 140 words per minute. What should he do?
::: solution
1. **How long would the draft take?** 1,600 ÷ 140 ≈ 11.4 minutes — more than three minutes over.
2. **How many words fit?** 8 × 140 = 1,120 words. To leave a little time for pauses and a slower start (nerves usually make the first minute faster, but pauses and slides add time), he should aim for about 1,000–1,100 words.
3. **What should he cut?** About a third of the content. Following [[presentations-and-discussion/planning-a-talk#rule-central-idea]], he keeps everything that serves the central idea and cuts or moves the rest to a handout or to the question time.
4. **What should he not do?** Plan to speak faster on the day: at 200 words per minute the audience would hear a stream of facts and remember few.
:::
:::

## Pausing

::: theorem Pause for meaning {#rule-pauses}
In a talk:

1. Divide speech into **tone units** — chunks of a few words, each with one nucleus ([[connected-speech/sentence-stress#def-nucleus]]) — and pause briefly at the end of each **unit of meaning**, not only at full stops.
2. Make the **longest pauses** at the boundaries of sections, before macro-signposts ([[presentations-and-discussion/signposting-a-talk#rule-pause-signpost]]).
3. **Pause before** a key word or number to create anticipation, and **after** an important point to let it be absorbed.
4. Replace fillers — *um*, *er*, *so*, *OK* — with silent pauses where you can. A silent pause of a second or two sounds deliberate; a filled one sounds like searching.
:::

::: proof
Pauses mark the structure of the message, give the listener processing time — which matters even more in a second language — and give the speaker time to breathe and plan. A second of silence feels long to the speaker and quite ordinary to the audience. Fillers are not mistakes: every fluent speaker uses some, and Clark and Fox Tree (2002, *Cognition* 84:1) argue that *uh* and *um* signal to the listener that a delay is coming. In a prepared talk, though, a filler before every sentence becomes a noise, while a silent pause in the same place sounds like thought.
:::

Here is part of Chen Yuxuan's talk as she read it from the script, with her pauses marked ‖:

*The water from the river is first passed through screens which remove large objects such as branches and plastic ‖ after which it enters the settling tanks where a chemical called a coagulant is added ‖ which causes small particles to stick together and sink to the bottom.*

And as she said it from notes a week later:

*So the water comes in from the river ‖ and the first thing that happens ‖ is screening. ‖ Big screens — they take out branches, ‖ plastic, ‖ anything large. ‖‖ Then it goes into the settling tanks. ‖ Here we add a chemical ‖ called a ↘**co**agulant ‖ — I'll put that word on the slide — ‖ and what it does is make the tiny particles ↘**stick** together, ‖ so they get heavy ‖ and ↘**sink**.*

The second version is longer and far easier to follow: each tone unit carries one piece of information, the technical term comes slowly with a promise of help, and the key actions (*stick*, *sink*) each carry a nucleus.

::: example Marking a passage for delivery {#ex-mark}
Mark tone-unit boundaries (|), longer pauses (‖) and the nucleus of each unit in this sentence from a talk by Ana Ribeiro on library opening hours. Assume the audience already knows that the library opens at nine.

*We looked at when students actually use the library and we found that the busiest time is not the morning but the evening between seven and ten which is exactly when we close.*
::: solution
*We ˈlooked at when ˈstudents ˈactually ↘**use** the library | and we ↘**found** ‖ that the ˈbusiest time | is ↘↗**not** the morning | but the ↘**eve**ning — | between ↘**sev**en and ↘**ten** ‖ — which is ↘**ex**actly | when we ↘**close**.*

Reasoning, step by step:

1. **Chunks.** The sentence is too long for one breath; it divides naturally into what they did, what they found, the contrast, the time and the punchline.
2. **Nuclei on new information.** *The morning* is known (the library opens then), so in *not the morning* the nucleus falls on *not*, with a fall-rise that sets up the contrast ([[connected-speech/intonation-meaning#rule-attitude]]); *evening* carries the new information with a fall.
3. **Numbers.** *Seven* and *ten* each get their own accent and a short pause, so that the listener catches both.
4. **The pause before the point.** The longest pause comes before *which is exactly when we close*, so that the surprise lands. The nucleus is on *exactly*, which carries the speaker's emphasis, and a second fall on *close*.

Other markings are possible; what matters is that each unit has one main idea and that the new information carries the nucleus ([[connected-speech/sentence-stress#rule-focus]]).
:::
:::

::: quiz
Where is a pause most useful in this sentence from a talk? *The answer, which surprised all of us, was twelve.*
- [ ] Between *The* and *answer*.
- [ ] After *was*, and nowhere else.
- [x] After *answer* and after *us*, with a short pause after *was* before the number.

::: solution
The commas mark a parenthesis (*which surprised all of us*), which is usually said in its own tone unit, slightly lower and faster ([[connected-speech/intonation-meaning#rule-range]]). A short pause after *was* sets up the number, which is the new information and carries the nucleus: *was ‖ ↘**twelve**.* Pausing between *The* and *answer* splits a noun phrase and sounds hesitant.
:::

## Stress and pitch over a whole talk

The rules of [[connected-speech/sentence-stress]] apply in a talk exactly as in conversation. What is harder is keeping them up: over ten minutes the voice settles into a habit, usually a narrow range, and a speaker concentrating on grammar has little attention left for it.

::: theorem Keep the pitch range alive {#rule-range-talk}
In a talk, use a **wider** pitch range than feels natural to you, especially on the nuclei of key words, on the central idea, and at the start of new sections; and let the pitch **fall low** at the end of points and summaries. A range that feels exaggerated to the speaker usually sounds normal to the audience.
:::

::: proof
[[connected-speech/intonation-meaning#rule-range]] explains that English listeners hear a narrow range as lack of involvement — boredom, routine, even unfriendliness — whatever the words say. In a talk, a narrow range has a second cost: it removes the pitch peaks that tell listeners which words are important and the pitch resets that mark new sections ([[presentations-and-discussion/signposting-a-talk#rule-pause-signpost]]). Everything sounds equally important, so nothing does. Speakers are poor judges of their own range while speaking; the reliable check is a recording.
:::

In practice, underline in your notes the few words in each section that must stand out — the central idea, key numbers, contrast words — and in rehearsal let your voice go higher and fall further on them than anywhere else.

## Clarity: articulation, volume and natural speech

- **Volume.** Speak to the back row; ask at the start: *Can everyone at the back hear me?* With a microphone, use your normal volume and keep the distance to it constant.
- **Word endings.** Final consonants carry grammar — plural *-s*, past *-ed* — and are the first sounds lost in a big room. Speakers of Mandarin, whose syllables end only in a vowel, *-n*, *-ng* or *-r*, may need to give them a little more weight.
- **Weak forms and contractions.** *We'll*, *it's*, *that's* and the weak forms of *to*, *of* and *and* ([[connected-speech/weak-forms]]) belong in a talk. To slow down, add pauses between tone units; do not stress every word. ✗ *ˈWe ˈwill ˈnow ˈlook ˈat ˈthe re↘**sults**.* ✓ *We'll ˈnow ˈlook at the re↘**sults**.* Stressing the small words removes the rhythm that helps listeners find the content words ([[connected-speech/rhythm]]).

## Eye contact, posture and gesture

A presentation is seen as well as heard. The listener's eyes go to the speaker's face, hands and position, and these can either support the words or distract from them.

::: theorem Use the body to support the words {#rule-body}
1. **Eye contact.** Look at the audience for most of the talk, not at the screen, the notes or the floor. Move your gaze slowly around the room, holding it on one person or one part of the room for about the length of a sentence. Look down at your notes during pauses, not while you are speaking.
2. **Posture.** Stand still and balanced, facing the audience, with weight on both feet. Move on purpose — towards the screen to point at something, back to the centre for the next section — and not out of nervousness.
3. **Gesture.** Let your hands move as they do in conversation: to show size, sequence (*first*, *second* — counting on the fingers), contrast (*on the one hand… on the other*) or direction. Gestures should start from the content, not be added from a list.
4. **Face.** Your face should match your words: if you say you are pleased to be here, look it.
:::

::: proof
Eye contact is a social signal of engagement — in most English-speaking settings a speaker who never looks up seems unsure or uninterested — and a source of information: the speaker can see who looks confused and when to slow down. Gestures that grow out of the content show structure visually: a speaker who counts *first, second, third* on her fingers is signposting twice. Gestures that come from nerves — touching the face, clicking a pen, rocking — draw the eye to something with no meaning. Expectations about eye contact and gesture vary across cultures and between individuals; this rule describes common conventions in English-speaking presentations, not a rule about people.
:::

::: warning The 7–38–55 myth
✗ *Research shows that only 7% of communication is words; 38% is tone of voice and 55% is body language.*
✓ *In two small experiments on how listeners judge a speaker's feelings, facial expression and tone influenced the judgements more than the meaning of single words.*

The figures come from two 1967 studies by Albert Mehrabian and colleagues (Mehrabian and Wiener, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 6:1; Mehrabian and Ferris, *Journal of Consulting Psychology* 31:3). Listeners heard **single words** such as *dear* or *terrible* spoken in a liking, neutral or disliking tone, in one study with photographs of faces, and judged the speaker's **attitude**. Mehrabian combined the results into a formula for inferring **liking** when words and tone conflict, and in *Silent Messages* (1971) he said it applies only to feelings and attitudes. If words carried 7% of the meaning of a talk, you could follow a lecture in a language you do not know. What the studies support is narrower: when your words and voice disagree, listeners tend to believe your voice about how you feel.
:::

## Nervousness

Almost everyone feels nervous before a talk, experienced speakers included.

::: definition Communication apprehension {#def-apprehension}
**Communication apprehension** is "an individual's level of fear or anxiety associated with either real or anticipated communication with another person or persons" (McCroskey 1977, *Human Communication Research* 4:1). Fear of public speaking is one kind of it. James McCroskey's questionnaires, the best known being the Personal Report of Communication Apprehension (PRCA-24, 1982), measure it separately for public speaking, meetings, group discussions and conversations.
:::

The physical symptoms — a fast heartbeat, a dry mouth, shaky hands, a voice higher or faster than usual — are the body's normal response to a situation it treats as important. They are strongest just before and in the first minute of a talk and usually fall once the speaker is under way, which is one reason why the opening is worth preparing in full ([[presentations-and-discussion/planning-a-talk#rule-opening]]).

::: theorem Techniques with evidence behind them {#rule-nerves}
These techniques for managing nervousness have support in published research:

1. **Prepare and rehearse aloud.** Knowing the content and having spoken it several times, preferably in front of one or two people, reduces the uncertainty that feeds anxiety.
2. **Gradual exposure.** Speaking often, starting with small, friendly audiences and moving to larger ones, reduces fear over time. Exposure-based methods, including systematic desensitisation, are among the treatments for public-speaking anxiety with the most research behind them (Bodie 2010, *Communication Education* 59:1, reviews them).
3. **Reappraise the arousal.** Telling yourself *I'm excited* rather than *I must calm down* can improve performance. In experiments by Alison Wood Brooks (2014, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 143:3), people who said *I am excited* before giving a short public speech were rated as more persuasive, competent and confident than those who said *I am calm*.
4. **Remember the illusion of transparency.** Speakers believe that their nervousness is much more visible than it is. In a study by Kenneth Savitsky and Thomas Gilovich (2003, *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 39:6), speakers who were told about this illusion before speaking reported feeling less anxious, and observers rated their speeches as better, than speakers who were not told.
:::

::: proof
The common thread is that anxiety before a talk is not only a feeling but an interpretation. A racing heart is the same physical state in excitement and in fear; Brooks's result suggests that labelling it as excitement points attention towards the opportunity rather than the threat, whereas trying to calm down fights a state that is hard to reverse quickly. The illusion of transparency works similarly: much of the fear in a talk is fear of being seen to be afraid, and knowing that the audience cannot see most of what you feel removes part of that fear. Exposure works by a different route: repeated experience of speaking without disaster weakens the expectation of disaster. None of these techniques removes nervousness entirely, and none needs to: moderate arousal gives a talk energy. For severe anxiety, counselling services offer structured help, and there is nothing unusual about using it.
:::

::: warning Techniques that are not what they seem
✗ *Stand in a "power pose" for two minutes before a talk to raise your testosterone and lower your stress hormones.*

A 2010 study reported such hormonal effects, but a larger replication (Ranehill et al. 2015, *Psychological Science* 26:5) did not find them, and one of the original authors, Dana Carney, said publicly in 2016 that she no longer believed the effects were real. Standing in a relaxed, upright way may still help you feel more confident, and it does no harm; but it is not a proven way to change your body chemistry.

:::

::: example Planning for nerves {#ex-nerves}
Leila Haddad has to give a five-minute talk to her department in two weeks. She says: *I know the topic well, but when I start speaking my mind goes blank and I speak too fast.* Make her a two-week plan using [[#rule-nerves]].
::: solution
- **Week 1.** Prepare speaking notes ([[#rule-notes]]), with the first and last sentences in full and memorised, since blank moments are likeliest at the start. Rehearse aloud alone three times with a timer, aiming at about 650–700 words; record one rehearsal and listen for pace.
- **Week 2.** Rehearse in front of one friend, then two or three people (gradual exposure). Mark a pause on the cards at each section boundary — planned moments to slow down. Remind herself that the audience sees much less of her nerves than she feels.
- **On the day.** Arrive early and check the equipment. Instead of *I must calm down*, tell herself *I'm excited about this*. Look at a friendly face for the first sentence, and take a full pause after it.

If her mind does go blank, the notes are there for exactly that moment: pause, look down, find the keyword, and continue. A pause of three seconds while a speaker checks her notes is barely noticed by an audience.
:::
:::

## Rehearsal and the day of the talk

::: application A rehearsal routine
1. **Rehearse aloud** from your notes, standing up, with a timer, at least twice. Silent reading through the slides is not a rehearsal: it is always faster than speech and it does not train the voice.
2. **Record one rehearsal** and listen to it for pace, pauses and pitch range.
3. **Rehearse the transitions** separately: the moments between sections are where speakers lose their way.
4. **Rehearse in front of someone**, and ask them two questions afterwards: *What were my main points?* and *Where did you lose track?*
5. **Check the room**: where you will stand, how the slides advance, whether you need a microphone, where the clock is.
6. **For an online talk**, put your notes near the camera and plan moments to stop for questions, because you will not see faces ([[listening-and-conversation/calls-online-meetings]]).
:::

::: example Diagnosing a recorded rehearsal {#ex-diagnose}
Matteo Russo records a rehearsal of a six-minute talk and notes: (a) it took four and a half minutes; (b) he said *so* at the start of almost every sentence; (c) his voice rose at the end of most statements; (d) he looked at the screen most of the time. Diagnose each problem and suggest a fix.
::: solution
(a) **Too fast**, probably from nerves. If his notes hold enough material for six minutes at about 130 words per minute, the fix is pauses at every section boundary and after key numbers, not extra content.

(b) **A filler habit.** *So* is a natural micro-signpost ([[presentations-and-discussion/signposting-a-talk#rule-macro]]), but not before every sentence; replace most with a silent pause.

(c) **Rising statements** can sound unsure or as if asking for approval, and they remove the sense of completion that falls give ([[connected-speech/intonation-basics]]). Some varieties use rising statements normally, but in a presentation, practise the main points and the summary with clear falls.

(d) **Talking to the screen.** He should glance at the slide, point, and turn back to the audience before speaking ([[#rule-body]]).
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Reading aloud and range.** A narrow pitch range in a Chinese speaker's English is usually not a copy of Mandarin, whose four tones use the full range of the voice. More likely causes are reading from a script, concentration on accuracy, nerves, and the fact that in Mandarin much of the pitch is already doing the work of lexical tone ([[connected-speech/tones-and-intonation]]). Speaking from notes and recording yourself help more than any rule.
- **Rhythm.** Mandarin syllables are more even in length than English ones, and transferring that evenness gives a word-by-word delivery in which every word seems stressed. Contracting the small words and lengthening the stressed syllables restores the English rhythm ([[connected-speech/rhythm]]).
- **脱稿 and 背稿.** Speaking without a script (脱稿) is valued in Chinese public speaking too, but in school speech competitions it often means a memorised text (背稿). In English presentations, "without notes" usually means extemporaneous speech from a well-learned plan, not recitation; a few cards are entirely normal.
- **Eye contact.** Some Chinese speakers, like speakers from many backgrounds, have been taught that looking steadily at a teacher or a senior person is impolite. In English-speaking presentations, looking at the audience — senior members included — is expected and is read as confidence and respect. You can look at the audience in general, moving between faces, without staring at any one person.
:::

::: history
Delivery was the fifth part of classical rhetoric (*actio*); Quintilian discussed voice and gesture in Book 11 of his *Institutio Oratoria* (about 95 CE). In the eighteenth century the **elocution movement** made it a school subject: Thomas Sheridan's *A Course of Lectures on Elocution* (1762) and John Walker's *Elements of Elocution* (1781) gave rules for pauses, emphasis and tones, and later manuals prescribed gestures for each emotion. Early twentieth-century American teachers reacted against this artificial style; James Winans's *Public Speaking* (1915) argued that a good speech has the qualities of lively conversation, the "conversational quality" that modern textbooks still teach.
:::

## Where this leads

Delivery continues into the question time, when the talk becomes a conversation: [[presentations-and-discussion/handling-questions]]. Using slides without turning your back on the audience is part of [[presentations-and-discussion/visual-aids]]. The voice skills behind this chapter — the nucleus, tones and pitch range — are in [[connected-speech/sentence-stress]], [[connected-speech/intonation-basics]] and [[connected-speech/intonation-meaning]], and the same delivery choices matter in seminar contributions ([[presentations-and-discussion/seminars-discussion]]) and in interviews ([[presentations-and-discussion/interviews]]).

::: summary
- Of the four methods of delivery, **speaking from notes** (extemporaneously) suits presentations best; memorise only the opening and the closing ([[#def-methods]], [[#rule-notes]]).
- Plan the length in words: time = words ÷ rate. Around 120–160 words per minute is common in talks; **vary the pace**, slowing for key points, numbers and transitions ([[#rule-pace]]).
- **Pause for meaning** at the ends of tone units, longest at section boundaries; prefer silent pauses to fillers ([[#rule-pauses]]).
- Put the nucleus on the new information, and keep the **pitch range** wide on the words that matter; record yourself to check ([[#rule-range-talk]]).
- Use contractions and weak forms; stressing every word makes speech harder to follow.
- Look at the audience, stand still, and let gestures come from the content ([[#rule-body]]); the 7–38–55 figures apply only to judging feelings from single words.
- Nervousness is normal; rehearsal, gradual exposure, reappraising arousal as excitement and knowing the illusion of transparency all have research support ([[#rule-nerves]]).
:::

A model answer: *Most ˈpeople ↘**think** | ˈcycling to work is ↘**dan**gerous. ‖ In ↘**fact**, | in our ˈcity last ↘**year**, | more ˈpeople were ˈinjured ↘**walk**ing | than ↘**cyc**ling. ‖‖ The ˈproblem ↘↗**is**n't the bike. ‖ It's the ↘**junc**tions.*

The widest range belongs on *walking* (the surprise) and *junctions* (the main point). In *The problem isn't the bike*, the bike is already known, so the nucleus goes on *isn't*, with a fall-rise that sets up the contrast. The longest pause comes before *The problem*, the start of the key claim. Other markings are acceptable if each unit has one nucleus on new or contrastive information.
:::

::: exercise Script to notes {level=2}
Reduce this script extract to speaking notes ([[#rule-notes]]) of no more than about twenty-five words, keeping every number exactly.

*The second thing I want to talk about is the cost. The new system cost about 40,000 euros to install, which sounds like a lot. However, it has reduced our energy bills by roughly 8,000 euros a year, so in five years it will have paid for itself, and after that it will save money every year.*
::: solution
A model answer:

> **2. COST** — install ≈ €40,000 ("sounds a lot")
> saves ≈ €8,000 / year
> → pays for itself in 5 yrs → then pure saving
> → *That brings me to…*

The notes keep the order, both numbers exactly, the turn of the argument (*sounds a lot* → *saves*) and a reminder of the transition. The sentences are left to be made on the day.

::: exercise Fillers and pauses {level=2}
Rewrite this transcript of a speaker's transition as you would say it, replacing fillers with pauses (‖) and marking the nucleus of each tone unit: *Um, so, OK, so that's, er, the first reason. Um, the second reason is, er, cost.*
::: solution
*So that's the ↘**first** reason. ‖‖ The ↘**sec**ond reason ‖ is ↘**cost**.* — one *So* is kept because it closes the section; the other fillers become a long pause before the new section and a short one before the new label, which carries the nucleus. In *the first reason*, the nucleus is on *first*, since *reason* is given; in the second unit, on *second*.

::: exercise Improve a rehearsal {level=3}
Here is a description of Park Seo-yeon's rehearsal of an eight-minute talk on noise in student housing: *She read from a printed script, holding it in both hands. She finished in five minutes. Her voice was clear but stayed on one pitch. She looked up at the end of each paragraph and said "sorry" twice when she lost her place. At the start, she said: "Sorry, I am very nervous and my English is not good."* Write her a short, specific feedback note with at least five suggestions, each linked to a point in this chapter.
::: hint
Separate method, pace, pausing, pitch, body and nerves. Begin with something she does well.
:::
::: solution
A model answer:

*Your voice is clear and easy to hear — a real strength. Some suggestions:*

1. *Turn the script into six or seven keyword cards, with the opening and closing in full ([[#rule-notes]]); you'll look up more and won't lose your place.*
2. *You were three minutes short. Add pauses, not content: a full second at each new section and after key figures ([[#rule-pauses]]).*
3. *Underline the key words in each section and let your voice rise on them ([[#rule-range-talk]]).*
4. *Hold the cards in one hand, gesture with the other, and look at one person per sentence ([[#rule-body]]).*
5. *Drop the opening apology: the audience sees less of your nerves than you feel ([[#rule-nerves]]), and your English is clear. If you lose your place, pause and look at the card — no need for "sorry".*
:::

::: exercise Speaking: two deliveries {level=3}
Choose a paragraph of about 120 words from any talk you have prepared (or from Chen Yuxuan's water-treatment passage above). Record yourself twice: first reading it as written, then speaking it from five keywords. Compare the two recordings for (a) total time, (b) the number and position of pauses, (c) pitch range on the key words, and (d) how easy it is to follow.
::: solution
Answers vary. Most speakers find that the version from keywords is slower, has more and better-placed pauses (at units of meaning rather than at punctuation), a wider range on the key words, and more spoken grammar — shorter clauses, *I'll*, *we'll*, *what this means is…*. It may also contain a few hesitations. If the spoken version is no better than the read one, practise the keywords version two or three more times: the benefit usually appears once the order of ideas is secure.
:::
