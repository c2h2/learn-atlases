Liu Yang has just finished a twelve-minute talk to the student union committee proposing that the campus canteen should offer smaller, cheaper portions to reduce food waste. The talk went well. Then the chair says: *Thank you, Liu Yang. We have about ten minutes for questions.* A committee member, Joseph Mwangi, puts up his hand:

**Joseph:** *Thanks — really interesting. I suppose my question is about cost. You said smaller portions would be cheaper, but the canteen still has the same staff and the same kitchen, so wouldn't the price per gram actually go up? And has the canteen manager agreed to any of this?*

**Liu Yang:** *Yes, this is a very good question. Thank you. So, as I mentioned, food waste is a big problem in many universities, and there are many reasons for it. In our survey, we asked 300 students, and we found that most of them… Er… sorry, what was the second part?*

Liu Yang knows the answer to both parts: the canteen's own figures show that ingredient costs, not staff, are most of the price of a meal, and the manager has agreed to a one-month trial. But she started with the background, lost track of the question, and never said either thing. Joseph heard a speaker who did not seem to know.

Most of what a talk achieves can be lost or gained in the questions. Listeners often remember the question time better than the talk, because it is unscripted and because it is where the speaker's real understanding shows. This chapter is about handling it: preparing for questions, inviting them, making sure you have understood, answering clearly, admitting what you do not know, and dealing with questions that are long, hostile or off the point. It builds on the conversational repair of [[listening-and-conversation/clarification]] and the delivery of [[presentations-and-discussion/delivery]].

## Preparing for questions

::: definition Question-and-answer session {#def-qa}
The **question-and-answer session** (**Q&A**, or **question time**) is the part of a talk, usually at the end, in which the audience asks the speaker questions. It may be run by the speaker or by a **chair** (also *moderator* or *host*), who invites questioners, keeps time and closes the session. In some settings — seminars, internal meetings, thesis defences — questions may also come during the talk.
:::

The questions are unscripted, but they are not unpredictable. Most questions after a talk ask for one of a few things: more detail on a point you mentioned briefly, the source or reliability of a number, how your idea would work in practice, what it would cost, what you think of an alternative, or what you would do next.

::: theorem Prepare for the questions you can predict {#rule-prepare}
Before the talk:

1. **List the five to ten questions** you would ask if you were in the audience — especially the hardest ones — and prepare a short answer to each.
2. **Keep the material you cut** ([[presentations-and-discussion/planning-a-talk]]): it is often exactly what the questions ask for.
3. **Prepare backup slides** after the final slide — a detailed table, a method, a second chart — which you show only if asked.
4. **Know the limits** of your evidence and say them in the talk, where they belong, rather than waiting to be caught.
5. **Agree the format** with the chair: when questions come, how long you have, whether online questions will be read out.
:::

::: proof
Anticipating questions changes them from threats into opportunities. An answer prepared in advance can be short and confident, and a backup slide shows that the speaker has gone deeper than the talk itself. Stating the limits of the evidence during the talk removes the most damaging kind of question — the one that suggests the speaker has hidden something — and it tells the audience that the speaker can judge her own work, which is part of credibility. Rehearsing aloud with a friend who is asked to play the most sceptical member of the audience is one of the most efficient ways to prepare.
:::

## Inviting questions

At the end of the talk, after the summary ([[presentations-and-discussion/planning-a-talk#rule-ending]]), the speaker hands over:

- *Thank you. I'd be happy to take any questions.*
- *That's all from me. Are there any questions?*
- *I'll stop there. I'd welcome any questions or comments.* (more formal)
- *So — what questions do you have?* (more inviting than *Any questions?*, because it assumes there will be some)

Then **wait**. Five seconds of silence feels long to a speaker and quite normal to an audience, which is deciding whether to speak. If nothing comes, a speaker can prime the room with a question of her own:

- *One question I'm often asked is whether this would work in smaller towns. The short answer is…*
- *While you're thinking — one thing I didn't have time to say is…*

With a chair, the chair does this work: *Who'd like to start? … Yes, the person at the back.* Online, the speaker or chair can invite questions in the chat and read them out: *We've got a question in the chat from Priya, who asks…* ([[listening-and-conversation/calls-online-meetings]]).

## Listening and making sure you have understood

::: theorem Listen to the end, then check {#rule-listen}
1. **Listen to the whole question** without interrupting or starting to answer in your head. Look at the questioner; a brief note helps with long or multi-part questions.
2. **If you are not sure what is being asked, check** before answering: paraphrase it (*So you're asking whether…?*), ask about a specific part (*When you say "cost", do you mean for students or for the canteen?*), or ask for repetition (*Sorry, could you repeat the last part?*).
3. **In a large room, repeat or summarise the question** for the audience, who may not have heard it — and for any recording.
4. **For a multi-part question**, say how many parts you heard and answer them in order: *There are two questions there — the cost and the manager. Let me take the cost first.*
:::

::: proof
Most bad answers are answers to the wrong question. A speaker who starts answering before the question ends usually answers the question she expected, not the one asked, and in a second language the risk is higher, because the end of a question often contains its point (*…so wouldn't the price per gram actually go up?*). Checking costs a few seconds and shows the questioner that you take the question seriously; it also gives you time to think. Repeating the question for the room is ordinary courtesy in large rooms, where people at the front cannot hear people at the back. Naming the parts of a multi-part question protects against Liu Yang's problem — losing the second part while answering the first — and lets the questioner remind you if you have missed one. The language of checking is the conversational repair of [[listening-and-conversation/clarification#rule-specific]], used in public.
:::

::: example Clarifying an unclear question {#ex-clarify}
After a talk on a new library booking system, a member of the audience says: *Yes, so, I was wondering about the, er, the thing with the rooms, because last year we had that problem with — you know, when the exams were on, and people were booking and not coming, so is it going to be the same, or…?* What should the speaker, Elena Petrova, say before answering?
::: solution
1. **Find the likely question.** The questioner seems to be asking whether the new system will stop people booking rooms during exams and not using them ("no-shows").
2. **Check it with a paraphrase**: *So, if I've understood you — you're asking whether the new system will stop people booking rooms in the exam period and then not turning up?*
3. **Wait for confirmation.** If the questioner says *Yes, exactly*, answer. If they add *Well, and also the time limit*, Elena now knows there is a second part: *OK — so no-shows and the time limit. Let me take no-shows first.*

The paraphrase also helps the rest of the audience, who heard a hesitant question and now hear it clearly stated.
:::
:::

## Answering

::: theorem Answer first, then explain {#rule-answer-first}
Give the **direct answer** to the question in the first sentence — *yes*, *no*, a number, a name, *it depends on…*, *I don't know* — and then the reason, evidence or example. Keep the answer short: often thirty seconds to a minute is enough. End by checking briefly, if it seems useful: *Does that answer your question?*
:::

::: proof
A questioner listens for the answer and treats everything before it as delay. If the answer comes first, the reasons that follow are heard as support; if the background comes first, it is heard as avoidance — even when, as with Liu Yang, the speaker is simply building up to the point. The same preference for the main point first, which [[presentations-and-discussion/planning-a-talk]] described for openings to senior audiences, is even stronger in answers, because the question has just told the speaker exactly what the listener wants. Short answers leave time for more questions and more questioners, and they let the questioner ask a follow-up if they want more. The check at the end hands the turn back politely ([[listening-and-conversation/turn-taking]]); it can be omitted if the questioner is obviously satisfied.
:::

::: example Rebuilding Liu Yang's answer {#ex-rebuild}
Rewrite Liu Yang's answer to Joseph's two-part question using [[#rule-listen]] and [[#rule-answer-first]]. Use the facts that she knew: ingredients are most of the cost of a meal (from the canteen's own figures), and the manager has agreed to a one-month trial.
::: solution
*Thanks, Joseph — two questions there: the cost, and whether the manager's on board.*

*On cost: no, we don't think the price per gram would go up much, because staff and the kitchen aren't most of the price of a meal — ingredients are. That's from the canteen's own figures, and I've got the breakdown on a backup slide if anyone wants to see it. So a smaller portion really does cost less to make.*

*And yes, the manager has agreed to a one-month trial, starting next term. We'll compare waste before and during the trial.*

*Does that answer both?*

**What changed.** She names the two parts, answers each in its first words (*no*; *yes*), gives one reason with its source, offers the detail without forcing it on everyone, and checks. The background about food waste in universities has gone: the questioner did not ask for it, and the audience heard it in the talk. The answer takes about thirty seconds.
:::
:::

::: warning *That's a very good question*
✗ *That's a very good question. Thank you for your question. That's a very interesting question.* — before every answer.
✓ *Thanks — that's an important point.* once, when it is true; otherwise just answer: *On cost: no, …*

Praising a question buys a second of thinking time, and once is fine. Said before every answer, it becomes a filler, it sounds insincere, and it suggests that the other questions were not good. A short *Thanks* or *Sure* followed by the answer is enough. If you need time to think, say so honestly: *Let me think about that for a second.*
:::

::: quiz
Someone asks: *Did the survey include part-time students?* Which answer best follows [[#rule-answer-first]]?
- [ ] *That's a very good question. Surveys are always difficult, because some groups are harder to reach than others, and we tried several methods, including…*
- [x] *Yes, it did — about a fifth of the people who replied were part-time. Their answers were very similar to the full-time students'.*
- [ ] *Part-time students are an important group, and I think the university should do more for them.*
- [ ] *I'll come back to that later.*
::: solution
The second gives the direct answer (*Yes*) first and then one useful detail. The first delays the answer with background; the third changes the subject; the fourth defers a question that can be answered in one sentence, which looks evasive.
:::
:::

## When you do not know the answer

Every speaker is eventually asked something she cannot answer. How she handles it often affects the audience's judgement of her more than the answers she does know.

::: theorem Say what you know, and what you don't {#rule-dont-know}
When you do not know the answer:

1. **Say so plainly**, early in your reply: *I don't know.* / *I'm afraid I don't know.* / *I'm not sure — I haven't looked at that.*
2. **Give what you can**: what you do know that is relevant, or how you would find out: *What I can say is…* / *My guess would be…, but I'd want to check.*
3. **Offer to follow up**, and do it: *I'll find out and email you.* / *Could you leave me your address after the session?*
4. **Pass it on** if someone present knows better: *I think Dr Okafor might know more about that than I do.*

Do not invent an answer, and do not hide a guess behind confident language.
:::

::: proof
Admitting ignorance seems risky, but bluffing is riskier. An audience usually contains someone who knows the answer, and a confident wrong answer, once noticed, casts doubt on everything else the speaker said. A plain *I don't know*, followed by what the speaker does know and a promise to find out, shows that she can tell the difference between what she knows and what she does not — which is the basis of trust in any evidence-based field. Guessing is acceptable if it is labelled as a guess: hedges such as *my guess would be* and *I'd want to check, but* mark the status of the claim honestly ([[modality/hedging-boosting]]). What audiences dislike is not the gap in knowledge but the attempt to cover it.
:::

Useful phrases:

| situation | phrases |
|---|---|
| you do not know | *I'm afraid I don't know.* / *I honestly don't know.* / *That's not something I've looked at.* |
| you know part of it | *I don't know the exact figure, but it's in the region of…* / *I can answer part of that.* |
| you can guess | *My guess would be…, but I'd want to check.* / *I'd expect…, though I haven't seen data on it.* |
| outside your topic | *That's a bit outside what I've looked at, but…* / *That's really a question for the engineers.* |
| you will follow up | *I'll look into it and get back to you.* / *Can I email you about that?* |

::: example A follow-up that helps {#ex-dont-know}
After her talk on bus routes, Siosaia Taufa is asked: *Do you know how many people use the night bus on weekdays?* She does not. Write two answers: a poor one and a good one.
::: solution
**Poor:** *Er… quite a lot, I think — maybe several thousand? It's very popular.* — The numbers are invented and presented with *I think* as if they were known. If someone in the room knows the real figure, the whole talk loses credibility.

**Good:** *I'm afraid I don't have the weekday figure — I only looked at weekends, when it's about four hundred a night. The transport office publishes the weekday numbers, so I'll check and send them round with the slides.* — The answer begins with the admission, offers what she knows with its limits, says where the information is, and promises a specific follow-up.
:::
:::

## Difficult questions

Some questions are hard not because of their content but because of their form or their tone. The general strategy is the same for all of them: **stay calm, find the real question, answer the substance briefly, and move on**.

::: definition Kinds of difficult question {#def-difficult}
- A **challenge** questions your evidence, method or conclusion: *Isn't your sample far too small?*
- A **hostile** question challenges you personally or rudely: *Did you actually read the report?*
- A **comment** is not a question at all, but a statement of the questioner's own view, sometimes long.
- An **off-topic** question is about something your talk did not cover.
- A **multi-part** question contains several questions at once.
- A **hypothetical** or **loaded** question rests on an assumption you do not accept: *Since the trial has failed, what will you do next?*
:::

::: theorem Answering challenges and hostile questions {#rule-challenge}
1. **Acknowledge** what is reasonable in the challenge — *That's a fair point about the sample.* — without apologising for your work.
2. **Answer the substance**: what the evidence does and does not show, why you chose the method, what would change your view.
3. **Concede honestly** where the challenge is right, and say what follows: *You're right that thirty people is a small sample, so I'd treat this as a pilot.*
4. **Ignore the tone**, not the question: answer a rude question as if it had been asked politely. Do not return the rudeness.
5. **Reject a false assumption** explicitly before answering: *I wouldn't say the trial has failed — waste fell in the first two weeks, but not in the third.*
:::

::: proof
A challenge is the audience doing its job. Treating it as an attack makes the speaker look defensive; treating it as a contribution — acknowledging, answering and conceding where right — makes the speaker look secure. Conceding a limitation does not weaken an argument as much as speakers fear, because the audience has usually noticed the limitation already, and the concession shows judgement. Answering a hostile question politely has a further effect: it moves the audience's sympathy to the speaker. A loaded question must be dealt with differently, because answering it directly accepts its assumption; the speaker first says which part she does not accept. These moves are the spoken versions of concession and rebuttal in writing ([[essay-writing/counter-arguments]]).
:::

For **comments**, thank the speaker and respond briefly to the point, or find a question in it: *Thanks — I think you're making a point about funding there, and I agree it's the hard part.* For **off-topic** questions, answer in a sentence if you can and say why you will not go further: *That's a bit beyond what I've looked at today, but briefly…* For questions that would take too long, or that only one person is interested in, **defer**: *That's quite a detailed question — could we talk about it afterwards?* Use deferral sparingly and keep the promise; a speaker who defers every hard question appears to be avoiding them.

::: example A difficult exchange {#ex-difficult}
At the end of a talk on cycling infrastructure by Mariam Yusuf, this exchange takes place. Label each of Mariam's moves.

**Questioner:** *I have to say, I don't really buy any of this. Most people in this town drive, they're always going to drive, and building cycle lanes just makes the traffic worse for everybody. Isn't this just a project for a small minority?*

**Mariam:** *Thanks — I can see you feel strongly about it, and you're right that most people here drive at the moment. I'd question "always", though. When we asked drivers in the survey, about a third said they'd consider cycling for short trips if the roads felt safer. On traffic — honestly, the evidence from other towns is mixed: in some, congestion went down; in others, not much changed. So I wouldn't promise less traffic. What I would say is that it gives the people who want to cycle a safe way to do it. But it's a fair challenge, and it's one reason we're proposing a trial on one road rather than across the whole town.*
::: solution
1. *Thanks — I can see you feel strongly about it* — **acknowledges** the questioner and the tone without matching it.
2. *you're right that most people here drive at the moment* — **concedes** the true part.
3. *I'd question "always", though* — **rejects an assumption** explicitly, then gives **evidence** (her survey).
4. *On traffic — honestly, the evidence from other towns is mixed* — **answers the substance** honestly, including the part that does not support her.
5. *So I wouldn't promise less traffic. What I would say is…* — **limits her claim** and restates what she does claim.
6. *But it's a fair challenge, and it's one reason we're proposing a trial* — turns the concession into a **reason for her proposal**.

Mariam neither apologises for her talk nor attacks the questioner. Her answer is longer than most because the challenge is a central one, but each sentence does a job.
:::
:::

## Ending the question time

The question time should end as deliberately as the talk did. When the time is nearly up, the chair or the speaker says so: *We've got time for one more question.* After the last answer, the speaker can return briefly to the central idea — the last thing the audience hears should be the message of the talk, not a detail from the final question:

*Thank you — those were really helpful questions. If I could just come back to the main point: smaller portions cut waste without costing the canteen more, and the trial next term will tell us how much. Thanks again.*

::: quiz
Which is the best way to deal with a long comment from the audience that contains no question?
- [ ] Interrupt and say: *Sorry, what's your question?*
- [x] Listen, thank the speaker, respond briefly to the main point, and invite the next question.
- [ ] Ignore it and wait silently until the person stops.
- [ ] Respond to every point in the comment in detail.
::: solution
A brief, polite response treats the comment as a contribution and keeps the session moving. Interrupting can sound rude, though a chair may politely ask for the question if a comment goes on too long (*Could I ask you to come to your question?*). Ignoring the comment and responding to every point both waste the time of the other questioners.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A questioner asks: *Given that your results are wrong, why should we believe your conclusion?* The results are not wrong. What should you do first?
- [ ] Explain your conclusion again.
- [ ] Apologise for any mistakes.
- [x] Say clearly that you don't accept that the results are wrong, and why.
- [ ] Ask the chair to move to the next question.
::: solution
This is a loaded question: answering it directly would accept its assumption. Reject the assumption first, briefly and calmly (*I don't think the results are wrong — they've been checked against… What I'd accept is that…*), and then answer any real concern behind it ([[#rule-challenge]]).
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **这个问题问得很好 and *good question*.** Praising the question is common in Chinese and English, and once is natural in both. In English, praising every question becomes a noticeable filler ([[#rule-answer-first]] and the warning above).
- **Answer first.** In some Chinese contexts, giving context before the answer shows thoroughness and respect, and the direct answer can sound abrupt. In most English question times, the answer-first order is expected, and the context sounds like delay. This is a convention of the genre, not of character; English-speaking speakers who are nervous also bury their answers.
- **Saying *I don't know*.** Admitting ignorance in public can feel face-threatening for anyone, and some speakers feel the pressure more strongly when they are students, juniors or second-language users ([[pragmatics/politeness-face]]). In most English-speaking academic and professional settings, a plain *I don't know, but…* is respected more than an uncertain improvisation. The phrases above let you say it without losing face.
- ***Do you understand?*** As a check after an answer, *Do you understand?* can sound as if you are testing the questioner. *Does that answer your question?* or *Is that what you were asking?* puts the responsibility on the answer, not the listener.
- **Asking questions as an audience member.** In many classrooms, in China and elsewhere, students are not used to questioning a speaker in public, and a silent audience may feel normal. In English-speaking seminars and conferences, a question is a sign of interest, not of criticism, and speakers generally hope to receive some. A short question with a frame works well: *Thanks for the talk. I wanted to ask about the survey — did it include part-time students?*
:::

::: history
Questioning a speaker in public has long been part of parliamentary and academic life. In the British House of Commons, questions to ministers became a regular part of business in the nineteenth century, and a fixed slot for questions to the Prime Minister was introduced in 1961; in universities, the oral examination or **viva voce** — in which a candidate answers questions on a thesis — goes back to medieval disputations. Research on how answers work is more recent. Conversation analysts studying broadcast news interviews, notably Steven Clayman ("Answers and evasions", *Language in Society*, 2001) and Clayman and John Heritage (*The News Interview*, 2002), showed that interviewers and audiences monitor closely whether a question has been answered, and that speakers who resist a question usually work to disguise it — for example by acknowledging the question or justifying a shift of topic. Their findings explain why a direct answer, or an open admission that you cannot give one, is usually received better than a subtle evasion.
:::

## Where this leads

Answering questions in the more demanding setting of an interview, where every answer is evaluated, is the subject of [[presentations-and-discussion/interviews]]. In a seminar, questions and answers become a discussion among equals ([[presentations-and-discussion/seminars-discussion]]), and in a debate, answering a challenge becomes rebuttal ([[presentations-and-discussion/debate-persuasion]]). The repair moves used for checking a question are in [[listening-and-conversation/clarification]], and the hedges for answers you are unsure of in [[modality/hedging-boosting]].

::: summary
- Predict the questions, keep the material you cut, prepare backup slides, and state the limits of your evidence in the talk ([[#rule-prepare]]).
- Invite questions clearly, then **wait**; if nobody asks, prime the room with a common question.
- **Listen to the end**, check unclear questions with a paraphrase, repeat questions in large rooms, and number the parts of multi-part questions ([[#rule-listen]]).
- **Answer first**, then give the reason; keep answers short; check if useful ([[#rule-answer-first]]). Praise a question at most once.
- When you do not know, **say so**, give what you do know, and offer to follow up; never bluff ([[#rule-dont-know]]).
- With challenges: acknowledge, answer the substance, concede where right, ignore hostile tone, and reject false assumptions before answering ([[#rule-challenge]]).
- End the question time by returning to the central idea.
:::

::: exercise Analyse an exchange {level=3}
Read this exchange from the end of a talk by Oskar Nilsson on four-day working weeks and say what the speaker does well and badly. Then rewrite his answer.

**Questioner:** *You said productivity went up in the trial. But wasn't it only twenty companies, all of which volunteered? Doesn't that make the results a bit meaningless?*

**Oskar:** *That's a very good question, thank you. Well, as I said, the trial was very successful, and productivity went up, and the staff were happier. I think the results speak for themselves. Many experts agree that this is the future of work. Next question?*
::: hint
Did he answer the challenge? What was reasonable in it?
:::
::: solution
**Badly:** praises the question by formula; does not acknowledge the real point (a small, self-selected sample); repeats his claim instead of answering; appeals to unnamed *experts*; and closes the exchange abruptly. The audience will conclude that he has no answer.

**Well:** he is calm and polite.

**Rewritten:** *That's a fair point — twenty companies is a small number, and because they volunteered, they were probably the ones most likely to make it work. So I wouldn't say the results show that a four-day week would raise productivity everywhere. What they do show is that it can work, at least in companies that want to try it. The next step would be a trial where companies are chosen at random. Does that answer it?* — acknowledgement, concession, a limited claim, a constructive next step, and a check ([[#rule-challenge]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Speaking: a mock question time {level=3}
Give a three-minute talk on any topic you know well to one or two friends or classmates. Ask them to prepare five questions: one simple, one multi-part, one challenge, one you probably cannot answer, and one loaded question. Answer all five, recording the session. Then check each answer against [[#rule-listen]], [[#rule-answer-first]], [[#rule-dont-know]] and [[#rule-challenge]].
::: solution
Answers vary. Check: Did you let each question finish? Did you number the parts of the multi-part question and answer each? Did each answer begin with the direct answer? Did you say *I don't know* plainly when you didn't, and offer something useful? Did you acknowledge the challenge and concede what was fair? Did you reject the assumption of the loaded question before answering it? Did you use *good question* more than once?
:::
:::
