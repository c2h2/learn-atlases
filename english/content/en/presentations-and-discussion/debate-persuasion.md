A university debating society is debating the motion *This house would ban private cars from the city centre*. Nomvula Dlamini, the first speaker for the proposition, has argued that a ban would cut air pollution and make the centre safer for pedestrians. Now the first opposition speaker replies. Here are two versions of how he might begin.

**Version A (Kenji):** *Thank you. I strongly disagree with the proposition. Cars are very important in modern life. Many people need cars. If we ban cars, the economy will be destroyed and the city will die. Also, cars are a symbol of freedom. So I think this motion is wrong.*

**Version B (Kenji):** *Thank you, Madam Chair. The proposition told us two things: that a ban would cut pollution, and that it would make the centre safer. I'm going to accept the second, challenge the first, and then give you the argument they ignored — who gets left behind. First, pollution. Nomvula said a ban would clean the air. But the cars don't disappear: they drive around the edge of the zone instead, so the pollution moves to the streets just outside — where, in our city, most of the cheaper housing is. So this motion doesn't cut pollution; it moves it onto poorer neighbourhoods.*

Version A states a position and several claims, but none of them is supported, and none answers anything the proposition said. *The city will die* is an exaggeration the audience will discount. Version B does three things that persuade: it **responds** directly to the other side's arguments, it gives a **reason** and a **mechanism** for its claim, and it **concedes** a point it cannot win, which makes the rest more credible. Whether Kenji's claim about traffic is true is a matter of evidence — the proposition will ask for it — but at least it is an argument that can be tested.

This chapter is about building and answering arguments aloud: the structure of a formal debate, how to construct a case, how to rebut an opponent, and how to recognise weak reasoning. It builds on the polite disagreement of [[listening-and-conversation/opinions]] and the seminar skills of [[presentations-and-discussion/seminars-discussion]], and its written counterparts are [[essay-writing/arguing-a-point]] and [[essay-writing/counter-arguments]].

## Formal debate

::: definition Motion, sides and speeches {#def-debate}
A **formal debate** is a structured contest of arguments on a **motion** — a proposal stated in advance, traditionally beginning *This house…* (*This house believes that…*, *This house would…*). The **proposition** (also called *government* or *affirmative*) argues for the motion, the **opposition** (*negative*) against it. Speakers take turns in a fixed order and for a fixed time, under a **chair** (or *speaker*) who keeps order. A **rebuttal** is an answer to the other side's arguments. In many formats, members of the opposing side may offer a **point of information** — a short question or comment during a speech, which the speaker may accept or decline. A debate is judged by an adjudicator or panel, or decided by an audience vote.
:::

There are many debate formats, used in schools, universities and competitions, and they differ in the number of teams, the length of speeches and whether points of information are allowed. In the format used at the World Universities Debating Championship, for example, four teams of two speakers each — two for the proposition, two for the opposition — give seven-minute speeches. You do not need to learn a format to use what this chapter teaches: the skills of building a case and rebutting are the same in a society debate, a classroom discussion, or a meeting where a decision is contested.

::: theorem The parts of a debate speech {#rule-speech}
A debate speech usually has:

1. A short **opening** that frames the issue or states the side's position: *We believe this motion would move pollution, not reduce it.*
2. **Rebuttal** of the arguments already made by the other side — except in the very first speech of the debate.
3. The speaker's own **constructive arguments** (usually two or three), each a claim with reasoning and evidence.
4. A short **summary** that restates why the side should win.

The speech is signposted throughout — *I'm going to do three things…*, *First, rebuttal…*, *My second argument is…* — exactly as in a presentation ([[presentations-and-discussion/signposting-a-talk]]).
:::

::: proof
The structure follows from what a debate is for. Arguments that are not answered are usually treated by judges as accepted, so a speaker who ignores the other side's case has conceded it; rebuttal therefore comes early, while the other side's points are fresh. Constructive arguments give the audience a positive reason to vote, not only reasons to doubt the other side. Signposting matters even more than in a presentation, because adjudicators and audiences are taking notes on a fast exchange and need to know which argument is being answered and which is new.
:::

## Building an argument

::: definition The Toulmin model {#def-toulmin}
Stephen Toulmin (*The Uses of Argument*, 1958; updated edition 2003) described everyday arguments as having six parts:

1. **Claim** — the conclusion the speaker wants accepted: *The ban will move pollution to poorer areas.*
2. **Data** (or **grounds**) — the facts offered in support: *Traffic diverted from the zone uses the ring road, which runs through the cheaper housing districts.*
3. **Warrant** — the general principle that links the data to the claim, often left unstated: *Pollution is concentrated where traffic is heaviest.*
4. **Backing** — support for the warrant: *Measurements of air quality near busy roads.*
5. **Qualifier** — how strongly the claim is made: *probably*, *in most cases*, *in our city*.
6. **Rebuttal** (in Toulmin's sense) — the conditions under which the claim would not hold: *unless the city also restricts traffic on the ring road.*
:::

The Toulmin model is useful in debate because each part is a point where an argument can be attacked or defended. An opponent can deny the data, question the warrant, demand the backing, or point to a condition under which the claim fails. A speaker who has thought about all six parts knows where her argument is weak before anyone else says so.

::: theorem Every argument needs a claim, reasoning, evidence and impact {#rule-argument}
In a debate, present each constructive argument in four steps:

1. **Claim** — state it in one sentence, as a label: *My second argument is about who gets left behind.*
2. **Reasoning** — explain **why** it is true: the mechanism, the causal chain, the principle.
3. **Evidence** — an example, a statistic with its source, a case, an analogy.
4. **Impact** — say **why it matters** for the motion: *So even if the centre is cleaner, the city as a whole is not, and the people paying for it are the ones with the least choice.*
:::

::: proof
Inexperienced debaters often give claims with evidence but no reasoning — a statistic, then the next point — or claims with no impact, leaving the audience to work out why they should care. The reasoning is usually the most persuasive part, because it is what makes the evidence relevant: a number alone can be answered with another number, but a clear mechanism is harder to dismiss. The impact links the argument back to the motion and tells the judge how much weight it should carry. The four steps correspond loosely to Toulmin's claim, warrant, data and the significance of the claim; many debate coaches teach a similar sequence under various names.
:::

::: example From assertion to argument {#ex-argument}
Turn Kenji's Version A assertion *Many people need cars* into a full argument using [[#rule-argument]].
::: solution
1. **Claim.** *My third argument is about the people who genuinely can't do without a car.*
2. **Reasoning.** *Public transport works well for people who are travelling at ordinary times, between places on the main routes, without much to carry. It works badly for people who don't fit that pattern — a nurse finishing a night shift, a parent with two small children, someone who uses a wheelchair and lives far from a step-free station.*
3. **Evidence.** *The proposition's own plan, as Nomvula described it, has no exemption for any of these groups.* (In a real debate, a figure such as the number of night-shift workers in the city, with its source, would strengthen this.)
4. **Impact.** *So this ban falls hardest on people for whom the car isn't a luxury but the only practical option — and that's a cost the proposition hasn't even mentioned.*

The claim is now a label the audience can note; the reasoning explains *why* some people need cars; the evidence points to a gap in the other side's case; and the impact says why it should decide the debate.
:::
:::

### Ethos, pathos and logos

Since Aristotle's *Rhetoric* (4th century BCE), persuasion has often been described in terms of three appeals: **ethos**, the speaker's credibility and character; **pathos**, the emotions of the audience; and **logos**, the reasoning itself. A strong debate speech uses all three. Kenji's concession in Version B builds ethos (he seems fair); his reference to poorer neighbourhoods appeals to pathos (a sense of injustice); his mechanism — the cars drive around the edge — is logos. The appeals are treated in detail for writing in [[style-and-rhetoric/rhetorical-appeals]].

## Rebuttal

::: theorem Four steps of rebuttal {#rule-rebuttal}
To rebut an argument:

1. **Signpost** which argument you are answering, in the other side's terms: *Nomvula said a ban would clean the air.*
2. **State your response** in one sentence: *But the pollution doesn't disappear — it moves.*
3. **Support** it with reasoning and, if possible, evidence: *Cars that can't cross the centre drive round it…*
4. **Conclude** by saying what follows for the debate: *So this argument, far from supporting the motion, is a reason to oppose it.*
:::

::: proof
Signposting the argument in the other side's own words shows the audience that you are answering what was actually said, not a weaker version of it — the opposite of a straw man (see below). A one-sentence response gives the listener the point before the detail, as in answering questions ([[presentations-and-discussion/handling-questions#rule-answer-first]]). The conclusion step is the one most often left out: without it, the audience hears a disagreement but not what it changes. These four steps are a spoken form of the rebuttal paragraph in writing ([[essay-writing/counter-arguments#rule-rebuttal]]).
:::

There are several ways to answer an argument, and they are not equally strong:

| strategy | what you say | example |
|---|---|---|
| **deny the evidence** | the facts are wrong or unsupported | *That figure comes from a city ten times our size.* |
| **deny the reasoning** | even if the facts are true, they do not lead to the claim | *Fewer cars in the centre doesn't mean fewer cars overall.* |
| **accept but outweigh** | the point is true, but something else matters more | *Yes, the centre would be safer — but that benefit is smaller than the cost to night workers.* |
| **turn** the argument | the point actually supports your side | *Cleaner air in the centre is exactly why rents there will rise and push poorer people out.* |

Accepting and outweighing is often the most persuasive, because it does not ask the audience to disbelieve something that sounds reasonable. **Weighing** — explaining why your arguments matter more than the other side's, by size, likelihood, number of people affected or reversibility — is what usually decides close debates.

::: example A rebuttal in four steps {#ex-rebuttal}
The second proposition speaker, Xu Ming, wants to answer Kenji's argument that the ban moves pollution to poorer areas. Write her rebuttal using [[#rule-rebuttal]] and at least one of the strategies in the table.
::: solution
1. **Signpost.** *Kenji's main argument was that a ban would push pollution out to the ring road, onto poorer neighbourhoods.*
2. **Response.** *That's a real concern — but it's an argument for doing the ban properly, not for not doing it.* (accept but outweigh; also a partial denial of the reasoning)
3. **Support.** *Diverted traffic is a problem only if the same number of people keep driving. A ban works alongside better buses and park-and-ride sites at the edge of the city, and in that case many drivers don't just go round — they stop driving into the city at all. And the ring road is already one of the busiest roads in the city; the extra traffic is a small share of what's there.* (In a real debate, she would give the source for any claim about how drivers behave.)
4. **Conclude.** *So the opposition has given us a reason to plan the ban carefully — which we've done — not a reason to vote against it.*

Notice that Xu Ming does not call Kenji's argument stupid or irrelevant. She takes it seriously, which makes her answer more credible to the audience.
:::
:::

::: quiz
An opponent argues: *Free museums are a good idea because visitor numbers rise when entry is free.* Which reply is an example of **accepting but outweighing**?
- [ ] *Visitor numbers don't rise when entry is free.*
- [ ] *Rising visitor numbers would actually damage the collections, so this supports our side.*
- [x] *Numbers may well rise — but the loss of ticket income would close smaller museums, and that matters more.*
- [ ] *My opponent obviously doesn't visit museums.*
::: solution
The third accepts the claim and argues that a larger cost outweighs it. The first denies the evidence; the second turns the argument; the fourth is an attack on the person (ad hominem), not an argument.
:::
:::

::: example Weighing two arguments {#ex-weighing}
By the end of the car-ban debate, both sides have one argument left standing: the proposition's *the centre becomes safer for pedestrians*, and the opposition's *night workers and disabled people lose their only practical way into the centre*. Write a weighing sentence or two for the opposition's last speaker, Ahmed Karimi, using at least two of these measures: size, likelihood, number of people affected, reversibility.
::: solution
*So let's weigh what's left. The proposition's benefit is real, but it's small and it's partly available without a ban — slower speed limits and wider pavements would make the centre safer too. Our harm is larger for each person affected: for a nurse on a night shift, it isn't a slower journey; it's no journey. And it's certain — the ban takes effect the day it starts — while the safety gains depend on how people adapt. When one side offers a modest benefit that can be had in other ways, and the other side shows a serious, certain cost to people with no alternative, you should oppose.*

**How it works.** Ahmed compares the two arguments on **size** (a slower journey versus no journey), **likelihood** (certain versus dependent on behaviour) and **alternatives** (the benefit can be achieved another way). He does not deny the proposition's point; he explains why his matters more. That comparison, not a new argument, is what a final speech is for.
:::
:::

::: quiz
In the last speech of a debate, which is usually the most valuable thing to do?
- [ ] Introduce two new arguments the other side has not had a chance to answer.
- [x] Compare the arguments still standing and explain why your side's matter more.
- [ ] Repeat your first speaker's speech in full.
- [ ] Thank the audience and the judges at length.
::: solution
Weighing — comparing the remaining arguments by size, likelihood, number of people affected or reversibility — tells the judges how to decide. New arguments in a final speech are often disallowed or discounted, because the other side cannot answer them.
:::
:::

## Recognising weak reasoning

Some arguments sound persuasive but contain a flaw in their reasoning. Recognising these flaws helps you rebut them — and avoid them in your own speeches. They are often called **fallacies**, but the label is less important than being able to explain the problem.

::: definition Common weaknesses in reasoning {#def-fallacies}
- **Straw man** — answering a weaker or distorted version of an argument: *The proposition wants to ban everyone from ever driving anywhere.*
- **False dilemma** — presenting two options as the only ones: *Either we ban cars, or we accept dirty air forever.*
- **Hasty generalisation** — drawing a broad conclusion from too few cases: *My cousin's city banned cars and it was a disaster, so bans don't work.*
- **Slippery slope** — claiming, without showing the steps, that one change will lead to a chain of extreme consequences: *First cars, then motorbikes, then they'll ban bicycles.*
- **Ad hominem** — attacking the person instead of the argument: *Of course she supports the ban — she doesn't even have a licence.*
- **Appeal to popularity** — treating the number of people who believe something as evidence that it is true: *Most people think the ban is a bad idea, so it must be.*
- **Correlation taken as cause** — assuming that because two things happen together, one causes the other: *Cities with car bans are richer, so bans make cities richer.*
- **Affirming the consequent** — reasoning from *if A, then B* and *B* to *A*: *If a ban works, pollution falls; pollution has fallen; so the ban worked.*
:::

The last of these is easy to miss, because it sounds like ordinary reasoning. *If the ban works, pollution falls* may be true, but pollution can also fall for other reasons — a windy year, cleaner engines, a factory closing — so the fall does not prove that the ban worked. The figure shows why: the statement *if A then B* and its **converse**, *if B then A*, are not equivalent.

::: widget truthtable
formula: p -> q
compare: q -> p
caption: Let p be *the ban works* and q be *pollution falls*. The first formula is *if the ban works, pollution falls*; the second is its converse, *if pollution falls, the ban worked*. They differ in the row where p is false and q is true — pollution fell for another reason — which is exactly the case that the argument "pollution has fallen, so the ban worked" ignores. A rebuttal names that row: *Pollution also fell in the cities that didn't ban cars.*
:::

::: example Naming the flaw {#ex-flaw}
Identify the weakness in each argument and write a one-sentence rebuttal.

1. *We can either build more roads or watch traffic get worse every year.*
2. *Since the new mayor arrived, crime has fallen. Clearly her policies are working.*
3. *The proposition says we should restrict cars in the centre. So they want to take away everyone's freedom to travel.*
::: solution
1. **False dilemma.** *There are other options — better buses, cycle lanes, congestion charges — so these aren't the only two choices.*
2. **Correlation taken as cause** (and possibly affirming the consequent). *Crime has also fallen in neighbouring cities with different policies, so we'd need more evidence to give the mayor the credit.*
3. **Straw man.** *We haven't said anything about stopping people from travelling — only about one kind of vehicle in one small area, with buses and bikes still running.*

In each case, the rebuttal explains the flaw in plain words rather than just naming it. Saying *That's a false dilemma* is less persuasive than showing the missing options.
:::
:::

::: warning Attacking the speaker
✗ *My opponent clearly knows nothing about transport.* / *That's a ridiculous argument.*
✓ *I don't think that argument holds, for two reasons.* / *That's an argument worth taking seriously — but it misses something.*

Personal attacks and dismissive labels lose more than they gain: audiences and judges usually penalise them, and they signal that the speaker has no better answer. Formal debates use third-person reference (*the proposition*, *the first speaker*, *my opponent*), which keeps the focus on arguments. Strong disagreement can be expressed entirely through the content.
:::

## The language of persuasion

Persuasion in speech relies on some patterns that are less common in writing.

- **Rhetorical questions**, which the speaker answers: *So who pays for this ban? Not the people in the centre. The people on the ring road.*
- **Lists of three** (tricolons): *cleaner, safer and fairer.*
- **Contrast** (antithesis): *This motion doesn't cut pollution; it moves it.*
- **Concession followed by a turn**: *Yes, the centre would be quieter. But quieter for whom?*
- **Direct address to the audience**: *Imagine you work a night shift…*
- **Signposted weighing**: *Even if you believe everything the proposition said, there's one reason to vote against this motion that outweighs it.*

::: theorem Address the other side, and calibrate your claims {#rule-persuade}
To persuade an audience that has not already decided:

1. **Acknowledge and answer** the strongest opposing argument, rather than ignoring it.
2. **Calibrate** your claims to your evidence: boost what is well supported (*The figures clearly show…*), hedge what is not (*This would probably mean…*) ([[modality/hedging-boosting]]).
3. **Avoid exaggeration**: an audience that catches one overstatement discounts the rest.
:::

::: proof
Research on persuasive messages supports the first point. Daniel O'Keefe's meta-analysis of studies comparing one-sided messages with two-sided ones (1999, *Communication Yearbook* 22) found that two-sided messages which **refute** the opposing arguments were, on average, more persuasive than one-sided messages, whereas two-sided messages that merely **mention** the other side without answering it were, on average, less persuasive. Mentioning an objection and leaving it unanswered gives the audience a reason to disagree; mentioning and answering it removes one. Calibration and avoiding exaggeration follow from credibility: an audience judges the speaker as well as the argument, and a speaker who is seen to overclaim on one point loses trust on all of them.
:::

::: application Points of information
In debate formats that allow them, a member of the other side may stand during a speech and say *Point of information* (or *On that point*). The speaker decides:

- **accept**: *Yes, please.* / *I'll take it.* — then the questioner has about fifteen seconds for a question or comment, and the speaker answers it briefly before continuing;
- **decline**: *No, thank you.* / *Not at the moment.* / *Let me finish this point first.*

A good point of information is short and pointed: *How does a ban help someone who works at night?* A good answer is shorter: *Night buses — which are in our plan — and I'll come to them in a moment.* Speakers usually accept one or two points during a speech; declining all of them can look evasive.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Chinese debating.** Chinese-language debate has a strong university tradition of its own, with competitions such as the International Varsity Debate (国际大专辩论会), held since 1993, and formats that include a free-debate section (自由辩论) in which the two sides alternate rapidly. Chinese debaters who move into English debate often bring excellent preparation and team coordination; the adjustment is usually to the different structure of speeches and the strong emphasis on direct rebuttal.
- **Idioms and quotations.** A well-chosen 成语 or a classical quotation can be very persuasive in Chinese. Translated literally into an English debate, it may sound decorative rather than probative, because English judges tend to look for the reasoning behind it. Use the idea, explain it in plain words, and keep quotations short.
- ***We* and *I*.** In Chinese debate, speakers often speak for the team (我方); in English, *we* (*We believe…*) for the team's case and *I* (*I'm going to show you…*) for the speaker's own role are both normal.
- **Directness.** Rebutting an opponent by name and to their face can feel confrontational. In English debate it is the expected form of respect: you show that you have listened by answering precisely. Keep the tone courteous and the content direct ([[pragmatics/directness-across-cultures]]).
- **Linkers.** As in presentations, *Firstly… Moreover… Furthermore…* sound written. *My first argument is… On top of that… And crucially…* sound spoken ([[presentations-and-discussion/signposting-a-talk]]).
:::

::: history
Arguing both sides of a question was part of Greek education from the time of the sophist Protagoras in the fifth century BCE, and Aristotle's *Rhetoric* analysed persuasion into the appeals of ethos, pathos and logos. Medieval universities trained students through formal disputations, in which a thesis was defended against objections. Modern student debating took shape in the nineteenth century: the Cambridge Union Society was founded in 1815 and the Oxford Union in 1823, and their debates on a motion, with a vote at the end, became a model for university societies elsewhere. Competitive debating grew in the twentieth century, and the World Universities Debating Championship was first held in 1981. For the study of argument, Stephen Toulmin's *The Uses of Argument* (1958) proposed a model based on how people actually argue, rather than on formal logic, which is still widely used in teaching debate and critical thinking.
:::

## Where this leads

Persuasion continues in meetings and negotiations, where the aim is agreement rather than victory ([[presentations-and-discussion/meetings-negotiation]]). The written counterparts of this chapter — building a thesis, arguing a point and answering counter-arguments — are [[essay-writing/thesis-introduction]], [[essay-writing/arguing-a-point]] and [[essay-writing/counter-arguments]]; persuasion as a matter of style is in [[style-and-rhetoric/rhetorical-appeals]] and [[style-and-rhetoric/persuasive-writing]]. Reading arguments critically, with attention to claims and evidence, is in [[academic-reading/claims-and-evidence]].

::: summary
- A formal debate has a **motion**, a **proposition** and an **opposition**, speeches in a fixed order, and often **points of information** ([[#def-debate]]).
- A debate speech: a framing opening, **rebuttal**, two or three **constructive arguments**, and a summary — all signposted ([[#rule-speech]]).
- Toulmin's model — claim, data, warrant, backing, qualifier, rebuttal — shows where an argument can be attacked ([[#def-toulmin]]).
- Present each argument as **claim, reasoning, evidence, impact** ([[#rule-argument]]).
- Rebut in four steps — signpost, respond, support, conclude — and choose a strategy: deny the evidence, deny the reasoning, accept but outweigh, or turn ([[#rule-rebuttal]]).
- Recognise and explain weak reasoning: straw man, false dilemma, hasty generalisation, slippery slope, ad hominem, appeal to popularity, correlation as cause, affirming the consequent ([[#def-fallacies]]).
- Answer the strongest opposing argument, calibrate claims to evidence, and never attack the speaker; refuted two-sided messages tend to persuade best (O'Keefe 1999; [[#rule-persuade]]).
:::

Read this extract from a debate speech by Rosa Delgado for the motion *This house would ban advertising aimed at children* and evaluate it: identify one strength, at least three weaknesses, and rewrite it.

*Advertising to children is evil. Everybody knows that. Children see thousands of adverts and they all want toys and sweets, and obviously that makes them unhealthy. The opposition will say parents should decide, but parents are lazy. If we don't ban it, soon advertisers will control our children's minds completely. So vote for the motion.*
::: hint
Check each claim for reasoning and evidence. Which weaknesses from [[#def-fallacies]] appear? How does she handle the opposition's argument?
:::
::: solution
**Strength:** she anticipates the opposition's main argument (parents should decide), which is good debating practice.

**Weaknesses:** *evil* is an exaggeration with no reasoning; *Everybody knows that* is an appeal to popularity; *thousands of adverts* has no source; *obviously that makes them unhealthy* assumes cause without a mechanism or evidence; *parents are lazy* is an ad hominem attack on a whole group and will alienate the audience; *advertisers will control our children's minds* is a slippery slope; there is no weighing and no impact beyond assertion.

**Rewrite (one possibility):** *Our case is simple: children can't yet recognise when they're being persuaded, so advertising aimed at them is unfair in a way that advertising to adults isn't. Young children, research suggests, often don't understand that an advert is trying to sell them something* (in a real debate, with the source). *The opposition will say parents should decide — and parents do matter. But parents can't be in the room every time a child watches a video or opens a game, and asking them to undo hundreds of adverts a week is asking a lot. So this motion doesn't take power from parents; it gives them back some of the power that advertisers have taken.*
:::

::: exercise Speaking: a two-minute rebuttal {level=3}
Choose a motion (for example, *This house would replace exams with coursework*). Write down three arguments the other side might make. Then prepare and record a two-minute speech that rebuts two of them in four steps and gives one constructive argument of your own with claim, reasoning, evidence and impact. Listen again and check: did you signpost each rebuttal in the other side's terms? Did you weigh?
::: solution
Answers vary. Check the recording for: a framing opening; two rebuttals, each with a signpost (*They said…*), a one-sentence response, support and a conclusion; at least one accept-but-outweigh or turn; one constructive argument with all four parts; a weighing sentence (*Even if you accept…, this matters more because…*); no personal attacks; calibrated claims; and a short summary.
:::
:::
