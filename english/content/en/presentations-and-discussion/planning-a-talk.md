Zhao Lin and Fatima Haddad are in the same environmental studies class, and each has ten minutes to present on the same topic: green roofs, the planted roofs that some cities now encourage on new buildings. Zhao Lin opens like this:

*Good morning, everyone. Today my topic is about green roofs. First, I will introduce the history of green roofs. Green roofs have a long history. In the 1960s, in Germany, engineers began to develop…*

Fatima opens like this:

*Last July, on the hottest afternoon of the year, the roof of our library reached sixty-one degrees. The roof of the science building next door — which is covered in plants — stayed below thirty. In the next ten minutes I want to convince you that every new building on this campus should have a green roof. I'll look at three things: what green roofs do for temperature, what they do for rainwater, and what they cost.*

Zhao Lin knows at least as much about green roofs as Fatima, and his English is just as accurate. But after twenty seconds Fatima's listeners know why the topic matters to them, what she is going to claim, and what route the talk will take. Zhao Lin's listeners know only the topic, and they are about to hear history whose purpose they cannot guess. The difference is not language. It is **planning**: deciding, before writing a single sentence, who the talk is for, what it is meant to do, and in what order the listener needs to hear things.

This chapter teaches that planning. It assumes the conversational skills of [[listening-and-conversation/opinions]] — giving a view with a reason — and stretches them over ten or twenty minutes of speech that the listener cannot reread. The next chapter, [[presentations-and-discussion/signposting-a-talk]], gives the language that makes the plan audible.

## Why a talk is not an essay read aloud

A reader controls the text: she can slow down, skip a paragraph, look back to check a figure, and see from the layout how long a section is and where it ends. A listener has none of this. Speech arrives once, at the speaker's speed, and the structure that headings and paragraphs show on a page is invisible unless the speaker says it. Three consequences follow, and much of this course rests on them.

1. **A talk must carry less.** A listener cannot hold as many details as a reader can look up. Fewer points, each developed with an example, are remembered better than many points mentioned once.
2. **A talk must say its structure.** What headings do in writing, spoken announcements must do in a talk: *I'll look at three things…*, *That brings me to the second point.*
3. **A talk can repeat.** In writing, saying the main point at the beginning, in the middle and at the end can look padded. In a talk it is a service to the listener who drifted for a moment.

::: intuition Plan for the listener who missed thirty seconds
In any audience, someone looks at a phone, someone thinks about lunch, someone is still finding a seat. A well-planned talk lets that person rejoin: the structure has been announced, each section is introduced, and the main message comes back at the end. If your plan works only for a listener who never stops paying attention, it does not work.
:::

## Audience and purpose

::: definition Audience analysis {#def-audience}
**Audience analysis** is the set of questions a speaker asks about the listeners before planning the content: *Who are they? What do they already know about the topic? What do they need or want from this talk? What do they expect — in length, formality and format? What might they disagree with?* The answers decide what to explain, what to assume, which examples to use and how formal to be.
:::

The same topic changes with the audience. A talk on green roofs to engineering students can assume that they know what *load-bearing* means and can go straight to the numbers; the same talk to first-year students in a general class needs a photograph and a sentence of definition; to the university's finance committee it needs costs and payback periods in the first two minutes.

::: definition Purpose and central idea {#def-purpose}
The **general purpose** of a talk is the broad kind of effect it aims at: to **inform** (explain what something is or how it works), to **persuade** (change what the audience believes or does), to **instruct** (teach a procedure), or to mark an **occasion** (welcome, thank, celebrate). The **specific purpose** says what this audience should know, believe or be able to do at the end. The **central idea** — also called the key message or take-home message — is the one sentence that sums up the talk.
:::

Fatima's plan, written down before she drafted anything, looked like this:

- **General purpose:** to persuade.
- **Specific purpose:** at the end, the class should agree that new campus buildings ought to have green roofs.
- **Central idea:** *Green roofs keep buildings cooler and reduce flooding, and over a building's life they cost less than people think.*

Zhao Lin's plan, if he had written one, would have said only: *Topic: green roofs.* A topic is not a purpose. *Green roofs* could become a history lecture, a technical guide, a debate speech or an advertisement; the speaker who has not chosen will drift between them.

::: theorem State the central idea in one sentence {#rule-central-idea}
Before planning the body of a talk, write its central idea as **one complete sentence** — a statement, not a topic or a question — that a listener could repeat to a friend afterwards. *Green roofs* is a topic; *How do green roofs work?* is a question; *Green roofs cool buildings by evaporation and shade, and they hold back rainwater* is a central idea. Everything in the talk should help the audience understand, believe or remember that sentence.
:::

::: proof
The one-sentence test is common to most public-speaking textbooks (Lucas and Stob, *The Art of Public Speaking*, 2020, call it the central idea), and the reason is practical. A speaker who can state the point in a sentence can judge every slide, example and story against it: does this help the listener grasp the point, or is it just something I know? A speaker who cannot state it usually has not decided what the talk is for, and listeners notice. The test also protects against the commonest planning fault, which is including everything one has read. It does not mean that the talk is simple: a single sentence can carry a complex argument, and the talk unpacks it.
:::

::: example From topic to central idea {#ex-central}
Turn each topic into a general purpose, a specific purpose and a central idea, for the audience given.

1. *Sleep* — for a group of first-year university students.
2. *The history of our city's tram system* — for visitors on a guided walk.
3. *A new booking system for the language lab* — for the teachers who will use it.
::: solution
1. **Purpose:** to persuade. **Specific purpose:** students should leave intending to keep a regular sleep schedule during exams. **Central idea:** *Cutting sleep to study usually costs more than it gains, because memory for what we learn is consolidated during sleep.* (The link between sleep and memory consolidation is well supported by research; in the talk itself the speaker would name a published review or textbook as the source.)
2. **Purpose:** to inform and entertain. **Specific purpose:** visitors should understand why the trams disappeared and why they came back. **Central idea:** *The city removed its trams when cars seemed to be the future, and rebuilt them when the roads filled up.*
3. **Purpose:** to instruct. **Specific purpose:** teachers should be able to book a room and cancel a booking by themselves. **Central idea:** *Booking now takes three steps online, and cancelling takes one.*

Notice how the purpose changes the content. The sleep talk needs evidence and a call to action; the tram talk needs a story; the booking talk needs a demonstration and almost no background.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which of these is a central idea rather than a topic or a question?
- [ ] *Public libraries in the digital age.*
- [ ] *Do we still need public libraries?*
- [x] *Public libraries are busier than ever, but now as places to work and meet rather than to borrow books.*
- [ ] *Some facts about public libraries.*
::: solution
Only the third is a complete statement that a listener could repeat and agree or disagree with. The first and fourth are topics; the second is a question, which can make a good opening but does not yet say what the speaker thinks.
:::
:::

## The shape of a talk

Almost every talk in English, from a five-minute class presentation to a conference keynote, has three parts: an **opening**, a **body** and an **ending**. The proportions vary, but a useful rule of thumb for a ten-minute talk is about one minute for the opening, eight for the body and one for the ending. These are conventions, not measurements, and a talk to a hostile audience may need a longer opening, a lecture a longer body.

::: theorem The jobs of the opening {#rule-opening}
In the first minute, a speaker usually does four things, in roughly this order:

1. **Gain attention** with something concrete: a short story, a striking fact with its source, a question to the audience, a problem the listeners recognise.
2. **Show relevance**: why this matters to *these* listeners.
3. **Establish who you are**, if the audience does not know: your role, or why you have looked into the topic. One sentence is enough.
4. **State the purpose and preview the structure**: *I'm going to argue that…; I'll look at three things: …*

Greetings and thanks (*Good morning, everyone. Thank you for coming.*) come first and are short. An apology — for one's English, one's slides or one's nerves — is usually unnecessary and is best left out.
:::

::: proof
Each job answers a question that every listener silently asks at the start: *Why should I pay attention?* (attention), *What has this got to do with me?* (relevance), *Why should I listen to you?* (credibility) and *Where are we going?* (preview). Classical rhetoric gave the opening — the *exordium* — the task of making the audience attentive, receptive and well disposed, and modern textbooks restate the same tasks. The preview matters most for second-language listeners, because it gives them a frame into which the details can be fitted, which is the top-down support described in [[listening-and-conversation/how-listening-works]]. The apology is discouraged because it spends the first seconds, when attention is highest, on something the speaker does not want the audience to think about, and it often draws attention to a problem nobody had noticed.
:::

### Main points and patterns of organisation

::: definition Main point {#def-main-point}
A **main point** is one of the few large divisions of the body, each a claim or a question in its own right that supports the central idea. Main points are supported by **sub-points**: explanations, examples, data, stories and quotations.
:::

How many main points? Most textbooks recommend two to four for a short talk, and many speakers favour three. The preference for threes is partly a rhetorical tradition — lists of three, or *tricolons*, sound complete in English and many other languages — and partly practical: Nelson Cowan (2001), reviewing research on short-term memory, argued that adults can hold only about three to five meaningful chunks in mind at once, far fewer than the "seven plus or minus two" that is often quoted from George Miller's (1956) paper, which Miller himself presented with caution. A listener who must keep track of seven main points while also understanding each one is being asked to do too much.

::: theorem Organise the body by a clear pattern {#rule-pattern}
Choose one pattern for the main points and keep to it. The usual patterns are:

| pattern | use it when… | example main points |
|---|---|---|
| **chronological** | the topic develops in time, or is a process | *how trams were built — removed — rebuilt* |
| **spatial** | the topic is a place or an object | *the roof layers from the top down* |
| **topical** | the topic divides into kinds or aspects | *temperature — rainwater — cost* |
| **cause and effect** | you explain why something happens | *causes of urban heat — its effects — what reduces it* |
| **problem and solution** | you propose a change | *the problem — the proposed solution — why it will work* |
| **comparison** | you weigh two options | *green roofs and white roofs on cost, cooling and upkeep* |

Main points should be **parallel** (similar in form: all nouns, or all questions, or all claims) and roughly **balanced** in time.
:::

::: proof
A pattern lets the listener predict. Once a speaker has said *First, the problem*, the audience expects a solution, and they can use that expectation to understand the second half faster. Mixing patterns — one point about history, one about a part of the roof, one about cost — removes that support, and the talk sounds like a list of things the speaker found. Parallel wording makes the points easier to remember and to announce: *what they do for heat, what they do for rain, what they cost* is easier to hold than *the temperature effect, how water is managed, and whether they're worth the money*. Balance prevents the familiar failure in which the first point takes seven minutes and the last two are rushed.
:::

### Supporting each point

A main point is a claim; the listener believes and remembers it because of what comes after it. The supporting material should be varied, because different listeners are persuaded by different things:

- **an explanation** of how or why: *the plants shade the roof, and water evaporating from the leaves takes heat with it*;
- **an example or case**, preferably concrete and local: *the science building next door*;
- **numbers**, with their source and rounded so that they can be heard: *about thirty degrees cooler*, not *29.6 degrees*;
- **a short story** — what happened to someone — which is remembered better than a statistic;
- **a comparison**: *it's like standing under a tree instead of on a car park*;
- **a quotation** from someone the audience respects, attributed.

::: example Rebuilding an outline {#ex-outline}
Zhao Lin's first outline had these main points: *1. History of green roofs. 2. Types of green roofs (extensive, intensive). 3. Green roofs in Germany. 4. Green roofs in Singapore. 5. Temperature. 6. Rainwater. 7. Cost. 8. Problems. 9. My opinion.* Diagnose the problems and rebuild it as an informative talk for his class.
::: solution
**Diagnosis.** There are nine main points for ten minutes — about a minute each, too many to remember. They follow no single pattern: history (chronological), types (topical), countries (spatial or by case), effects, and an opinion at the end. The speaker's view appears only as the last point, so the audience does not know until minute nine why they heard the rest. Germany and Singapore are examples, not main points.

**Rebuilt plan.** Central idea: *Green roofs cool buildings and hold back rain, but they are not right for every roof.* Pattern: topical, three points.

1. **What a green roof is** — the layers, the two main types (one minute of history as background, not as a point).
2. **What green roofs do** — temperature and rainwater, with Germany and Singapore as examples.
3. **Where they do not work** — weight, cost and upkeep.

Opening: the hot library roof (a local example). Ending: summary of the three points and the central idea again. The history, the types and the countries are all still there, but now each serves a point.
:::
:::

::: warning Too much content
✗ Planning to say everything you have read, then speaking faster to fit it in.
✓ Choosing the two to four points that serve your central idea, and cutting the rest — or moving it to a handout or to your answers in the question time.

The commonest planning mistake is not a lack of material but too much. A ten-minute talk at a comfortable speed contains roughly 1,200–1,500 words, about three pages of an essay ([[presentations-and-discussion/delivery]] explains the arithmetic). Material you cut is not wasted: it is what lets you answer questions confidently ([[presentations-and-discussion/handling-questions]]).
:::

## The ending

::: theorem The jobs of the ending {#rule-ending}
A good ending usually:

1. **signals** that the end is coming: *To sum up…*, *So, to conclude…*;
2. **summarises** the main points briefly, in the same words used in the preview;
3. **restates the central idea**, and for a persuasive talk says what the audience should now think or do;
4. **closes** with a final thought — often a return to the opening story, example or question;
5. **hands over**: *Thank you. I'm happy to take any questions.*

It does **not** introduce new information. A new point at the end has no time to be developed and makes the summary look incomplete.
:::

Fatima's ending: *So, to sum up: green roofs keep buildings cooler, they hold back rainwater, and over the life of a building they cost less than we tend to assume. That's why I think every new building on this campus should have one. Remember that sixty-one-degree library roof — next July, it doesn't have to be that hot. Thank you. I'd be happy to take any questions.*

The return to the opening — the hot library roof — is called a **frame** or a **bookend**. It gives the talk a sense of completion, and it reminds the audience of the concrete case that started it.

::: quiz
Which is the weakest last sentence for a talk on the benefits of learning to cook?
- [ ] *So, if you take one thing from today, let it be this: one cooked meal a week is a good place to start.*
- [x] *Oh, and one more thing I forgot to mention: cooking also helps with mental health, according to some studies.*
- [ ] *That's all from me. Thank you — I'm happy to take questions.*
- [ ] *And remember the student I mentioned at the start, living on instant noodles? She now cooks for her whole flat on Sundays.*
::: solution
The second introduces a new, undeveloped point with a vague source (*some studies*) and the signal *I forgot*, which tells the audience that the talk was not fully planned. The first restates a call to action, the fourth returns to the opening story, and the third is a plain but acceptable hand-over.
:::
:::

## From plan to notes

Should you write out the whole talk? Many experienced speakers write a full draft once, to find the words, and then reduce it to **notes**: the opening and closing sentences in full, because these are the moments when nerves are worst; then, for the body, keywords for each point and sub-point, numbers and names exactly, and the transitions between sections. They speak from the notes, not the draft.

The reason is that reading aloud and speaking are different activities. A written text has the long noun phrases and embedded clauses of written grammar, which are hard to follow by ear; read aloud, it also tends to come out at an even pace, with a narrow pitch range and few real pauses ([[presentations-and-discussion/delivery]]). Speaking from notes forces the speaker to make sentences in the moment, which produces the shorter, more spoken grammar that listeners follow more easily — at the cost of some hesitation, which audiences forgive much more readily than monotony.

::: example A keyword outline {#ex-notes}
Here is the full draft of Fatima's second main point. Reduce it to speaking notes.

*My second point is about rainwater. When heavy rain falls on an ordinary roof, almost all of it runs straight into the drains, and in a storm the drains can overflow. A green roof works like a sponge: the soil and plants hold much of the water and release it slowly, some of it back into the air. The amount depends on the depth of the soil and on the season, so I'll give a range rather than a single number. Studies in several cities have found that green roofs hold back a large share of the rain that falls on them over a year. For our campus, which flooded twice last winter, that matters.*
::: solution
Speaking notes (on one card or one line of the speaker view):

> **2. RAIN** — ordinary roof → all into drains → overflow in storms
> green roof = SPONGE: soil + plants hold, release slowly
> depends on depth + season → give range, cite source [card 5]
> CAMPUS: flooded twice last winter
> → *That brings me to my last point: cost.*

The notes keep the order, the key image (*sponge*), the local example and the transition sentence in full. They leave out the full sentences, so that Fatima speaks them freshly. The numbers she will quote come from a source she has checked and are written on a separate card exactly as the source gives them — notes are where speakers make mistakes with figures, and a vague *studies have found* is weaker than a named study.
:::
:::

::: application Planning a talk in seven steps
1. Analyse the audience: who, what they know, what they need ([[#def-audience]]).
2. Write the general purpose, the specific purpose and the central idea in one sentence ([[#rule-central-idea]]).
3. Choose a pattern and two to four main points ([[#rule-pattern]]).
4. Gather support for each point and cut what does not serve the central idea.
5. Plan the opening ([[#rule-opening]]) and the ending ([[#rule-ending]]) last, when you know what they introduce and close.
6. Budget the time: roughly 10 % opening, 80 % body, 10 % ending, divided between the points.
7. Make speaking notes, and rehearse aloud with a timer at least twice.
:::

## Choosing an opening

There is no single correct way to start. The opening should suit the audience, the purpose and the speaker's own style. Five common kinds:

| kind | example (talk on reducing food waste in the canteen) |
|---|---|
| a story | *On Monday I stood by the canteen bins for twenty minutes and counted the trays…* |
| a fact with its source | *According to the canteen's own records, about a quarter of the food it cooked last term was thrown away.* (Only if the records really say so.) |
| a question | *Hands up if you've ever left half a plate of rice because the portion was too big.* |
| a problem | *The canteen has to cut its budget by a tenth next year, and it doesn't want to raise prices.* |
| a quotation | *My grandmother used to say that a full bin is an empty purse.* |

Questions to the audience work well with small groups and in informal settings; with a large or formal audience, a **rhetorical question** — one the speaker answers — is safer: *So why does so much food end up in the bin? There are three reasons.* A joke is risky in a second language and across cultures; a light, friendly tone is enough.

::: example Same talk, three audiences {#ex-openings}
Write the first two or three sentences of a talk proposing that a company's staff should be able to work from home two days a week — (a) to the staff at a team meeting, (b) to the senior managers, (c) at a public seminar on the future of work.
::: solution
(a) *Morning, everyone. Most of us spent about an hour and a half commuting yesterday — I asked around. I want to suggest a way of getting some of that time back: two days a week at home. Let me explain how it could work and what we'd need to agree on.* — informal, relevant to the listeners' own day, collaborative *we*.

(b) *Good morning. I'd like to propose a two-day home-working policy, and I'll focus on three questions you're likely to ask: what it does to output, what it costs, and how we'd measure it.* — the proposal first, then the managers' concerns as the structure; senior audiences generally want the point before the background.

(c) *Good afternoon. Before the pandemic, home working was something most employers allowed only as an exception. For many, it is now part of the normal week. Today I'd like to look at what one mid-sized company learned when it made that change — what worked, what didn't, and what surprised us.* — wider context, a case-study frame, and a preview.

The central idea is the same in all three; the opening differs because the audience's knowledge, interests and power differ ([[#def-audience]]).
:::
:::

::: quiz
You are giving a talk to senior managers who have fifteen minutes between two other meetings. Which order of content is most likely to suit them?
- [ ] Background — history of the problem — your analysis — your recommendation in the last minute.
- [x] Your recommendation — the two or three main reasons — the cost — what you need from them.
- [ ] A long personal story — the background — the recommendation.
- [ ] An apology for taking their time — the background — the recommendation.
::: solution
Busy decision-makers usually want the recommendation first and the support after it, so that they can decide how much detail they need. This is sometimes called putting the **bottom line up front**. A background-first order is natural in a narrative or in some academic talks, but here the audience might leave before reaching the point.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- ***Today my topic is about…*** is a common blend of *My topic today is…* and *Today I'm going to talk about…*. Use one or the other: ✓ *My topic today is green roofs.* ✓ *Today I'm going to talk about green roofs.* A topic *is* something; it is not *about* something.
- **Modesty at the start.** In some Chinese academic and formal settings an opening that lowers the speaker — 我准备得不够充分, 讲得不好的地方请大家批评指正 — is a polite convention, and a closing formula asking for criticism is common. Translated into English (*I'm sorry, I didn't prepare well; please forgive my poor English*), it is often taken literally: the audience lowers its expectations, or wonders why the speaker did not prepare. An English-speaking audience usually reads confidence as respect for their time. If you want a polite closing, *I'd welcome your comments and questions* does the same social work.
- **Point first or background first?** Claims that Chinese speakers "think in circles" go back to Robert Kaplan's 1966 article on "cultural thought patterns", which has been widely criticised for generalising from a few student essays; Chinese has its own ideal of getting straight to the point — 开门见山. What differs is often the genre: a school speech competition, a thesis defence and a business briefing have different conventions in both languages. For most English talks to colleagues and teachers, put the purpose and the preview in the first minute.
- **首先、其次、最后** map well onto *first*, *second*, *finally*, and Chinese speakers often organise talks clearly. The next chapter shows how to make the transitions between points as clear as the list.
:::

::: history
Planning a speech is one of the oldest subjects of formal education in Europe. Roman teachers divided rhetoric into five stages — invention (finding the arguments), arrangement, style, memory and delivery — in works such as the anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium* and Cicero's *De Inventione*, both from the first century BCE, and Quintilian's *Institutio Oratoria* (about 95 CE). Arrangement (*dispositio*) prescribed parts much like ours: the opening (*exordium*), a statement of the facts, a division announcing what would be proved (*partitio* — our preview), the proof, the refutation of opponents, and the closing (*peroratio*). Modern public-speaking textbooks, such as Lucas and Stob's *The Art of Public Speaking* (13th edition, 2020), keep this skeleton and add audience analysis and the one-sentence central idea.
:::

::: exercise Make the points parallel {level=2}
Rewrite each set of main points so that they are parallel.

(a) *The cost. How long does it take? Being safe.* (b) *Why people volunteer; the benefits for the community; volunteers' problems are…* (c) *Saving energy at home, transport, and what you eat.*
::: solution
Model answers: (a) *The cost, the time, the safety* — or *How much does it cost? How long does it take? Is it safe?* (b) *Why people volunteer, what volunteering gives the community, and what makes volunteers give up.* (c) *Saving energy at home, on the road, and at the table* — or *at home, in transport, and in food.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Budget the time {level=2 check="3"}
A fourteen-minute talk has an opening of about 1 minute and an ending of about 1 minute, and four main points of equal length in the body. Leaving no time for anything else, how many minutes should each main point take? Then say what you would do if your rehearsal of the first point took five minutes.
::: solution
The body has 14 − 1 − 1 = 12 minutes; divided by four points, that is **3** minutes each. If the first point takes five minutes in rehearsal, cut its sub-points to the strongest one or two (keep the best example, drop the second), or reduce the talk to three main points. Do not plan to "speak faster on the day".
:::
:::

::: exercise Rebuild an outline {level=3}
A student's outline for a ten-minute talk on *public transport in my home city* reads: *1. My city's population. 2. The history of the bus company. 3. The new metro line. 4. Ticket prices in 2010 and today. 5. My personal experience. 6. Problems: crowding, delays, cost. 7. Comparison with London. 8. Conclusion: transport is important.* Diagnose the problems and write a better plan, including a central idea, an opening idea and an ending idea.
::: hint
Start with the central idea. Which items are main points, which are examples, and which are background?
:::
::: solution
**Diagnosis:** eight items with no single pattern; background (population, history) given main-point status; the conclusion (*transport is important*) is a truism, not a central idea; the personal experience is isolated instead of being used as an opening or example; the comparison with London has no stated purpose.

**A model plan.** Central idea: *My city's new metro has made journeys faster, but crowding and cost mean it still doesn't serve the people who need it most.* Pattern: problem and solution (or topical).
- Opening: personal experience — the student's own ninety-minute commute before the metro, and forty minutes now.
- Point 1: what the metro has improved (speed, reliability), with one or two figures from the transport authority.
- Point 2: what it has not solved — crowding at peak times and fares that are high for lower-income workers; prices in 2010 and today as evidence.
- Point 3: what could help — off-peak fares, more feeder buses; London's off-peak pricing as one example, briefly.
- Ending: summary, central idea, and a return to the commute: *forty minutes is better than ninety — but only for those who can pay.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Speaking: a one-minute opening {level=3}
Choose a topic you know well (a hobby, your hometown, your field of study). Write and rehearse a one-minute opening for a class of students from other subjects that does all four jobs of [[#rule-opening]]. Record it, time it, and check: Could a listener say, after one minute, what your talk is about, why it matters to them, and what its main parts will be?
::: solution
Answers vary. A good opening has: a concrete attention-getter in the first one or two sentences (a story, a sourced fact, a question); a sentence that connects the topic to the listeners (*If you've ever…*, *This matters for anyone who…*); one sentence about your connection to it, if needed; a purpose statement; and a preview with two to four parallel points. It has no apology and no *my topic is about*. If the recording runs well over a minute, the opening probably contains background that belongs in the body.
:::
:::
