Zhou Xin and Gabriel Santos are both being interviewed for a part-time job as student guides at a city museum. Each is asked the same question: *Tell us about a time when you had to deal with a difficult situation in a team.*

**Zhou Xin:** *I think teamwork is very important. In my university, we often do group projects, and sometimes there are problems, for example some members don't do their work. In this situation, I think good communication is the key. We should talk to each other and find a solution together. I always try my best to help the team.*

**Gabriel:** *Last year I was in a group of four doing a survey project for a statistics course. Two weeks before the deadline, one member stopped replying to messages, and his section — the data analysis — wasn't started. I suggested a short call with the others, and we agreed I'd contact him directly rather than complain to the tutor straight away. It turned out he was ill and embarrassed to say so. We split his section between the three of us, he took on the final proof-reading when he recovered, and we handed in on time. We got a good mark, but the main thing I learned was to check in with people early, before a silence becomes a crisis.*

Zhou Xin's English is accurate and her views are sensible. But she has answered a different question — *What do you think about teamwork?* — and the panel has learned nothing about what she has actually done. Gabriel told one specific story, with a situation, his own actions, a result, and what he learned. Every sentence of it is evidence. The interviewers will score Gabriel's answer higher, not because his experience was more impressive, but because he gave them something to score.

This chapter is about interviews for jobs and for academic places: how interviewers decide, how to prepare, how to answer behavioural, motivational and situational questions, how to present your achievements without either boasting or hiding them, and how to ask good questions of your own. It applies the skills of [[presentations-and-discussion/handling-questions]] — listening to the whole question and answering it directly — to a conversation in which every answer is being weighed.

## What interviewers are looking for

::: definition Kinds of interview and interview question {#def-interview}
- In a **structured interview**, every candidate is asked the same questions, usually based on an analysis of the job, and the answers are scored against agreed criteria; in an **unstructured interview**, the interviewer asks whatever seems useful, and the judgement is overall.
- A **behavioural question** asks what you **did** in a past situation: *Tell me about a time when…* / *Give me an example of…*
- A **situational question** asks what you **would do** in a hypothetical situation: *What would you do if a visitor complained about…?*
- A **motivational question** asks why you want the role and what you would bring: *Why do you want to work here?* / *Why this programme?*
- A **competency** (also *criterion* or *selection criterion*) is a quality the role requires — teamwork, communication, problem-solving, reliability — often listed in the job advertisement or **person specification**.
:::

Research on selection has consistently found that structured interviews predict job performance better than unstructured ones. In Frank Schmidt and John Hunter's widely cited review of eighty-five years of research (1998, *Psychological Bulletin* 124:2), the estimated validity of structured interviews was 0.51 and that of unstructured interviews 0.38, on a scale where 1 would be perfect prediction. A later reanalysis by Paul Sackett and colleagues (2022, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 107:11) revised many of these estimates downward but still placed structured interviews among the strongest predictors of performance. Reviews of how interviews are structured (Campion, Palmer and Campion 1997, *Personnel Psychology* 50:3; Levashina et al. 2014, *Personnel Psychology* 67:1) describe the main features: questions based on the job, the same questions for every candidate, behavioural and situational questions, and rating scales with examples of good and poor answers.

::: theorem Answer the question that is being scored {#rule-scored}
In a structured interview, each question is designed to find evidence of a particular competency. Before answering, identify **which competency the question is testing**, and give an answer that provides **specific evidence** of it. Views about the competency (*Teamwork is very important*) are not evidence; an account of what you did is.
:::

::: proof
When interviewers score answers against criteria, a general opinion — however sensible — has nothing in it that matches the criteria, so it receives a low score. A specific example contains the actions and results that the scoring guide describes. This is why Zhou Xin's answer would be marked down even by an interviewer who liked her: the interviewer has no evidence to write in the box. Knowing that interviews are often structured also explains other features that candidates notice: the panel may read the questions from a sheet, take notes throughout, and not react much to answers. None of this is coldness; it is the procedure that makes the interview fairer and more predictive.
:::

## Preparing

::: application Preparing for an interview
1. **Read the advertisement and person specification** and list the competencies. For an academic place, read the programme description and the work of the people who would teach or supervise you.
2. **Prepare six to eight stories** from your study, work, volunteering or other activities, each of which shows one or more competencies. A story can be used for several questions.
3. **Prepare the predictable questions**: *Tell me about yourself*, *Why this role or programme?*, *What are your strengths?*, *What would you find difficult?*, *What questions do you have for us?*
4. **Rehearse aloud**, with a friend asking the questions in a different order from your notes. Time your answers: most should take one to two minutes.
5. **Check the practical details**: time, place or link, who will be on the panel, whether there is a presentation or a task.
:::

## Answering behavioural questions

::: definition STAR {#def-star}
**STAR** is a widely taught structure for answering behavioural questions:

- **Situation**: the context, briefly — where, when, what was happening.
- **Task**: what you had to achieve, or the problem you faced.
- **Action**: what **you** did, step by step, and why.
- **Result**: what happened, with evidence if possible — and, often added as a fifth step, what you **learned** or would do differently.
:::

::: theorem Tell one specific story, and make it yours {#rule-star}
In answering a behavioural question:

1. **Choose one specific occasion**, not a summary of what you usually do: *Last year, in a group of four…* rather than *In group projects, I always…*
2. **Keep the situation and task short** — a sentence or two each — and spend **most of the time on the action**.
3. **Say *I* for what you did**, and *we* only for what the group did together. The panel is assessing you.
4. **Give the result**, with a number or a concrete outcome if you can, and end with what you learned.
5. **Use the past simple** for the story (*I suggested…*, *we agreed…*), and the present for what you have learned (*I now check in early…*).
:::

::: proof
A specific story is more credible than a general claim, because it has details that the interviewer can believe or ask about; and a summary of what one "always" does is, by its nature, not evidence of anything in particular. Candidates often spend most of their answer on the situation, because the context feels necessary to understand the story; but the action is where the competencies are, so it deserves most of the time. *I* matters because a story told entirely in *we* leaves the interviewer unable to tell what the candidate contributed; for candidates used to presenting achievements as a group's, this can feel immodest, but in a selection interview it is simply accurate. The result closes the story, and the reflection — what you learned — shows the ability to improve, which many scoring guides reward explicitly.
:::

::: example Rebuilding Zhou Xin's answer {#ex-star}
Zhou Xin has a real story she could have told: in a group project on museum visitor numbers, two members disagreed about whether to use an online survey or interviews, and the project stalled for a week. She suggested doing a small number of interviews first and using them to design the survey, and the group agreed. Rebuild her answer using STAR and [[#rule-star]].
::: solution
**Situation.** *In my second year, I was in a group of five researching why visitor numbers at a local museum had fallen.*

**Task.** *Two members disagreed strongly about method — one wanted an online survey, the other wanted face-to-face interviews — and after a week we still hadn't started collecting data.*

**Action.** *I suggested that the two of them weren't really in conflict: interviews would tell us what to ask, and the survey would tell us how common each answer was. So I proposed that we do six short interviews first and use them to write the survey questions. I set up a call to discuss it, and I offered to do two of the interviews myself so that it wouldn't add work for the others.*

**Result.** *Both of them agreed, we finished the interviews in four days, and the survey got over a hundred responses. Our tutor said the combination of methods was the strongest part of the project.*

**Learning.** *What I took from it was that a disagreement about method is often a disagreement about what question you're answering — and that it helps to say that out loud early.*

**What changed.** One specific occasion; a short situation and task; a detailed action told in *I*; a concrete result with a number and an outside judgement (the tutor's); and a reflection in the present tense. The answer takes about a minute and a half to say.
:::
:::

::: warning *We* for everything
✗ *We organised the event, we found the speakers, we did the publicity, and we got two hundred people.*
✓ *We were a team of four. My part was the publicity: I designed the posters and ran the social media accounts, and I also persuaded two of the speakers to come.*

The panel cannot score the team. If every sentence uses *we*, they cannot tell whether you led the project or attended the meetings. Credit the team honestly in one sentence — *It was a team effort* — and then describe your own contribution with *I*.
:::

::: quiz
Which opening is the best start to an answer to *Give me an example of when you had to meet a tight deadline*?
- [ ] *I'm very good under pressure, and I always meet my deadlines.*
- [ ] *Deadlines are very important in any job, because…*
- [x] *Last spring I had three days to translate a forty-page report for a visiting delegation.*
- [ ] *We usually have lots of deadlines at university.*
::: solution
The third begins a specific story with a situation and a task in one sentence. The first is a claim without evidence; the second is an opinion about deadlines; the fourth is a generalisation.
:::

## Other common questions

### *Tell me about yourself*

This is often the first question, and it is not an invitation to give your life story. A useful shape is **present – past – future**: what you are doing now, the experience that led here, and why this role or programme is the natural next step. Keep it to about a minute.

::: example *Tell me about yourself* {#ex-yourself}
Write a one-minute answer for Gabriel Santos, a third-year history student applying to be a museum guide, who has worked weekends in a café and volunteered with a children's reading club.
::: solution
*I'm in my third year of a history degree here, specialising in the nineteenth-century city — so I've spent quite a lot of time in this museum's archive, actually. Alongside my studies, I've worked weekends in a café for two years, which has taught me a lot about dealing with the public when things are busy, and I volunteer with a children's reading club on Wednesday afternoons, where I've learned that the way you tell a story matters as much as the facts. This role brings those things together: talking to visitors of all ages about history, in a place I already know well. So I'm really keen to find out more about it.*

**Shape.** Present (*I'm in my third year*), past (the café and the reading club, each with what it taught him), future (*This role brings those things together*). Each part is connected to the job; nothing is there just because it is true.
:::
:::

### *Why this role?* / *Why this programme?*

A strong answer shows that you **know** something specific about the organisation or programme and **connect** it to your own interests or experience: *I was particularly interested in your outreach work with schools, because…* / *I'd like to work with Professor Adeyemi's group because my dissertation used the same method on a different kind of data.* A general answer — *It is a famous university* — tells the panel that you have not looked.

### Strengths, weaknesses and difficulties

For **strengths**, give one or two, each with brief evidence: *I'm good at explaining things clearly — the reading club children's parents often tell me their kids talk about the stories at home.* For **weaknesses** or **difficulties**, give a real one that is not central to the role, and say what you are doing about it: *I used to find it hard to say no to extra work, and I'd end up overcommitted. I now keep a weekly plan and check it before I agree to anything new.* A "weakness" that is really a strength in disguise (*I'm a perfectionist*) is widely recognised by interviewers and usually sounds evasive.

### Situational questions

Situational questions — *What would you do if…?* — call for the **second conditional** and *would*: *If a visitor complained that a tour was too long, I would apologise, ask what they'd most like to see, and offer to take them straight there.* The same principles apply as for behavioural answers: be specific, give your reasons, and, if you have faced a similar situation, say so: *Actually, something similar happened at the café…*

## Presenting achievements

::: theorem Describe achievements accurately, not modestly {#rule-achievements}
In an interview, describe what you have done **precisely and factually**: your role, your actions, and the outcome, with numbers or outside recognition where they exist. Let the **facts** carry the evaluation; you do not need to praise yourself (*I am excellent at…*), but do not reduce or hide your contribution (*I just helped a little*). Hedge only where you are genuinely uncertain.
:::

::: proof
Interviewers in most English-speaking settings expect candidates to talk about their own achievements, and they interpret what they hear at face value. A candidate who says *I only helped a bit with the publicity* when she in fact ran it is believed, and receives a lower score than one who says *I ran the publicity*. Factual description solves the apparent conflict between modesty and self-presentation: *We had two hundred people, compared with eighty the year before* is not boastful, but it lets the panel draw the conclusion. Hedges are part of accuracy, not of modesty ([[modality/hedging-boosting#rule-what-to-hedge]]): *I think it was partly because of the posters* is honest if you do not know; it is a mistake if you do.
:::

::: example Too modest, too boastful, accurate {#ex-achievement}
Write three versions of the same achievement — a student who organised a fundraising run that raised money for a local children's hospital — one too modest, one too boastful, and one accurate.
::: solution
- **Too modest.** *It wasn't really me — lots of people helped, and I just did some small things. It was nothing special.* — The panel learns nothing and may believe it.
- **Too boastful.** *I single-handedly organised the most successful charity event in the university's history. I'm a natural leader.* — Unverifiable claims and self-praise; the panel will discount it.
- **Accurate.** *I organised a five-kilometre charity run with three other students. I was responsible for getting permission from the council and the university, and for recruiting runners — I spoke at fourteen society meetings in two weeks. In the end a hundred and sixty people took part, and we raised about four thousand pounds for the children's hospital.*

The accurate version uses *I* for her own actions, credits the team in passing, and gives facts — fourteen meetings, a hundred and sixty runners, the amount raised — from which the panel can judge for themselves.
:::
:::

## Academic interviews

Interviews for postgraduate places, scholarships and research posts have their own emphasis: the panel wants to know whether you understand the field, whether your interests fit what they can offer, and whether you can discuss your own work critically.

- **Research interests.** Be able to say in two or three sentences what you want to study and why: *I'm interested in how small museums use volunteers, because almost all the research is on large national museums, and the small ones work very differently.* Then be ready to say more.
- **Your previous work.** Expect detailed questions about your dissertation or projects — your method, your results, what you would change. These are handled like questions after a talk ([[presentations-and-discussion/handling-questions]]): answer first, admit limitations, and say plainly what you do not know.
- **The fit.** Know whose work you would like to build on and why. *I read Dr Okafor's paper on volunteer motivation, and I'd like to apply her framework to small museums* shows real interest; *Your university has a good reputation* does not.
- **Discussion.** Academic panels sometimes push back on an answer to see how you respond. Treat it as the seminar it is ([[presentations-and-discussion/seminars-discussion#rule-disagree-seminar]]): consider the point, defend your view with reasons, and concede if they are right.

::: example An academic panel pushes back {#ex-academic}
Lin Mei is interviewing for a master's place in museum studies. She has said that she wants to research how small museums use volunteers. A panel member, Dr Okafor, says: *But surely small museums just use volunteers because they can't afford staff? Is there really anything to research?* Write Lin Mei's answer, following the advice above.
::: solution
*That's certainly part of it — cost is the reason most small museums give. But in the museum where I volunteered, the volunteers weren't just filling gaps: several of them had run the local history society for decades and knew more about the collection than anyone. So I think there's a real question about whether small museums depend on volunteers for **knowledge**, not only for labour — and if they do, what happens when those volunteers retire. I haven't seen much research on that, though I'd be glad to be pointed to some if it exists.*

**How it works.** She **concedes** the true part (*cost is the reason most give*), offers **evidence from her own experience**, **reframes** the question more precisely, and ends with an **honest limit** to her knowledge that also invites help. She neither gives up her idea nor treats the challenge as an attack ([[presentations-and-discussion/handling-questions#rule-challenge]]). The panel is not testing whether she is right, but whether she can think about the question.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A panel asks: *Why do you want to join this programme?* Which answer is strongest?
- [ ] *Because it is a very famous university with a high ranking.*
- [ ] *Because I want to get a good job after I graduate.*
- [x] *Mainly because of the placement year — I'd like to test my interest in policy work in practice, and the links you have with local councils would let me do that.*
- [ ] *My parents recommended it.*
::: solution
The third shows specific knowledge of the programme (the placement year, the council links) and connects it to the candidate's own goals. The others could be said about almost any programme, and the fourth gives the decision to someone else.
:::
:::

## Asking your own questions

::: theorem Ask informed questions {#rule-your-questions}
At the end of most interviews you will be asked *Do you have any questions for us?* Prepare **two or three questions** that show you have thought about the role and that you genuinely want answered: about the work itself, training and support, the team, or what success would look like. Avoid questions whose answers are on the website, and save detailed questions about pay and conditions for a later stage, unless they have not been made clear at all.
:::

::: proof
The candidate's questions are a last piece of evidence: an informed question shows research and real interest, while *No, I think you've covered everything* can suggest neither. Questions are also genuinely useful, because an interview is a decision for the candidate too, and some things — what a typical week is like, how new staff are supported — can only be learned by asking. Questions about pay and holidays are reasonable, but at a first interview they may give the impression that conditions matter more than the work; they are usually discussed once an offer is in prospect.
:::

Good questions:

- *What would a typical week look like for a guide in the first month?*
- *How are new guides trained — is there shadowing before the first tour?*
- *What do the best guides here do differently, in your experience?*
- For an academic interview: *How often do students in the group meet their supervisors?* / *Are there opportunities to present work in progress?*

## Online interviews and the end of the interview

Online interviews add the challenges of [[listening-and-conversation/calls-online-meetings]]: delay, turn-taking without visual cues, and the temptation to read notes off the screen. Look at the camera when you speak, keep notes to a few keywords placed near it, and, if the connection fails, say so at once: *Sorry, you froze for a moment — could you repeat the question?*

At the end, thank the panel briefly and, if appropriate, confirm your interest: *Thank you very much for your time. I've really enjoyed hearing about the role, and I'm very interested in it.* A short thank-you email afterwards is common in some sectors and countries and unusual in others; it does no harm if it is brief ([[pragmatics/writing-to-people]]).

::: note English and Chinese
- **Modesty.** Many Chinese speakers have been taught that presenting oneself modestly is good manners, and phrases such as 我只是做了一点小事 are polite in Chinese. Translated into an English interview, they are often taken literally. Modesty is valued in English-speaking interviews too, but it is shown by **accurate, factual** description rather than by understatement ([[#rule-achievements]]). This is a difference of convention, and many English speakers also find self-presentation uncomfortable; the techniques in this chapter help everyone.
- **我们 and *we*.** Describing achievements as the group's (我们) is natural and often appropriate. In an interview, add your own role in *I* sentences ([[#rule-star]]).
- **Your name.** Panels may not know which of your names is the family name. Introduce yourself clearly: *I'm Zhou Xin — Zhou is my family name, and Xin is fine.* Using an English name is a personal choice, not an expectation.
- **Tenses.** ✗ *I have graduated in 2023.* ✓ *I graduated in 2023.* A finished time (*in 2023*, *last year*) takes the past simple; the present perfect is for experience up to now without a time: *I've worked with children for three years.* ([[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect]])
- **Formality.** *Dear interviewers, it is my great honour to be here* sounds overly formal in most interviews. *Thank you for inviting me — I'm very pleased to be here* is warm and suitable.
:::

::: history
Selection by examination has a long history — China's imperial civil-service examinations (科举) were written tests held from the Sui and Tang dynasties until 1905, and Britain's Northcote–Trevelyan Report (1854) proposed competitive examinations for the civil service — but the research on interviews is a twentieth-century subject. Early reviews, such as Ralph Wagner's in 1949, found the typical unstructured interview unreliable. John Flanagan's critical incident technique (1954) — collecting accounts of specific behaviour that made the difference between success and failure in a job — laid the ground for questions about specific past events. Gary Latham and colleagues described the **situational interview** in 1980, and Tom Janz the **patterned behaviour description interview** in 1982, the ancestors of today's *What would you do if…?* and *Tell me about a time when…* questions. Reviews from the 1990s onwards established that structure makes interviews fairer and more predictive.
:::

::: exercise Speaking: a mock interview {level=3}
Choose a real job advertisement or programme description. List three competencies it asks for, and prepare a STAR story for each. Ask a friend to interview you with *Tell me about yourself*, two behavioural questions, one situational question and *Do you have any questions for us?*, in any order. Record the interview and check each answer against [[#rule-scored]], [[#rule-star]] and [[#rule-achievements]].
::: solution
Answers vary. For each answer, check: Did you identify the competency? Did you tell one specific story? Was most of the time on the action? Did you use *I* for your part? Did you give a concrete result and a reflection? Was the length one to two minutes? Did you describe achievements factually, without understatement or self-praise? Did your own questions show research? If an answer began with an opinion (*I think teamwork is very important*), practise starting with the situation instead.
:::
