Five students and a tutor are discussing a short article that argues cities should make public transport free. The tutor, Dr Amara Nwosu, opens: *So — is the author right? Should buses be free?* Here are the first ninety seconds.

**Tom:** *I think it's obviously a good idea. If buses are free, more people use them, fewer people drive, less pollution. And the article says ticket machines and inspectors cost a lot anyway, so you save money there too. And actually the other thing is that poorer people spend more of their income on transport, so it's fairer —*

**Lucía:** *But does it actually get people out of cars? I thought the article said —*

**Tom:** *— yeah, but that's a separate issue. The main thing is fairness.*

**Dr Nwosu:** *Mm. Others? Aiko?*

**Aiko:** *I agree with Tom.*

**Dr Nwosu:** *OK… Sun Hao, you look like you're thinking about something.*

**Sun Hao:** *Yes. I think… the article's evidence on car use is weak. The example city, the number of bus passengers went up a lot, but most new passengers were people who walked or cycled before. So pollution didn't change much.*

**Lucía:** *That's exactly what I was trying to say!*

Sun Hao had the most important point of the discussion, and Lucía had started to make it. But Tom held the floor with *and… and actually the other thing is…*, brushed Lucía's question aside, and Sun Hao waited for an invitation that came only because the tutor noticed his face. In many seminars, it never would have come.

This chapter is about taking part in seminars and group discussions: preparing to contribute, taking the floor, building on other people's points, disagreeing constructively, and bringing others in. The everyday versions of these skills — taking turns, back-channelling, repair, polite disagreement — were taught in [[listening-and-conversation/turn-taking]], [[listening-and-conversation/clarification]] and [[listening-and-conversation/opinions]]. Here they are applied to a group of four to fifteen people discussing ideas, usually with an expectation that everyone will contribute.

## What a seminar is for

::: definition Seminar and group discussion {#def-seminar}
A **seminar** is a teaching session in which a small group discusses a topic, a reading or a participant's work, usually led by a tutor; a **tutorial** is a smaller version (in some British universities, one to four students). A **group discussion** is any discussion among several people with a shared task — to understand a text, to solve a problem, to reach a decision. In all of them, participants are expected not only to listen but to **contribute**: to give views with reasons, to respond to each other, and to help the group make progress.
:::

In many English-speaking universities, seminar contribution is expected and sometimes assessed. Tutors are usually looking less for correct answers than for **engagement with the material and with each other**: a point that refers to the reading, a question that moves the discussion on, a response that takes someone else's idea further. A short, well-placed contribution is worth more than a long one, and a good question is a contribution.

::: theorem Prepare something to say {#rule-prepare-seminar}
Before a seminar, prepare **two or three points** and **one question** about the reading or topic, each written in one sentence with a page reference or an example: *The author's evidence on car use comes from one city (p. 4) — is that enough?* Say one of them early in the discussion.
:::

::: proof
Preparation makes contribution possible under time pressure. Composing a point from nothing while following a fast discussion in a second language is very hard; saying a point you have already formulated is much easier, because you only need to find the moment and connect it to what has been said. A page reference anchors the point in the text and makes it easy for others to respond to. Speaking early matters because the first contribution is usually the hardest: the longer a participant stays silent, the higher the threshold becomes, and the more the group's pattern of speakers settles without them. A short early contribution — even a question — lowers the threshold for the rest of the session. Reading notes that make this possible are described in [[academic-reading/notes-and-summaries]].
:::

## Taking the floor

In conversation, people take the turn at **transition points** — the moments when the current speaker's turn could be complete ([[listening-and-conversation/turn-taking#def-trp]]). A seminar works the same way, with two differences: there are more people competing for each transition point, and turns are longer, so there are fewer of them. A participant who waits for a long, empty pause may wait for the whole session.

::: theorem Getting into a group discussion {#rule-floor}
1. **Watch for transition points**: the end of a grammatical unit with a falling tone, the speaker looking around the group, a summary such as *…so that's my view.*
2. **Signal before you speak**: lean forward, catch the tutor's or the speaker's eye, raise a hand slightly, or take an audible breath. Online, use the hand-raise button or say your name: *Sorry — Sun Hao here — could I add something?* ([[listening-and-conversation/calls-online-meetings#rule-online-turns]])
3. **Start with a short preface** that holds the floor while you begin: *Can I just come in here?* / *Could I add something to that?* / *Just to pick up on what Lucía said…* / *Sorry, can I go back to the question of car use?*
4. **Make the point early in your turn**, then give the reason or reference. A long build-up gives others time to take the floor back.
:::

::: proof
Turn-taking research describes a system in which the next speaker is usually the one who starts first at a transition point (Sacks, Schegloff and Jefferson 1974, *Language* 50:4). In a large group, transition points are contested, and the person who starts quickest gets the turn. Non-verbal signals let a would-be speaker "book" the next turn before the transition point arrives, so that the current speaker or the tutor can hand it over. A preface does two jobs: it claims the turn in a polite form that others recognise, and it buys a second to start the sentence. Putting the point first follows from the same competition: if the turn is interrupted after ten words, the important ten words have already been said.
:::

Useful prefaces, from gentle to firm:

| job | phrases |
|---|---|
| asking to come in | *Can I just come in here?* / *Could I add something?* / *May I say something about that?* (formal) |
| linking to the last speaker | *Just to pick up on what you said about…* / *Following on from that…* / *On that point…* |
| going back | *Can I go back to what Lucía was saying?* / *Sorry, just going back to the question of…* |
| changing direction | *Could we look at this from a different angle?* / *Something we haven't talked about yet is…* |
| holding the floor | *If I could just finish…* / *Sorry, just one more thing…* / *Let me just finish this point.* |

::: example Getting in at the right moment {#ex-floor}
Here is Tom's opening turn again. Mark the transition points where Sun Hao could have come in, and write what he might have said.

*I think it's obviously a good idea. ① If buses are free, more people use them, fewer people drive, less pollution. ② And the article says ticket machines and inspectors cost a lot anyway, so you save money there too. ③ And actually the other thing is that poorer people spend more of their income on transport, so it's fairer.* ④
::: solution
1. **After ①** — the end of a complete opinion, with a falling tone on *idea*. But nothing has been said yet that Sun Hao wants to respond to, so it is early.
2. **After ②** — the best moment. Tom has just said *fewer people drive, less pollution*, which is exactly the claim Sun Hao doubts. A preface and the point: *Can I just come in on the pollution point? I'm not sure the evidence supports it — in the article's example city, most new bus passengers used to walk or cycle.*
3. **After ③** — possible, but Tom has moved on to cost, so Sun Hao would need to go back: *Sorry — can I go back to the pollution point for a second?*
4. **After ④** — the end of the turn, but by now Lucía has jumped in. Sun Hao can still link to her: *I think Lucía's right to ask that — the article's evidence on car use is quite weak…*

The point is not to interrupt in the middle of a sentence but to be ready at the end of one — with the preface already in your mouth.
:::
:::

- [ ] Wait until the tutor asks you directly.
- [ ] Start speaking immediately, in the middle of her sentence.

::: warning Waiting to be invited
✗ Sitting silently with a point ready, waiting for the tutor to ask you, or for a long pause.
✓ Signalling, then taking the floor at the next transition point with a short preface: *Could I add something here?*

In many English-speaking seminars, tutors do invite quieter students, but they cannot always tell who has something to say, and a fast group may not leave a pause long enough to feel like an invitation. Silence is often read as having nothing to say, not as politeness. Taking the floor briefly and politely is not rude; it is what the discussion needs from you.
:::

## Building on other people's points

A seminar is not a series of separate mini-talks. The contributions that move a discussion forward are the ones that **connect** to what someone else has said.

::: theorem Connect your contribution {#rule-build}
Begin a contribution by **linking it** to an earlier one — by name, or by a short summary of the point — and then say what you are doing with it:

- **adding**: *And on top of what Aiko said, …*
- **supporting**: *That fits with what the author says on page 6…*
- **extending**: *Taking that a step further, …*
- **qualifying**: *I agree, but only up to a point — …*
- **questioning**: *Can I ask what you mean by "fair"?*
- **challenging**: *I see it slightly differently…*
- **summarising**: *So it sounds like we agree on X but not on Y.*
:::

::: proof
Linking makes a contribution easier to understand, because the listeners already have the earlier point in mind and only need to process the new part. It also shows that the speaker has been listening — the main thing tutors look for — and it gives credit to the earlier speaker, which keeps the group cooperative. Naming the move (*I agree, but only up to a point*) tells the group in advance how to hear what follows, as signposts do in a talk ([[presentations-and-discussion/signposting-a-talk]]). Summarising is especially valuable in a long discussion: it gives everyone a chance to check where the group stands, and the person who does it often shapes what happens next.
:::

::: example Labelling contributions {#ex-moves}
Here is the seminar a few minutes later. Label each contribution with its link and its move from [[#rule-build]].

**Dr Nwosu:** *So Sun Hao's saying the pollution argument is weak. What does that do to the article's case?*

**Ravi:** *I think it weakens it, but it doesn't kill it. Going back to what Tom said about fairness — that argument doesn't depend on car use at all.*

**Lucía:** *True, but I'd question whether "free" is the fairest way to do it. If it's free for everyone, it's free for rich people too. Couldn't you just make it free for people on low incomes?*

**Aiko:** *Following on from that — the article mentions on page 7 that checking incomes costs money too. So maybe free for everyone is simpler.*

**Sun Hao:** *That's a good point. So it sounds like the real question is not "free or not", but whether simplicity is worth the cost.*
::: solution
- **Ravi**: links to the tutor's question and to Tom (*Going back to what Tom said*); **qualifies** (*weakens it, but doesn't kill it*) and **supports** Tom's fairness argument on new grounds.
- **Lucía**: links to Ravi (*True, but*); **challenges** (*I'd question whether…*) and proposes an alternative as a question.
- **Aiko**: links to Lucía (*Following on from that*); **adds** evidence from the text with a page reference — and this time she says more than *I agree*.
- **Sun Hao**: links to Aiko (*That's a good point*); **summarises** and reframes the debate.

Every contribution starts from someone else's. Compare the opening ninety seconds, in which Tom's turn linked to nothing and Aiko's *I agree with Tom* added nothing.
:::
:::

Which contribution best builds on the previous speaker, who has just said: *The article assumes everyone lives near a bus route*?

- [ ] *I agree.*
- [x] *That's a really important point — and in rural areas, a free bus doesn't help if there's only one a day.*

## Disagreeing constructively

The shape of a polite disagreement — preface, partial agreement, softened opinion, reason, alternative — is in [[listening-and-conversation/opinions#rule-disagree]]. In a seminar, disagreement is not only allowed but valued: it is how a group tests ideas. Two additions make it academic.

::: theorem Disagree with the idea, using the evidence {#rule-disagree-seminar}
In an academic discussion:

1. **Address the idea, not the person**: *I'm not sure that argument works* rather than *You're wrong.*
2. **Ground the disagreement** in the text, the data or an example: *On page 4, though, the author says…* / *In the case study, the numbers went the other way.*
3. **Hedge the strength of your claim** to match your evidence, not to hide your view ([[modality/hedging-boosting]]): *I'd say the evidence is fairly weak* is clear; *Maybe possibly it's not totally certain* is not.
4. **Be open to being wrong**: *Unless I've misread it…* / *I might have missed something, but…* — and if you are persuaded, say so: *OK, that's convinced me.*
:::

::: proof
Academic discussion depends on separating claims from the people who make them, so that a weak argument can be criticised without anyone losing face ([[pragmatics/politeness-face]]). Grounding a disagreement in evidence moves the discussion from opinion against opinion to a question that the group can examine together — what does page 4 actually say? Hedging calibrates the claim: academic English expects claims to be as strong as the evidence and no stronger, and seminar talk follows the same convention, which is why the example in [[modality/hedging-boosting#ex-seminar]] matters. Openness to being wrong is not weakness; it is the attitude the seminar is designed to train, and it makes others more willing to concede in return.
:::

::: example From blunt to constructive {#ex-constructive}
Rewrite each contribution so that it disagrees constructively ([[#rule-disagree-seminar]]).

1. *No, Tom, that's just wrong. Free buses don't reduce pollution.*
2. *I don't like this article.*
3. *Maybe it's possible that perhaps the author might not be completely right, possibly.*
::: solution
1. *I'm not sure the pollution argument works, actually. In the article's own example, most new passengers used to walk or cycle, so car use didn't change much.* — addresses the argument, not Tom, and grounds it in the text.
2. *I found the article less convincing than I expected — mainly because almost all the evidence comes from one city. I'd want to see whether it's the same elsewhere.* — turns a reaction into a reasoned judgement with a specific criticism.
3. *I think the author overstates the case a bit — the evidence on car use seems quite thin.* — keeps one or two hedges (*a bit*, *seems*) so that the strength matches the evidence, and removes the rest, which made the view impossible to find.
:::
:::

## Asking questions in a discussion

Questions are among the most valuable contributions in a seminar, and they are the easiest way in for a participant who is not yet confident. They come in three main kinds:

- **Clarifying** another speaker: *When you say "fair", do you mean fair to passengers or to taxpayers?* / *Could you say a bit more about that?* ([[listening-and-conversation/clarification]])
- **Probing** — asking for reasons or evidence: *What makes you say that?* / *Is there evidence for that in the reading?* / *Would that still be true in a smaller city?*
- **Opening** a new line: *Can I ask something slightly different — does the article say anything about who would pay?*

A probing question is a polite form of challenge: it invites the speaker to defend a claim rather than telling them that it is wrong.

## Bringing others in

Who speaks, and how much, is not a side issue in group discussion. In a study of groups solving a range of problems, Anita Woolley and colleagues (2010, *Science* 330:6004) found evidence of a "collective intelligence" factor that predicted how well a group performed across tasks. It was not strongly related to the average or highest individual intelligence of the members, but it was related to the members' social sensitivity and to how **equally** they took turns: groups in which a few people dominated the conversation tended to do less well. The study does not show that equal turn-taking causes better results, and later attempts to replicate a single collective-intelligence factor have had mixed results (for example Credé and Howardson, *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 2017), but it is consistent with what tutors observe: a discussion in which everyone contributes usually finds more.

::: theorem Bringing quieter members in {#rule-bring-in}
1. **Invite by connecting to something the person has said or knows**, rather than putting them on the spot: *Aiko, you mentioned the page 7 point earlier — what did you make of it?* / *Ravi, you've worked on transport data — does this match what you've seen?*
2. **Open the floor to a part of the group**: *Can we hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet?* / *What do the rest of you think?*
3. **Give time**: after inviting someone, wait; do not answer the question for them.
4. **Manage a dominant speaker politely**: *That's really helpful, Tom — can we hear some other views on it?* / *Let's hold that thought and come back to it.*
5. **Credit the contribution**: *That's a good point — so we've got two issues now.*
:::

::: proof
A direct invitation with no context (*Aiko, what do you think?*) can feel like a test, especially for a nervous speaker or a second-language user, who then has to compose an answer under the group's gaze. An invitation that refers to something the person has already said or knows gives them a starting point and a reason to speak. Opening the floor to "someone who hasn't spoken" lets quieter members volunteer without being singled out. Managing a dominant speaker is easiest when it credits them first, so that the redirection is about the group, not about them. These moves are not only for tutors: any participant can bring others in, and doing so is itself a valued contribution.
:::

::: example Chairing a discussion {#ex-chair}
Lucía has been asked to lead a group of five students preparing a joint presentation. Tom has spoken for most of the first ten minutes; Aiko and Sun Hao have hardly spoken. Write four things Lucía could say, in order, to rebalance the discussion without embarrassing anyone.
::: solution
1. **Summarise and credit**: *OK, so Tom's given us a really clear case for focusing on fairness.* — acknowledges Tom before redirecting.
2. **Open the floor**: *Before we decide, it'd be good to hear from everyone. Has anyone got a different angle?* — invites without singling out.
3. **Invite by connection**: *Sun Hao, you said earlier you'd looked at the data on car use — would that work as one of our sections?* — gives Sun Hao a specific starting point.
4. **Protect the new contribution**: if Tom comes in again immediately — *Hang on, Tom, let's let Sun Hao finish — then we'll come back to you.*

Then she waits after each invitation. Silence of a few seconds after a question is the time it takes to answer it.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Speed of entry.** Learners from many backgrounds — not only Chinese speakers — report that seminar discussion in English moves too fast to find a way in. Partly this is processing time in a second language; partly it is a convention: in many English-speaking seminars, people take the floor at transition points rather than waiting for a pause or an invitation. Preparing a preface (*Could I add something?*) and a point in advance makes the speed manageable.
- **Teachers and disagreement.** In some classrooms, in China and elsewhere, disagreeing with a teacher or the author of a set reading can feel disrespectful. In most English-speaking seminars, disagreeing with the reading — or with the tutor — is expected, provided it is reasoned and courteous ([[#rule-disagree-seminar]]). Tutors often hope for it.
- **A stereotype to set aside.** The image of the "passive" Chinese or Asian student who memorises rather than thinks has been widely challenged. David Kember (2000, *Higher Education* 40:1) reviewed research showing that Asian students' approaches to study are often misread by Western teachers, and that students adapt readily when a course makes its expectations clear. The difficulty in seminars is usually one of unfamiliar conventions and language confidence, not of thinking.
- ***I agree with you* and 我同意.** *I agree* alone is a back-channel more than a contribution in an English seminar. Add a reason, an example or a page reference: *I agree — and the point on page 7 supports that.*
- **Names.** In a seminar, referring to classmates by first name (*As Lucía said…*) is normal in most English-speaking universities, and tutors are often addressed by first name too, though not always; follow what others do. Chinese names are usually said in the order their owners give them: *Sun Hao*, family name first, unless he has said otherwise.
:::

::: history
The seminar as a teaching form grew up in eighteenth-century German universities such as Göttingen and Halle, where small groups of advanced students worked on texts with a professor; in the nineteenth century, the research seminar spread to universities in other countries, alongside the lecture. The scientific study of how groups talk began later. Robert F. Bales's *Interaction Process Analysis* (1950) gave researchers a way to code every act in a small-group discussion — giving or asking for opinions, suggestions and information, showing agreement, disagreement, tension or solidarity — and showed that group discussions have regular patterns, such as a few members doing most of the talking. Conversation analysis, from Harvey Sacks, Emanuel Schegloff and Gail Jefferson's 1974 paper on turn-taking, explained the moment-by-moment mechanics of who speaks next. Woolley and colleagues' 2010 study of collective intelligence linked the equality of turn-taking to group performance.
:::

## Where this leads

Formal debate turns disagreement into a structured contest ([[presentations-and-discussion/debate-persuasion]]); meetings add the job of reaching a decision ([[presentations-and-discussion/meetings-negotiation]]). The turn-taking, repair and disagreement skills behind this chapter are in [[listening-and-conversation/turn-taking]], [[listening-and-conversation/clarification]] and [[listening-and-conversation/opinions]]; the conventions of politeness and face that shape disagreement are explained in [[pragmatics/politeness-face]] and [[pragmatics/directness-across-cultures]]. Group discussions held online follow [[listening-and-conversation/calls-online-meetings]].

::: summary
- Seminars expect **contribution**: views with reasons, responses to others, and questions ([[#def-seminar]]).
- **Prepare** two or three points and a question with page references, and say one early ([[#rule-prepare-seminar]]).
- **Take the floor** at transition points: signal first, use a short preface, and put the point early in your turn ([[#rule-floor]]). Do not wait to be invited.
- **Connect** each contribution to an earlier one and name the move — adding, extending, qualifying, questioning, challenging, summarising ([[#rule-build]]).
- **Disagree with the idea, using the evidence**, with hedges that match your evidence ([[#rule-disagree-seminar]]).
- **Bring others in** by connecting to what they know, opening the floor, waiting, and crediting contributions; more equal turn-taking is associated with better group performance (Woolley et al. 2010; [[#rule-bring-in]]).
:::

::: exercise Speaking: a recorded discussion {level=3}
With three or four classmates or friends, hold a ten-minute discussion on a short article or question of your choice (for example, *Should homework be abolished in primary schools?*). Record it. Afterwards, count for yourself: how many times you took the floor, how many of your contributions linked to someone else's, how many questions you asked, and whether you brought anyone in. Set yourself one target for the next discussion.
::: solution
Answers vary. A useful self-assessment might read: "I spoke four times; two contributions linked to someone else's (*Following on from Ana's point…*), two did not. I asked one probing question. I didn't bring anyone in. I waited too long the first time — about three minutes. Target: make my first contribution in the first two minutes, and link every contribution to an earlier one.\" If one person took more than half the turns, discuss as a group how to share the floor next time ([[#rule-bring-in]]).
:::
:::
