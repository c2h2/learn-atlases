The committee of a community sports centre meets to decide its summer opening hours. The meeting is supposed to take thirty minutes. Here is how it begins.

**Hiroshi:** *Right, shall we start? So, summer. Anyone got any thoughts?*

**Olumide:** *Well, the thing is, the pool's been really busy this year, and actually I was talking to someone from the swimming club, and they said the changing rooms are a mess on Saturdays —*

**Elif:** *That's a cleaning issue, though, isn't it? We should talk about the cleaning contract.*

**Olumide:** *Sure, but —*

**Hiroshi:** *Oh, and before I forget — the car park lights are broken again.*

Forty minutes later, the committee has talked about the swimming club, the cleaning contract, the car park lights and a complaint about the café, and has not decided the opening hours. Nobody is sure who will deal with the lights. Two weeks later, at the next meeting, Wang Fang chairs. She opens differently:

**Wang Fang:** *Good evening, everyone. We've got thirty minutes and one main decision to make: the summer opening hours. There are two other short items — the car park lights and the cleaning contract — which I've put at the end. Olumide, could you start us off with the usage figures from last summer?*

The people are the same. The difference is that the meeting now has a **purpose**, an **agenda** and a **chair** who uses them. This chapter is about the language and the structure of meetings — chairing, taking part, reaching and recording decisions — and about negotiation, in which people with different interests try to reach an agreement that both can accept. It builds on discussion skills from [[presentations-and-discussion/seminars-discussion]], on the polite suggestions and refusals of [[modality/modality-politeness]], and, for meetings held on screen, on [[listening-and-conversation/calls-online-meetings]].

## How a meeting works

::: definition Meeting vocabulary {#def-meeting}
- The **agenda** is the list of items to be discussed, in order, usually circulated before the meeting. **Any other business** (**AOB**) is a final item for matters not on the agenda.
- The **chair** (also *chairperson*; in some organisations *convener* or *facilitator*) leads the meeting: opens and closes it, introduces each item, manages the discussion and keeps time.
- The **minutes** are the written record of a meeting: who attended, what was discussed, what was **decided**, and the **action points** — who will do what, by when.
- In formal meetings, a **motion** is a proposal put to a vote; it is **seconded** by another member, may be **amended**, and is **carried** (passed) or **defeated**. A **quorum** is the minimum number of members needed for decisions to be valid.
:::

The formal vocabulary comes from parliamentary procedure, codified for associations in *Robert's Rules of Order* (1876; 12th edition 2020). Most workplace and student meetings are far less formal, but the words *agenda*, *minutes*, *action point* and *AOB* are used everywhere, and the basic structure is the same.

::: theorem Chairing a meeting {#rule-chair}
A chair usually:

1. **Opens**: greets, states the **purpose** and the **time available**, and goes through the agenda: *We've got thirty minutes and one main decision to make.*
2. **Introduces each item** and invites someone to start: *Item two is the opening hours. Olumide, could you start us off?*
3. **Manages the discussion**: invites views, brings quieter members in, keeps to the item: *Can we park the cleaning question until item four?*
4. **Summarises and decides**: *So we seem to agree on later closing on weekdays. Are we happy with that?*
5. **Records actions**, with a name and a date: *So, Elif, you'll contact the cleaning company by Friday?*
6. **Keeps time**: *We've got five minutes left on this item.*
7. **Closes**: summarises the decisions and actions, takes AOB, and confirms the next meeting.
:::

::: proof
Each step solves a problem that the first sports-centre meeting had. Stating the purpose tells participants what kind of contribution is needed — information, opinions, a decision. An agenda prevents the drift from topic to topic, because the chair can point to it: a new topic is not rejected, only postponed (*parked*). Summarising after discussion is the step that turns talk into a decision: it forces the group to say whether it agrees. Recording actions with a named person and a date is what makes decisions happen — "someone will look into it" usually means no one will. Steven Rogelberg's *The Surprising Science of Meetings* (2019), drawing on research on meetings in organisations, makes similar recommendations: build the agenda around the questions the meeting must answer, invite only the people needed, and keep meetings as short as their purpose allows.
:::

Useful language for chairing:

| stage | phrases |
|---|---|
| opening | *Shall we make a start?* / *Thanks for coming. The aim of today's meeting is to…* / *We've got until half past.* |
| moving through the agenda | *Let's move on to item three.* / *The next item is…* / *Can we come back to that under AOB?* |
| inviting | *Olumide, would you like to start?* / *What does everyone else think?* / *Wang Fang, you haven't had a chance to come in yet.* |
| keeping on track | *I think we're getting a bit off the point.* / *Can we park that for now?* / *Let's stick to the agenda.* |
| deciding | *So, are we agreed that…?* / *Can I take it that everyone's happy with…?* / *Shall we put it to a vote?* |
| actions | *Who's going to take that on?* / *So the action is: Elif to…, by Friday.* |
| closing | *Just to sum up…* / *Is there any other business?* / *Thanks, everyone. Same time next month.* |

::: example Rescuing the first meeting {#ex-rescue}
Go back to the first sports-centre meeting at the start of this chapter. At three moments the chair, Hiroshi, could have changed its course: when he opened it, when Elif raised the cleaning contract, and when he mentioned the car park lights. Write what he could have said each time.
::: solution
1. **Opening.** Instead of *So, summer. Anyone got any thoughts?*: *Right, let's make a start. We've got thirty minutes, and the main thing we need to decide today is the summer opening hours. Olumide, you've got last summer's figures — could you start us off?* — purpose, time and a named first speaker ([[#rule-chair]], step 1).
2. **The cleaning contract.** When Elif says *We should talk about the cleaning contract*: *That's a fair point, Elif — can we park it for now and take it at the end, so we make sure we get the hours decided?* — the topic is postponed, not rejected (step 3).
3. **The car park lights.** Instead of raising a new topic himself: *I'll add the car park lights under AOB.* — the chair is the person who most needs to stay on the item.

At the end, he would summarise the decision on opening hours, check agreement, and give each action a name and a date (steps 4–5). None of these sentences is difficult; what matters is that the chair says them at the right moment.
:::
:::

### Taking part

A participant's job is to prepare for the items on the agenda, contribute to the item being discussed, and leave with clear actions.

- **Proposing**: *I'd like to suggest that we…* / *What if we opened an hour later on Sundays?* / *Could we look at closing at ten instead?*
- **Supporting a proposal**: *I'd go along with that.* / *That makes sense to me.* / *I'm in favour.*
- **Agreeing with conditions**: *I could live with that, as long as…* / *I'm happy with that, provided we review it in September.*
- **Objecting politely**: *I'm not sure that would work, because…* / *I have some reservations about…* ([[listening-and-conversation/opinions#rule-disagree]]).
- **Clarifying**: *Sorry, does that mean…?* / *Just to be clear, are we deciding this today?*
- **Volunteering**: *I can take that on.* / *Leave that with me — I'll get back to everyone by Tuesday.*

::: example Turning discussion into a decision {#ex-decision}
Here is part of the second meeting. Identify where Wang Fang summarises, checks agreement and records actions, and write the action points as they would appear in the minutes.

**Olumide:** *Last summer the pool was busiest between six and nine in the evening. Mornings were quiet.*

**Elif:** *So could we open later in the morning and close later at night? Say ten till ten?*

**Hiroshi:** *I'd go along with that, as long as the staff are OK with the late finish.*

**Wang Fang:** *Good point. So the proposal is ten till ten on weekdays, subject to checking with staff. Is everyone happy with that in principle?*

**All:** *Yes.* / *Fine.*

**Wang Fang:** *Great. Hiroshi, could you talk to the staff this week?*

**Hiroshi:** *Sure — I'll do it by Thursday.*

**Wang Fang:** *And Olumide, could you put the new hours on the website once Hiroshi's confirmed?*

**Olumide:** *Will do.*
::: solution
- **Summarises**: *So the proposal is ten till ten on weekdays, subject to checking with staff.* — includes Hiroshi's condition, so his concern is part of the decision.
- **Checks agreement**: *Is everyone happy with that in principle?* — *in principle* marks that the decision depends on the staff.
- **Records actions**: *Hiroshi, could you talk to the staff this week?* and *Olumide, could you put the new hours on the website…?* — each with a person and a time.

**Minutes (extract):**

> **Item 2. Summer opening hours.** Agreed in principle: weekday opening 10:00–22:00 from July, subject to staff agreement.
> **Actions:** HS to consult staff on late finish — by Thursday. OB to update website once HS confirms.

Minutes record decisions and actions, not the whole conversation. Initials are commonly used for names in action points.
:::
:::

::: warning *Discuss about*
✗ *Let's discuss about the opening hours.* ✗ *We need to discuss about it.*
✓ *Let's discuss the opening hours.* ✓ *We need to talk about it.* ✓ *We had a discussion about it.*

*Discuss* is a transitive verb and takes a direct object, without *about*; the noun *discussion* takes *about* or *of*. The error is common among learners whose languages say "discuss about", including speakers who carry over 讨论一下关于…; it is also influenced by *talk about*, which does need *about*.
:::

::: quiz
At the end of an agenda item, the chair says: *OK, so someone will look into the parking problem.* What is missing?
- [ ] A vote.
- [x] A named person and a deadline for the action.
- [ ] A motion and a seconder.
- [ ] A summary of the discussion.
::: solution
An action point needs a person and a time: *Olumide, could you contact the council about the parking by next Friday?* Without them, the action is unlikely to happen. A vote or a motion is needed only in formal meetings, and a summary would help but would not make anyone responsible.
:::
:::

## Negotiation

A meeting decides among people who share a goal. A **negotiation** brings together parties whose interests differ at least in part, and who each need something from the other. Negotiation is not only for business: a student society asking the university for a room, two flatmates dividing the bills, and a sports club agreeing a price for a pitch are all negotiating.

The most influential modern account of negotiation is Roger Fisher and William Ury's *Getting to Yes*, written at the Harvard Negotiation Project (1981; 3rd edition with Bruce Patton, 2011). They contrasted **positional bargaining**, in which each side states a position and then concedes step by step, with **principled negotiation**, built on four points.

::: definition Principled negotiation {#def-principled}
Fisher, Ury and Patton (2011) set out four principles:

1. **Separate the people from the problem**: deal with relationships and emotions directly, and treat the negotiation as a shared problem rather than a contest.
2. **Focus on interests, not positions**: a **position** is what someone says they want (*We want the pitch for £40 an hour*); an **interest** is why they want it (*We need to keep the club's fees low enough for families*).
3. **Invent options for mutual gain**: before deciding, generate several possible agreements that could serve both sides' interests.
4. **Insist on using objective criteria**: base the agreement on standards independent of either side's will — market prices, precedent, expert opinion, a fair procedure.

They also introduced the **BATNA** — the **best alternative to a negotiated agreement**: what you will do if no agreement is reached. Knowing your BATNA tells you when to accept and when to walk away.
:::

::: theorem Find the interests behind the positions {#rule-interests}
When the other side states a position, ask **why**: *What's most important to you about that?* / *Can you help me understand why Saturday mornings matter so much?* / *What would make this work for you?* Then state your own interests, not only your position: *What we really need is…* Agreements are found where the interests can both be met, even when the positions conflict.
:::

::: proof
Positions conflict more often than interests do. Fisher and Ury illustrate this with a story of two men in a library — a story that goes back to the management writer Mary Parker Follett's lectures on conflict in the 1920s: one wants the window open, the other wants it closed, and they argue about how far to open it. The librarian asks each one why. One wants fresh air; the other wants to avoid a draught. She opens a window in the next room, which gives fresh air without a draught — a solution that no compromise between the two positions would have found. Behind most positions there are several interests, and some of them are shared, some different but compatible, and only some opposed. Asking about interests also changes the tone of a negotiation, because it treats the other side's needs as legitimate, which is the first principle in practice.
:::

::: example Positions and interests {#ex-interests}
The manager of the sports centre, Elif Yılmaz, is negotiating with Dmitri Volkov, the coach of a youth football club, over the hire of the main pitch. Their opening positions are:

- **Elif:** *The pitch is £60 an hour, and Saturday mornings are already booked by the adult league.*
- **Dmitri:** *We can pay £35 an hour, and we need Saturday mornings.*

List the interests that might lie behind each position, and suggest options for mutual gain.
::: solution
**Possible interests.**
- *Elif (the centre)*: cover the cost of maintaining the pitch; keep the adult league, which has booked for years; fill the pitch at quiet times; show the council that the centre serves young people (which may affect its funding).
- *Dmitri (the club)*: keep fees low for families; train at a time when children are free and parents can drive them; have a good surface for matches; a stable, long-term arrangement.

**Shared or compatible interests:** both want the pitch used, both benefit from a long-term arrangement, and the centre's interest in serving young people matches the club's purpose.

**Options for mutual gain.**
1. Saturday **afternoons**, which are usually quieter, at a lower rate.
2. A **season booking** at a discount in exchange for guaranteed income.
3. Weekday evening training on the smaller pitch at a low rate, plus a few Saturday-morning **matches** when the adult league is away.
4. The club helps with something the centre needs — volunteers at the centre's open day, for example — in part exchange.

None of these splits the difference between £60 and £35; each tries to meet more of both sides' interests.
:::

### The language of negotiation

Negotiation language is tentative and conditional, because each offer must leave room for the other side to respond, and because a concession should be linked to something in return.

| move | phrases |
|---|---|
| opening and framing | *Thanks for making the time. We're hoping to find something that works for both of us.* |
| exploring interests | *What's most important to you here?* / *Could you tell me a bit more about…?* / *What's driving the Saturday requirement?* |
| proposing | *We'd like to propose…* / *One option would be…* / *How would you feel about…?* |
| conditional offers | *If you could…, we would be able to…* / *We could agree to that, provided that…* / *As long as…, we'd be happy to…* |
| rejecting politely | *I'm afraid that would be difficult for us.* / *I'm not sure we could go that far.* / *That's more than we can manage at the moment.* |
| counter-offers | *What if we met you halfway?* / *Could we look at…instead?* / *We could go to £45 if…* |
| referring to criteria | *What do other centres charge?* / *Is there a standard rate for youth clubs?* / *What would be fair here?* |
| checking and summarising | *So, if I understand you correctly…* / *Let me just check what we've agreed so far.* |
| closing | *I think we've got a deal.* / *Shall we put that in writing?* / *Let me send you a summary by email.* |

::: theorem Link every concession to a condition {#rule-conditional}
When you make a concession, **attach a condition** to it, using a conditional: *If you could commit to the whole season, we could reduce the rate.* / *We could move to Saturday afternoon, provided the rate comes down.* Use *would* and *could* to make offers tentative, and *if*, *provided that*, *as long as* and *on condition that* to make them conditional.
:::

::: proof
An unconditional concession gives something away and signals that more may follow; a conditional one keeps the negotiation balanced, because each step forward by one side requires a step by the other. The conditional form also protects the speaker: if the condition is not accepted, the offer is not made. *Would* and *could* add distance, so that a proposal sounds like an option to consider rather than a demand ([[modality/modality-politeness#rule-suggestions]]); they also allow either side to withdraw without loss of face. *Provided that* and *on condition that* are more formal than *as long as*, and *on condition that* sounds firmest.
:::

::: example A negotiation, step by step {#ex-negotiation}
Here is part of Elif and Dmitri's negotiation. Label each move and say which principle of [[#def-principled]] it applies.

**Dmitri:** *Can I ask why Saturday mornings are so difficult?*

**Elif:** *The adult league has had that slot for six years. If we move them, we'll probably lose them.*

**Dmitri:** *That makes sense. For us, the real issue is that the kids are at school during the week. Saturday afternoon could work, actually, as long as it's before four — younger children can't stay out late in winter.*

**Elif:** *Two till four would be fine. On the price — I can't go down to thirty-five on the main pitch. What do other clubs your size pay?*

**Dmitri:** *The rugby club across town charges youth teams forty.*

**Elif:** *OK. If you could book the whole season in advance, I could match that — forty an hour.*

**Dmitri:** *I think we can work with that. Let me check with the committee, and I'll confirm by Monday.*

**Elif:** *Great. I'll send you a summary of what we've said.*
::: solution
1. *Can I ask why…?* — **exploring interests** (principle 2).
2. *The adult league has had that slot…* — Elif **states her interest** honestly (principle 2), which also builds trust (principle 1).
3. *That makes sense. For us, the real issue is…* — Dmitri **acknowledges** her interest (principle 1) and **states his own**; then a **new option** (principle 3) with a condition (*as long as it's before four*).
4. *I can't go down to thirty-five… What do other clubs your size pay?* — a polite **rejection**, then an appeal to an **objective criterion** (principle 4).
5. *If you could book the whole season, I could match that* — a **conditional concession** ([[#rule-conditional]]).
6. *Let me check… I'll confirm by Monday* / *I'll send you a summary* — **closing**, with an action and a written record.

Both positions have moved, but neither side has simply split the difference: the time changed because of interests, and the price because of a criterion and a condition.
:::
:::

::: warning *That's difficult* means no — usually
✗ Hearing *That would be quite difficult for us* or *We'll certainly think about it* and reporting to your team that the other side has agreed.
✓ Treating these as probable refusals and checking: *So is that a no for now, or is there something that would make it possible?*

In British English especially, refusals in negotiation are often indirect: *I'm afraid that might be a bit tricky*, *I'm not sure we could do that*, *Let me get back to you on that*. They are polite ways of saying no or not yet, not invitations to wait. Indirect refusals exist in every language — Chinese 研究研究 and 再考虑一下 can work the same way — but the particular forms differ, and second-language listeners may take them literally. When it matters, check politely ([[pragmatics/requests-refusals]]).
:::

::: quiz
Which offer best follows [[#rule-conditional]]?
- [ ] *OK, we'll lower the price.*
- [ ] *You must book the whole season.*
- [x] *If you could book the whole season, we could lower the price to forty.*
- [ ] *Maybe we could lower the price, maybe not.*
::: solution
The third links a concession (a lower price) to a condition (a season booking), using *if… could*. The first concedes with nothing in return; the second is a demand; the fourth is so vague that it is neither an offer nor a refusal.
:::
:::

::: quiz
In Fisher, Ury and Patton's terms, what is a BATNA?
- [ ] The best price you can hope to get.
- [ ] The point at which both sides' positions overlap.
- [x] What you will do if no agreement is reached.
- [ ] A formal proposal put to a vote.
::: solution
The **best alternative to a negotiated agreement** is your fallback. If the club's BATNA is a free school field with a poor surface, any agreement must be better than that to be worth accepting; if the centre's BATNA is leaving the pitch empty on Saturday afternoons, it has a reason to agree.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Indirect refusals.** Both English and Chinese have conventional indirect refusals, and both sides of an intercultural negotiation can misread them. *We'll consider it* in English, like 我们考虑一下, may mean no; equally, a Chinese colleague's 有点难度 may be heard by an English speaker as a real but solvable difficulty. When an answer matters, check politely: *Just so I'm clear — is that something you could do, or is it not possible at the moment?*
- **Relationship and task.** Many negotiators from every background value building a relationship before discussing terms, and research on intercultural negotiation describes differences in how much time different groups spend on it. These are tendencies with wide individual variation, not national rules ([[pragmatics/directness-across-cultures]]). Fisher and Ury's first principle — separate the people from the problem — is compatible with both: attend to the relationship, and discuss the problem on its merits.
- **Seniority in meetings.** In some meetings, in China and elsewhere, junior members speak after senior ones or not at all. In many English-speaking workplaces, chairs actively invite juniors, and the expectation is that anyone with relevant information will offer it. *Could I add something here, from the data side?* is an acceptable way to come in.
- ***I'm afraid*** does not mean fear in negotiation: *I'm afraid that won't be possible* is a polite way of saying *Unfortunately*.
- **Minutes and 会议纪要.** English minutes usually record decisions and action points briefly, with names or initials; they rarely record who said what, unless the meeting is formal. Write *Agreed:* and *Action:* lines rather than a narrative of the discussion.
:::

::: history
Rules for meetings came from parliaments. Henry Martyn Robert, an American army engineer who had found himself unable to chair a church meeting, published his *Pocket Manual of Rules of Order for Deliberative Assemblies* in 1876; as *Robert's Rules of Order* it became the standard guide to motions, amendments and voting for many associations, and its twelfth edition appeared in 2020. In the 1920s, the management writer Mary Parker Follett argued that conflicts could often be settled by **integration** — finding a solution that satisfies both sides' underlying desires — rather than by domination or compromise. That idea became central to the negotiation research of the 1970s and 1980s; Roger Fisher and William Ury's *Getting to Yes* (1981), from the Harvard Negotiation Project, made it widely known, together with the terms *interests*, *positions* and *BATNA*. Research on meetings themselves grew later; Steven Rogelberg's *The Surprising Science of Meetings* (2019) summarises much of it.
:::

## Where this leads

Interviews are a special kind of meeting, in which one side asks the questions and both are deciding ([[presentations-and-discussion/interviews]]). Polite suggestions, offers and refusals are in [[modality/modality-politeness]]; requests and refusals across cultures in [[pragmatics/requests-refusals]] and [[pragmatics/directness-across-cultures]]. Meetings held by phone or video add their own difficulties ([[listening-and-conversation/calls-online-meetings]]), and the email that follows a meeting — the summary, the action points, the confirmation — is a piece of writing in its own right ([[pragmatics/writing-to-people]]).

::: summary
- A meeting has an **agenda**, a **chair**, **minutes** and **action points**; formal meetings add motions, seconding, amendments and votes ([[#def-meeting]]).
- The chair opens with purpose and time, introduces items, manages discussion, **summarises and decides**, records **actions with a name and a date**, keeps time, and closes ([[#rule-chair]]).
- Participants propose, support, agree with conditions, object politely, clarify and volunteer.
- Principled negotiation: separate people from the problem; focus on **interests, not positions**; invent options for mutual gain; use **objective criteria**; know your **BATNA** (Fisher, Ury and Patton 2011; [[#def-principled]]).
- Ask **why** behind positions; agreements are found where interests can both be met ([[#rule-interests]]).
- Make offers tentative with *would* and *could*, and **link every concession to a condition** with *if*, *provided that*, *as long as* ([[#rule-conditional]]).
- Treat *That would be difficult* as a probable refusal, and check politely when it matters.
:::

::: exercise Plan a negotiation {level=3}
A student society wants to hold a weekly language exchange in the university café on Wednesday evenings, free of charge. The café manager wants customers who buy food and drink, and closes at six. Plan the negotiation for the society's side using [[#def-principled]]: list both sides' likely interests, at least three options for mutual gain, one objective criterion you could refer to, and your BATNA. Then write the society's opening proposal and one conditional offer.
::: hint
Why does the café close at six? What could the society bring that the café wants?
:::
::: solution
**Interests.** Society: a regular, central, free or cheap space; a relaxed setting; evenings, when members are free. Café: income; covering staff costs if it stays open; attracting new regular customers; not losing space at busy times.

**Options.** (1) The café stays open until eight on Wednesdays, and the society guarantees a minimum number of members buying a drink. (2) The society uses the café after closing with one member of café staff, and pays a small fee covered by a one-pound contribution from each member. (3) The society meets at four to six, before closing, at the café's quietest time. (4) The café advertises the event as part of a "café evening" and keeps any extra sales.

**Objective criterion.** What other rooms on campus cost per hour for student societies; how many customers the café normally has between four and six.

**BATNA.** A free seminar room in the library, which is less pleasant but available.

**Opening proposal.** *We'd love to run a weekly language exchange here — it's usually thirty to forty people. We know your quiet time is late afternoon, and we think we could bring you a lot of new customers.*

**Conditional offer.** *If you could stay open until eight on Wednesdays, we could make sure everyone buys at least one drink, and we'd advertise the café on all our posters.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Speaking: chair a meeting {level=3}
With three or four others, hold a fifteen-minute meeting with a written agenda of three items (for example, planning a class trip: destination, date, cost). Take turns to chair one item each. Record the meeting. Afterwards, check: did each chair open the item, invite views, summarise, check agreement and assign actions with names and dates? Write the minutes in under 100 words.
::: solution
Answers vary. A successful meeting has: an opening that states the purpose and time; for each item, an introduction, invited contributions from everyone, a summary sentence, an explicit check (*Are we agreed?*), and at least one action point with a name and a date ([[#rule-chair]]). The minutes list decisions and actions only. If one person spoke much more than others, or an item ended without a decision, note what the chair could have said at that moment.
:::
