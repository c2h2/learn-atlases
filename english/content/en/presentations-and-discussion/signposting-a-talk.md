Here are two versions of the same forty seconds from the middle of a talk on how cities can reduce summer heat. The content is identical; only a few words differ.

**Version A.** *Trees lower street temperatures through shade and evaporation. In one study, streets with full tree cover were several degrees cooler in the afternoon. Trees take decades to grow. White roofs reflect sunlight. They can be installed in a day. Their effect is mainly on the building itself.*

**Version B.** *So that's the first approach: trees, which cool the street through shade and evaporation. In one study, streets with full tree cover were several degrees cooler in the afternoon. The catch, of course, is that trees take decades to grow. Which brings me to my second approach — one that works much faster: white roofs. White roofs reflect sunlight, and they can be installed in a day. But — and this is the important point — their effect is mainly on the building itself, not on the street.*

Reading Version A, you can work out the structure: you can look back and see that the first three sentences are about trees and the last three about roofs. A listener cannot look back. In Version B, the speaker closes one section (*So that's the first approach*), announces the next (*Which brings me to my second approach*), marks a contrast (*The catch, of course*), and flags the key point (*and this is the important point*). Those few words are **signposts**, and they are to a talk what headings, paragraphs and bold type are to a page.

This chapter teaches the signposting language of presentations, following the plan of [[presentations-and-discussion/planning-a-talk]]: introducing, previewing, starting, moving on, digressing and returning, emphasising, summarising and concluding. Linking words inside written paragraphs — *however*, *moreover*, *therefore* — are covered in [[essay-writing/signposting]]; here the focus is on speech, where signposts are more frequent, more explicit and carried as much by the voice as by the words.

## What signposts are

::: definition Signpost {#def-signpost}
A **signpost** (or **signposting expression**) is a word or phrase in which speakers talk about their own talk: what they are going to say, what they have said, how the parts relate, and what matters most. *I'll look at three things*, *Let's move on to…*, *To go back to my first point…*, *What I want to stress is…* and *To sum up* are signposts. Linguists call language about the discourse itself **metadiscourse**.
:::

Signposts are of two sizes. **Macro-signposts** mark the large structure: the purpose, the preview, the start and end of each main point, the summary and the conclusion. **Micro-signposts** are the small words that link sentences: *so*, *well*, *now*, *and then*, *right*, *OK*.

::: theorem Large signposts help listeners most {#rule-macro}
In a talk, the signposts that most help listeners — especially second-language listeners — are the **macro-signposts** that announce and mark the structure. Small markers such as *so* and *well* are natural and useful in rhythm, but they cannot replace explicit statements of what the talk is doing.
:::

::: proof
Craig Chaudron and Jack Richards (1986, *Applied Linguistics* 7:2) gave versions of a recorded lecture to second-language listeners. Versions with macro-markers — expressions such as *What I'm going to talk about today is…* and *Let's go back to the beginning* — improved recall of the lecture, while versions with only micro-markers such as *well*, *OK* and *so* did not produce a comparable improvement. Later studies (for example Jung 2003, *Modern Language Journal* 87:4) also found that explicit signalling helped second-language listeners recall lecture content. The explanation is the one given in [[listening-and-conversation/how-listening-works]]: a listener builds meaning partly from the top down, and macro-signposts supply the frame. Micro-markers are signals of rhythm and turn, not of content.
:::

## Signposts for each part of a talk

The phrases below are grouped by job. You do not need all of them; choose two or three for each job that feel natural to you, and use them until they come without thinking.

### Introducing the topic and purpose

- *Today I'd like to talk about…* / *I'm going to talk about…*
- *In this talk, I want to argue that…* / *What I'd like to do today is show you…*
- *The aim of this presentation is to…* (more formal)
- *I've been asked to give you an update on…*

### Previewing the structure

- *I'll look at three things: first…, then…, and finally…*
- *I've divided my talk into three parts.*
- *I'll start by…, then I'll move on to…, and I'll finish with…*
- *There are two questions I want to answer.*

### Starting the first point

- *So, let's start with…* / *Let's begin with…*
- *First of all, …* / *To begin with, …*

### Finishing one point and moving to the next

- *So that's [the first point]. Now let's turn to…*
- *That brings me to my second point, which is…*
- *Moving on to…* / *Let's move on to…*
- *So we've looked at the problem. What about the solutions?*
- *OK — that's the background. Now for the main part.*

### Giving examples, adding, contrasting

- *Let me give you an example.* / *Take…, for instance.* / *A good example of this is…*
- *Another thing to bear in mind is…* / *On top of that, …* (informal)
- *On the other hand, …* / *The catch is…* / *The problem with that is…*

### Digressing and returning

- *Just as an aside, …* / *Incidentally, …* / *I'll come back to that later.*
- *Anyway, getting back to…* / *Where was I? Yes — the second reason.*

### Referring back and forward

- *As I mentioned earlier, …* / *Going back to what I said about…*
- *I'll say more about this in a moment.* / *We'll come back to this when we look at…*

### Emphasising

- *What I want to stress is…* / *The key point here is…*
- *This is really important.* / *If you remember one thing from today, …*
- *And — this is the interesting part — …*

### Summarising and concluding

- *So, to sum up, …* / *To summarise, …* / *Let me just recap.*
- *So, in conclusion, …* (more formal) / *To wrap up, …* (informal)
- *So what does all this mean? …*
- *Thank you for listening. I'd be happy to take any questions.*

::: theorem Close the old section, then open the new one {#rule-transition}
A transition between main points in a talk usually has **two halves**: a phrase that **closes** the previous section, often with a short summary (*So that's how the heat builds up*), and a phrase that **opens** the next one, using the same label as in the preview (*Now let's look at what we can do about it*). In a short talk one half can be dropped, but not both.
:::

::: proof
The closing half tells a listener who drifted that a section is over and what it amounted to; the opening half tells everyone where the talk is now. Together they create the spoken equivalent of the white space and the heading between two sections of a report. Using the preview's labels makes the map consistent: if the preview promised *three approaches — trees, roofs and water*, the listener can tick them off: *That brings me to the third approach — water.* A new label (*Now, about fountains…*) makes listeners wonder whether this is the third point or something extra.
:::

::: example Labelling the signposts {#ex-label}
Here is the opening and first transition of a short talk by Ngozi Eze, a hospital pharmacist, to new nursing staff. Underline the signposts and say what each does.

*Good morning, everyone, and welcome. I'm Ngozi, one of the pharmacists here. What I'd like to do in the next fifteen minutes is show you how medicines are ordered on the wards, so that you know who to call when something goes wrong. I'll cover three things: how an order is made, how it reaches you, and what to do when it doesn't. Let's start with the order itself. … So that's how an order is made. You'll notice I've said nothing yet about urgent medicines — I'll come back to those at the end. Now, the second part: how the order actually reaches your ward.*
::: solution
- *What I'd like to do in the next fifteen minutes is show you…* — introduces the purpose (and states the length, which audiences appreciate).
- *I'll cover three things: …, …, and …* — previews the structure, with three parallel labels.
- *Let's start with…* — starts the first point.
- *So that's how an order is made.* — closes the first section with a summary ([[#rule-transition]]).
- *I'll come back to those at the end.* — refers forward, and so answers a question listeners might be forming.
- *Now, the second part: how the order actually reaches your ward.* — opens the next section, repeating the label from the preview (*how it reaches you*).

There are six signposts in about a hundred words — far more than a written text would use — and none of them sounds excessive when spoken.
:::
:::

## The voice is a signpost too

The words of a signpost are only half of it. Listeners also hear structure in the voice: a speaker who is starting a new section pauses, takes a breath, and starts higher in the pitch range; a speaker who is finishing a section slows slightly and lets the pitch fall low. These "spoken paragraphs" — sometimes called **paratones** — are what make a transition audible even if the listener misses the words. [[connected-speech/intonation-meaning#rule-range]] described how starting a tone unit higher marks something as new; in a talk, the same signal marks a new section.

::: theorem Pause around macro-signposts {#rule-pause-signpost}
Before a macro-signpost, **pause** — a full second or more is normal in a talk. Say the signpost in its own tone unit, starting **higher** than the speech before it, with the nucleus on the new label: *‖ That brings me to my ↘**sec**ond point ‖ — ↘**cost**.* After a summary, let the pitch **fall low** before you pause.
:::

::: proof
Without the pause and the pitch reset, a signpost runs into the surrounding sentences and is heard as one more piece of content; with them, it stands out as a boundary. This is also why reading a script tends to flatten structure: when people read aloud, they tend to pause by punctuation rather than by meaning, and the long pauses that mark sections in natural speech are often shortened ([[presentations-and-discussion/delivery]]). Pauses also give second-language listeners time to finish processing the previous section before the next begins.
:::

::: quiz
Which transition is most helpful to a listener in a talk with the preview *I'll talk about the causes, the effects and the solutions*?
- [ ] *Moreover, solutions.*
- [ ] *Now let me talk about some other things.*
- [x] *So those are the main effects. That leaves the most hopeful part: the solutions.*
- [ ] *Furthermore, the third point is that we should plant more trees.*
::: solution
The third closes the previous section (*So those are the main effects*), opens the next using the preview's label (*the solutions*), and even says why it matters. *Moreover* and *Furthermore* are written linkers that add a point within an argument; they do not mark the start of a new section and sound stiff in speech. *Some other things* gives no label.
:::
:::

## Spoken and written signposts

Many signposts that learners know from writing sound stiff or bookish in a talk. Speech prefers shorter, more personal forms with *I*, *we*, *you* and *let's*.

| written (essay) | spoken (talk) |
|---|---|
| *Firstly, … Secondly, …* | *First, …* / *Let's start with…* / *The first thing is…* |
| *Moreover, …* / *Furthermore, …* | *And another thing is…* / *On top of that, …* / *Also, …* |
| *In conclusion, …* | *So, to sum up, …* / *So what does this all mean?* |
| *As stated above, …* | *As I said a minute ago, …* |
| *This essay will examine…* | *I'm going to look at…* / *Today I want to show you…* |
| *It should be noted that…* | *What's worth noticing here is…* |

This is a tendency, not a rule: a formal lecture or a conference keynote uses more written forms than a team briefing, and *In conclusion* is perfectly acceptable in most talks. But a talk full of *moreover* and *furthermore* sounds like an essay being read, which is often what it is.

::: example From written to spoken signposts {#ex-spoken}
Wang Jiahui wrote this paragraph as part of a talk script. Rewrite it so that it sounds spoken, keeping the content.

*Firstly, the causes of the problem shall be discussed. Moreover, it should be noted that the data were collected in 2023. Secondly, solutions will be proposed. In conclusion, it is hoped that the audience will understand the significance of the issue.*
::: solution
*I'll start with what's causing the problem — and I should say up front that the data I'm using are from 2023. Then I'll suggest a few solutions. And by the end, I hope you'll see why this matters.*

Changes: the passives (*shall be discussed*, *will be proposed*, *it is hoped*) become *I*-sentences, which are shorter and sound like a person speaking; *Firstly … Secondly* becomes *I'll start with … Then*; *Moreover, it should be noted that* becomes *I should say up front*; and *In conclusion* — which was misused here, since this is a preview, not a conclusion — becomes *by the end*. This is a preview, so it would come in the first minute of the talk.
:::
:::

::: warning *Next, I will introduce…*
✗ *Next, I will introduce the second part. Next, I will introduce the data. Next, I will introduce our conclusion.*
✓ *That brings me to the second part.* / *Let's look at the data.* / *So what can we conclude?*

*Introduce* in English usually means presenting a person to someone, bringing in something new (*introduce a law*), or opening a topic for the first time. Using it for every transition — a common carry-over from 下面我来介绍… — makes a talk sound mechanical, and *Next* repeated at every boundary tells the listener only that something else is coming, not what. Vary the signposts and name the content.
:::

## Emphasis and repetition

Signposts that emphasise tell the listener which of the many things they are hearing to keep. Two kinds of grammar are especially useful.

- **Wh-clefts** put the important part at the end, where English places new information: *What really matters here is the cost.* / *What surprised us was the speed.* (See [[complex-sentences/emphasis-inversion]].)
- **Direct address** brings the listener in: *If you remember one thing from this talk, let it be this: …* / *Notice that…* / *You might be wondering why…*

::: theorem Repeat on purpose {#rule-repeat}
In a talk, **repeat the central idea** at least three times — in the opening, at an important moment in the body, and in the conclusion — and **repeat key labels** in the preview, the transitions and the summary. Vary the wording of the surrounding sentence, but keep the labels the same.
:::

::: proof
A reader who misses a point can reread it; a listener can only hear it again if the speaker says it again. Repetition in speech is therefore not padding but the spoken equivalent of rereading. Keeping labels constant matters because a listener who hears *the cost*, then *the financial side*, then *the money question* may think these are three different things — in writing, the variation looks elegant; in speech, it is confusing. The wording around the labels can vary, and should, so that the repetition does not sound mechanical.
:::

::: example Too much and too little {#ex-balance}
Here are two transitions from a five-minute talk. Say what is wrong with each and write a better one.

1. *OK. So. Right. Now, as I said in my introduction, I'm now going to move on from my first point, which was about the causes, to my second point, which is going to be about the effects, which I will now talk about.*
2. *Effects. People get ill.*
::: solution
1. **Over-signposting.** Three micro-markers (*OK. So. Right.*), and then thirty words to say "point 1 is done, point 2 begins". In a five-minute talk, that is a noticeable share of the time, and the audience's attention drops before the content arrives. Better: *So those are the causes. What about the effects?*
2. **Under-signposting.** A bare label and then content. A listener who was taking notes has not heard the transition at all. Better: *So that's why it happens. Now — what does it actually do to people? The first effect is on health.*

The right amount depends on the length and formality of the talk: a forty-minute lecture needs more explicit signposting (and mid-talk summaries) than a three-minute update.
:::
:::

::: example Signposting a mini-talk {#ex-minitalk}
Add signposts to this unsignposted two-minute talk by Rahul Mehta, so that it has an introduction, a preview, transitions, an emphasis and a conclusion.

*Our team wants to change the weekly report. The report takes about three hours a week to write. Most people only read the first page. We propose a one-page summary with a link to the full data. It would save time. People would read it. The data would still be available. We'd like to try it for a month.*
::: solution
*Good morning, everyone. I'd like to suggest a small change to the weekly report — and I'll keep this short. I'll explain what the problem is, what we're proposing, and why we think it'll work.*

*So, first, the problem. The report takes about three hours a week to write — and as far as we can tell, most people only read the first page.*

*That brings me to our proposal: a one-page summary, with a link to the full data for anyone who needs it.*

*Why do we think it'll work? Three reasons. It would save time. People would actually read it. And — this is the important part — none of the data would be lost; it would all still be there.*

*So, to sum up: shorter report, same data, more readers. What we'd like is to try it for a month and then review it. Thanks — any questions?*
:::
:::

::: quiz
In which situation is *Just as an aside…* the right signpost?
- [x] The speaker briefly mentions an interesting fact that is not part of the main argument, and will then return to it.
- [ ] The speaker begins the last main point.
- [ ] The speaker summarises the talk.
- [ ] The speaker answers a question from the audience.
::: solution
An aside is a short digression from the main line of the talk. It should be marked going out (*Just as an aside*, *Incidentally*) and coming back (*Anyway, back to…*), so that listeners know that it is not a new main point.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence uses a wh-cleft to emphasise the key point?
- [ ] *The most important thing is very important.*
- [x] *What we found most surprising was how quickly the effect wore off.*
- [ ] *It surprised us, the effect, how quickly it wore off.*
- [ ] *Very surprising was the effect wearing off quickly.*
::: solution
*What we found most surprising was…* is a wh-cleft: the *What*-clause sets up the frame and the new, emphasised information comes at the end. The first is circular; the third is a loose spoken pattern that is acceptable in conversation but not an emphasis signpost; the fourth is unidiomatic.

::: note English and Chinese
- **下面 / 接下来 and *Next*.** Chinese presentations often move between sections with 下面我来介绍… or 接下来. Their direct equivalents, *Next, I will introduce…*, are grammatical but become mechanical when repeated ([[#rule-transition]]). Vary them: *Let's turn to…*, *That brings me to…*, *Now, what about…?*
- ***Firstly … lastly*** is fine, but in speech *first … second … finally* or *the first thing … the second thing … and lastly* sound more natural. ✗ *At last, I will talk about the cost* — *at last* means 'after a long wait' (终于); use *finally* or *lastly*.
- ***In a word* (总之).** In English *In a word* introduces a one-word summary (*In a word: no.*). For a summary of a talk, use *To sum up* or *So, in short*.
- **好，那么 and *OK, so*.** Many speakers of every language fill transitions with a small word. *OK, so…* and *Right, …* are natural spoken English in moderation; repeated before every sentence they become a habit listeners notice ([[#ex-balance]]).
- ***我的报告就到这里*** corresponds well to *That's all from me* or *That brings me to the end of my talk* — but first give the summary, which Chinese and English talks both value, and then the hand-over: *Thank you. I'm happy to take questions.*
:::

::: history
The word **metadiscourse** is usually credited to the linguist Zellig Harris, who used it in 1959 for the parts of a text that comment on the text itself. The idea was developed for writing in the 1980s, notably by William Vande Kopple (1985), and became widely known through Ken Hyland's *Metadiscourse: Exploring Interaction in Writing* (2005). For speech, research on university lectures — which grew with the recording of lecture corpora in Britain and the United States from the 1990s — showed how heavily lecturers signpost, and studies of second-language listeners, beginning with Chaudron and Richards (1986), showed that explicit macro-signposts help comprehension. Teaching materials for academic and business presentations have taught signposting phrases ever since.
:::

## Where this leads

The voice that carries signposts — pausing, pitch reset and pace — is developed in [[presentations-and-discussion/delivery]]. Signposts for slides and charts (*As you can see here…*) follow in [[presentations-and-discussion/visual-aids]], and signposts in discussion (*Going back to what Ana said…*) in [[presentations-and-discussion/seminars-discussion]]. For listeners, signposts are the cues that make note-taking possible: compare [[listening-and-conversation/gist-and-detail]]. Their written equivalents are in [[essay-writing/signposting]].

::: summary
- **Signposts** are metadiscourse: language about the talk itself ([[#def-signpost]]).
- **Macro-signposts** — purpose, preview, transitions, summary — help listeners far more than small markers like *so* and *well* (Chaudron and Richards 1986; [[#rule-macro]]).
- A transition **closes** the previous section and **opens** the next, using the preview's labels ([[#rule-transition]]).
- **Pause** before a macro-signpost and start it higher; let the pitch fall low after a summary ([[#rule-pause-signpost]]).
- Spoken signposts are shorter and more personal than written linkers: *Let's turn to…*, not *Moreover, …*.
- **Repeat** the central idea and the key labels on purpose; vary the words around them ([[#rule-repeat]]).
- Avoid both over-signposting and bare labels; avoid *Next, I will introduce* at every boundary.
:::

This is the transcript of the body of a four-minute talk by Ivana Kovač on *why our club should move its meetings online*. Add or improve signposts, using [[#rule-transition]], [[#rule-repeat]] and at least one emphasis, and remove over-signposting.

Find the preview the talk should have had, and use its labels in every transition. Is there a summary?

::: solution
A model answer:

*I want to suggest that we move our meetings online, and I'll look at three things: the problems with our current room, what we'd gain online, and what we might lose.*

*So, first, the problems with the room. Many of our members live a long way out — some travel an hour each way. The room also costs money, and in winter it's often cold.*

*That brings me to what we'd gain by going online. People could join from home, and — this is the part I think matters most — we could record the talks for anyone who can't make it.*

*Now, to be fair, there's a cost too. Some people really prefer meeting in person, and we'd lose some of the chat over coffee.*

*So, to sum up: less travel, no rent, recordings for everyone — but less face-to-face contact. On balance, I think it's worth trying for a term. Thank you — any thoughts?*

The micro-marker strings (*OK so right, um*; *OK so now next*) are gone; there is a preview with three labels, each transition repeats a label, *Moreover* and *Furthermore* are replaced, the key point is emphasised, and the ending summarises and hands over.
