The question is: *Should cities make public transport free for all passengers?* Here are the last sentences of three introductions to it.

> **A.** *This essay will discuss the advantages and disadvantages of free public transport.*

> **B.** *Public transport is used by millions of people every day.*

> **C.** *Free public transport is a poor use of a city's money: the people it most needs to attract — drivers — care more about speed and convenience than about the fare, so the same money would cut more car journeys if it were spent on faster and more frequent services.*

A tells the reader what the essay will be about, which the question has already done. B states a fact nobody disputes. Only C answers the question: it takes a position (*no*), gives the main reason in a form that can be argued against, and tells the reader what the rest of the essay will have to show. C is a **thesis statement**. You may disagree with it — many people would — and that is precisely what makes it a thesis.

The previous lesson, [[essay-writing/understanding-the-question]], showed how to read a question and narrow a topic. This lesson turns the answer into a thesis statement and shows how to write an introduction that leads the reader to it. Together, the thesis and the introduction are the reader's first evidence that the writer knows what they think.

## The thesis statement

::: definition Thesis statement {#def-thesis}
The **thesis** of an essay is its central claim: the writer's answer to the question, which the rest of the essay explains and supports. The **thesis statement** is the sentence (occasionally two or three) in which the thesis is stated. In most English essays it comes in the introduction, usually at or near its end.
:::

The word comes from Greek *thesis* ‘a placing, a proposition’: something put forward to be defended. A thesis statement does for the whole essay what a topic sentence does for a paragraph ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/topic-sentences]]): it states the point that everything else supports.

::: theorem What makes a good thesis {#rule-good-thesis}
A good thesis statement:

1. **answers the question** that was asked, in its own terms (degree for *to what extent*, a decision for *should*);
2. is **arguable**: a reasonable, informed reader could disagree with it, so it needs support;
3. is **specific**: it says exactly what is claimed, about whom, and how far, rather than *has many effects* or *is a complex issue*;
4. is **unified**: its parts add up to one position, not several unrelated claims;
5. is **supportable** in the space available, with the evidence the writer can actually provide.
:::

::: proof
An essay is an argument, and an argument needs something to argue for. A statement that cannot be disputed (B above) needs no essay, and a statement that only announces a topic (A) commits the writer to nothing, so the reader has no way of judging whether the body paragraphs succeed. Specificity matters because vague claims cannot be tested: *Technology has many effects on education* is true whatever the paragraphs say. Unity matters because a thesis with two unrelated claims produces two half-essays. And a claim must fit the evidence and the length: *Free public transport has failed everywhere it has been tried* would need far more evidence than a 1,000-word essay can give, while C makes a claim about a mechanism (what drivers care about) that a short essay can support with reasons and a few examples.
:::

::: example Improving weak thesis statements {#ex-weak-thesis}
Say what is wrong with each thesis statement and rewrite it. The question is *Should universities require all students to take a course in a foreign language?*

(a) *Learning foreign languages is very important in today's world.* (b) *This essay will look at both sides of this question.* (c) *Universities should require a foreign language course, and many students also need help with mental health.* (d) *Foreign language courses are good and bad.*
::: solution
1. **(a)** does not answer the question (*should universities require it?*), and it is too general to argue with: nobody will dispute that languages are important. Rewrite: *Universities should require a foreign language course only of students whose degrees involve international work, because for others the time is better spent on courses they choose.*
2. **(b)** is an announcement, not a position. Rewrite with a position: *Universities should require every student to take one year of a foreign language, because it is the only way to make sure that students who would never choose it discover what they are capable of.*
3. **(c)** is not unified: mental health is a different topic. Remove the second claim: *Universities should require a foreign language course, because learning a language develops understanding of other cultures in a way no other course does.*
4. **(d)** is vague: good in what way, bad in what way? Rewrite with the degree made specific: *A compulsory language course benefits students who start with some knowledge of the language, but for complete beginners one year is too short to reach a useful level.*

All four rewrites are different positions. What they share is that each answers the question and could be disagreed with.
:::
:::

### Open and closed thesis statements

::: definition Open and closed thesis {#def-open-closed}
A **closed thesis** (sometimes called a thesis with an **essay map**) states the position and lists the main reasons in the order the essay will give them: *Universities should record lectures, because recordings help students review difficult material, include students who cannot attend, and cost little once the equipment is installed.* An **open thesis** states the position without listing the reasons: *Universities should record their lectures.*
:::

::: theorem Choosing between open and closed {#rule-open-closed}
1. In short essays and examination essays, a closed thesis, or an open thesis followed by a short sentence that maps the essay, helps the reader and the marker see the structure at once.
2. In longer essays, list only the main lines of argument, not every point, and keep the thesis to one or two sentences.
3. If you list reasons, the body paragraphs must follow them, in the same order.
4. A thesis may include the main limitation of the position (*although …*, *except …*): this often makes it more precise, not weaker.
:::

::: proof
Readers process a text more easily when they know its plan in advance, and the "map" at the end of an introduction is a long-standing convention of English expository writing; in classical rhetoric it was called the *partitio*, the division of the subject announced before the proof. But a long list in one sentence becomes hard to read, and in a long essay the list can become a table of contents. The concession clause (rule 4) is a way of showing early that the writer has seen the other side: *Although free transport helps low-income passengers, it is a poor way to reduce car use* answers the question more precisely than either half alone, and it prepares the counter-argument that the essay will deal with ([[essay-writing/counter-arguments]]).
:::

::: quiz
Which is the best thesis statement for *To what extent has remote work benefited employees?*
- [ ] *Remote work has become very common since 2020.*
- [ ] *Remote work has advantages and disadvantages for employees.*
- [x] *Remote work has benefited most employees in time and flexibility, but it has disadvantaged those early in their careers, who learn less without colleagues nearby.*
- [ ] *I will discuss the benefits of remote work in this essay.*
::: solution
The third answers the question of degree (*most employees … but … those early in their careers*), is specific about what kind of benefit and for whom, and could be argued with. The first is a fact; the second is a non-answer that fits any question; the fourth is an announcement.
:::
:::

::: warning Announcing instead of arguing
✗ *In this essay, I will discuss whether cities should make public transport free.*
✗ *This essay will explore the advantages and disadvantages of …*
✗ *There are many different opinions on this issue.*

These sentences repeat the question or describe the essay instead of answering. A sentence that tells the reader how the essay is organised is acceptable — sometimes useful — **after** the thesis, and in longer academic texts it is normal (*Section 2 reviews …*): ✓ *Free public transport is a poor use of a city's money … This essay first examines why fares matter less to drivers than speed, and then compares the cost of free fares with the cost of better services.* What is weak is the announcement **instead of** the thesis.
:::

## The introduction

::: definition The introduction and its moves {#def-intro}
The **introduction** of an essay prepares the reader for the thesis and states it. It usually makes some or all of these moves, in roughly this order:

1. an **opening** that connects the topic to something the reader cares about or recognises;
2. **background**: the context needed to understand the question;
3. **narrowing**: from the general topic to the specific question, including definitions of key terms or the scope of the essay;
4. the **thesis statement**;
5. optionally, a short **outline** of the essay's structure.

Because the introduction moves from the general to the specific, it is often pictured as a **funnel**.
:::

::: widget graph
title: The moves of an introduction
nodes: opening@0,5; background@2,4; narrowing and definitions@4,3; thesis statement@6,2; outline of the essay@8,1; body paragraph 1@8,-0.5
edges: opening>background; background>narrowing and definitions; narrowing and definitions>thesis statement; thesis statement>outline of the essay; outline of the essay>body paragraph 1; thesis statement>body paragraph 1
caption: The "funnel" of a typical essay introduction, from the general to the specific. The thesis statement is the narrow end; an outline of the essay may follow it or be built into it, and the first body paragraph begins from there. In a short essay some moves are a single clause, and the opening and the background often merge. Drag the moves to compare other orders.
:::

::: theorem Writing an introduction {#rule-intro}
1. **Length**: in most essays, about one tenth of the total — two to five sentences in a short essay, one or two paragraphs in a long one.
2. **Start close to the question.** The opening should lead quickly to *this* question, not to the history of the world.
3. **Give only the background the reader needs** to understand the thesis.
4. **End with the thesis** (or the thesis and a short outline), so that the reader moves straight from it to the first body paragraph.
5. **Write or rewrite it last**, when you know what the essay argues ([[essay-writing/writing-process#rule-draft]]).
:::

::: proof
An introduction that is too long uses words that the body needs, and an introduction that starts too far from the question makes the reader wait. The general-to-specific shape reflects what a reader needs: enough context to see why the question matters, then the precise claim. Swales (1990) described the introductions of research articles in a similar way, as a sequence of moves that establish a territory, show a gap in it and then occupy the gap; essay introductions are simpler, but the principle — context, then the writer's contribution — is the same, and is developed for research writing in [[academic-writing/research-questions]] and [[academic-writing/abstracts-titles]].
:::

### Openings to avoid, and openings that work

Some openings are so common that markers notice them only as delay:

- **"Since ancient times …"**, **"Throughout history …"**, **"Nowadays, with the development of society …"** — too general to say anything, and often untrue.
- **A dictionary definition of a common word**: *The Oxford Dictionary defines technology as …* Define a term only if its meaning is disputed, and then in your own words for your purpose ([[essay-writing/understanding-the-question#rule-define]]).
- **A string of rhetorical questions**: *Is technology good? Is it bad? What does the future hold?*
- **A famous quotation that does not lead to the thesis**, used only to sound impressive.
- **Restating the question word for word.**

Openings that work usually do one of these:

- **a specific fact or figure**, with its source: *In 2013 Tallinn, the capital of Estonia, began offering free public transport to its registered residents.*
- **a short concrete situation**: *At seven in the morning, a student in a large city can spend longer travelling to her first lecture than listening to it.*
- **the debate itself**: *Few questions divide city planners as sharply as whether public transport should be free.*
- **a common belief that the essay will qualify**: *It seems obvious that making buses free would take cars off the road.*

::: example Rewriting an empty introduction {#ex-rewrite-intro}
Rewrite this introduction for the question *Should schools teach financial skills such as budgeting and saving?*

*Since ancient times, money has played an important role in human life. Nowadays, with the rapid development of the economy, money is more and more important. What is money? According to the dictionary, money is "a medium of exchange". Should schools teach financial skills? This is a controversial question. There are many advantages and disadvantages. This essay will discuss them.*
::: solution
1. **Cut the empty moves**: *Since ancient times …*, *Nowadays, with the rapid development …*, the dictionary definition (nobody needs *money* defined), *This is a controversial question*, *There are many advantages and disadvantages*, *This essay will discuss them*.
2. **Find a specific opening close to the question**: the situation of young people making financial decisions for the first time.
3. **Add the background the reader needs**: what *financial skills* means here.
4. **Write a thesis that answers *should*.**

> *Many young people make their first important financial decisions — signing a phone contract, renting a room, taking out a student loan — within a year or two of leaving school, often with no more preparation than advice from family. By financial skills, this essay means practical abilities such as budgeting, understanding interest and comparing contracts, rather than investment. Schools should teach these skills, because they are needed by every student, are learned unequally at home, and can be taught through mathematics lessons that already exist.*

The new introduction is shorter than the old one, starts close to the question, defines the one term that needed it, and ends with a closed thesis whose three reasons can become three paragraphs.
:::
:::

::: example Analysing a model introduction {#ex-analyse-intro}
Identify the moves in this introduction (written for the atlas) to *To what extent has the growth of food delivery apps changed eating habits in cities?*

*(1) In many large cities, a hot meal can now arrive at the door within half an hour of a few taps on a phone. (2) Food delivery apps, which connect customers, restaurants and couriers, have grown rapidly over the past decade, especially among younger, working adults. (3) "Eating habits" here means where, when and what people eat, rather than how much they spend. (4) The apps have changed where and when people eat far more than what they eat: they have moved restaurant meals into homes and offices and made late meals easier, but the dishes ordered are largely those that restaurants already served.*
::: solution
1. Sentence (1) is the **opening**: a concrete, recognisable situation that leads directly to the topic.
2. Sentence (2) gives **background**: what the apps are and who uses them. It avoids exact figures it cannot support; in a real essay a sourced figure would be useful here.
3. Sentence (3) **narrows and defines**: it says what *eating habits* will mean, which tells the reader what kind of evidence to expect.
4. Sentence (4) is the **thesis**: an answer of degree (*far more than*) with the main lines of the argument (*where and when* vs *what*). The structure of the body is implied: where, when, then what.

There is no separate outline sentence: in a short essay, the thesis does that work.
:::
:::

::: example Where to put the thesis {#ex-thesis-place}
A student's introduction states the thesis in its first sentence and then gives three sentences of background. Is this wrong? Rewrite it if it would help: *Schools should start later in the morning. Teenagers' sleep patterns change during adolescence. Many teenagers find it hard to fall asleep before eleven at night. Most secondary schools start lessons before half past eight.*
::: solution
1. **Is it wrong?** No. Placing the thesis first is possible, and in some genres — reports, news, emails — it is normal. In essays, however, readers expect the background first and the thesis at the end of the introduction, where it leads directly into the body.
2. **The cost here**: after the thesis, the three background sentences read like the start of the argument, and the reader may not realise where the introduction ends.
3. **Rewrite** with the background leading to the thesis, and make the thesis more specific:

*During adolescence, the body clock shifts later, and many teenagers find it hard to fall asleep before eleven at night. Yet most secondary schools start lessons before half past eight. Schools should move the start of the day later for older students, because the earlier start costs them sleep that they cannot make up and that affects both their health and their learning.*
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which opening sentence leads most directly to the question *Should supermarkets be banned from selling single-use plastic bags?*
- [ ] *Since ancient times, humans have carried their belongings in bags.*
- [ ] *The dictionary defines plastic as "a synthetic material".*
- [x] *Many countries already charge for plastic bags at the checkout, and some have gone further and banned the thinnest ones.*
- [ ] *Is plastic good? Is it bad? Can we live without it?*
::: solution
The third sentence gives relevant background — charges and partial bans — that leads straight to the question of a full ban. The others are the empty openings described above: a vague historical claim, an unnecessary definition, and a string of rhetorical questions.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Where the point comes.** In English academic essays the thesis is usually stated in the introduction and the rest of the essay supports it — a *deductive* order, from claim to support. Some Chinese essay traditions build up to the main point more gradually, and Chinese school writing often values an elegant lead-in (引子, 铺垫). Hinds (1990) argued that expository writing in Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Thai could be "quasi-inductive", delaying the thesis. Other researchers have warned against treating any of this as a fixed cultural pattern: Kirkpatrick (1997) found that the writing textbooks used in mainland Chinese schools in the 1990s advised structures much like those taught in English-speaking countries, and Chinese essays that state their point at once (开门见山) are widely admired. The practical point is simple: in an English essay, state your answer in the introduction.
- **Quotations and set phrases.** Chinese school essays often open with a well-known saying, a line of poetry or a 成语, and this can be skilful in Chinese. Translated into English, such openings tend to sound general or decorative (*As an old Chinese saying goes, …*). Use a quotation only if it leads directly to your thesis, and explain its relevance.
- **"With the development of society".** 随着社会的发展 and 随着科技的进步 are common openings in Chinese school writing; their English translations (*With the development of society/science and technology, …*) are among the most recognisable openings in learners' English essays. Replace them with a specific fact or situation.
- **"In my opinion".** The thesis is already your opinion; *In my opinion* or *I think* in front of it is not wrong, but it is usually unnecessary in a formal essay, and many writers leave it out. Some exam tasks ask for *your* view directly; then *I believe that …* or *In my view, …* is natural.
:::

::: history
The parts of an introduction are much older than the essay. The handbooks of classical rhetoric, such as the anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium* (first century BC) and Quintilian's *Institutio Oratoria* (late first century AD), divided a speech into parts: an *exordium* that won the audience's attention and goodwill, a *narratio* that set out the facts, a *partitio* that stated the points to be proved, a *confirmatio* that proved them, a *refutatio* that answered the other side, and a *peroratio* that summed up and moved the audience. These terms were taught in European schools for centuries, and the modern essay's introduction, thesis and essay map, body, counter-argument and conclusion still follow them closely. The phrase *thesis statement* itself belongs to twentieth-century composition teaching in the United States, where the five-paragraph essay — an introduction ending in a three-part thesis, three body paragraphs and a conclusion — also became a standard school exercise.
:::

::: summary
- A thesis statement states the essay's central claim — the writer's answer to the question — usually at the end of the introduction ([[#def-thesis]]).
- A good thesis answers the question in its own terms and is arguable, specific, unified and supportable in the space available ([[#rule-good-thesis]]).
- A closed thesis lists the main reasons; if it does, the body must follow them in order. A concession clause can make a thesis more precise ([[#def-open-closed]], [[#rule-open-closed]]).
- Do not announce (*This essay will discuss …*) instead of arguing; a short outline is acceptable after the thesis.
- An introduction moves from opening and background through narrowing and definitions to the thesis, and is usually about a tenth of the essay ([[#def-intro]], [[#rule-intro]]).
- Avoid empty openings — *since ancient times*, dictionary definitions, rhetorical questions, decorative quotations — and start close to the question with a specific fact, situation or belief.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Thesis or not? {level=1}
Which of these are thesis statements (arguable claims that answer a question)? (a) *Many students work part-time while they study.* (b) *Students should not work more than fifteen hours a week during term, because longer hours harm their grades more than the income helps them.* (c) *This essay will examine part-time work.* (d) *Part-time work gives students skills that their degree courses do not, and employers value these skills more than grades.* (e) *Part-time work is an interesting topic.*
::: solution
(b) and (d) are thesis statements. (a) is a fact; (c) is an announcement; (e) is a vague opinion about the topic that does not answer any question.
:::
:::

::: exercise Name the problem {level=1}
Each thesis has one problem: *not arguable*, *too vague*, *not unified* or *does not answer the question*. The question is *Should children learn to swim at school?* (a) *Swimming is a sport.* (b) *Swimming lessons have many benefits.* (c) *Schools should teach swimming, and school lunches should be healthier.* (d) *Swimming is my favourite sport.*
::: solution
(a) not arguable (a fact). (b) too vague (which benefits? and does not say *should*). (c) not unified (two unrelated claims). (d) does not answer the question (a personal preference).
:::
:::

::: exercise Order the introduction {level=1}
Put these sentences in order to make an introduction. (a) *This essay argues that such programmes help students most when they are linked to their studies.* (b) *Many universities now encourage students to spend a semester abroad.* (c) *Supporters claim these programmes develop independence and language skills, while critics point to their cost and to the time they take from the degree.*
::: solution
(b), (c), (a): opening and background, then the debate, then the thesis.
:::

::: exercise Sharpen the thesis {level=2}
Make each thesis more specific and arguable. (a) *Social media affects teenagers.* (b) *Tourism has good and bad effects.* (c) *Exams are important.*
::: solution
Answers will vary. Models:

(a) *Social media harms teenagers' sleep more than it harms their friendships, because the main damage comes from late-night use rather than from what they see.*
(b) *Mass tourism benefits the economies of historic towns but drives residents out of their centres, unless the number of short-term rentals is limited.*
(c) *Final exams remain the fairest way to assess school students, because coursework is easier for families to influence.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Open to closed {level=2}
Turn each open thesis into a closed one by adding the main reasons, and then write the topic of each body paragraph. (a) *Cities should plant more trees along streets.* (b) *Schools should not ban mobile phones completely.*
::: solution
Answers will vary. Models:

(a) *Cities should plant more trees along streets, because trees cool streets in summer, reduce flooding after heavy rain and make walking more pleasant.* Paragraphs: cooling; flooding; walking.

(b) *Schools should not ban mobile phones completely, because phones are useful in some lessons, help parents contact children after school, and a total ban teaches less than rules for responsible use.* Paragraphs: use in lessons; contact with families; learning responsible use.
:::
:::

::: exercise Fix the opening {level=2}
Replace each empty opening with one that leads to the question *Should public libraries stay open late in the evening?* (a) *Since ancient times, people have loved reading.* (b) *What is a library? According to the dictionary, it is a place where books are kept.*
::: solution
Answers will vary. Models: (a) *For students who share small flats and for adults who work during the day, a quiet place to read or study in the evening can be hard to find.* (b) *Public libraries have changed from places that lend books into places where people study, use computers and meet, but most still close at the end of the working day.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Write an introduction {level=3}
Write an introduction of 70–100 words for the question *To what extent should universities judge applicants on interviews as well as exam results?* Include an opening, background, a definition or narrowing if needed, and a thesis of degree.
::: hint
Decide your answer of degree first — for example, interviews for some subjects but not others, or interviews only as a second step.
:::
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

> Exam results tell a university what an applicant has achieved, but not always what they could achieve with the right teaching. For this reason, some universities interview applicants as well, while others rely on results alone. Interviews should play only a limited part in admissions: they are valuable for courses such as medicine and teaching, where communication is part of the work, but for most subjects they add little that results do not show, and they favour applicants who have been coached.

Check: specific opening; background on current practice; a thesis that answers *to what extent* with a limit and reasons.
:::
:::

::: exercise Evaluate two introductions {level=3}
Compare these two introductions to *Should employers allow staff to work a four-day week?* Say which is more effective and why, with reference to the moves and rules in this lesson.

**A.** *Nowadays, with the development of society, work is more and more important in our lives. Some people think we should work four days a week, while others disagree. Both sides have their reasons. In this essay I will discuss both sides and give my opinion.*

**B.** *Several companies have experimented with a four-day week in which staff keep their full pay. Supporters report that employees are less tired and more focused; critics doubt whether the results last, or whether they are possible in shops and hospitals. Employers should offer a four-day week where work is measured by results rather than hours, but it cannot simply be copied in jobs that need someone present every hour of the day.*
::: solution
B is more effective.

- **Opening**: A begins with an empty generalisation (*Nowadays, with the development of society*); B begins with a specific development close to the question.
- **Background**: A says only that people disagree; B says what each side claims.
- **Thesis**: A has none — it announces a discussion and postpones the opinion. B gives an answer with a condition (*where work is measured by results*) and a limit (*jobs that need someone present*), which is arguable and specific and suggests the structure of the body.
- **Length**: both are short, but every sentence in B does a job.
:::
:::
