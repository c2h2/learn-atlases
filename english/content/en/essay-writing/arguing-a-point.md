Two body paragraphs from essays answering *Should secondary schools start later in the morning?* Both writers think they should.

> **A.** *Starting school later is good for students. Students are very tired in the morning. As we all know, sleep is very important. If students sleep more, they will study better. Also, they will be happier. Therefore, schools should start later.*

> **B.** *The strongest reason for a later start is biological. During adolescence, the body's internal clock shifts later, so that many teenagers are not physically ready to sleep until around eleven at night, however early they go to bed. A school day that begins at eight therefore does not just require teenagers to get up early; it requires them to be short of sleep on every school day. A later start would not make teenagers lazy, as is sometimes claimed: it would let them sleep at the time their bodies already demand. This is why medical organisations in several countries, among them the American Academy of Pediatrics in 2014, have recommended that secondary schools start no earlier than half past eight.*

Paragraph A makes several claims and supports none of them. *As we all know* is not evidence, and the sentences do not depend on each other: they could be put in almost any order. Paragraph B makes one claim (*the strongest reason is biological*), explains the mechanism that makes it true, answers a likely objection in passing, and ends with a piece of evidence that a reader could check. A reader can disagree with B, but has to work to do so. That is what an argument is.

The thesis states the position ([[essay-writing/thesis-introduction]]). The body paragraphs are where the essay earns it. This lesson shows how to build a paragraph that argues a point: the claim, the explanation, the evidence and the link back, together with the kinds of evidence and reasoning that convince a careful reader and the common faults that do not.

## The argumentative paragraph

::: definition Claim, support and reasoning {#def-claim}
In an argumentative paragraph:

- the **claim** (or *point*) is the statement the paragraph argues for, usually given in its topic sentence;
- the **support** is the material that gives the reader grounds to accept the claim: explanation, examples, facts, data, research findings, expert judgement;
- the **reasoning** is the connection between the support and the claim: why this evidence shows that this claim is true.

Each body paragraph's claim, in turn, supports the thesis of the essay.
:::

::: theorem The shape of a body paragraph {#rule-paragraph-shape}
A body paragraph in an argumentative essay usually does four things, in roughly this order:

1. **Point**: state the claim in a topic sentence that is clearly connected to the thesis.
2. **Explain**: say what the claim means and why it is true — the reason or mechanism.
3. **Support**: give specific examples, evidence or data, and say how they support the claim.
4. **Link**: show how the point helps to answer the question, either at the end of the paragraph or in the opening of the next.

Each paragraph should argue **one** claim. If you find a second, it probably needs its own paragraph.
:::

::: proof
The order mirrors a reader's questions: *What are you saying? What do you mean, and why? How do you know? So what?* Many schools teach the pattern under acronyms — PEEL (Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link), PEE, PEA — and these are useful as a check that nothing is missing. They are not laws: explanation and evidence often alternate, a strong example may come before the explanation, and the link may be unnecessary when the topic sentence already makes the connection obvious. What does not change is that each element must be present somewhere and that the paragraph stays on one claim; the topic-sentence principle of [[sentences-and-paragraphs/topic-sentences]] applies with greater force to an argument, because a paragraph that wanders weakens the thesis it is meant to support.
:::

::: example Building a paragraph {#ex-build}
Build a body paragraph from these notes for the thesis *Cities should plant more trees along their streets.* Notes: *trees cool streets — shade + water evaporating from leaves — heatwaves more frequent — older people at risk — streets with trees can be several degrees cooler in summer (various city studies).*
::: solution
1. **Point**: the notes are about cooling, so the claim is that trees cool streets, and this matters: *The most urgent reason to plant street trees is that they cool cities during heatwaves.*
2. **Explain** the mechanism: shade and evaporation.
3. **Support**: the measured difference — but the notes say "various city studies" without details. The writer must either find a specific study or present the finding cautiously, without an invented number.
4. **Link**: who benefits and why it answers the question.

> *The most urgent reason to plant street trees is that they cool cities during heatwaves. Trees lower temperatures in two ways: their leaves shade the pavement and buildings, which would otherwise absorb the sun's heat, and they release water through their leaves, which cools the surrounding air as it evaporates. Studies in several cities have measured streets with full tree cover as noticeably cooler on summer afternoons than similar streets without trees. As heatwaves become more frequent, this cooling is not a matter of comfort: it protects older people and others whose health is most at risk from heat, which makes street trees a public health measure as much as a decoration.*

The paragraph has one claim, explains how it works, gives support honestly (without a figure the writer cannot cite), and ends by linking the point to the case for planting.
:::
:::

## Kinds of support

::: definition Evidence {#def-evidence}
**Evidence** is information that can be checked and that makes a claim more or less likely to be true. In essays, the main kinds are:

- **facts and statistics**: dates, quantities, survey results, official figures;
- **research findings**: the results of studies, with their source;
- **specific examples and cases**: a particular city, school, company or event;
- **expert judgement**: the view of a person or organisation with relevant expertise;
- **personal experience**: what the writer has observed — useful as an illustration, weak as proof.

**Explanation** and **reasoning** are not evidence, but they are part of the support: they show why the evidence matters.
:::

::: theorem Using evidence well {#rule-evidence}
Evidence persuades a careful reader when it is:

1. **relevant**: it bears on this claim, not on the topic in general;
2. **specific**: a named case or a figure, not *some people* or *many studies*;
3. **reliable**: from a source the reader can trust and check, given accurately;
4. **sufficient**: enough of it for the strength of the claim — a single example can illustrate, but cannot prove a general rule;
5. **explained**: the writer says what it shows. Evidence does not speak for itself.

In timed essays without sources, use specific, realistic examples and reasoning; never invent statistics or studies.
:::

::: proof
A reader who doubts a claim asks: *what makes you think so?* General phrases (*many experts agree*, *studies show*) give the reader nothing to check, so they persuade only those who already agree. A specific case can be checked and shows that the writer knows the subject, but one case may be unusual, which is why the strength of a conclusion has to match the amount of evidence. Explanation is needed because the same evidence can support different claims: a fall in car traffic after a bus fare cut might be caused by the fare, by higher fuel prices or by a new bridge, and only the writer's reasoning connects the evidence to the claim being made. Inventing evidence is a breach of honesty in any piece of writing, and examiners are trained to recognise suspiciously precise figures in essays written without sources; realistic illustrations presented as such (*for example, a student who …*) are acceptable.
:::

::: example Judging evidence {#ex-judge-evidence}
The claim is *Learning a musical instrument improves children's concentration.* Rank these pieces of support from strongest to weakest and say why.

(a) *My little brother started the piano last year and now he can sit still for longer.* (b) *Everyone knows that music is good for the brain.* (c) *A study that compared children who were randomly assigned to music lessons with children assigned to other activities found differences in attention tests after a year.* (d) *Many famous scientists, such as Einstein, played musical instruments.*
::: solution
1. **(c)** is strongest: a study with a comparison group, and random assignment, which makes it more likely that the difference was caused by the lessons. In an essay it needs a full reference, and the writer should not claim more than it shows (one study, one age group, one kind of test).
2. **(a)** is a specific personal example. It illustrates the claim but cannot prove it: the brother might have become more patient simply by growing a year older.
3. **(d)** is weak. Famous scientists who played instruments do not show that playing improved their concentration — they may have played because they were already the kind of people who concentrate, and we do not hear about the many musicians who did not become scientists.
4. **(b)** is not evidence at all: it appeals to what "everyone knows" and gives the reader nothing to check.

(The study in (c) is described here as a type, not a particular publication; in a real essay the writer must give the actual source.)
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence adds the most useful **explanation** after this evidence? *After the city reduced the speed limit from 50 to 30 km/h in residential streets, the number of serious injuries to pedestrians fell.*
- [ ] *This is very good news for everyone.*
- [x] *At lower speeds, drivers have more time to stop, and a pedestrian who is hit suffers far less force.*
- [ ] *Many other cities have also changed their speed limits.*
- [ ] *Pedestrians are very important.*
::: solution
The second sentence explains the mechanism that connects the evidence (the fall in injuries) to the claim (lower limits make streets safer). The others either comment emotionally, add unrelated information, or state something obvious without connecting it.
:::
:::

## Reasoning: from evidence to claim

Every argument from evidence to a claim relies on a link that is often not stated. In the terms of Toulmin's model, which [[academic-reading/claims-and-evidence#def-toulmin]] explains in detail, the **data** support the **claim** through a **warrant**, a general principle that connects them. *This city's injuries fell after the speed limit was lowered* (data) supports *lower speed limits make streets safer* (claim) only through a warrant such as *the fall was caused by the lower limit, not by something else*.

::: theorem Make the reasoning visible {#rule-reasoning}
1. After giving evidence, say **what it shows** and **why**: *This suggests that …*, *This matters because …*.
2. If the link between evidence and claim depends on an assumption a reader might question, **state the assumption** and, where you can, defend it.
3. **Match the strength of the claim to the evidence**: one case *suggests*, several consistent studies *show* or *strongly indicate*; a general principle with obvious exceptions holds *usually* or *in most cases* (see [[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]]).
:::

::: proof
Writers leave warrants unstated because they seem obvious to the writer — but the reader may not share them, and a critic will attack exactly there. Stating the assumption shows the reader that the writer has seen it, and gives the writer a chance to defend it. Calibrating the claim is equally a matter of honesty and of persuasion: a reader who sees *proves* attached to a single example stops trusting the rest of the essay, while a writer who claims exactly what the evidence supports is believed when the evidence is strong.
:::

::: example Stating the warrant {#ex-warrant}
Identify the unstated assumption in this argument and rewrite it so that the reasoning is visible: *Students who eat breakfast get higher marks than students who do not. Therefore schools should give all students breakfast.*
::: solution
1. **Data**: students who eat breakfast get higher marks.
2. **Claim**: schools should give all students breakfast.
3. **Unstated assumptions**: (i) eating breakfast *causes* the higher marks, rather than both being linked to something else (families with more time and money may be more likely both to provide breakfast and to support schoolwork); (ii) breakfast provided by the school would have the same effect as breakfast at home.
4. **Rewrite**, stating and testing the assumptions:

*Students who eat breakfast tend to get higher marks than students who do not. This does not by itself show that breakfast causes better marks, since families who provide breakfast may also support learning in other ways. However, studies that compare students before and after a school starts providing breakfast give more direct evidence, because they follow the same students. If such studies show an improvement, schools have a stronger case for providing breakfast to all students.*

The rewrite is longer, but it is an argument rather than a leap.
:::
:::

The truth table below shows one of the most common errors in reasoning from evidence: treating a consequence as proof of a cause.

::: widget truthtable
title: Affirming the consequent
formula: ((p -> q) & q) -> p
compare: ((p -> q) & p) -> q
caption: Let p be *the new bus lane reduced traffic* and q be *air quality improved*. If p → q is true and q is observed, it does **not** follow that p: the first formula is false in one row (p false, q true), where air quality improved for some other reason — a windy month, a factory closing. The second formula (if p → q and p, then q) is true in every row: it is a valid argument. Essays that say *air quality improved, so the bus lane worked* use the first, invalid form; the reasoning needs evidence that rules out the other causes.
:::

::: warning Common faults in reasoning
- **Hasty generalisation**: one or two cases treated as proof of a rule. ✗ *My cousin studied abroad and found a good job, so studying abroad guarantees a good career.*
- **Correlation taken as cause**: two things occur together, so one must cause the other. ✗ *Countries with more universities are richer, so building universities makes countries rich.* (Richer countries can also afford more universities.)
- **False choice**: only two options are presented when there are more. ✗ *Either we ban cars from the centre or we accept polluted air.*
- **Appeal to popularity or tradition**: ✗ *Most people believe it, so it must be true.* ✗ *We have always done it this way.*
- **Attacking the person instead of the argument**: ✗ *Critics of the plan live outside the city, so their views do not matter.*
- **Slippery slope** without evidence for each step: ✗ *If phones are allowed at lunch, soon they will be used in every lesson and no one will learn anything.*

Each of these can be repaired: by more evidence, by a weaker claim, by considering other causes or options, or by answering the argument itself.
:::

::: example Repairing a weak paragraph {#ex-repair}
Revise Paragraph A from the start of the lesson into a convincing argument, using what you have learned.
::: solution
1. **Choose one claim.** Paragraph A mixes tiredness, sleep, study and happiness. Choose the strongest link to the thesis: lack of sleep harms learning.
2. **Explain**: why are students tired in the morning? Because their sleep is cut short, not because they are lazy.
3. **Support**: replace *As we all know* with something the reader can check, or with honest reasoning and an example.
4. **Calibrate**: *will study better* becomes a claim the evidence can carry.
5. **Link** back to the thesis.

> *A later start would help students learn, because it would reduce the sleep they lose on every school day. A teenager who cannot fall asleep before eleven and must get up at half past six for an eight o'clock start sleeps around seven and a half hours, less than most health guidelines recommend for this age group, and the shortfall builds up across the week. Lack of sleep is known to affect attention and memory, the two abilities that morning lessons depend on most. Moving the start of the day even by forty minutes would not solve every problem, but it would give students more of the sleep they need at the time their bodies allow it — which is the main reason schools should consider the change.*

The paragraph now argues one claim, explains it, supports it with checkable reasoning and a careful general statement, and links back to the thesis.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which fault does this argument contain? *Since the new library opened, the number of students who pass their exams has risen. The library has clearly improved students' results.*
- [ ] attacking the person
- [ ] false choice
- [x] treating a correlation (or sequence) as a cause
- [ ] appeal to tradition
::: solution
The rise in pass rates came after the library opened, but the argument gives no evidence that the library caused it. Other changes — new teachers, easier exams, a different group of students — could explain it. The claim needs evidence that rules out other causes, or a weaker form: *may have contributed*.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **论点、论据、论证.** Chinese argumentative writing (议论文) is taught through three elements: the thesis or point (论点), the evidence (论据) and the reasoning (论证). These match the claim, support and reasoning of this lesson closely, so Chinese-educated writers already have the right framework.
- **Famous examples as evidence.** Chinese school essays often support a point with a story about a famous person — a scientist who failed many times before succeeding, a poet who overcame hardship. In English argumentative essays, such stories serve as illustrations at most: a reader will ask whether the case is typical. Prefer evidence that bears directly on the claim — data, research, specific comparable cases — and explain it.
- **"As we all know".** 众所周知, translated as *As we all know* or *It is well known that*, is one of the most frequent openings in learners' essays. In English it is risky: it asserts that the reader agrees instead of showing why they should, and if the reader does not know, it sounds like pressure. Give the evidence, or state the claim plainly.
- **Strength of claims.** Learners often move between overstatement (*This will definitely solve the problem*, *Everyone agrees*) and piles of hedges (*It may possibly perhaps be*). Choose one level that fits the evidence, as in [[#rule-reasoning]].
:::

::: history
The study of argument in Europe begins with Aristotle's *Rhetoric* (fourth century BC), which distinguished three means of persuasion: the character of the speaker (*ethos*), the emotions of the audience (*pathos*) and the argument itself (*logos*). Aristotle observed that public arguments rarely set out every premise; they rely on the audience to supply what is left unsaid, a form he called the *enthymeme*, and they use examples to suggest general rules. Much later, Stephen Toulmin's *The Uses of Argument* (1958) analysed arguments in law and daily life into claim, data and warrant, with qualifiers and rebuttals; his model gave teachers of writing a vocabulary for the unstated step that Aristotle had noticed. The persuasive appeals are the subject of [[style-and-rhetoric/rhetorical-appeals]].
:::

::: summary
- An argumentative paragraph has a claim, support and reasoning, and its claim supports the thesis ([[#def-claim]]).
- A body paragraph usually states its point, explains it, supports it with evidence, and links back to the question — one claim per paragraph ([[#rule-paragraph-shape]]).
- Evidence includes facts and statistics, research findings, specific examples, expert judgement and, as illustration, personal experience ([[#def-evidence]]).
- Good evidence is relevant, specific, reliable, sufficient and explained; never invent statistics or studies ([[#rule-evidence]]).
- Make the reasoning visible: say what the evidence shows, state assumptions a reader might question, and match the strength of the claim to the evidence ([[#rule-reasoning]]).
- Avoid hasty generalisations, correlation taken as cause, false choices, appeals to popularity, personal attacks and unsupported slippery slopes.
:::

::: exercise Find the claim {level=1}
Underline (or write out) the claim in each paragraph opening. (a) *Online courses suit some learners better than others. Those who already know how to plan their time …* (b) *Last year our town opened three new cycle lanes. As a result, cycling has become safer, which is the main argument for building more …* (c) *Of all the costs of commuting, the least visible is time.*
::: solution
(a) *Online courses suit some learners better than others.* (b) *cycling has become safer*, presented as *the main argument for building more* (the first sentence is background). (c) *Of all the costs of commuting, the least visible is time.*
:::

::: exercise Kind of evidence {level=1}
Name the kind of evidence: fact or statistic, research finding, specific example, expert judgement, or personal experience. (a) *The World Health Organization recommends that adults do at least 150 minutes of moderate physical activity a week.* (b) *When I lived in Hangzhou, I cycled to work every day.* (c) *In 2013 Tallinn made public transport free for its registered residents.* (d) *A study of commuters found that those who cycled reported less stress than those who drove.*
::: solution
(a) expert judgement (an official recommendation). (b) personal experience. (c) fact (a specific example). (d) research finding — in a real essay it needs a full reference.
:::
:::

::: exercise Name the fault {level=1}
Name the fault in each argument: hasty generalisation, correlation taken as cause, false choice, or appeal to popularity. (a) *Most of my friends prefer e-books, so paper books will soon disappear.* (b) *People who drink coffee live longer, so coffee makes people live longer.* (c) *Either we close the old library or the town goes bankrupt.* (d) *This phone is the best because it sells the most.*
::: solution
(a) hasty generalisation. (b) correlation taken as cause. (c) false choice. (d) appeal to popularity.
:::

::: exercise Add the explanation {level=2}
Each paragraph gives a claim and evidence but no explanation. Add one or two sentences that explain how the evidence supports the claim. (a) *Working from home can make employees more productive. In surveys, many home workers report fewer interruptions than in an open-plan office.* (b) *Free museum entry widens access. When some national museums stopped charging for entry, visitor numbers rose.*
::: solution
Answers will vary. Models:

(a) *Work that needs long periods of concentration — writing, analysis, programming — suffers most from interruptions, because each one costs time to refocus; fewer interruptions therefore mean more finished work, at least for these tasks.*

(b) *If the rise came partly from people who had not visited before — families, students, local residents — then the charge had been keeping them away, and removing it brought them in. The number alone does not show who the new visitors were, so the claim is strongest if visitor surveys show more first-time visitors.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Calibrate the claim {level=2}
Rewrite each claim so that its strength matches the evidence given in brackets. (a) *Video games cause violence.* (evidence: some studies find short-term increases in aggressive feelings after playing violent games; others find no effect on violent behaviour.) (b) *Learning Latin improves English vocabulary.* (evidence: one small study of one school.)
::: solution
Answers will vary. Models: (a) *Some studies suggest that violent video games can increase aggressive feelings for a short time, but there is little evidence that they cause violent behaviour.* (b) *One small study suggests that learning Latin may help English-speaking students recognise words with Latin roots, though the finding needs to be tested more widely.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Order the paragraph {level=2}
Put these sentences in order to make a body paragraph: (a) *For example, a student who misses a step in a proof can replay that part until it makes sense.* (b) *This is why recordings support the learning that lectures exist for, rather than replacing it.* (c) *Recorded lectures help students understand difficult material.* (d) *A live lecture moves at one speed for everyone, but students understand at different speeds.*
::: solution
(c) point, (d) explanation, (a) example, (b) link.
:::
:::

::: exercise State the warrant {level=3}
Identify the unstated assumption in each argument, say whether it is reasonable, and rewrite the argument with the assumption stated and, if possible, defended. (a) *Teenagers spend hours on their phones every day, so phones are the reason they read fewer books.* (b) *The new motorway will reduce traffic in the town, because cars will use the motorway instead of the town roads.*
::: hint
For (b), think about what happens to traffic when a new road makes driving easier.
:::
::: solution
Answers will vary. Models:

(a) Assumption: phone time has replaced reading time, and nothing else explains the fall. This is plausible but not shown: homework, sport or other media might also have taken time, and some reading happens on phones. Rewrite: *Teenagers who spend hours on their phones have less time for books, and if the hours spent on phones have grown as book reading has fallen, it is reasonable to suspect that phones have taken some of that time — though part of teenagers' reading has also moved onto phones.*

(b) Assumption: the total number of car journeys stays the same and some simply move to the motorway. This is questionable: new roads can encourage more driving, as people make trips they would not have made before. Rewrite: *The new motorway will take through traffic off the town roads, provided that the extra road space does not encourage more people to drive; to keep that benefit, the town should combine the motorway with limits on through traffic in the centre.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Write a body paragraph {level=3}
Write a body paragraph of 120–160 words that supports one reason for or against the thesis *Universities should require students to do an internship before they graduate.* Follow the point–explain–support–link shape, use at least one specific (realistic) example, and calibrate your claim.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

> A compulsory internship would help students to choose their careers with better information. At present, many students choose a career on the basis of what they imagine a job involves, and some discover only after graduating that the daily work does not suit them. A few weeks inside an organisation show them what the work is actually like: a student who wants to work in publishing, for example, may discover that it involves far more marketing and far less reading than she imagined, and either adjust her plans or confirm them. An internship cannot show students everything about a career, and a short placement may be unrepresentative, but it gives them evidence instead of guesses at the point when changing direction is still easy. That is a strong reason to make it part of every degree.

Check: one claim; explanation of why information matters; a realistic example presented as an example; a limitation that calibrates the claim; a link back to the thesis.
:::
