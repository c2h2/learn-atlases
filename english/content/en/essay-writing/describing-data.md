The table below was made up for this lesson. It shows how students at a fictional university travelled to campus in two years.

| mode of travel | 2015 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| bus | 46% | 38% |
| car | 22% | 9% |
| bicycle | 8% | 27% |
| walking | 18% | 21% |
| other | 6% | 5% |

Here are two descriptions of it.

> **A.** *In 2015, 46% of students went by bus, 22% by car, 8% by bicycle, 18% on foot and 6% by other means. In 2025, 38% went by bus, 9% by car, 27% by bicycle, 21% on foot and 5% by other means.*

> **B.** *The table compares how students travelled to campus in 2015 and 2025. Over the decade, the most striking change was the rise of cycling, which more than tripled its share, from 8% to 27%, while car use fell by more than half. The bus remained the most common way to travel in both years, although its share fell by eight percentage points. Walking rose slightly, and other modes changed little.*

Description A contains every number and is completely accurate. It is also almost useless: it repeats the table in sentences, and the reader still has to work out what has changed. Description B begins by saying what the table shows, picks out the main features — the rise of cycling, the fall in car use, the bus still on top — and compares the figures in a way the table alone does not. It uses fewer numbers, and each one has a job.

Describing data and processes is a skill in its own right. It is needed when an essay uses a chart as evidence, in reports, and in some examinations that ask candidates to describe a chart, table or diagram in writing. This lesson covers the structure of a data description, the language of trends, quantities and comparison, and how to describe a process from a diagram. Reading charts critically — scales, baselines, misleading graphics — is the subject of [[academic-reading/reading-data]]; here the focus is on writing.

## Selecting and summarising

::: definition Data description, overview and key features {#def-data-description}
A **data description** is a text that reports what a chart, table or diagram shows. Its **key features** are the most important patterns in the data: the largest and smallest values, the biggest changes, the general direction of a trend, clear exceptions, and striking similarities or differences. The **overview** is a sentence or two that summarises the key features without detail — what a reader should remember if they remember nothing else.
:::

::: theorem Select, group and compare {#rule-select}
1. **Select**: report the key features, not every number. Choose figures that support a point.
2. **Summarise**: give an overview of the main patterns early, before the details.
3. **Group**: organise details by pattern (the categories that rose, those that fell) or by time, not in the order of the table's rows.
4. **Compare**: put figures in relation to each other — *more than doubled*, *the highest*, *twice as many as* — rather than listing them.
5. **Report, do not invent**: describe what the data show. If you suggest a reason, mark it as your interpretation (*probably*, *this may reflect …*), and in a pure description task leave reasons out.
:::

::: proof
A reader can read a table; what they want from a description is what a careful reader would *notice* in it. Selecting and comparing is the work of interpretation that turns data into information. Grouping by pattern lets the reader see the structure of the data; following the rows of a table lets the arbitrary order of the table decide the order of the text. The last point matters because data rarely show their own causes: the table above shows that cycling rose, not why, and a writer who states a cause as if it were in the data (*because the university built cycle lanes*) is reporting something the reader cannot check against the table. Examination descriptions of data tasks generally ask for an overview and for key features selected and compared, and they do not reward explanations that go beyond the data.
:::

::: theorem The structure of a data description {#rule-structure}
A short description (150–250 words) usually has:

1. an **introduction**: one sentence that says what the chart shows, in your own words — *what*, *where*, *when*, and in what units;
2. an **overview**: the two or three main features, without detailed figures;
3. **one or two paragraphs of detail**, each grouping related features, with selected figures and comparisons.

A separate conclusion is not needed when the overview has already summarised the data. When the description is part of an essay, the overview becomes the point of the paragraph, and the details become its evidence.
:::

::: example Writing an overview {#ex-overview}
Write an overview for this fictional table of the average number of hours per week that people in three age groups spent on four activities.

| | 16–24 | 25–44 | 45–64 |
|---|---|---|---|
| watching video online | 14 | 9 | 5 |
| television | 4 | 8 | 13 |
| reading | 3 | 3 | 5 |
| exercise | 5 | 3 | 3 |
::: solution
1. **Look across the rows for patterns**: online video falls with age (14 → 9 → 5); television rises with age (4 → 8 → 13). These two mirror each other — the strongest pattern.
2. **Look for the largest values**: online video for the youngest group (14) and television for the oldest (13) are the highest figures.
3. **Look at the rest**: reading and exercise vary little; the youngest exercise most, the oldest read most.
4. **Write the overview without detailed figures**:

*Overall, the youngest group spent far more time watching video online than on television, while the oldest group showed the opposite pattern. Time spent on reading and exercise was much lower and varied little between the groups.*

The overview names the main contrast and the general picture; the figures (14 hours, 13 hours) belong in the detail paragraphs.
:::
:::

## The language of change

| direction | verbs | nouns |
|---|---|---|
| up | *rise*, *increase*, *grow*, *climb*, *go up*; *double*, *triple* | *a rise*, *an increase*, *growth* |
| down | *fall*, *decrease*, *decline*, *drop*, *go down*; *halve* | *a fall*, *a decrease*, *a decline*, *a drop* |
| no change | *remain stable*, *stay the same*, *level off*, *stabilise* | *a period of stability*, *a plateau* |
| up and down | *fluctuate* | *fluctuations* |
| highest / lowest point | *peak (at)*, *reach a peak*, *reach a low* | *a peak*, *a high*, *a low* |

| size of change | speed of change |
|---|---|
| large: *considerably*, *significantly* (careful in academic texts — see below), *substantially*, *dramatically* (very large) | fast: *sharply*, *rapidly*, *steeply* |
| moderate: *moderately* | steady: *steadily*, *gradually* |
| small: *slightly*, *marginally* | slow: *slowly* |

::: theorem Prepositions with numbers {#rule-prepositions}
1. ***to*** gives the new level; ***by*** gives the size of the change: *Car use fell **to** 9%.* *Car use fell **by** 13 percentage points.*
2. ***from … to …*** gives both levels: *Cycling rose **from** 8% **to** 27%.*
3. With nouns, use ***in*** for what changed and ***of*** for the amount: *a rise **in** cycling*, *a rise **of** 19 percentage points*.
4. ***at*** gives a level reached at a point: *Visits peaked **at** 2.4 million in 2019.* *Prices stood **at** \$200.*
5. ***Rise*** and ***fall*** are intransitive (*prices rose*); ***raise*** and ***lower*** take an object (*the company raised prices*). *Increase* and *decrease* can be both.
:::

::: proof
These prepositions encode different relations to the number: *to* marks an end point, *by* a difference, *at* a position. Confusing them changes the meaning, not just the style: *Sales rose by 20%* and *Sales rose to 20%* describe different situations, and only context can show which the writer meant. The *rise*/*raise* distinction follows a general pattern of English pairs of intransitive and transitive verbs (*lie*/*lay*, *fall*/*fell* a tree), and learner's dictionaries mark it with [I] and [T] (see [[verb-patterns]]).
:::

::: example Correcting trend language {#ex-trend}
Correct the errors. (a) *The number of visitors was increased from 2 million to 3 million.* (b) *Prices raised by 10% in 2023.* (c) *There was an increase of the number of cyclists.* (d) *Sales reached the peak in 2019 at 500 units.* (e) *Unemployment has fallen to 2%, from 7% to 5%.*
::: solution
1. **(a)** *increase* describing a change on its own is active and intransitive: *The number of visitors **increased** from 2 million to 3 million.* (The passive is possible only if someone increased it: *The budget was increased by the council.*)
2. **(b)** *raise* needs an object: *Prices **rose** by 10% in 2023.* (Or *The company **raised** prices by 10%.*)
3. **(c)** the noun takes *in* for what changed: *There was an increase **in** the number of cyclists.*
4. **(d)** *peak* is usually a verb with *at*, or *reached a peak*: *Sales **peaked at** 500 units in 2019.* / *Sales **reached a peak** of 500 units in 2019.*
5. **(e)** *to* and *by* are confused. A fall from 7% to 5% is a fall *to* 5%, *by* two percentage points: *Unemployment has fallen **from 7% to 5%**, a fall **of** two percentage points.*
:::
:::

## Quantities, percentages and approximation

::: theorem Per cent and percentage points {#rule-percentage-points}
When a figure that is already a percentage changes, distinguish:

1. the change in **percentage points** — the simple difference: from 8% to 27% is a rise of **19 percentage points**;
2. the change in **per cent** — the relative change: from 8% to 27% is a rise of about **240 per cent** (19 ÷ 8 ≈ 2.4), or *more than tripled*.

Both are correct; they answer different questions. Say which you mean. *Per cent* is written as one word in British English and usually as one word in American English (*percent*); the symbol *%* is common in descriptions of data.
:::

::: proof
A percentage is already a ratio, so "an increase of 19%" is ambiguous when the starting figure is 8%: the reader cannot tell whether the writer means 8% + 19 points or 8% × 1.19. Statistical writing, journalism style guides and academic texts use *percentage points* for differences between percentages for exactly this reason. Small starting figures make the relative change look dramatic (a rise from 1% to 3% is "tripling", but only two points), so a careful writer gives both, or chooses the one that fits the point honestly ([[academic-reading/reading-data]]).
:::

::: example Points or per cent? {#ex-points}
Using the table at the start of the lesson, describe the change in car use in two ways, and say which is more useful for the reader.
::: solution
1. **Find the figures**: 22% in 2015, 9% in 2025.
2. **Percentage points**: 22 − 9 = 13. *Car use fell by 13 percentage points.*
3. **Per cent (relative change)**: 13 ÷ 22 ≈ 0.59. *Car use fell by about 59 per cent* — that is, *by more than half*.
4. **Which is more useful?** The relative change (*by more than half*) shows how much car use itself changed, which is the point of interest; the points figure shows how the share of all students moved. A good description might use both: *The share of students driving fell from 22% to 9%, a drop of more than half.*
:::
:::

Data descriptions often need to approximate. The table gives exact figures, but the description should usually round them and state proportions in words:

- **approximation**: *about*, *around*, *approximately*, *roughly*, *just over*, *just under*, *nearly*, *almost*, *well over*;
- **fractions and proportions**: *a quarter*, *a third*, *half*, *two-thirds*, *three-quarters*; *one in five*; *the majority*, *a minority*, *most*, *few*;
- **multiples**: *twice as many as*, *three times as high as*, *double*, *half as much as*;
- **ranking**: *the highest*, *the second largest*, *the lowest*, *followed by*.

So 27% is *just over a quarter*, 38% is *nearly two-fifths* (or *just under 40%*), and 9% is *fewer than one in ten*.

::: warning Number, amount and agreement
- ***The number of*** + plural countable noun takes a **singular** verb: ✓ *The number of students **has** risen.* (✗ *The number of students have risen.*) But ***A number of*** (= ‘several’) takes a plural: *A number of students **have** complained.*
- ***Amount*** is used with uncountable nouns (*the amount of money*, *of time*), ***number*** with countable ones (*the number of cars*). ✗ *the amount of people*.
- ***Percentage*** and ***proportion*** go with *of* and take a verb that agrees with the noun after *of*: *A large percentage of the budget **was** spent …*; *A large percentage of students **were** …*. (Both singular and plural are found here; the plural is usual when the noun after *of* is plural.)
- Avoid ***significantly*** in descriptions of data unless you mean *statistically significant*, which a chart alone cannot show; use *considerably* or *substantially* instead.
:::

::: quiz
The unemployment rate fell from 10% to 8%. Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *Unemployment fell by 2%.*
- [x] *Unemployment fell by two percentage points.*
- [ ] *Unemployment fell to two percentage points.*
- [ ] *Unemployment fell by 8%.*
::: solution
The difference between 10% and 8% is two percentage points. *Fell by 2%* would mean a relative fall (10% → 9.8%), and the relative fall here is actually 20 per cent (2 ÷ 10). *Fell to two percentage points* gives the wrong final level, and *fell by 8%* confuses the final level with the change.
:::
:::

## Describing a process

Some data tasks show a **process** — how something is made, how a natural cycle works, how an application is handled — in a diagram with stages and arrows.

::: theorem Describing a process {#rule-process}
1. **Introduce** the process: what it is, and how many main stages it has.
2. **Overview**: where it starts and ends, and whether it is linear or a cycle.
3. **Describe the stages in order**, using sequence language: *first*, *to begin with*, *next*, *then*, *after that*, *once …*, *after being …*, *at the following stage*, *finally*. Vary it, and don't number every sentence.
4. Use the **passive** for man-made processes, where the doer is unimportant or obvious: *The bottles **are collected** and **sorted** by colour.* Use the **active** for natural processes: *Water **evaporates** from the sea.*
5. Include every stage in the diagram; combine minor ones with *and*, *before* or *-ing* clauses.
:::

::: proof
Process descriptions are about what happens to the material, not who does it, so the passive keeps the material as the subject of each sentence, which keeps the text cohesive (old information first; see [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]]). Natural processes have no human agent, so the active with an intransitive verb is natural (*evaporates*, *falls*, *flows*). Sequence words make the order visible, but a sequence word on every sentence becomes mechanical; clauses such as *once the glass has melted* and *after being cooled* link stages more smoothly ([[complex-sentences/participle-infinitive-clauses]]).
:::

::: example Describing the process {#ex-process}
Write a description of the process in the diagram above, in about 120 words.
::: solution
1. **Introduce**: what the diagram shows and how many stages.
2. **Overview**: it is a cycle, from used bottles back to new ones.
3. **Stages**: passive verbs, sequence language, minor stages combined.

> *The diagram shows how glass bottles are recycled, in eight stages from collection to sale. Overall, the process is a cycle: bottles that have been used become the raw material for new ones.*
>
> *To begin with, used bottles are collected and sorted by colour. They are then washed, and their labels are removed, before being crushed into small pieces. Next, these pieces are mixed with sand and other materials, and the mixture is melted in a furnace. Once the glass has melted, it is moulded into new bottles, which are filled and sold. After use, these bottles can be collected again, so the cycle begins once more.*

The description includes every stage, uses the passive throughout, combines stages with *and*, *before being* and *once*, and does not number each sentence.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence describes a stage of a natural process appropriately?
- [ ] *The water is evaporated by the sea.*
- [x] *Water evaporates from the surface of the sea.*
- [ ] *Firstly, the sea evaporates the water.*
- [ ] *The water evaporated is the first stage.*
::: solution
Evaporation has no human agent, so the active intransitive *evaporates* is natural. The first sentence makes the sea the agent of a passive, which misrepresents the process; the third does the same in the active; the fourth is ungrammatical as a description of a stage.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **增长了 and 增长到.** Chinese distinguishes *by* and *to* with 了 and 到 (增长了10% ‘rose by 10%’, 增长到10% ‘rose to 10%’), but the difference is small on the page and easily lost in translation. Check every *by* and *to* against the figures.
- **倍.** In careful Chinese usage, 增加了两倍 means ‘increased to three times the original’ (an increase of 200%), while 增加到两倍 means ‘doubled’; in everyday use the two are often confused. English has the same problem with *two times more*, which some readers take as ‘double’ and others as ‘triple’. Use unambiguous forms: *doubled*, *tripled*, *rose to three times its earlier level*, *rose by 200%*.
- **百分点 and 百分比.** Chinese has the same distinction as English between percentage points (百分点) and per cent (百分比). Use it in English as you would in Chinese.
- **Large numbers.** Chinese counts in 万 (ten thousand) and 亿 (hundred million); English in thousands, millions and billions. 3.5万 = *35,000*; 2亿 = *200 million*; 15亿 = *1.5 billion*. Check every conversion twice, and write *million* and *billion* without *-s* after a number (*two million people*, ✗ *two millions people*).
- **Overusing *dramatically*.** Learners' descriptions often use *dramatically* or *sharply* for every change. Keep strong words for changes that are large compared with the rest of the data.
:::

::: history
Most of the charts that essays describe are less than 250 years old. The Scottish engineer and economist William Playfair published the first line graphs and bar charts of economic data in *The Commercial and Political Atlas* (1786), and the first pie charts in his *Statistical Breviary* (1801). In 1858 Florence Nightingale used diagrams of the causes of soldiers' deaths in the Crimean War to persuade the British government that most of them had died of preventable disease rather than wounds — an early case of a chart used as the evidence in an argument. In the twentieth century the statistician John Tukey's *Exploratory Data Analysis* (1977) argued for looking at data graphically before calculating anything, and Edward Tufte's *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* (1983) set out principles for honest, clear charts that writers about data still cite.
:::

::: summary
- A data description reports the key features of a chart, table or diagram, with an overview of the main patterns ([[#def-data-description]]).
- Select, summarise, group and compare; report what the data show and mark any interpretation as yours ([[#rule-select]]).
- Use an introduction (what the chart shows), an overview (main features) and detail paragraphs grouped by pattern ([[#rule-structure]]).
- Describe change with precise verbs and adverbs; use *to* for the new level, *by* for the size of the change, *in* after a noun of change, and *rise* (intransitive) vs *raise* (transitive) ([[#rule-prepositions]]).

- Describe processes in order, with the passive for man-made processes and the active for natural ones, and every stage included ([[#rule-process]]).

Rewrite this process description using the passive where appropriate: *First, workers pick the tea leaves by hand. Then they spread the leaves out to dry. After that, machines roll the leaves, and the leaves oxidise in the air. Finally, workers heat the leaves to stop the oxidation and pack them.*

::: exercise Describe the table {level=3}
Write a description of 150–180 words of the table at the start of the lesson (modes of travel, 2015 and 2025), with an introduction, an overview and one or two detail paragraphs. Do not copy Description B.
::: hint
Group the modes by what happened to them: grew (cycling, walking), fell (car, bus), stayed similar (other). Give some changes in percentage points and some as relative changes.

::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

> The table shows the percentage of students at a university who used each of five ways of travelling to campus in 2015 and 2025.
>
> Overall, there was a clear shift away from motor vehicles towards cycling and walking, although the bus remained the most popular choice in both years.
>
> The largest increase was in cycling. Only 8% of students cycled in 2015, but by 2025 the figure had risen to 27%, more than three times the earlier level, making the bicycle the second most common mode of travel. Walking also grew, though only slightly, from 18% to 21%.
>
> By contrast, the share of students who drove fell from 22% to just 9%, a drop of more than half. Bus use also declined, by eight percentage points, but at 38% it still accounted for nearly two-fifths of all journeys. The proportion using other modes stayed almost unchanged, at around 5%.

Check: no list of every figure; overview before detail; features grouped (growth / decline); varied comparison language; no invented reasons.

::: exercise Use data in an argument {level=3}
Write a body paragraph (100–130 words) for an essay arguing that the fictional university in the table should invest in more cycle parking. Use two or three figures from the table as evidence, and explain what they show. Mark any interpretation clearly as yours.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

> The strongest reason to invest in cycle parking is that the way students travel has changed far faster than the space set aside for them. Between 2015 and 2025, the share of students cycling to campus more than tripled, from 8% to 27%, while the share driving fell from 22% to 9%. In other words, about one student in four now arrives by bicycle, three times as many as arrive by car. The table does not show why the change happened, but whatever its causes, a campus that still provides most of its space for cars and too little for bicycles is planning for the students it used to have. Moving some of that space to cycle parking would match provision to the way students actually travel.

Check: a claim; figures selected and compared; *The table does not show why …* marks the limit of the evidence; a link to the recommendation.
:::
