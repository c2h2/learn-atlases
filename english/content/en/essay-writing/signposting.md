Here is a body paragraph from a draft essay on whether public libraries should lend tools and equipment as well as books. Its ideas are sound. Read it and count the linking words.

> *Firstly, lending tools saves money for residents. Moreover, most people use a drill for only a few minutes a year. Furthermore, buying one costs a lot. In addition, it takes up space at home. Besides, libraries already have systems for lending. What's more, they have staff who can explain how to use equipment safely. Therefore, lending tools is a good idea.*

There are seven sentences and seven connectives, and five of them say the same thing: *and here is another point*. Yet the sentences are not simply a list. The fact that a drill is used for a few minutes a year is the *reason* that buying one is poor value; the lending systems and the staff are a *different* argument — that libraries are well placed to do this — not more evidence for the first. The connectives hide those relations instead of showing them. Here is a revision:

> *Lending tools would save residents money on things they rarely use. A household drill, for example, may be used for only a few minutes a year, yet it has to be bought, stored and eventually replaced; borrowing one when it is needed costs nothing and takes up no space. Libraries are also well placed to offer such a service. They already have systems for lending, tracking and recalling items, and staff who could show borrowers how to use equipment safely.*

The revision has three connectives (*for example*, *yet*, *also*), and each marks a relation the reader needs to see. Most of the linking is done in other ways: *things they rarely use* prepares for the drill; *borrowing one* contrasts with *buying*; *such a service* points back to the whole first half; *They already have* continues with the same subject. The paragraph is easier to follow with fewer signposts, because the ones that remain mean something.

This lesson is about how an essay holds together and how it shows its reader the way: the links between paragraphs, the signposts that mark the stages of an argument, the right and wrong uses of connectives, and the quieter devices — reference, repetition, the order of information — that do most of the work. The basic linking adverbials and their punctuation are taught in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]], and the grammar of adverbial clauses in [[complex-sentences/adverbial-clauses]]; here the unit is the whole essay.

## Cohesion and signposting

::: definition Cohesion, coherence and signposting {#def-signposting}
- **Coherence** is the quality of a text whose ideas make sense together, in an order the reader can follow ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence#def-coherence]]).
- **Cohesion** is the set of visible links in the language of a text that connect its parts: reference (*this*, *they*, *such*), repetition and related words, substitution and ellipsis, and connectives.
- **Signposting** is language that tells the reader about the text itself: where the argument is going (*This essay argues that …*), what stage it has reached (*A second problem is …*, *Turning to costs, …*), how one part relates to another (*as noted above*), and what a sentence is doing (*for example*, *in other words*, *to conclude*).

Signposts are one kind of **metadiscourse** — writing about the writing — and connectives are one kind of signpost.
:::

Cohesion and coherence are related but not the same. A text can be full of cohesive links and still incoherent — *I bought a bicycle. Bicycles have two wheels. Wheels were invented in Mesopotamia.* — and a coherent text can have few explicit links, if the reader can see how each sentence follows from the last. The goal is coherence; cohesion and signposting are the means of making it visible.

::: theorem Large and small signposts {#rule-macro-micro}
An essay uses signposts at two scales:

1. **Large-scale** (macro) signposts show the structure of the whole essay: the thesis and any preview in the introduction; the topic sentence of each body paragraph, which names its point and its place in the argument; the marker of the counter-argument; the opening of the conclusion.
2. **Small-scale** (micro) signposts link sentences within a paragraph: connectives such as *however*, *for example*, *as a result*, and reference words such as *this problem*.

Get the large signposts right first. A reader who knows the point of each paragraph needs few small ones; a reader who does not will not be rescued by them.
:::

::: proof
Readers build a mental outline of a text as they read, and they use the beginnings of paragraphs and sections to do it. That is why skimming works: reading the first sentence of each paragraph gives most of the structure of a well-written text ([[academic-reading/academic-texts#rule-key-places]]). A writer who makes those first sentences do their job has signposted the essay. Small connectives, by contrast, work only within the frame the large signposts provide: *however* tells the reader that a contrast follows, but not what the contrast is about or why it matters to the thesis. Research on listening makes a similar point: in a study of lecture comprehension, Chaudron and Richards (1986) found that macro-markers announcing structure helped second-language listeners' recall more than micro-markers between sentences ([[presentations-and-discussion/signposting-a-talk#rule-macro]]).
:::

## Linking paragraphs

The point where one paragraph ends and the next begins is where readers most often lose the thread. A reader who finishes a paragraph about cost and begins one about staff needs to know whether the second is another reason, an objection, an example of the first or a change of subject.

::: theorem Topic sentences that link {#rule-paragraph-links}
Make the first sentence of a body paragraph do two jobs: **state its point** and **show how that point relates** to the thesis or to the previous paragraph. Common ways of doing this:

1. **Name the relation in the topic sentence**: *A second reason …*, *A more serious objection …*, *The cost is not the only obstacle.*, *Even if the cost could be met, …*
2. **Pick up a word or idea from the previous paragraph**: … *without any extra cost.* / *Cost, however, is only part of the picture.*
3. **Use *this*, *these* or *such* + a summary noun**: *These savings would mean little if …*
4. **Refer back to the thesis**: *The strongest argument for lending tools, however, concerns the environment.*

Avoid empty announcements of the paragraph's place (*Firstly*, *Secondly*, *Thirdly* alone) when a sentence that names the relation is possible.
:::

::: proof
A paragraph that begins *Secondly, …* tells the reader that it is the second something, but not the second what: a second reason, a second example, a second objection? A topic sentence such as *A second, less obvious benefit is …* answers that question in the same number of words. The techniques in points 2 and 3 apply the principle of old information first ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]]) at the level of the paragraph: the new paragraph starts from what the reader has just read and moves to something new. Point 4 keeps the paragraph tied to the essay's answer, which is the job of a topic sentence in an argumentative essay ([[essay-writing/arguing-a-point#rule-paragraph-shape]]).
:::

::: example Writing linking topic sentences {#ex-paragraph-links}
An essay argues that cities should plant more street trees. Paragraph 1 ends: *… on the hottest days, a street lined with mature trees can be several degrees cooler than a bare one.* Paragraph 2 is about cleaner air; paragraph 3 answers the objection that trees are expensive to maintain. Write the first sentence of paragraphs 2 and 3 so that each links to what came before.
::: solution
1. **Paragraph 2** is a second benefit. Pick up *cooler* and add the new point: *Trees do more than cool the street; they also clean the air that people breathe in it.* The structure *more than X; also Y* shows that this is a second reason, without *Secondly*.
2. Alternatively, refer back to the thesis: *A second benefit of street trees, less visible than shade, is cleaner air.*
3. **Paragraph 3** is an objection. Name it as an objection and summarise the benefits so far with a summary noun: *These benefits come at a cost, and critics argue that cities cannot afford to maintain thousands of new trees.*

In each case the reader knows from the first sentence what kind of paragraph follows and how it fits.
:::
:::

::: warning *In this paragraph, I will …*
✗ *In this paragraph, I will discuss the environmental benefits of lending tools.*

Announcing what a paragraph will do is common in learners' essays and is rarely needed in a short one: the topic sentence can simply make the point (✓ *Lending tools also reduces waste.*). In long essays, reports and dissertations, signposts that describe the text are useful — *This section examines …*, *Chapter 3 turns to …* — because the reader needs a map. In a 300-word essay, the essay is its own map.
:::

## Connectives: fewer, and the right ones

::: theorem Choose connectives by the relation {#rule-connectives}
1. **Use a connective where the relation between two sentences or paragraphs is not clear without it** — usually contrast, concession, cause and result, or a change of direction. Additions and examples are often clear from the content.
2. **Choose the connective for the relation actually present**: addition (*also*, *in addition*, *moreover*), contrast (*however*, *in contrast*, *on the other hand*), concession (*nevertheless*, *even so*, *admittedly*), cause and result (*therefore*, *as a result*, *consequently*, *thus*), example (*for example*, *for instance*), restatement (*in other words*, *that is*), and summary (*in short*, *overall*).
3. **Do not stack connectives** that mean the same thing (✗ *Moreover, in addition, …*), and do not start every sentence with one.
4. **Vary the position**: a connective inside the sentence (*The cost, however, is …*) or a conjunction (*but*, *so*, *because*) is often smoother than a sentence-initial adverbial.
:::

::: proof
A connective is an instruction to the reader about how to read the next sentence. An instruction that is unnecessary costs a little attention; one that is wrong costs more, because the reader looks for a relation that is not there. Research on learner writing has repeatedly found overuse of connectives, especially at the start of sentences. Crewe (1990) argued that connectives in student writing were often used as decoration, to make a text look logical rather than to show its logic. Granger and Tyson (1996), comparing essays by French-speaking learners with essays by native speakers, found that the learners overused some connectives and underused others, and Field and Yip (1992) found that Cantonese-speaking students in Hong Kong used more connectives than native-speaker students did, mostly at the beginning of sentences ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]]). The lesson of these studies is not that connectives are bad, but that their number is not a measure of organisation.
:::

### Words that are often confused

| connective | meaning and use | not for |
|---|---|---|
| *moreover*, *furthermore* | formal; adds a point that **strengthens** the same argument, often a stronger one | a neutral list of items, or a contrast |
| *in addition*, *also* | neutral addition | — |
| *besides* | informal; adds a further, often decisive, reason (*Besides, it's too late.*) | formal essays (use *in addition* or *moreover*) |
| *what's more* | informal and spoken | formal essays |
| *however* | contrast or concession with what was just said | joining two clauses after a comma alone |
| *on the other hand* | the other side of the **same** question, often after *on the one hand* | adding a similar point |
| *in contrast*, *by contrast* | a difference between two things compared | correcting a false belief (use *in fact*) |
| *on the contrary* | the opposite of what was just said is true | a simple contrast between two things |
| *nevertheless*, *nonetheless*, *even so* | concession: the main point holds despite what was said | — |
| *therefore*, *thus*, *consequently*, *as a result* | result or logical conclusion; *thus* is the most formal | a mere next point |
| *in fact*, *indeed* | *in fact* corrects or strengthens; *indeed* confirms and strengthens | introducing an unrelated new point |
| *last but not least* | final item in a list (spoken, informal) | a formal essay |

::: example Removing and replacing connectives {#ex-connectives}
Revise this paragraph, keeping only the connectives that are needed and correcting any that are wrong.

*On the one hand, many students prefer printed textbooks. Moreover, they say that it is easier to concentrate on paper. Furthermore, on the contrary, digital textbooks are cheaper. In addition, they can be updated. Therefore, they are lighter to carry. On the other hand, students can also search them quickly.*
::: solution
1. **Find the relations.** Sentences 1–2: a preference and the reason for it (not an addition). Sentence 3 turns to the other side: a contrast. Sentences 3–5: three advantages of digital books. Sentence 6 is a fourth advantage, not the other side.
2. **Wrong connectives.** *Moreover* in sentence 2 introduces a reason, not a new point; *on the contrary* is wrong (nothing is being denied); *Therefore* in sentence 5 suggests that being lighter results from being updatable; *On the other hand* in sentence 6 introduces a similar point, not a contrast.
3. **Unnecessary ones.** *Furthermore* and *In addition* are not needed once the advantages are grouped in one sentence.
4. **Rewrite.**

> *Many students prefer printed textbooks, because they find it easier to concentrate on paper. Digital textbooks, on the other hand, are cheaper, lighter to carry and easy to search, and they can be updated when the material changes.*

Six connectives have become two (*because*, *on the other hand*), both marking real relations, and the paragraph is half as long.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which connective fits? *Most of the town's residents support the new cycle lanes. ___, some shopkeepers on the high street fear that they will lose customers who drive.*
- [ ] *Moreover*
- [x] *However*
- [ ] *Therefore*
- [ ] *On the contrary*
::: solution
The second sentence contrasts with the first: residents support the lanes, but some shopkeepers have doubts. *Moreover* would add a point in the same direction; *Therefore* would make the shopkeepers' fear a result of the residents' support; *On the contrary* would deny the first sentence, which the second does not do.
:::
:::

## The grammar of linking

Many linking errors are not about meaning at all, but about grammar: the same relation can be expressed by words of different classes, and each class has its own syntax.

::: theorem Three kinds of linking word {#rule-link-grammar}
| relation | conjunction (joins clauses in one sentence) | linking adverbial (links sentences) | preposition (+ noun phrase or *-ing*) |
|---|---|---|---|
| contrast / concession | *but*, *although*, *though*, *while*, *whereas* | *however*, *nevertheless*, *in contrast* | *despite*, *in spite of*, *unlike* |
| cause | *because*, *since*, *as* | — | *because of*, *due to*, *owing to* |
| result | *so*, *so that* | *therefore*, *as a result*, *consequently* | *as a result of* (gives the cause) |
| addition | *and* | *also*, *in addition*, *moreover* | *in addition to*, *as well as*, *besides* |

1. A **linking adverbial** cannot join two clauses after a comma: ✗ *It was expensive, however it was popular.* ✓ *It was expensive. However, it was popular.* ✓ *It was expensive; however, it was popular.* ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence#rule-linker-punct]])
2. A **preposition** takes a noun phrase, not a clause: ✗ *Because of it was expensive* ✓ *Because of the cost* / ✓ *Because it was expensive*.
3. Use **one** marker for one relation: ✗ *Although it was expensive, but …* ✗ *Because it was expensive, so …* ([[essay-writing/counter-arguments#rule-concession-grammar]]).
4. *In addition to*, *as well as* and *besides* as prepositions take *-ing*, not a bare verb: ✓ *In addition to saving money, it saves space.* ✗ *In addition to save money …*
:::

::: proof
English keeps the grammatical classes of linking words apart, while their meanings overlap: *although*, *however* and *despite* can all express concession, but only *although* introduces a finite clause in the same sentence, only *however* can begin a new sentence and refer back to the previous one, and only *despite* is followed by a noun phrase ([[complex-sentences/adverbial-clauses#rule-concession]]). Dictionaries label these classes (*conj*, *adv*, *prep*), and checking the label is the quickest way to settle a doubt. The double-marking errors in point 3 have a clear source for Chinese speakers, since 虽然……但是 and 因为……所以 are normal pairs in Chinese; but they are common among learners from many languages, because each marker seems to need its partner.
:::

::: example Correcting linking grammar {#ex-link-grammar}
Correct the errors. (a) *Because of the library has a lending system, it could lend tools easily.* (b) *Tools are expensive, therefore many people cannot buy them.* (c) *Because most tools are rarely used, so lending them makes sense.* (d) *Besides lend books, libraries could lend tools.* (e) *Despite tools can be dangerous, libraries can teach people to use them.*
::: solution
1. **(a)** *because of* + clause → *Because the library has a lending system, …* or *Because of its lending system, …*
2. **(b)** comma splice with *therefore* → *Tools are expensive; therefore, many people cannot buy them.* or *Tools are expensive, so many people cannot buy them.*
3. **(c)** double marker → *Because most tools are rarely used, lending them makes sense.* or *Most tools are rarely used, so lending them makes sense.*
4. **(d)** *besides* + bare verb → *Besides lending books, libraries could lend tools.*
5. **(e)** *despite* + clause → *Although tools can be dangerous, …* or *Despite the dangers of some tools, …*
:::
:::

## The quieter links: reference, repetition and the order of information

Connectives are the most visible form of cohesion, but they are not the most common. Most of what holds a good paragraph together is reference and repetition.

::: theorem Let reference and key words carry the thread {#rule-reference-chains}
1. **Use *this*, *these* and *such* with a noun** that names what is referred to, especially at the start of a sentence or paragraph: *This shift*, *These costs*, *Such a scheme*. A bare *This* can refer to too many things.
2. **Repeat key terms.** In an argument, the words that name the essay's central concepts — *free entry*, *lending*, *street trees* — should stay the same throughout. Replacing them with synonyms for variety (*free admission*, *no-charge access*, *open museums*) makes the reader wonder whether these are different things.
3. **Put known information first.** Begin sentences, where possible, with something the reader has just met, and put new information at the end, where it receives most emphasis.
4. **Use substitution and ellipsis** to avoid heavy repetition of whole phrases: *Some cities have introduced free transport; others have chosen not to* ([[complex-sentences/ellipsis-substitution]]).
:::

::: proof
Halliday and Hasan (1976) showed that the links between sentences in English texts are made by a small number of devices — reference, substitution, ellipsis, conjunction and lexical cohesion (repetition and related words) — and that conjunction is only one of them. A summary noun after *this* both links the sentence to what came before and labels it, which is why it is so useful at paragraph boundaries ([[reading-skills/following-the-thread#rule-this-summary]]). The advice to repeat key terms runs against the school rule that repetition is bad style; but in argument and explanation, consistent terms are a form of precision. Elegant variation belongs to description and narrative, where it does less harm. The principle of known-before-new is treated in detail in [[style-and-rhetoric/cohesion-emphasis]].
:::

::: example *This* + noun {#ex-this-noun}
Add a summary noun after each *this* or *these*, and say what it refers to. (a) *Many universities now record lectures and put them online. This has changed how students prepare for exams.* (b) *Bus fares in the city rose three times in two years, while services were cut. These led many residents to buy cars.* (c) *Some schools have replaced homework with reading in class. This is popular with parents.*
::: solution
1. **(a)** *This practice* (or *This change*) — the whole first sentence: recording lectures and putting them online.
2. **(b)** *These changes* (or *These increases and cuts*) — both the fare rises and the service cuts. *These* alone could be read as referring only to the fares.
3. **(c)** *This policy* (or *This approach*) — replacing homework with reading in class. Without a noun, *This* might refer to *reading in class* alone.

In each case the noun makes the reference precise and summarises the previous sentence in one word, which is often the word the next sentence needs.
:::
:::

::: warning Elegant variation
✗ *Free museum entry widens access. Moreover, no-charge admission brings in families. In addition, open-door policies help tourism.*

The writer has used three expressions for one idea, and a reader may think that *open-door policies* means something different. Repeat the key term, or refer to it with a pronoun or *this* + noun: ✓ *Free entry widens access, especially for families with low incomes. It also brings in tourists who might not otherwise visit.*
:::

## How much signposting?

::: theorem Signpost in proportion to length {#rule-proportion}
1. **Short essays** (up to about 500 words): the thesis, the topic sentences and the opening of the conclusion are usually enough; within paragraphs, use connectives only where the relation would otherwise be unclear. A preview of the structure is optional.
2. **Longer essays** (about 1,500 words or more): add a preview of the structure in the introduction (*This essay first considers …, then …*), short transitions between sections, and occasional references back (*as argued in the previous section*).
3. **Reports and dissertations**: headings, numbered sections and explicit statements of purpose are expected.
4. In all of them, **a signpost must be true**: a paragraph introduced as *A second reason* must contain a reason, and *Finally* must introduce the last point.
:::

::: proof
The amount of guidance a reader needs grows with the length and complexity of the text: in a short essay the reader can hold the whole structure in mind, while in a long one they need reminders. Hyland's (2005) studies of academic writing describe these devices as interactive metadiscourse — frame markers that sequence and label stages (*first*, *to conclude*, *my purpose here is*), transitions between ideas (*but*, *thus*), endophoric markers that refer to other parts of the text (*see section 2*, *as noted above*) and code glosses that rephrase or exemplify (*in other words*, *for example*) — and show that their use varies between genres and disciplines. The essay writer's task is to choose the amount that fits the text, not to show that they know the forms.
:::

::: quiz
In a 300-word essay, which signpost is **least** useful?
- [ ] *A more serious objection is that tools can be dangerous.*
- [x] *In this essay I will first introduce the topic, then give my reasons, then discuss an objection and finally conclude.*
- [ ] *These savings would mean little if few residents used the service.*
- [ ] *It follows that libraries are well placed to offer tool lending.*
::: solution
The second sentence describes the structure of every essay and uses words that the reader of a short essay does not need. The others each name a relation that matters to the argument: an objection, a condition on the savings, and a conclusion.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **首先、其次、再次、最后.** Chinese school essays often list points with 首先, 其次, 再次 and 最后, and learners translate them as *Firstly*, *Secondly*, *Thirdly*, *Lastly* or *Finally*. These are correct English, and in some texts — instructions, short exam answers — they are useful. But an English essay that numbers every paragraph in this way can read like a list; a topic sentence that names the relation (*A second, more practical reason …*) usually reads better.
- **此外、而且、另外.** These are often translated *Moreover*, *Furthermore* or *In addition*, with the result that formal additive connectives appear far more often in learners' English than in expert writing. Often *also*, *and*, or no connective at all is the natural translation.
- **Parataxis and hypotaxis.** Comparisons of English and Chinese (连淑能《英汉对比研究》 is a standard account) describe English as relying more on explicit grammatical links between clauses (形合, hypotaxis) and Chinese more on meaning and order (意合, parataxis). Some learners draw the conclusion that English needs a connective in every sentence. That is not what the contrast means: English marks the grammatical relation between clauses within a sentence more explicitly, with conjunctions and subordinate clauses, but it does not require a linking word between every pair of sentences.
- **Double markers.** 虽然……但是 and 因为……所以 produce ✗ *Although … but* and ✗ *Because … so*. Use one marker in English.
- **Full sentences and commas.** A Chinese paragraph can join several clauses with commas (一逗到底); in English, a comma before *however*, *therefore* or *moreover* between two complete clauses produces a comma splice.
:::

::: history
The study of how texts hold together grew out of linguistics in the 1960s and 1970s. M. A. K. Halliday and Ruqaiya Hasan's *Cohesion in English* (1976) classified the links between sentences into reference, substitution, ellipsis, conjunction and lexical cohesion, and distinguished cohesion, a property of the language, from the coherence a reader perceives. The idea that some language in a text is about the text itself — *metadiscourse*, a term usually credited to the linguist Zellig Harris (1959) — was developed in composition studies in the United States, notably by Joseph Williams in *Style* (first edition 1981) and by William Vande Kopple (1985), and later given a large corpus basis by Ken Hyland's *Metadiscourse* (2005). Meanwhile, studies of learner corpora, such as the International Corpus of Learner English begun at the University of Louvain in the 1990s under Sylviane Granger, made it possible to compare learners' use of connectives with that of expert writers, and gave evidence for what teachers had long suspected: that learners tend to use connectives more often, and more often at the start of sentences, than writers who are not consciously trying to sound organised.
:::

## Where this leads

The large signposts of an essay are its thesis ([[essay-writing/thesis-introduction]]), its topic sentences ([[essay-writing/arguing-a-point]]), its counter-argument markers ([[essay-writing/counter-arguments]]) and its conclusion ([[essay-writing/conclusions]]); checking them is one of the passes of [[essay-writing/revising-editing]]. Following cohesive links when reading is practised in [[reading-skills/following-the-thread]], and signposts in academic texts in [[academic-reading/academic-texts]]. Known-before-new and the cohesion of sentences are developed in [[style-and-rhetoric/cohesion-emphasis]], and spoken signposts in [[presentations-and-discussion/signposting-a-talk]].

::: summary
- Coherence is ideas that make sense together; cohesion is the visible links between parts of a text; signposting is language that tells the reader about the text's structure ([[#def-signposting]]).
- Get the large signposts right first — thesis, topic sentences, counter-argument markers, conclusion — and use small ones within paragraphs only where needed ([[#rule-macro-micro]]).
- Make each topic sentence state its point and show how it relates to the previous paragraph or the thesis ([[#rule-paragraph-links]]).
- Use a connective only where the relation is not clear without it, choose it for the relation actually present, and do not stack or repeat connectives ([[#rule-connectives]]).
- Conjunctions, linking adverbials and prepositions differ in grammar: no comma splices with *however*, no clauses after *despite* or *because of*, one marker per relation ([[#rule-link-grammar]]).
- Reference with *this* + noun, consistent key terms and known-before-new order do most of the work of cohesion ([[#rule-reference-chains]]).
- Signpost in proportion to the length of the text, and make every signpost true ([[#rule-proportion]]).
:::

::: exercise Add summary nouns {level=2}
Complete each *this* or *these* with a suitable noun. (a) *The city has closed three libraries and cut the opening hours of the rest. These ___ have hit students hardest.* (b) *Some employers let staff choose when they start and finish work. This ___ is popular with parents.* (c) *Sea levels rose by several centimetres over the period. This ___ threatens low-lying towns.*
::: solution
Possible answers: (a) *closures and cuts*, *changes*, *decisions*. (b) *arrangement*, *flexibility*, *policy*. (c) *rise*, *increase*, *trend*.
:::

::: exercise Link the paragraphs {level=2}
An essay argues that secondary schools should start later in the morning. Paragraph 1 ends: *… so teenagers who must be at school by eight are, in effect, being asked to start the day in the middle of their biological night.* Paragraph 2 argues that later starts improve attendance; paragraph 3 answers the objection that later starts make it harder for parents to get to work. Write the first sentence of paragraphs 2 and 3.
::: solution
Answers will vary. Models:

Paragraph 2: *The effects of that lost sleep are visible in the classroom register as well as in the classroom: later starts tend to improve attendance.* (In a real essay this claim would need a source.)

Paragraph 3: *These benefits would count for little if a later school day made family life impossible, and some parents argue that it would, because their own working day starts early.*

Check: each sentence names the point of its paragraph and links to the previous one (*that lost sleep*, *These benefits*), without *Secondly* or *On the other hand*.
:::
:::

::: exercise Cut the connectives {level=2}
Revise this paragraph so that it uses no more than two connectives, without losing any relation the reader needs. *Firstly, cycling is good for health. Moreover, it is cheap. Furthermore, it causes no pollution. However, many people do not cycle. Therefore, the main reason is safety. In addition, they are afraid of traffic.*
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

> *Cycling is healthy, cheap and clean, yet many people do not cycle. The main reason they give is safety: they are afraid of traffic.*

The three advantages are one sentence with one subject; *yet* marks the real contrast; the colon introduces the explanation. *Therefore* was wrong (safety is not a result of people not cycling), and *In addition* introduced not a new point but the explanation of the previous one.
:::
:::

::: exercise Signpost a longer essay {level=3}
You are writing a 2,000-word essay on *To what extent has remote working changed city centres?* It has four sections: (1) changes in office use, (2) effects on shops and restaurants, (3) effects on public transport, (4) whether the changes are permanent. Write (a) a preview sentence for the end of the introduction, (b) a transition sentence between sections 2 and 3, and (c) the first sentence of the conclusion, with a reference back to the body.
::: solution
Answers will vary. Models:

(a) *This essay first examines how the use of offices has changed, then considers the effects on the shops, restaurants and transport that depend on office workers, and finally asks whether these changes are likely to last.*

(b) *The fall in weekday spending in the centre is closely linked to a second change: fewer people are travelling there at all.*

(c) *The evidence considered in the previous sections suggests that remote working has changed city centres less by emptying them than by changing their rhythm — quieter on some weekdays, busier on others.*

Check: the preview is true to the structure and shows the logic of the order; the transition picks up the previous section (*The fall in weekday spending*) and names the next topic; the conclusion refers back and gives an answer of degree.
:::

::: exercise Revise for cohesion {level=3}
Revise this paragraph (about 110 words) so that it is cohesive without being mechanical: remove unnecessary connectives, correct wrong ones, use *this* + noun where helpful, and keep key terms consistent.

*Firstly, a four-day working week can improve productivity. Moreover, workers who have three days off are less tired. Furthermore, a shorter week makes employees more focused. On the contrary, some companies say it is impossible. In addition, hospitals cannot close for a day. Therefore, shorter working time is not suitable for every job. On the other hand, a reduced week can be organised with shifts. Besides, the trial results were positive. This shows it works.*
::: hint
Find the main point, the reason, the objection and the answer. Which sentences are evidence for which? Which connectives mark a relation that is not there?
:::
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

> *A four-day working week can make workers more productive, because people with three days off return to work less tired and more focused. Not every organisation can close for a day, of course: a hospital or a power station must run every day of the week. But a four-day week does not have to mean a closed workplace. Staff can work in shifts, so that each person works four days while the organisation stays open on all seven. Where this arrangement has been tried, employers have often reported positive results.*

Changes: *Moreover* and *Furthermore* replaced by *because* (the tiredness is the reason); *On the contrary* (wrong) replaced by an objection marked by *of course* and answered with *But*; *Therefore* removed; *On the other hand* and *Besides* removed; key term kept as *four-day week* (not *shorter working time*, *reduced week*); *This shows it works* replaced by *this arrangement*, and the claim about trials hedged (*often reported*) and generalised, since the original *the trial results* referred to no trial the reader knew about. In a real essay a source for the trials would be needed.
:::
