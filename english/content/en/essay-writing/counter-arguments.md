Imagine reading an essay that argues that every city centre should ban private cars. It gives three reasons — cleaner air, safer streets, more space for people — each well supported. But it never mentions the people who will be most affected: the shopkeeper whose customers drive in, the disabled resident who cannot walk to a bus stop, the nurse whose shift ends after the last train. A reader who thinks of them at once will think the writer did not, and will trust the whole essay less.

Now imagine the same essay with one more paragraph:

> *The strongest objection to a ban is that it would cut off people who depend on cars: residents with disabilities, workers on night shifts, and businesses whose customers drive in. This objection is serious, but it is an argument for exemptions and better services rather than against the ban itself. Most of the cities that have closed their centres to private cars allow access for disabled drivers, deliveries and emergency vehicles, and a ban can be timed to begin only once night buses and park-and-ride services are in place.*

The paragraph does three things. It states the opposing view fairly — in fact, at its strongest. It accepts what is true in it. And it answers it, by showing that it changes the details of the policy rather than the case for it. The essay is now more convincing, not less, because the reader can see that the writer has considered the other side and still holds the position.

This lesson shows how to present opposing views and answer them, where to put them in an essay, and the language of concession and rebuttal. It builds on the paragraphs of [[essay-writing/arguing-a-point]].

## Counter-argument, concession and rebuttal

::: definition Counter-argument, concession and rebuttal {#def-counter}
- A **counter-argument** (or *opposing view*, *objection*) is an argument against the writer's position, or against one of its claims, that a reasonable reader might make.
- A **concession** is the writer's acknowledgement that something in the counter-argument is true or has force.
- A **rebuttal** (or *refutation*, *response*) is the writer's answer to the counter-argument: why it does not defeat the position, or how the position allows for it.

In an essay, the three usually come together: *Opponents argue that X* (counter-argument). *It is true that …* (concession). *However, …* (rebuttal).
:::

::: theorem Address the strongest opposing view {#rule-strongest}
1. In an argumentative essay, deal with **at least one** serious counter-argument — usually the one a thoughtful reader is most likely to raise.
2. State it **fairly**, in a form its supporters would accept, and preferably at its strongest.
3. **Answer it**: never raise an objection and leave it unanswered, unless you are deliberately conceding the point and changing your thesis.
4. Keep the counter-argument **shorter** than your own case, but long enough to be taken seriously.
:::

::: proof
An argument is addressed to readers who have not yet agreed, and those readers have objections in mind. Raising the strongest one first shows that the writer has thought about the question, and answering it removes a reason for doubt. Research on persuasion supports this. In a review of many experiments, O'Keefe (1999) found that messages which acknowledged opposing arguments *and refuted them* were on average more persuasive than one-sided messages, while messages which mentioned opposing arguments without answering them were less persuasive than one-sided ones. A weak or distorted version of the opposing view (a *straw man*) has the opposite effect: readers who hold the view notice the distortion, and readers who do not may later meet the real argument and feel misled. The principle of stating an opponent's view in its strongest form is sometimes called the **principle of charity**.
:::

::: example Fair or unfair? {#ex-fair}
The thesis is *Secondary schools should not set homework for students under fourteen.* Which version of the counter-argument is fair? Rewrite the unfair one.

(a) *Some people think children should do homework because they enjoy making children suffer.* (b) *Supporters of homework argue that practice outside lessons helps students remember what they have learned, and that it teaches them to work independently — habits they will need in later years.*
::: solution
1. **(a)** is a straw man. It assigns a motive no supporter of homework would accept, and it gives no argument at all. Answering it proves nothing, and a reader who supports homework will stop reading in good faith.
2. **(b)** is fair. It states two real arguments — memory and independent work — in terms that teachers who set homework would recognise.
3. A rewrite of (a), keeping its suggestion that homework causes stress but making it fair to the other side: *Supporters of homework accept that it can be stressful, but argue that a moderate amount teaches children to manage their time and to work without a teacher's help.*

The essay must now answer (b), which is harder than answering (a). That is the point: an essay that survives the strongest objection is stronger for it.
:::
:::

## Ways of answering a counter-argument

::: theorem Four kinds of rebuttal {#rule-rebuttal}
A writer can answer a counter-argument in four main ways:

1. **Refute it**: show that it is false or not supported by the evidence. *The claim that free transport is always overcrowded is not borne out by …*
2. **Outweigh it**: accept that it is true, but show that the reasons for the position are more important. *Free transport does cost money, but the costs of congestion are greater.*
3. **Limit it**: accept that it applies in some cases, and narrow the position to exclude them. *The objection holds for rural areas, where buses are rare; this essay's proposal concerns cities.*
4. **Turn it**: show that the objection, properly understood, supports the position or calls for a change in detail rather than in principle. *The concern about disabled residents is an argument for exemptions, not against the ban.*

Choose the answer the evidence supports. If none of them works, the objection is decisive, and the thesis must change.
:::

::: proof
These four responses correspond to the main ways an objection can relate to a claim: it can be false (refute), true but less important (outweigh), true in some cases (limit), or true and compatible with the claim in another form (turn). In classical rhetoric, the *refutatio* was a fixed part of a speech, placed after the speaker's own proof; teachers of argument have since distinguished outright refutation from the subtler responses of concession and qualification. Limiting a claim is not a defeat: a thesis that has been narrowed to fit the evidence is a better thesis. That is why the counter-argument stage is also a stage of revision ([[essay-writing/writing-process#rule-revise]]): an objection the writer cannot answer is a signal to change the thesis.
:::

::: example Choosing a response {#ex-response}
The thesis is *Universities should record all lectures.* For each objection, choose a type of rebuttal and write one or two sentences.

(a) *Recording lectures will reduce attendance.* (b) *Recordings cost money to make and store.* (c) *Lecturers will be less willing to discuss controversial material if they know they are being recorded.*
::: solution
1. **(a) Limit or outweigh.** The evidence on attendance is mixed, and the writer must not claim it is clear in either direction. A careful answer: *Attendance may fall in some courses, but students who choose recordings over lectures are often those who could not attend in any case, because of work, illness or care for relatives; for them, the choice is not between a lecture and a recording but between a recording and nothing.*
2. **(b) Outweigh.** *Recording does have costs, but once equipment is installed, the extra cost of each recording is small compared with the cost of the teaching it preserves.* (If the writer has a reliable figure, it belongs here.)
3. **(c) Turn or limit.** *This concern is real, but it is an argument for letting lecturers pause the recording for discussion, not for refusing to record at all.* This also changes the thesis slightly: *all lectures* becomes *all lectures, with the option to pause for discussion*.

The third objection improved the thesis. That is common: a writer who takes objections seriously ends with a more precise position.
:::

## Where to put counter-arguments

There are three common patterns:

1. **A counter-argument paragraph** after the writer's own reasons and before the conclusion. This is the most common pattern in short essays, and the one most examiners expect.
2. **Objections inside each body paragraph**: each reason is followed by a likely objection and the answer to it. This suits essays where each reason has its own obvious objection.
3. **The opposing view first**: the essay begins by setting out the case it will argue against, then answers it and builds its own case. This suits questions phrased as a claim to evaluate (*"X is the best way to …" Discuss.*) and essays addressed to readers who hold the opposing view.

::: theorem Placing the counter-argument {#rule-placing}
1. Place your strongest counter-argument where the reader is most likely to raise it — often after your main case, before the conclusion.
2. Do not end the essay on the counter-argument: the reader should finish with your rebuttal and your conclusion.
3. Signal the counter-argument clearly (*Critics of this view argue that …*) so that the reader does not mistake it for your own position.
:::

::: proof
Readers remember beginnings and endings best, and an essay that ends with an unanswered objection leaves the reader with doubt rather than the writer's case. Signalling matters because a counter-argument presented without a marker looks like a change of mind: a reader who meets *Cars are essential for many people* in the middle of an essay against cars will wonder whether the writer has switched sides. The phrases in the next section are the markers that prevent this.
:::

::: quiz
Where is a counter-argument **least** effective?
- [ ] in its own paragraph after the writer's main reasons
- [ ] inside a body paragraph, followed at once by a rebuttal
- [x] in the last paragraph, with no rebuttal, just before the essay ends
- [ ] at the start of the essay, as the view to be argued against
::: solution
A counter-argument left unanswered at the very end leaves the reader with the opposing case. The other three placements are all used by skilled writers, provided the objection is answered.
:::
:::

## The language of concession and rebuttal

| move | language |
|---|---|
| introducing the opposing view | *Critics argue that …*, *Opponents of … claim that …*, *It is sometimes argued that …*, *Some would object that …*, *A common objection is that …* |
| conceding | *It is true that …*, *Admittedly, …*, *Granted, …*, *There is some truth in this.*, *This is a fair point.*, *… certainly …* |
| concession in one sentence | *Although …*, *Even though …*, *While …*, *Whilst …* (BrE, formal), *Despite …* / *In spite of …* + noun or *-ing* |
| rebutting | *However, …*, *Nevertheless, …*, *But …*, *This argument overlooks …*, *This does not mean that …*, *The evidence suggests otherwise.*, *This is less convincing when …* |
| outweighing | *… but this is outweighed by …*, *… but the benefits are greater.*, *Even so, …* |

::: theorem Grammar of concession {#rule-concession-grammar}
1. *Although*, *even though* and *while* introduce a clause and are **not** followed by *but* in the same sentence: ✗ *Although it is expensive, but it is effective.* ✓ *Although it is expensive, it is effective.* (See [[sentences-and-paragraphs/subordination]].)
2. *Despite* and *in spite of* are prepositions: they take a noun phrase or an *-ing* form, not a clause: ✗ *Despite it is expensive* ✓ *Despite its cost* / *Despite being expensive* / *Despite the fact that it is expensive*.
3. *Admittedly* and *Granted* concede at the start of a sentence; the rebuttal follows in the **next** sentence or clause, usually with *but* or *however*: *Admittedly, the scheme is expensive. However, …*
4. *It is true that X, but Y* is a natural two-part pattern; so is *X may be true, but Y*: *Recordings may reduce attendance, but …*
:::

::: proof
English marks the relation of concession once, either with a subordinator on the conceded clause (*although*) or with a coordinator or linker on the main point (*but*, *however*); using both duplicates the relation, which is why *although … but* is ungrammatical in standard English (Swan 2016; Quirk et al. 1985), unlike Chinese 虽然……但是. *Despite* belongs to a different word class, so the clause after it must be turned into a noun phrase. The modal pattern *X may be true, but …* is especially useful in argument, because *may* concedes the possibility without committing the writer to the claim ([[modality]]).
:::

::: theorem Reporting verbs and stance {#rule-reporting-verbs}
The verb used to introduce an opposing view shows the writer's attitude to it:

- **neutral**: *argue*, *say*, *suggest*, *maintain*, *contend* (formal);
- **distancing** — the writer doubts it: *claim*, *allege*, *assert*;
- **endorsing** — the writer accepts it: *point out*, *show*, *demonstrate*, *note*.

Choose the verb to match your response: ✗ *Critics point out that cars are essential; this is wrong.* (*point out* has already accepted the claim) ✓ *Critics claim that cars are essential; this overlooks …*
:::

::: proof
*Point out* and *show* are factive verbs: they present what follows as true, so a writer who uses them and then rejects the claim contradicts themselves. *Claim* signals that the writer does not take responsibility for the statement, and is often used before a rebuttal. Overusing *claim* for every view one disagrees with can sound dismissive, however; *argue* is the safe neutral choice. The same distinctions matter when reporting sources in academic writing ([[academic-writing/citation-referencing]]).
:::

::: example A counter-argument paragraph {#ex-paragraph}
Write a counter-argument paragraph for the thesis *Museums should be free to enter*, answering the objection about cost.
::: solution
1. **Introduce the objection fairly**, at its strongest: museums need income, and free entry may mean less money for collections and staff.
2. **Concede** what is true.
3. **Rebut**, here by *outweighing* and *limiting*: other sources of income exist, and the public benefit is greater.
4. **Return** to the thesis.

> *The most serious objection to free entry is financial. Museums need money to conserve their collections, pay specialist staff and mount exhibitions, and critics argue that giving up ticket income would force them to cut exactly these things. This concern is reasonable: free entry is not free to provide. However, it does not follow that visitors at the door must pay. Museums that do not charge for entry raise money in other ways, from public funding, donations, shops and paid special exhibitions, and these can be expanded. More importantly, a ticket price falls hardest on the people a public museum most needs to reach — families with low incomes, students and local residents who might visit often — so the cost of free entry is better seen as the price of a public service than as a loss.*

The paragraph states the objection clearly, concedes its truth, and answers it in two ways, ending on the writer's position.
:::
:::

::: example Correcting concession sentences {#ex-correct}
Correct the errors. (a) *Although online learning is flexible, but many students find it lonely.* (b) *Despite online learning is flexible, many students find it lonely.* (c) *Admittedly, online learning is flexible, many students find it lonely.* (d) *Critics show that online learning is lonely, but this is not true.*
::: solution
1. **(a)** *although* + *but*: remove one. ✓ *Although online learning is flexible, many students find it lonely.* or ✓ *Online learning is flexible, but many students find it lonely.*
2. **(b)** *despite* + clause: use a noun phrase. ✓ *Despite its flexibility, online learning is lonely for many students.* or ✓ *Despite the fact that online learning is flexible, …*
3. **(c)** a comma splice after *Admittedly*: ✓ *Admittedly, online learning is flexible. However, many students find it lonely.* or ✓ *Admittedly, online learning is flexible, but many students find it lonely.*
4. **(d)** *show* presents the claim as true, which contradicts the rebuttal. ✓ *Critics claim (argue) that online learning is lonely, but this is true only for …*
:::
:::

::: warning Conceding too much, or too little
Two opposite faults:

- **Undermining the thesis**: the writer concedes the objection and does not answer it, or answers it weakly. ✗ *Critics say free transport is too expensive. This is true, and cities do not have enough money.* — and the essay goes on as if the thesis still stood. If you cannot answer an objection, narrow your thesis ([[#rule-rebuttal]], point 3).
- **The token counter-argument**: one sentence that names an objection only to dismiss it. ✗ *Some people disagree, but they are wrong.* This tells the reader nothing and suggests that the writer has not thought about the other side.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *Even though the plan is popular, but it is too expensive.*
- [ ] *In spite of the plan is popular, it is too expensive.*
- [x] *While the plan is popular, it is too expensive to carry out.*
- [ ] *Despite of its popularity, the plan is too expensive.*
::: solution
*While* introduces a concessive clause, and the main clause follows without *but*. The first uses *even though* with *but*; the second uses *in spite of* with a clause; the fourth adds *of* after *despite* (*despite* is never followed by *of*: *despite its popularity* or *in spite of its popularity*).
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **虽然……但是.** Chinese pairs the two connectives; English uses one. *Although … but* is among the most frequent errors in Chinese speakers' English essays, and it tends to appear exactly in the counter-argument paragraph. Check every *although*, *even though* and *while* in your draft.
- **立论 and 驳论.** Chinese argumentative writing distinguishes essays that establish a position (立论) from essays that refute one (驳论), and teaches techniques for both. English essays usually combine them: the writer establishes a position and, within the same essay, refutes the strongest objection.
- **Disagreeing in writing.** Some writers, in many cultures, feel that disagreeing openly with others' views is impolite, or prefer to avoid conflict on the page. In English argumentative and academic writing, disagreeing with an *argument* is expected and is not a personal attack; what readers do find impolite is misrepresenting or mocking the other side. Fairness, not agreement, is the courtesy.
- **"Every coin has two sides".** *Every coin has two sides* (凡事都有两面性) and *There are advantages and disadvantages* are common ways of introducing the other view in learners' essays. They are general and do not say which view or why; name the objection specifically.
:::

::: history
Answering the other side is one of the oldest elements of argument. The *refutatio* had its own place in the speeches taught by classical rhetoricians, after the speaker's own proof. Medieval universities trained students through the *disputatio*, a formal debate in which objections were stated and answered, and the scholarly writing of the period followed the same pattern: each article of Thomas Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae* (thirteenth century) states the objections to its position first, then the position and its reasons, and then replies to each objection in turn. In the twentieth century, Richard Young, Alton Becker and Kenneth Pike's *Rhetoric: Discovery and Change* (1970) adapted the ideas of the psychologist Carl Rogers into what is now taught as *Rogerian argument*: begin by stating the opposing view so fairly that its supporters accept your summary, identify common ground, and only then present your own position. Its aim is not to defeat the other side but to reach readers who would otherwise stop listening.
:::

::: summary
- A counter-argument is an objection to the writer's position; a concession accepts what is true in it; a rebuttal answers it ([[#def-counter]]).
- Address the strongest opposing view, state it fairly, and answer it; arguments that acknowledge and refute objections tend to persuade more than one-sided ones ([[#rule-strongest]]).
- Rebut by refuting, outweighing, limiting or turning the objection; if none works, change the thesis ([[#rule-rebuttal]]).
- Place counter-arguments where the reader would raise them, signal them clearly, and never end on an unanswered objection ([[#rule-placing]]).
- *Although*, *even though* and *while* take no *but*; *despite* and *in spite of* take a noun phrase or *-ing*; *admittedly* is followed by a rebuttal in a new clause ([[#rule-concession-grammar]]).
- Reporting verbs show stance: *argue* is neutral, *claim* distances, *point out* and *show* endorse ([[#rule-reporting-verbs]]).
:::

::: exercise Write a counter-argument paragraph {level=3}
Write a counter-argument paragraph of 100–140 words for the thesis *Cities should replace some car parking spaces in the centre with parks and seating.* State the strongest objection fairly, concede what is true, and rebut it.
::: hint
The strongest objection probably concerns shops and businesses that depend on customers who drive. Which kind of rebuttal fits best?
:::
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

> The strongest objection comes from shopkeepers, who argue that fewer parking spaces will mean fewer customers. Their concern deserves to be taken seriously: a shop that loses even a small share of its customers may not survive. However, the objection assumes that most customers arrive by car, and shopkeepers often overestimate how many do. In centres where many shoppers already walk, cycle or come by bus, a pleasant street where people stay longer can bring more trade than the parking spaces it replaces. Where shops do depend on drivers, the answer is to keep some short-stay spaces and loading bays nearby, not to abandon the change altogether.

Check: a fair objection, a concession, a rebuttal that both refutes an assumption and limits the proposal, and an ending on the writer's position.

::: exercise Revise the thesis {level=3}
A student's thesis is *All schools should replace textbooks with tablets.* While planning, she lists these objections: *tablets are expensive; screen time affects sleep and eyesight; some families cannot repair or replace a broken tablet; many textbooks are not available digitally in some subjects.* Decide which objections she can answer and how, and rewrite her thesis to take account of any she cannot fully answer.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

- *Tablets are expensive*: outweigh — over several years, digital materials can cost less than buying new printed books, and one device replaces many books. (She needs real figures for her own setting.)
- *Screen time*: limit — reading textbooks is a small part of screen time, and schools can set rules; but this objection has some force for younger children.
- *Families cannot repair or replace tablets*: turn — an argument for schools to own and maintain the devices, not against using them.
- *Not available digitally*: cannot be fully answered for every subject — a real limit.

Revised thesis: *Secondary schools should move most of their textbooks to school-owned tablets, keeping printed books for subjects without good digital materials and for younger pupils, because over time tablets cost less and allow materials to be updated.*

The objections have made the thesis narrower and more defensible.
:::
