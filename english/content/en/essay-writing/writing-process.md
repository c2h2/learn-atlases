Two students are given the same essay question and the same afternoon to answer it: *Should universities record all their lectures and put them online?*

Chen Jie opens a blank document and starts with the first sentence. She rewrites it four times, because it does not sound impressive enough. The second sentence takes another ten minutes, and she stops twice to check a word in the dictionary. After two hours she has a polished introduction and two body paragraphs, but she has just realised that her third paragraph says the opposite of her first, and there is no time left to change the plan she never made.

Daniel spends the first half hour without writing a single sentence of the essay. He lists every point he can think of on both sides, crosses half of them out, decides what he thinks, and writes a plan of five lines. He then writes a rough draft quickly, leaving gaps where he needs a word or a fact. The next morning he reads it through, moves one paragraph, rewrites his conclusion, and only then checks the grammar and spelling.

Daniel's essay is better, and not because he knows more English. He has done four different kinds of work — planning, drafting, revising and editing — and has done them one at a time. Chen Jie tried to do all four at once, in every sentence. This lesson describes the stages of the writing process, why it helps to keep them apart, and how experienced writers move between them. It is the foundation for the rest of the course.

## The stages of writing

::: definition The writing process {#def-process}
The **writing process** is the sequence of activities by which a writer produces a text. It is usually described in four stages:

1. **planning** (also called *prewriting*): understanding the task, generating ideas, choosing a position and organising the ideas into a plan;
2. **drafting**: turning the plan into connected sentences and paragraphs;
3. **revising**: changing the content and organisation of the draft — the argument, the paragraphs, what is included and what is left out;
4. **editing and proofreading**: correcting and improving sentences, grammar, words, spelling and punctuation.

The stages overlap, and writers return to earlier ones: the process is **recursive**, not a straight line.
:::

The four names are used in most writing textbooks, though some split them further (*prewriting* and *outlining*; *editing* and *proofreading*) and some add *publishing* or *submitting* at the end. What matters is not the number of stages but the difference between the kinds of thinking they require. Planning is about ideas and order; drafting is about getting words down; revising is about whether the text works for its reader; editing is about whether each sentence is correct and clear.

::: theorem Keep the stages apart {#rule-stages-apart}
1. **Plan before you draft.** Decide what you think and in what order you will say it before you write paragraphs.
2. **Do not edit while you draft.** When a word, fact or grammar point stops you, mark the place and keep going.
3. **Revise before you edit.** Check the argument and the structure before you polish sentences that you may later cut.
4. **Edit in separate passes**, one kind of problem at a time ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/editing#rule-large-small]]).

This is a working method, not a law: experienced writers move between stages freely, but they know which kind of work they are doing at any moment.
:::

::: proof
Writing makes heavy demands on attention: a writer has to hold ideas, plans, words, grammar and spelling in mind at the same time, and in a second language the demands on vocabulary and grammar are greater. Separating the stages lowers the load at each moment. In an experiment with American university students, Kellogg (1988) found that writers who prepared an outline before drafting produced texts that were rated higher in quality than those of writers who did not, and he explained the effect by attentional overload: the outline had already done the work of organising, so the writers could attend to expressing ideas. Flower and Hayes (1981), studying writers who thought aloud as they wrote, described writing as a set of processes — planning, translating ideas into text, and reviewing — controlled by the writer's goals and used again and again in any order, not as a fixed sequence. The rule therefore does not say that a writer must finish planning before writing a word; it says that mixing every kind of work in every sentence, as Chen Jie did, overloads the writer and hides the larger problems.
:::

## Planning

Planning is the stage learners most often skip, especially under time pressure, and the one that most affects the quality of an essay. It has three parts: understanding the task (the subject of [[essay-writing/understanding-the-question]]), generating ideas, and selecting and ordering them.

### Generating ideas

There are several well-tried ways of finding something to say:

- **Listing** (brainstorming): write down every idea, example and argument you can think of, on both sides of the question, without judging them.
- **Freewriting**: write without stopping for five or ten minutes, in sentences, ignoring grammar and spelling. The point is to discover what you think; most of the text will be thrown away. The technique was made popular by Peter Elbow's *Writing Without Teachers* (1973).
- **Mind mapping**: write the topic in the centre of a page and branch out to related ideas, examples and questions.
- **Questions**: ask *who, what, when, where, why, how*; *what causes this?*; *what are the effects?*; *who gains and who loses?*; *what would an opponent say?*
- **Talking**: explain your ideas to someone else, or to yourself aloud.

Any of these can be done in English, in Chinese or in a mixture; what matters at this stage is the ideas, not the language they are in (see the note on English and Chinese below).

### Selecting and ordering

A list of ideas is not a plan. Most of the items on it will be weak, repeated or off the point, and the good ones need an order.

::: definition Outline {#def-outline}
An **outline** is a plan of an essay that shows its position and the order of its main points, usually as a short list or a numbered hierarchy: the thesis, then each body paragraph's main point with its main support, then the conclusion. A **working outline** is written before drafting and changed freely; a **reverse outline** is made from a finished draft, by writing down the point of each paragraph, to check its structure (see [[essay-writing/revising-editing]]).
:::

::: theorem From ideas to a plan {#rule-plan}
To turn a list of ideas into a plan:

1. **Decide your position**: what is your answer to the question? Write it in one sentence, even if you change it later.
2. **Select**: keep the ideas that support or test that position; cut the rest, however interesting.
3. **Group**: put related ideas together; each group will become a paragraph with one main point.
4. **Order**: choose an order for the groups — often the strongest reason last, or a logical sequence (cause before effect, problem before solution).
5. **Check against the question**: does every group help to answer the question that was asked?
:::

::: example From a list to an outline {#ex-outline}
For the question *Should universities record all their lectures and put them online?*, a student's ideas list reads:

*students can review difficult parts · good for students who are ill · lecturers may feel watched · attendance might fall · some students work part-time · recordings cost money and time · students for whom English is a second language can pause and replay · lecture halls are crowded · some lectures have discussion, which recordings miss · privacy of students who ask questions · my cousin's university does it · exams are easier to prepare for*

Turn it into an outline.
::: solution
1. **Decide a position.** The list has strong points on both sides. A defensible position: *universities should record most lectures, but with limits that protect discussion and privacy.*
2. **Select.** Cut *my cousin's university does it* (an anecdote, not a reason) and *lecture halls are crowded* (not clearly connected to recording). *Exams are easier to prepare for* overlaps with *review difficult parts*; merge them.
3. **Group.**
   - Learning: review difficult parts; pause and replay in a second language; exam preparation.
   - Access: illness; part-time work.
   - Costs and risks: attendance; lecturers feel watched; cost; discussion lost; privacy.
4. **Order.** The two groups in favour come first; the risks follow, answered by the limits in the position.

**Outline**

- **Thesis**: Universities should record most lectures, because recordings help students learn and widen access, but recording should exclude discussion and protect students' privacy.
- **Paragraph 1**: Recordings help learning — reviewing difficult explanations, especially for second-language students.
- **Paragraph 2**: Recordings widen access — students who are ill, care for relatives or work.
- **Paragraph 3**: The risks — lower attendance, cost, discussion that recording discourages, privacy — and how limits answer them.
- **Conclusion**: Record lectures, not seminars; let students ask questions off the record.

The outline is a few lines long, but every paragraph has a job, and the position is decided before drafting begins.
:::
:::

::: warning Writing without a position
✗ *I will discuss the advantages and disadvantages of recording lectures.*

Many learners plan an essay as a list of points on each side and leave the decision for the conclusion, or never make it. The result is a list, not an argument: the reader cannot see why the paragraphs are in that order or what they add up to. Decide what you think during planning. If the question asks for your view ([[essay-writing/understanding-the-question]]), your plan should start from it; if you change your mind while drafting, change the plan.
:::

## Drafting

::: theorem Draft for flow {#rule-draft}
When you draft:

1. **Follow the plan**, but let it change if you find a better idea.
2. **Keep moving.** If you cannot find a word, write the nearest one, or a Chinese word, in brackets, and mark it to check later: *the [影响 — effect? impact?] of recording*.
3. **Start where it is easiest.** Many writers draft the body paragraphs first and write the introduction last, when they know what it is introducing.
4. **Write complete paragraphs**, each with its point, even if the sentences are rough.
5. **Leave time** between drafting and revising when you can: an hour, or a day.
:::

::: proof
Stopping to correct every sentence breaks the line of thought that connects one sentence to the next, and the energy goes into the surface of the text before its shape is settled. A marked gap is cheap to fill later, when the paragraph around it shows which word is needed. Drafting the introduction last is common advice in writing handbooks, because an introduction has to point to the thesis and the structure that follows, and these are often clearer after drafting than before. The pause before revising has a practical reason that every editor knows: immediately after writing, a writer reads what they meant, not what is on the page.
:::

::: example Drafting with marked gaps {#ex-draft}
Here is a first draft of paragraph 1 from the outline above. Identify the marks the writer has left and say what each one is for.

*Recordings help students to learn, because a lecture moves at one speed and students do not. A student who misses a step in an argument or an equation cannot ask the lecturer to [repeat? go back?], but with a recording they can replay it as often as they need. This matters most for students who are listening in a second language: [find figure — how many international students at our univ?]. They can pause to look up a term, or slow the recording down. Recordings also help before exams, when [CHECK: is "revise" BrE only?] students want to review a whole course.*
::: solution
1. *[repeat? go back?]* — a word choice the writer was unsure of. Either works; *go back over it* is natural here. The writer did not stop to decide, which was right.
2. *[find figure — …]* — a gap for evidence. The writer knows a number would strengthen the point but does not have it yet. At the revising stage, they must find a reliable figure or rewrite the sentence without it; they must not invent one.
3. *[CHECK: …]* — a question about language. (*Revise* in the sense ‘study again for an exam’ is British; American English says *review* or *study*.) This is an editing question and can wait.

The paragraph already has its point, its explanation and its support. Every mark is a small job that will take a minute later; stopping for each while drafting could have cost the writer the thread of the argument.
:::
:::

## Revising and editing

Revising and editing are often confused, and many students do only the second. In a classic study, Sommers (1980) compared the revisions of first-year American university students with those of experienced adult writers. The students saw revision mainly as changing words — finding a better word, removing a repetition — and rarely changed anything larger than a sentence. The experienced writers revised at every level: they added and deleted whole sections, reordered paragraphs and reconsidered their argument and their reader, and they said they were trying to find the shape of what they wanted to say.

::: theorem Revise for the reader {#rule-revise}
When you revise, read the whole draft as a reader would, and ask:

1. Does the essay answer the question that was asked?
2. Is the position clear, and is it the same at the end as at the start?
3. Does each paragraph have one point that supports the position?
4. Is there enough support — explanation, examples, evidence — for each point, and is anything irrelevant?
5. Is the order the best one, and can the reader see it?

Only when the answers satisfy you should you start editing sentences. The lesson [[essay-writing/revising-editing]] gives methods for each question; editing for language is covered in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/editing]].
:::

::: intuition
A useful image: an essay is built like a house. Planning is the drawing; drafting puts up the walls; revising checks whether the rooms are in the right place and whether anyone can live in them; editing is the paint. Nobody paints a wall they are about to move.
:::

### Knowledge telling and knowledge transforming

Bereiter and Scardamalia (1987) described two ways of writing. In **knowledge telling**, the writer takes the topic, retrieves what they know about it, and writes it down in the order it comes to mind; each sentence prompts the next. This is how most children write, and how many adults write when they are in a hurry. In **knowledge transforming**, the writer works on two problems at once — what they think about the topic, and what the text needs to do for its reader — and each problem changes the other: trying to explain a point to a reader shows the writer that the point is unclear, and rethinking the point changes the text. The writing process of this lesson, with its planning and revising, is a way of making knowledge transforming happen deliberately.

::: example Telling or transforming? {#ex-transform}
Two students answer *Is it better to study alone or in a group?* Read their opening sentences and say which approach each shows.

**A.** *There are many ways to study. Some people study alone and some study in groups. I usually study alone in the library. The library at my university has three floors and is open until eleven. Studying in a group is also popular. My friends often study in groups before exams.*

**B.** *Whether studying alone or in a group is better depends on what is being learned. Memorising and practising, which need concentration and repetition, are usually done best alone; understanding difficult ideas, which needs explanation and questioning, often goes better in a group.*
::: solution
1. Look at how each sentence follows the last. In A, each sentence is prompted by a word in the one before (*alone* → *library* → *library at my university*); in B, the second sentence develops the claim of the first.
2. Look for a position. A has none yet; B states one in its first sentence (*it depends on what is being learned*) and divides the question into two parts.
3. Look for the reader. A gives details the reader does not need (*three floors*); B gives the reader a way to think about the question.

A shows **knowledge telling**; B shows **knowledge transforming**. B's writer has clearly thought about the question before writing — the kind of thinking the planning stage is for.
:::
:::

## Purpose and reader

Every decision in the process — which ideas to keep, what to explain, how formal to be — depends on who will read the text and why.

::: theorem Know your reader and purpose {#rule-reader}
Before you plan, answer three questions:

1. **Who is the reader?** A teacher marking an essay, an examiner, a general reader, an expert? What do they already know?
2. **What is the purpose?** To argue for a position, to explain, to compare, to describe? (See [[essay-writing/essay-types]].)
3. **What are the conditions?** The length, the time, the required format and register.

Write for an intelligent reader who does not know your examples and cannot ask you what you meant. In most essays, this is the reader the marker is asked to imagine.
:::

::: proof
A writer who imagines no reader tends to leave out steps that seem obvious to them, or to include everything they know. A teacher who sets an essay usually knows the subject better than the student, but marks the essay as if the student were explaining it to someone who does not: the essay is evidence of what the student understands, and an unexplained step is not evidence. That is why essay advice so often says "explain as if to an intelligent outsider". The conditions matter too: a 250-word answer and a 2,500-word coursework essay on the same question need different plans, not just different lengths.
:::

::: example Same topic, different readers {#ex-reader}
A student is writing about the same topic — recording lectures — for (a) a 300-word opinion piece in the university's student magazine and (b) a 1,500-word essay for a course on higher education. List three differences in how she should plan them.
::: solution
1. **Reader and purpose.** (a) Readers are other students, reading for interest; the purpose is to persuade them, perhaps to support a campaign. (b) The reader is a lecturer judging her understanding and reasoning; the purpose is to argue a position with evidence.
2. **Scope and support.** (a) One or two strong reasons with a vivid example are enough; there is no room for a full counter-argument. (b) She needs several reasons, evidence from research or university reports, and a fair treatment of the opposing view ([[essay-writing/counter-arguments]]).
3. **Register and form.** (a) A lively title, short paragraphs, *you* and *we*, perhaps a question to the reader. (b) Formal academic register, a thesis in the introduction, references to sources ([[academic-writing/citation-referencing]]).

The topic is the same; the plans are not.
:::
:::

- [x] by changing individual words and phrases

The students in Sommers's study treated revision mainly as a matter of vocabulary — replacing words and removing repetition. It was the experienced adult writers who added, deleted and reordered whole sections and reconsidered their argument.

::: note English and Chinese
- **Planning in Chinese.** Many Chinese-speaking writers find it easier to generate ideas in Chinese, and there is nothing wrong with that. A plan in Chinese, or in a mixture of Chinese and English, is still a plan. Research comparing writing directly in a second language with writing first in the first language and then translating has found mixed results that depend on the writer and the task: in a study of intermediate learners of French, Cohen and Brooks-Carson (2001) found that two-thirds of the students did better writing directly, while a third did better when they wrote in their first language and translated. A reasonable approach is to plan in whichever language helps you think, and to draft in English, so that the sentences are built as English sentences rather than translated from Chinese ones.
- **"One draft" habits.** In many Chinese classrooms, as in many others, essays have been written mostly under exam conditions, in one draft, with time to plan but little time to revise. Those conditions train a valuable skill ([[essay-writing/timed-essays]]), but they can leave a writer without the habit of revising. When you have time, use it: put the draft away and come back to it.
- **Outlines (提纲).** Chinese schools often teach students to write an outline (列提纲) before an essay, and this transfers directly. What sometimes needs to change is the content of the outline: English essays usually expect the position to be decided at the planning stage and stated near the start ([[essay-writing/thesis-introduction]]).
- **Words for the stages.** 修改 covers both *revising* and *editing*; 润色 is closer to *polishing* the language. The English distinction between revising (ideas and structure) and editing (language) is worth keeping, because the two need different readings.
:::

::: history
Until the 1960s, writing was taught in English-speaking schools mainly through its products: students read model essays, learnt the modes of discourse — description, narration, exposition, argument — that Alexander Bain's *English Composition and Rhetoric* (1866) had helped to establish, wrote, and received a corrected text. In 1971 Janet Emig's *The Composing Processes of Twelfth Graders* reported on students observed as they wrote and thought aloud, and a year later the teacher and journalist Donald Murray published an essay with the title "Teach Writing as a Process Not Product" (1972). Researchers then studied the process directly: Nancy Sommers (1980) on revision, Linda Flower and John Hayes (1981) on planning, translating and reviewing, and Carl Bereiter and Marlene Scardamalia (1987) on knowledge telling and knowledge transforming. The "process approach" spread to second-language teaching in the 1980s, encouraged by researchers such as Vivian Zamel (1982). Later critics argued that it said too little about the kinds of text students need to produce, and genre-based approaches, which teach the structure and language of particular types of text, grew alongside it; many writing courses today, including this one, use both (Hyland 2003).
:::

The thesis that comes out of planning is the subject of [[essay-writing/thesis-introduction]], and the paragraphs of the draft of [[essay-writing/arguing-a-point]]. ::: summary
- The writing process has four main stages — planning, drafting, revising, editing — and it is recursive: writers return to earlier stages ([[#def-process]]).
- Keep the stages apart: plan before drafting, do not edit while drafting, revise before editing ([[#rule-stages-apart]]); trying to do everything at once overloads attention.
- Plan by generating ideas (listing, freewriting, mind maps, questions), then deciding a position, selecting, grouping and ordering into an outline ([[#def-outline]], [[#rule-plan]]).
- Draft for flow: follow the plan, mark gaps and doubts instead of stopping, and leave time before revising ([[#rule-draft]]).
- Revising changes content and organisation; editing changes sentences. Inexperienced writers tend to do only the second (Sommers 1980) ([[#rule-revise]]).
- Good writers transform their knowledge for a reader rather than telling it in the order it comes to mind (Bereiter and Scardamalia 1987).
- Every decision depends on the reader, the purpose and the conditions of the task ([[#rule-reader]]).
:::

Write a working outline (thesis, three paragraph points, conclusion) for the question *Should museums be free to enter?* Decide your position first.

- **Thesis**: National museums should be free to enter, because free entry widens access to shared culture, but they may charge for special exhibitions to cover costs.

- **Paragraph 2**: Museums hold collections that belong to the public, and free entry treats them as a public service like libraries.

Rewrite this knowledge-telling paragraph so that it answers the question *Is social media good for friendships?* with a position and a reason the reader can follow. ::: hint

::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

> Social media is good for keeping friendships alive across distance, but it is a poor place to manage disagreements. Since leaving school, several of my closest friends have moved to other cities, and sharing photos and short messages lets us follow each other's lives in a way that occasional phone calls could not. When we have disagreed online, however, short messages without tone have turned small differences into arguments that a conversation face to face would have settled in minutes.

The new paragraph starts with a position, uses two of the original details as evidence for its two halves, and drops details that do not answer the question (the number of accounts, hours per day, the cousin's view).
