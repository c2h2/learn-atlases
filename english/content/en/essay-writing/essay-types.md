Four essay questions on the same broad topic, housing:

1. *Some people think young adults should live with their parents until they marry; others think they should live independently as soon as they can. Discuss both views and give your opinion.*
2. *Compare renting and buying a home as choices for young professionals in a large city.*
3. *Why have house prices in many large cities risen faster than incomes?*
4. *Many young people in large cities cannot afford a home of their own. What can be done about this problem?*

Each question needs a thesis and paragraphs that argue it, as the previous lessons showed. But each also needs a different **shape**. The first weighs two views and comes down on one side. The second sets two options against the same standards. The third traces causes. The fourth defines a problem and argues for a solution. A writer who uses the same shape for all four — say, *advantages, disadvantages, opinion* — will answer at most one of them well.

This lesson describes the four most common types of essay in school, university and examination writing — discursive, compare-and-contrast, cause-and-effect and problem–solution — with a standard way of organising each and the language each needs. These are patterns, not boxes: real essays often combine them. The type of essay that describes data and processes has its own lesson, [[essay-writing/describing-data]].

## Patterns of organisation

::: definition Essay type {#def-essay-type}
An **essay type** (or *pattern of organisation*, *rhetorical pattern*) is a conventional way of organising an essay according to its purpose: to weigh views, to compare, to explain causes and effects, or to propose solutions. Each type has typical structures and typical language. The type is decided mainly by the **task** in the question ([[essay-writing/understanding-the-question]]), not by the topic.
:::

::: theorem Let the question choose the pattern {#rule-pattern}
1. Read the task words and the question's form to identify the type: *discuss both views*, *do you agree* → discursive; *compare*, *contrast*, *similarities and differences* → compare-and-contrast; *why*, *what are the causes / effects*, *explain* → cause-and-effect; *what can be done*, *how can … be solved*, *suggest measures* → problem–solution.
2. Choose a structure for that type, and make the thesis fit it.
3. If the question combines types (*What are the causes of this problem, and how can it be solved?*), answer each part, in proportion to its weight in the question.
:::

::: proof
Each type answers a different underlying question — *which view is right?*, *how do these differ?*, *why?*, *what should be done?* — and the reader expects the essay's structure to follow the answer. The patterns go back to the modes of discourse in nineteenth-century composition teaching (see the historical note), and remain the basis of many writing textbooks (for example Oshima and Hogue 2006). They are a help in planning, not a constraint: a cause-and-effect essay may end by proposing a solution, and a discursive essay may compare. What matters is that the main question of the essay decides its main structure.
:::

## The discursive essay

::: definition Discursive essay {#def-discursive}
A **discursive essay** (also called a *for-and-against*, *argumentative* or *opinion* essay) discusses a question on which reasonable people disagree. There are two main forms:

- the **balanced** form presents the arguments on both sides, and then gives and justifies the writer's judgement;
- the **one-sided** (or *argument-led*) form argues for one position throughout, dealing with the opposing view as a counter-argument ([[essay-writing/counter-arguments]]).
:::

| balanced form | one-sided form |
|---|---|
| introduction: the issue, and a thesis that says the essay will weigh both sides and where it will come down | introduction: the issue and a thesis stating the position |
| arguments for view A (one or two paragraphs) | reason 1 for the position |
| arguments for view B (one or two paragraphs) | reason 2 for the position |
| (optional) a paragraph weighing the two | counter-argument and rebuttal |
| conclusion: judgement with its main reason | conclusion: the position, reinforced |

::: theorem Choosing the discursive form {#rule-discursive-form}
1. Use the **balanced** form when the question asks you to *discuss both views* or *consider the arguments for and against*.
2. Use the **one-sided** form when the question asks *do you agree*, *to what extent*, *should …?*, *evaluate*, or *argue*.
3. In either form, **state your view in the introduction**, not only at the end. In the balanced form, put the side you agree with **second**, so that the essay moves towards your judgement.
4. Do not present the two sides as equal and then choose one without a reason: the conclusion must say *why* one side is stronger.
:::

::: proof
The balanced form follows the question's request to give each view its due; the one-sided form follows a question that asks for the writer's judgement to organise the essay. Stating the view early, even in a balanced essay, tells the reader how to read the paragraphs that follow: as a fair account of the opposing side, not as the writer's own position. Ending the body with the view the writer supports puts it closest to the conclusion, which is why many textbooks recommend that order. The common weakness of balanced essays is a conclusion that simply chooses (*In my opinion, the second view is better*) without weighing — which turns the essay into two lists.
:::

::: example Planning a balanced discursive essay {#ex-discursive}
Plan an answer to the first question at the start of the lesson: *Some people think young adults should live with their parents until they marry; others think they should live independently as soon as they can. Discuss both views and give your opinion.*
::: solution
1. **Identify the type**: *discuss both views and give your opinion* → balanced discursive essay.
2. **Decide the opinion first**, so that the introduction can state it: living independently is better *if* it can be afforded without debt; otherwise staying at home for a few years is the sensible choice. This is a position of degree, and the opposing view will be presented first.
3. **Arguments for living at home**: saving money (no rent) for a deposit or further study; family support and company; in many families, being close to and helping older parents.
4. **Arguments for living independently**: learning to manage money, cooking and household decisions; freedom to choose where to live, close to work; more equal adult relationships with parents.
5. **Weighing**: the benefits of independence come from managing one's own life, and those benefits are lost if rent takes so much income that the young person is in constant financial difficulty.
6. **Outline**:
   - Introduction: the choice, and the thesis — *independence brings benefits that living at home cannot, but only when it does not come at the cost of serious debt*.
   - Paragraph 1: the case for living at home.
   - Paragraph 2: the case for independence (the view the writer leans towards, placed second).
   - Paragraph 3: weighing — the deciding factor is cost.
   - Conclusion: the judgement and its reason.

The opposing view gets a full paragraph, stated fairly; the writer's view comes second and is then qualified by the weighing paragraph. Note that the question is about a personal and family choice that differs between cultures and families: the essay should avoid presenting either choice as the "normal" one.
:::
:::

## The compare-and-contrast essay

::: definition Compare-and-contrast essay {#def-compare}
A **compare-and-contrast essay** examines two (occasionally more) subjects — options, places, periods, theories — in terms of the same **criteria** (points of comparison), to show their similarities and differences and to reach a judgement about them. It has two main structures:

- **block** (or *subject-by-subject*): everything about subject A, then everything about subject B;
- **point-by-point** (or *criterion-by-criterion*): one criterion at a time, with A and B compared within each paragraph.
:::

::: theorem Organising a comparison {#rule-compare}
1. Compare the two subjects on the **same criteria**, in the same order.
2. Prefer **point-by-point** for longer essays and for comparisons with many criteria: the reader sees each difference directly. **Block** suits short essays with few criteria, and comparisons where each subject needs to be understood as a whole first.
3. Give the comparison a **thesis**: what the comparison shows — which option is better for whom, or what the main difference is. A list of similarities and differences is not yet an essay.
4. Use the language of comparison accurately: *both … and*, *similarly*, *likewise*, *whereas*, *while*, *in contrast*, *unlike*, *compared with*, *more/less … than*, *as … as*, *the same as*, *different from*.
:::

::: proof
Comparing on the same criteria is what makes a comparison fair: if one option is judged on cost and the other on convenience, the reader cannot compare them. In the block structure the reader must remember everything about A while reading about B, which becomes hard after two or three criteria; point-by-point puts the two side by side. The thesis matters because comparisons are usually made for a purpose — to choose, to explain, to evaluate — and the essay should say what that purpose leads to.
:::

::: example Block or point-by-point? {#ex-compare}
Organise an essay comparing studying at a university in one's home city with studying in another city, using both structures. Which is better here?
::: solution
1. **Choose criteria**: cost; independence and personal growth; support network; opportunities (courses, jobs).
2. **Block structure**:
   - Paragraph 1: home city — cost (lower), independence (less), support (family nearby), opportunities (limited to local universities).
   - Paragraph 2: another city — the same four criteria in the same order.
3. **Point-by-point structure**:
   - Paragraph 1: cost — home city cheaper (no rent), another city more expensive.
   - Paragraph 2: independence — limited at home; greater away.
   - Paragraph 3: support — family nearby versus new networks.
   - Paragraph 4: opportunities — wider choice of courses in other cities.
4. **Choose**: with four criteria, point-by-point is clearer; the reader sees each trade-off directly.
5. **Thesis**: *Studying in another city costs more and offers less support, but for students who can afford it, the independence and wider choice of courses it brings are worth more than the savings of staying at home.*

The comparison now leads somewhere: the thesis tells the reader what the four criteria add up to.
:::
:::

## The cause-and-effect essay

::: definition Cause-and-effect essay {#def-cause-effect}
A **cause-and-effect essay** explains why something happens (its causes), what follows from it (its effects), or both. It may describe **several causes** of one effect, **several effects** of one cause, or a **causal chain** in which each effect becomes the cause of the next.
:::

::: theorem Explaining causes {#rule-causes}
1. **Distinguish** immediate causes (what triggered the event) from underlying ones (the conditions that made it likely), and main causes from contributing ones.
2. **Rank** the causes: say which matter most and why, and order the paragraphs accordingly.
3. **Show the mechanism**: how does the cause produce the effect?
4. **Avoid single-cause explanations** of complex events, and do not treat events that merely happen together, or one after the other, as cause and effect ([[essay-writing/arguing-a-point]]).
5. **Use causal language accurately**: *cause*, *lead to*, *result in*, *contribute to*, *bring about* (cause → effect); *result from*, *be caused by*, *be due to*, *stem from* (effect ← cause); *because*, *since*, *as* (clauses); *because of*, *owing to*, *due to*, *as a result of* (+ noun phrase); *therefore*, *as a result*, *consequently* (linking sentences).
:::

::: proof
Most social and economic events have several causes acting together, and an essay that names only one usually oversimplifies. Distinguishing kinds of cause gives the essay its structure and its thesis: *the main cause is X, but it worked only because of Y*. Showing the mechanism is what turns a list of factors into an explanation, and it guards against the error of taking correlation for cause. The causal verbs differ in direction (*lead to* goes from cause to effect, *result from* from effect to cause) and in strength (*contribute to* claims less than *cause*), so choosing them carefully is part of making a claim no stronger than the evidence allows.
:::

::: example Language of cause and effect {#ex-cause-language}
Rewrite each sentence using the word in brackets, keeping the meaning. (a) *Prices rose because demand increased.* (due to) (b) *The factory closed, so many people lost their jobs.* (led to) (c) *The flooding was caused by heavy rain.* (resulted from) (d) *Better street lighting was one of the reasons crime fell.* (contributed to)
::: solution
1. **(a)** *due to* takes a noun phrase, so *demand increased* must become one: *Prices rose due to an increase in demand.* (In formal writing, *due to* is most safely used after *be*: *The rise in prices was due to an increase in demand.* Some style guides object to *due to* used like *because of*, though the use is widespread.)
2. **(b)** *lead to* goes from cause to effect and takes a noun phrase: *The closure of the factory led to the loss of many jobs.*
3. **(c)** *result from* goes from effect to cause: *The flooding resulted from heavy rain.*
4. **(d)** *contribute to* means ‘be one of the causes of’: *Better street lighting contributed to the fall in crime.*

In each case the cause or effect has become a noun phrase (*an increase in demand*, *the closure of the factory*, *the fall in crime*), which is typical of formal written English.
:::
:::

## The problem–solution essay

::: definition Problem–solution essay {#def-problem-solution}
A **problem–solution essay** describes a problem, explains its causes or seriousness, proposes one or more solutions, and evaluates them. Its classic structure follows the pattern that Hoey (1983) described in many kinds of text: **situation** → **problem** → **response** (solution) → **evaluation**.
:::

::: theorem Proposing solutions {#rule-solutions}
1. **Define the problem** precisely: who is affected, how much, and why it matters. A vague problem invites vague solutions.
2. Link each **solution to a cause**: a solution that does not address a cause of the problem is unlikely to work.
3. **Be specific**: who should do what? *Governments should do more* is not a solution.
4. **Evaluate**: is the solution effective, affordable, fair and practical? What are its costs and side effects? Compare solutions if there are several, and say which is best.
5. Use the language of recommendation accurately: *should*, *ought to*, *could*, *it would be better to*, *one solution would be to*, *the most effective measure would be …*, *it is essential that … (should) …*.
:::

::: proof
The link between solutions and causes is what makes a problem–solution essay an argument rather than a wish list: a reader can judge a proposal only if they can see how it acts on the problem. Evaluation is the stage most often missing in students' essays, and the one that shows judgement: real solutions have costs, and a writer who names them and argues that the benefits are greater is more convincing than one who presents every proposal as perfect. Hoey's pattern also describes many news reports, advertisements and research introductions, which is why readers recognise it easily ([[academic-writing/research-questions]]; Swales and Feak 2012).
:::

::: example Structuring a problem–solution essay {#ex-problem-solution}
Outline an answer to *Many young people in large cities cannot afford a home of their own. What can be done about this problem?*
::: solution
1. **Situation**: young adults move to large cities for work.
2. **Problem**: house prices and rents have risen faster than young people's incomes, so many cannot buy, and rent takes a large share of their pay. (A real essay needs figures for a specific city or country, with sources.)
3. **Causes** (briefly, to link to solutions): too few homes built; demand from investors; high deposits.
4. **Solutions**, each linked to a cause:
   - build more homes, especially smaller ones near public transport → supply;
   - limit or tax homes left empty or bought as investments → demand;
   - schemes that reduce deposits for first-time buyers → deposits.
5. **Evaluation**: building is the only solution that addresses the root cause but takes years; deposit schemes help quickly but may push prices up if supply does not rise; investment taxes are effective but politically difficult and can reduce rental supply.
6. **Thesis**: *The problem can be solved only by building more homes; other measures help some buyers in the short term but risk raising prices if they are not combined with new supply.*

The evaluation produces the thesis: one solution ranked above the others, for a reason.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which essay type does this question ask for? *Why do many people find it hard to keep up regular exercise, and what could help them?*
- [ ] compare-and-contrast
- [ ] discursive (balanced)
- [x] cause-and-effect combined with problem–solution
- [ ] description of a process
::: solution
The first part (*why*) asks for causes, and the second (*what could help*) asks for solutions. The essay should answer both — for example, causes in the first half, with each cause linked to a solution in the second.

::: quiz
Which sentence uses causal language correctly?
- [ ] *The fall in sales was led to the closure of the shop.*
- [ ] *Due to the price rose, fewer people bought it.*
- [x] *The shortage of teachers has contributed to larger class sizes.*
- [ ] *The accident resulted in heavy rain.*
::: solution
*Contribute to* + noun phrase correctly presents the shortage as one cause of larger classes. *Lead to* is active (*The fall in sales led to the closure*); *due to* needs a noun phrase (*Due to the rise in price*); and *result in* goes from cause to effect, so the fourth sentence says the accident caused the rain (*The accident resulted from heavy rain*).
:::

::: warning The five-paragraph essay as a law
Many students learn the **five-paragraph essay**: an introduction ending in a thesis with three reasons, three body paragraphs, and a conclusion. It is a useful training shape and fits many short exam essays. But it is not a rule of English writing:

- Not every question has three reasons. A compare-and-contrast essay may need four criteria; a problem–solution essay needs a problem, solutions and an evaluation.
- Forcing material into three paragraphs produces padding (a weak third reason) or crowding (two points in one paragraph).
- University essays are longer and are organised by the argument, not by a fixed number.

Use as many body paragraphs as the argument needs, each with one point.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Chinese school essay types.** Chinese schools teach 记叙文 (narrative), 说明文 (expository) and 议论文 (argumentative) writing. The discursive essay corresponds to 议论文; compare-and-contrast, cause-and-effect and problem–solution essays fall partly under 说明文 and partly under 议论文. The English categories cut the field differently, by purpose, so a single Chinese category may need different English structures.
- **总分总.** The general–specific–general (总分总) structure widely taught in Chinese schools is close to the English introduction–body–conclusion. What sometimes differs is the first 总: in English the introduction should end with a specific thesis, not only a general statement of the topic.
- **起承转合 and the eight-legged essay.** Discussions of Chinese students' English writing often mention 起承转合 — a four-part structure of opening, development, turn and conclusion, used for regulated verse and some prose — and the eight-legged essay (八股文), the rigid examination form of the Ming and Qing dynasties. Some writers on contrastive rhetoric have suggested that the "turn" (转) explains digressions in Chinese students' English, or that the eight-legged essay left a lasting taste for formula. Others point out that the eight-legged essay disappeared from the examinations more than a century ago, that modern Chinese schools teach other structures, and that there is little direct evidence that either pattern shapes how students write in English today (Kirkpatrick and Xu 2012). It is wiser to treat them as part of the history of Chinese writing than as an explanation of any individual's English.
- **因为……所以 and *because … so*.** Causal pairs, like concessive ones, take only one connective in English: ✗ *Because prices rose, so people bought less.* ✓ *Because prices rose, people bought less.* ✓ *Prices rose, so people bought less.*
:::

::: history
The idea that writing comes in a few basic kinds goes back to nineteenth-century rhetoric textbooks. Alexander Bain's *English Composition and Rhetoric* (1866) helped to fix the four "modes of discourse" — description, narration, exposition and argument — which dominated American and British composition teaching for decades. Exposition was later subdivided into methods of development such as comparison and contrast, cause and effect, definition, classification and process, which survive as the essay types of many modern textbooks. The historian of rhetoric Robert Connors traced the rise and decline of the modes in an article of 1981, arguing that they had been taught more for their convenience than because writers actually think in them. In 1983 the linguist Michael Hoey described the problem–solution pattern (situation, problem, response, evaluation) as one of the most common ways texts of all kinds are organised, a description later taken up widely in the teaching of academic writing.
:::

::: exercise Link solutions to causes {level=2}
The problem is *Many university students feel lonely in their first year.* For each cause, suggest a specific solution and name one cost or limit. (a) Students arrive not knowing anyone. (b) Large lectures give few chances to talk. (c) Many students are reluctant to ask for help.
::: solution
Answers will vary. Models:

(a) Small mentor groups led by second-year students in the first weeks; limit: depends on volunteers and may not suit every student.
(b) Weekly small-group tutorials or discussion sections; cost: more teaching staff.
(c) Make support services visible and normal — introduced in every first-year course, with drop-in times; limit: some students will still not come.

::: exercise Balanced or one-sided? {level=2}
Decide whether each question calls for the balanced or the one-sided form of the discursive essay, and write a thesis for each. (a) *Some people believe zoos protect endangered animals; others believe they should be closed. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.* (b) *To what extent do you agree that all schools should teach a second foreign language?*
::: solution
(a) Balanced. Thesis, for example: *Although zoos confine animals that should be free, the best zoos play a role in protecting endangered species that cannot yet be replaced, so they should be reformed rather than closed.*

(b) One-sided (a position of degree, with counter-argument). Thesis, for example: *Schools should offer a second foreign language to every student who wants it, but making it compulsory takes time from subjects that many students need more.*

::: exercise Plan a combined essay {level=3}
Write an outline (thesis and paragraph points) for *Why do many people not recycle household waste, and what could be done to change this?* Link each solution to a cause and include an evaluation.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

- **Thesis**: People fail to recycle mainly because it is inconvenient and confusing rather than because they do not care, so the most effective solutions make recycling easier, not just more widely advertised.
- **Cause 1**: inconvenience — bins far away, few collections.
- **Cause 2**: confusion — different rules in different places, unclear labels.
- **Cause 3**: (less important) doubt about whether recycled waste is actually recycled.
- **Solutions**: collection from every home, with bins provided (→ cause 1); simple national rules and clear labels on packaging (→ cause 2); publish what happens to collected waste (→ cause 3).
- **Evaluation**: home collection is effective but costly; national rules are cheap but take time to agree; transparency builds trust but changes behaviour less.
- **Conclusion**: start with clear rules and labels, then extend home collection.
:::

::: exercise Write a comparison paragraph {level=3}
Write one point-by-point paragraph (100–130 words) comparing two ways of travelling to work or school — for example, cycling and taking the bus — on **one** criterion. Use at least three expressions of comparison and end with a judgement on that criterion.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

> On cost, cycling has a clear advantage over taking the bus. Both involve some expense: a bicycle has to be bought and maintained, while a bus pass has to be renewed each month. However, unlike the bus pass, a bicycle is a one-off purchase, and once it has been bought, the cost of each journey is close to nothing. A monthly bus pass, in contrast, is paid again and again, whether or not it is used every day. Over a year, therefore, a cyclist is likely to spend far less than a bus passenger, even after repairs. For anyone who travels the same route every day and can store a bicycle safely, cycling is the cheaper choice.

Check: one criterion (cost); comparison language (*Both*, *unlike*, *in contrast*, *far less than*); a judgement at the end.
:::
