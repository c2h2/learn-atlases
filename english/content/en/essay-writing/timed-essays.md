Two students sit the same practice test. They have forty minutes and one question, written for this lesson: *Should cities charge drivers a fee to enter the centre at the busiest times of day?*

Mehmet reads the question once and starts writing at once. After a general opening about traffic in the modern world, he writes a paragraph about pollution, then one about the cost of living, then realises that he has not yet said whether he supports the fee. With eight minutes left he begins a fourth paragraph on public transport, and with two minutes left he writes a one-sentence conclusion: *In conclusion, there are advantages and disadvantages.* He has written 340 words.

Liu Yang spends the first six minutes without writing a sentence of the essay. She underlines *should*, *a fee* and *the busiest times*, decides that her answer is *yes, if the money goes into public transport*, and writes a plan of six lines in the margin. She writes for twenty-eight minutes, glancing at the plan before each paragraph. In the last six minutes she rereads the essay, checks that her conclusion gives the same answer as her introduction, and corrects four errors of agreement and two missing articles. She has written 290 words.

Liu Yang's essay is shorter and better. It answers the question, its paragraphs follow from its thesis, and its conclusion confirms the answer. Mehmet's essay is a record of his thinking in the order it happened. The difference is not speed of writing, or knowledge of English, but the use of time: Liu Yang did in forty minutes the same four kinds of work that this course has described — planning, drafting, revising and editing ([[essay-writing/writing-process]]) — compressed but kept apart.

This last lesson of the course is about writing a complete essay in a fixed time: how to divide the time, how to plan in a few minutes, how to write and check under pressure, and what examinations of English writing generally ask for. It does not teach the tricks of any particular examination. It shows how to keep the habits of the whole course when there is no time for a second draft.

## The process, compressed

::: definition Timed essay {#def-timed-essay}
A **timed essay** is an essay written in one sitting within a fixed time, usually without sources and without the chance to put the draft away and return to it. Examinations, in-class assignments and some job applications use them. Typical lengths and times for learners range from about 150 words in twenty minutes to two or three pages in an hour or more.
:::

::: theorem Keep the stages, shorten them {#rule-compress}
1. **Plan before you write**, however little time you have. A rough guide for a single essay: about one sixth of the time for reading the question and planning, about two thirds for writing, and the rest for checking — in forty minutes, roughly five to eight minutes, twenty-five to thirty minutes and five minutes.
2. **Write one draft**, following the plan. Do not write a rough version and copy it out: there is not time to do both well.
3. **Leave time to check**, and protect it: the last few minutes are for reading, not for adding a new paragraph.
4. **Watch the clock** at fixed points — when the plan should be finished, when the body should be half done, when checking should begin.
:::

::: proof
The proportions are a common recommendation in writing guides and in teachers' advice for examinations, not a result of research, and they should be adjusted to the task and the writer: a writer who plans quickly can write for longer. What research supports is the principle of planning first. Kellogg (1988) found that university students who wrote an outline before drafting produced essays rated higher in quality, and explained the effect by the reduced load on attention during writing ([[essay-writing/writing-process#rule-stages-apart]]). In second-language writing, Ellis and Yuan (2004) compared Chinese learners of English who wrote a narrative after ten minutes of planning, without planning under time pressure, or without planning and without time pressure; the planners wrote more and with greater syntactic variety, while the writers given unlimited time wrote more accurately. Under a time limit, a few minutes of planning are not time lost from writing: they are what makes the writing go quickly.
:::

::: quiz
A writer has thirty minutes for an essay of about 200 words. Which plan of time is most sensible?
- [ ] write for thirty minutes without stopping, to produce as many words as possible
- [ ] plan for fifteen minutes, write a rough draft for ten, and copy it out neatly for five
- [x] plan for about five minutes, write for about twenty, and check for about five
- [ ] write for twenty-five minutes and plan the conclusion in the last five
::: solution
Five, twenty and five minutes keep all the stages and leave enough time to write 200 words. Writing without a plan risks an essay without a position; copying out a draft wastes the time that checking needs; and a conclusion planned at the end may not match the thesis.

## Planning in five minutes

A plan for a timed essay is short — a few lines in the margin or on the question paper — but it makes the decisions that a longer plan would make.

::: theorem A five-minute plan {#rule-quick-plan}
1. **Read the question twice.** Underline the task word, the topic and any limiting words ([[essay-writing/understanding-the-question#def-question-parts]]). Note every part of the question that needs an answer: a question with two parts (*Why …? What can be done?*) needs both.
2. **Decide your answer in one sentence.** Write it at the top of the plan. This will become your thesis.
3. **Choose two or three main points** that support the answer, with one example or reason for each. Two well-developed points are better than four undeveloped ones.
4. **Note the strongest objection** and your answer to it, if the question asks for a view or allows one ([[essay-writing/counter-arguments#rule-strongest]]).
5. **Note the conclusion**: the answer in other words, and a closing move ([[essay-writing/conclusions#rule-closing]]).

Use key words, symbols and arrows; write the plan in whatever language helps you think quickly.
:::

::: example A five-minute plan {#ex-quick-plan}
Make a plan for Liu Yang's question: *Should cities charge drivers a fee to enter the centre at the busiest times of day?* The essay should be about 250 words.
::: solution
1. **Read the question.** Task: *should* — a position is required. Topic: a fee for driving into the centre. Limit: *at the busiest times* — not a fee at all times, and not a ban.
2. **Decide the answer.** *Yes, if the money is used to improve public transport.* The condition will answer the main objection.
3. **Points**: (1) a fee at peak times reduces congestion, because some drivers change the time of their journey or the way they travel — example: deliveries moved to the evening; (2) the money can pay for more frequent buses, which gives drivers a real alternative.
4. **Objection**: unfair to people on low incomes who have to drive → answer: exemptions for some groups, and better buses help them most.
5. **Conclusion**: the fee is a means, not an end; it works if drivers have somewhere else to go.

The plan in the margin might look like this:

*YES — if money → public transport*
*1. less congestion at peak — drivers change time / mode (deliveries → evening)*
*2. money → more buses → real alternative*
*OBJ: unfair, low incomes → exemptions; better buses help them most*
*CONC: fee = means, not end; works if alternatives exist*

That is five lines and about a minute of writing, after four minutes of thinking.
:::
:::

::: warning The memorised essay
✗ Writing out a prepared essay, or long prepared paragraphs, on a topic that resembles the question.

Learners preparing for examinations sometimes memorise whole essays or "all-purpose" paragraphs and sentences and reproduce them in the test. Prepared text rarely fits the question exactly, so it tends to answer a different question, and examiners are generally trained to notice language that does not respond to the task. What is worth preparing is different: knowledge of the essay types and their structures ([[essay-writing/essay-types]]), a flexible stock of phrases for moves such as conceding and concluding, ideas and examples on common topics, and practice in planning quickly. These are tools for answering a new question, not answers to an old one.
:::

## Writing

::: theorem Writing under time pressure {#rule-timed-writing}
1. **Keep the introduction short**: two to four sentences that lead to the thesis. Do not spend time on an elaborate opening ([[essay-writing/thesis-introduction#rule-intro]]).
2. **One paragraph per point**, each with a topic sentence, an explanation and an example. Look at the plan before each paragraph.
3. **Keep moving.** If a word will not come, use a simpler one; if a sentence goes wrong, cross it out with a single line and write it again more simply. There is no time to search for the perfect expression.
4. **If you fall behind, shorten — do not skip.** Write a shorter final body paragraph, or merge two points, so that the conclusion is still written. An essay without a conclusion usually loses more than an essay with a short body paragraph.
5. **Write clearly.** In a handwritten essay, legible handwriting and clear corrections are part of being understood. In a typed essay, do not let the ease of deleting tempt you into editing while you draft.
:::

::: proof
The conclusion matters out of proportion to its length, because it is where the reader learns whether the essay has answered the question ([[essay-writing/conclusions#rule-conclusion-answer]]); a missing conclusion leaves an argument without its end. Simple, correct sentences are worth more under time pressure than ambitious ones that go wrong, because a reader who has to reconstruct a broken sentence is distracted from the argument. This is not advice to write only simple sentences — variety of structure is part of good writing, and examinations commonly assess it — but advice to use the structures you control. The single line through a mistake is a convention of handwritten examinations: it shows what has been withdrawn without making the page hard to read.
:::

::: example Estimating length {#ex-length}
A student's handwriting averages about ten words a line. The task asks for at least 250 words. After twenty minutes of writing, she has filled 19 lines, and she has fifteen minutes left, including five for checking. Is she on course, and what should she do?
::: solution
1. **Estimate the length so far**: 19 lines × 10 words ≈ 190 words.
2. **Estimate the rate**: 190 words in 20 minutes ≈ 9–10 words a minute.
3. **Estimate what remains**: 10 minutes of writing × about 9 words ≈ 90 words more, so about 280 in total.
4. **Decide**: she is on course for the minimum, with a little to spare. She should write her last body paragraph and the conclusion as planned, and keep the final five minutes for checking. She should not add a further paragraph, and she should not count every word: an estimate by lines is accurate enough and takes seconds.
:::
:::

::: example A timed essay {#ex-timed-essay}
Here is the essay written from the plan in [[#ex-quick-plan]], as a writer working to forty minutes might produce it. It was written for this lesson and is a model, not a sample of real student work. Identify its parts and check it against the plan.

> *Many large cities are crowded with traffic at the beginning and end of the working day, and one proposal is to charge drivers a fee to enter the centre at those times. Such a fee is justified, provided that the money it raises is used to improve public transport.*
>
> *The main argument for a peak-time fee is that it reduces congestion when congestion is worst. Most drivers have some choice about when or how they travel, and a fee gives them a reason to use it. A delivery company, for example, may move some of its deliveries to the evening, and a commuter who lives near a bus route may leave the car at home. Even a small change in the number of cars can make a large difference to traffic at the busiest times.*
>
> *However, a fee works only if drivers have an alternative, and this is where the money matters. If the income is spent on more frequent and reliable buses, drivers who change their habits will find that the bus is a real choice rather than a punishment.*
>
> *Critics argue that the fee is unfair to people on low incomes who have to drive. This concern is serious, but it is an argument for exemptions — for example, for disabled drivers and some essential workers — and for better buses, which help people on low incomes more than anyone.*
>
> *A peak-time fee, then, is not an end in itself but a way of paying for better alternatives. Cities that introduce one should spend its income on the transport that makes it fair.*
::: solution
1. **Introduction** (two sentences): background, then the thesis — *justified, provided that the money … improve public transport*. The condition from the plan is there.
2. **Body paragraph 1**: point 1 of the plan, with a topic sentence, an explanation (drivers have some choice) and examples (deliveries, the commuter). The last sentence is hedged appropriately (*can make*), with no invented figures.
3. **Body paragraph 2**: point 2, linked by *However* and *this is where the money matters* — a topic sentence that shows the relation to the previous paragraph ([[essay-writing/signposting#rule-paragraph-links]]).
4. **Counter-argument**: stated fairly, conceded (*This concern is serious*) and answered by turning it ([[essay-writing/counter-arguments#rule-rebuttal]]).
5. **Conclusion**: the thesis restated in new words (*not an end in itself but a way of paying …*) and a recommendation.

The essay is about 265 words — a little above the target, which is where to aim. Paragraph 3 is brief; under time pressure, that is the right paragraph to keep short, because the conclusion depends on the others.
:::
:::

## Checking in the last minutes

::: theorem A five-minute check {#rule-check}
In the last minutes, read the essay once, quickly, and then make two short passes:

1. **The answer**: read the last sentence of the introduction and the first sentence of the conclusion. Do they give the same answer to the question? If not, change the one that does not match the body — usually the introduction, by one clause ([[essay-writing/revising-editing#rule-two-tests]]).
2. **Your own errors**: search for the two or three kinds of error you make most often — for many writers, verb agreement, articles and plurals, or comma splices ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/editing#rule-feedback]]) — one kind at a time.

Correct neatly: a single line through the error and the correction above it. Do not start a new paragraph or rewrite a whole one in these minutes.
:::

::: proof
In a timed essay there is no time for a full revision, but the two checks above are the ones with the highest return. A mismatch between thesis and conclusion is common in timed essays, because the writer's view develops as they write and the introduction was written first; correcting one clause can make the whole essay consistent. A targeted search for known errors is quicker and finds more than a general reread, because a reader looking for everything sees little, and because each writer's errors cluster in a few types. Larger changes in the final minutes are risky: a new paragraph started with three minutes left is often unfinished when time ends.
:::

::: example Checking a paragraph {#ex-check}
A writer knows that her most frequent errors are subject–verb agreement, missing articles and comma splices. Check this paragraph from her timed essay for those three, and correct it.

*A fee at peak times reduce congestion, because many drivers can change time of their journey. For example, delivery company can make some deliveries in the evening, this means fewer lorries are on the roads in the morning.*
::: solution
1. **Agreement**: the subject of *reduce* is *A fee* (singular) → *reduces*.
2. **Articles**: *change time of their journey* → *change **the** time of their journey*; *delivery company* (singular countable) → ***a** delivery company*.
3. **Comma splice**: *… in the evening, this means …* joins two independent clauses with a comma → *… in the evening, which means …* or *… in the evening. This means …*.

✓ *A fee at peak times reduces congestion, because many drivers can change the time of their journey. For example, a delivery company can make some deliveries in the evening, which means that fewer lorries are on the roads in the morning.*

Four corrections, each a word or two, made in under a minute because she knew what to look for.
:::
:::

::: quiz
With three minutes left, a writer notices that her conclusion supports the fee but her introduction says *There are advantages and disadvantages to this idea.* What should she do?
- [ ] add a new paragraph explaining that she changed her mind
- [ ] rewrite the conclusion so that it also lists advantages and disadvantages
- [x] change the last sentence of the introduction so that it states the position the body and conclusion support
- [ ] leave both as they are, since there is no time
::: solution
Changing one sentence of the introduction makes the essay consistent in a minute or two, and gives it a thesis it lacked. A new paragraph would not be finished; weakening the conclusion would remove the essay's answer; leaving the mismatch leaves the reader unsure of the position.
:::
:::

## Essays in examinations

Many examinations of English include a timed writing task, and their formats change from time to time, so what follows is general and dated: it describes the situation as of 2026, and the official information published by each examination is the only reliable source for its current format, timing and assessment.

- **Common task types.** Timed writing tasks in examinations of English for learners commonly include an argumentative or discursive essay on a general topic, often of roughly 150–300 words; a description of data or a diagram ([[essay-writing/describing-data]]); and functional writing such as an email or a letter. Some tasks ask for a contribution to a discussion, or for a response based on a reading or listening passage.
- **Examples.** IELTS Academic, for instance, has two writing tasks in one hour — a report on visual information and an essay — and its published guidance suggests spending about twice as long on the essay as on the first task. China's CET-4 and CET-6 each include a short timed writing task. The writing tasks of the gaokao English papers differ between provinces and between versions of the paper. TOEFL iBT has redesigned its writing section more than once in recent years, replacing its longer independent essay with shorter tasks. None of these descriptions is a guide to a particular test: check the current official materials.
- **What is assessed.** Published marking criteria of examinations commonly cover four broad areas: how fully and relevantly the task is answered, how clearly the text is organised and linked, the range and accuracy of vocabulary, and the range and accuracy of grammar. These are the concerns of this whole course. No method of preparation guarantees a particular score.
- **Word limits.** Where a task gives a minimum length, an essay well below it usually cannot answer the task fully; an essay far above it uses time that checking needs. Aim a little above the minimum.

::: note English and Chinese
- **Templates (模板) and all-purpose sentences (万能句).** Exam preparation materials in China, as elsewhere, often offer essay templates and sentences said to fit any topic. A clear structure is useful, and a few flexible phrases for conceding or concluding are worth knowing. Whole prepared paragraphs are not: they make essays sound alike, they rarely answer the specific question, and they are easy for a reader to recognise ([[#rule-quick-plan]]).
- **Planning in Chinese.** Under time pressure, a plan in Chinese key words, or a mixture, is quicker for many writers than one in English, and there is nothing wrong with it. Drafting whole sentences in Chinese and translating them is slower, and tends to produce Chinese sentence patterns in English; write the essay itself in English from the plan.
- **Handwriting and typing.** Many Chinese learners write examination essays by hand and draft coursework on a computer. The skills differ: handwriting requires a plan, because large changes are hard; typing makes changes easy but encourages editing while drafting. Practise in the form the test or task will use.
- **Counting words.** The habit of counting characters (字数) does not transfer: English length is counted in words. Estimate by lines, as in [[#ex-length]], rather than counting each word.
- **Set phrases.** Proverbs, 成语 translated into English, and quotations memorised for essays (*As the saying goes, …*) are common in learners' timed essays. Use one only if it says something your argument needs ([[essay-writing/thesis-introduction]]).
:::

::: history
Timed written examinations have a long history in China, where the imperial civil service examinations, with the eight-legged essay (八股文) as their fixed form from the Ming dynasty onwards, were abolished in 1905. In the English-speaking world, written composition entered entrance examinations in the late nineteenth century: Harvard College began requiring a short English composition of applicants in the 1870s, and many American colleges followed. Essay examinations soon raised a problem that has shaped them ever since — readers disagree. In a well-known study for the Educational Testing Service, Paul Diederich, John French and Sydell Carlton (1961) had 300 student essays graded by 53 readers from different professions on a nine-point scale, and found that more than nine papers in ten received at least seven of the nine possible grades. The response, from the 1960s onwards, was to train readers, give them scoring guides with sample essays, and use more than one reader for each paper, methods that remain the basis of the marking criteria and band descriptors that examinations publish today.
:::

## Where this leads

This lesson ends the course. The essay skills it brings together — reading the question, a thesis, argued paragraphs, counter-arguments, a conclusion that answers, signposting in proportion, revising and editing — are the foundation of [[academic-writing]], where essays become research papers with sources, citations and new kinds of section, and of [[style-and-rhetoric]], which turns from organisation to the sentence and the reader. The spoken counterpart of the timed essay — a short talk or a turn in a discussion prepared in a few minutes — is practised in [[presentations-and-discussion/seminars-discussion]] and [[presentations-and-discussion/debate-persuasion]].

::: summary
- A timed essay compresses the writing process but keeps its stages: plan, write one draft, check ([[#def-timed-essay]], [[#rule-compress]]).
- Divide the time roughly into a sixth for planning, two thirds for writing and the rest for checking, and watch the clock at fixed points; planning makes the writing faster, not slower.
- Plan in about five minutes: mark the question, decide the answer in one sentence, choose two or three points with examples, note the objection and the conclusion ([[#rule-quick-plan]]).
- Do not reproduce memorised essays; prepare structures, flexible phrases, ideas and practice instead.
- Write a short introduction, one paragraph per point, simple correct sentences where in doubt, and shorten rather than skip the conclusion if time runs out ([[#rule-timed-writing]]).
- In the last minutes, check that the introduction and conclusion give the same answer, and search for your own most frequent errors ([[#rule-check]]).
- Examination formats change: describe and prepare for them from current official information, and expect them to assess task response, organisation, vocabulary and grammar.
:::

Which of these are useful preparation for a timed essay, and which are risky? Say why. (a) Memorising a 250-word essay on technology. (b) Learning several ways to introduce a counter-argument. (c) Practising five-minute plans for questions on different topics. ::: exercise Check in two minutes {level=2}
A writer's error log shows that she often omits plural *-s* and writes comma splices. Check this passage for those two errors only, and correct it. *Many city have introduced a fee for drivers, the results have been mixed. In some place, traffic fell sharply. In others, driver simply changed their routes.*
::: solution
Plurals: *Many city* → *Many cities*; *In some place* → *In some places*; *driver simply changed their routes* → *drivers simply changed their routes*. Comma splice: *… for drivers, the results …* → *… for drivers, and the results …* or *… for drivers; the results …* or two sentences.

::: exercise Write a timed essay {level=3}
Set a clock for forty minutes and write an essay of about 250 words on this question, written for this exercise: *Should employers allow staff to choose their own working hours?* Plan for about six minutes and keep the last five for checking. Afterwards, note how long each stage took.
::: hint
Decide first whether your answer is *yes*, *no* or *yes, with conditions*. What is the strongest objection an employer would make?
:::
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model essay (written for the atlas, about 240 words):

> *In many jobs, the hours of work are fixed by tradition rather than need: an office opens at nine because offices always have. Employers should allow staff to choose their own hours wherever the work does not depend on everyone being present at the same time.*
>
> *The strongest reason is that people work best at different times. Some are most alert early in the morning, others later in the day, and a fixed schedule asks all of them to do their hardest work at the same hours. Staff who can choose are more likely to work when they are most productive.*
>
> *Flexible hours also make work possible for people whose lives do not fit a fixed day. A parent who must collect a child at three, or a student with morning classes, may be an excellent employee if allowed to start early or finish late.*
>
> *Employers may object that flexibility makes it hard to arrange meetings or to serve customers. This is true for some jobs, such as a shop that must be staffed when it is open. But for many others, the problem can be solved by agreeing a few shared hours in the middle of the day, when everyone is present, and leaving the rest to choice.*
>
> *Fixed hours, then, should be the exception for jobs that need them, not the rule for all. Allowing choice costs employers little and gives them staff who work when they work best.*

Check your own essay against the model's features: a thesis with a condition; two reasons with examples; an objection conceded and answered; a conclusion that restates and recommends; no invented figures.
:::

Check: the review is honest about time, structure and errors, and names one specific change.
