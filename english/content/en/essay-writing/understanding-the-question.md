Here is an essay question, and the first sentences of two answers to it:

> *To what extent has the spread of smartphones changed the way young people read?*

> **A.** *Smartphones have become part of daily life for almost all young people. They use them to send messages, watch videos, play games, shop and read the news. Many apps are designed to keep users on the screen for as long as possible …*

> **B.** *Smartphones have changed where and how often young people read more than they have changed whether they read. Short texts on screens have taken a large share of reading time, but long reading for study and pleasure has not disappeared; it has moved partly to screens and partly survived on paper …*

Answer A is fluent and accurate, and it will probably continue for several hundred words about smartphones. But it is answering a different question — perhaps *How do young people use smartphones?* Answer B has noticed three things in the question: the topic (*the way young people read*), the cause it asks about (*smartphones*), and above all the task (*to what extent* — a question about *how much*). Its first sentence already gives an answer of degree: *more … than*.

Examiners and teachers say the same thing year after year: the commonest reason a good writer gets a weak mark is that the essay does not answer the question. This lesson shows how to read a question closely, what the common task words ask for, how to narrow a broad topic to one that can be argued in the space you have, and how to get from the question to ideas. It is the first step of planning in the process described in [[essay-writing/writing-process]].

## The parts of a question

::: definition Task, topic and focus {#def-question-parts}
An essay question has three kinds of element:

- the **task words** (or *instruction words*, *directive words*), which say what to do with the topic: *discuss*, *evaluate*, *compare*, *to what extent*, *explain why*;
- the **topic**: the general subject (*smartphones*, *reading*);
- the **focus** (or *limiting words*): the parts that narrow the topic — a group (*young people*), a place, a period, an aspect (*the way … read*), or a relation (*changed*).

Some questions also contain **assumptions** — things the question takes as true, such as *the spread of smartphones* — and **key terms** that need defining.
:::

A good habit is to mark up the question on paper before planning: underline the task words, circle the topic, and box each limiting word. Then rewrite the question in your own words. If you cannot, you do not yet understand it.

::: example Marking up a question {#ex-markup}
Analyse this question: *Evaluate the claim that working from home has made employees in large companies more productive since 2020.*
::: solution
1. **Task words**: *Evaluate the claim that* — weigh the evidence for and against a stated claim and reach a judgement about how far it is true.
2. **Topic**: working from home; productivity.
3. **Focus**:
   - *employees in large companies* — not small businesses, not self-employed people;
   - *more productive* — the claim is about productivity, not happiness, cost or culture;
   - *since 2020* — a period with a clear starting point (the move to home working during the pandemic).
4. **Key term**: *productive* needs defining. Output per hour? Output per employee? Measured by whom? The answer may depend on the definition.
5. **Rewrite in your own words**: *Is it true that people in large firms have got more done since they began working from home in 2020? How strong is the evidence, and what does it depend on?*

An essay on the advantages and disadvantages of working from home would miss the task (*evaluate the claim*), the focus (*productivity*, *large companies*) and the period.
:::
:::

## Task words

The task words decide what kind of essay you write. Two questions on the same topic with different task words need different plans.

| task words | what they ask for |
|---|---|
| *describe*, *outline* | say what something is like or what happened; *outline* asks for the main points only |
| *explain*, *account for* | say why or how something happens, giving causes, reasons or mechanisms |
| *analyse*, *examine* | break the topic into parts and show how they work and relate |
| *compare* | show similarities and differences (in common use, often both) |
| *contrast* | show differences |
| *discuss* | consider the topic from different angles, usually with arguments on more than one side, and reach a view |
| *evaluate*, *assess*, *critically evaluate* | weigh strengths and weaknesses, or evidence for and against, and reach a judgement |
| *to what extent …?*, *how far …?* | judge the degree to which something is true, with reasons for both the extent and the limits |
| *justify*, *argue* | give reasons for a position (sometimes a given one) and answer the obvious objections |
| *do you agree?*, *what is your view?* | state a position and support it; consider the other view |

::: theorem Answer the task, not the topic {#rule-task}
1. Identify the task words first, and plan the essay to do what they ask.
2. Questions that ask for a **judgement** — *discuss*, *evaluate*, *assess*, *to what extent*, *do you agree* — need a clear position, stated early and supported throughout. Description alone does not answer them.
3. Questions that ask for **explanation** or **comparison** still need a controlling idea: which cause matters most, which difference is most important.
4. If a question has more than one part (*Why …, and what should be done?*), answer every part, in proportion.
:::

::: proof
Task words correspond to different kinds of thinking. Describing and outlining report what is known; explaining and analysing show how things work; evaluating weighs and judges. University marking schemes and examination descriptors generally reward the higher kinds of thinking — analysis and evaluation — over description, and they treat failure to address the task as a serious weakness, whatever the quality of the language. That is why a fluent essay that describes when it was asked to evaluate can score below a less fluent one that evaluates. The meaning of some task words also varies a little between institutions and subjects (in some departments, *discuss* expects a strongly argued position, in others a balanced survey); when your teacher or department publishes its own definitions, follow them.
:::

::: theorem Questions of degree {#rule-degree}
*To what extent*, *how far* and *how much* ask for an answer of **degree**, not *yes* or *no*. A good answer:

1. says how far the statement is true (*largely*, *only partly*, *in some respects but not others*, *for some groups but not others*);
2. gives the reasons and evidence that support that much;
3. gives the reasons and evidence that set the limits;
4. ends with a judgement that weighs the two.
:::

::: proof
A *to what extent* question presupposes that the statement is true to some degree and asks the writer to measure it. *Completely* and *not at all* are possible answers, but they are rarely convincing, because they have to dismiss all the evidence on the other side. The language of degree is the language of this kind of essay: *largely*, *to a considerable extent*, *only in part*, *more … than*, *mainly … but also*. Answer B at the start of the lesson uses it in its first sentence.
:::

::: example One topic, three tasks {#ex-three-tasks}
Plan the first sentence and the paragraph structure for each of these three questions on the same topic.

(a) *Explain why many cities have introduced shared bicycle schemes.* (b) *Compare shared bicycle schemes with private bicycle ownership as ways of getting around a city.* (c) *To what extent have shared bicycle schemes reduced car use in cities?*
::: solution
1. **(a) Explain why** asks for causes. Structure: one paragraph per main cause, most important first or last.
   *Cities have introduced shared bicycle schemes mainly to reduce short car journeys, but also to cut pollution and to connect residents with public transport.*
   Paragraphs: short trips and congestion; air quality and health; the "last mile" from stations; cheaper technology (apps, GPS locks) that made the schemes possible.
2. **(b) Compare** asks for similarities and differences, organised by criteria.
   *Shared and private bicycles do the same job in different ways: shared bicycles are cheaper and more flexible for occasional trips, while private ones are more reliable for daily commuting.*
   Paragraphs by criterion: cost; convenience and availability; maintenance and security; who each suits ([[essay-writing/essay-types]]).
3. **(c) To what extent** asks for a judgement of degree.
   *Shared bicycle schemes have reduced car use only modestly, because many of their trips replace walking or bus journeys rather than car journeys.*
   Paragraphs: evidence of some car trips replaced; evidence that many trips replace other modes; conditions under which schemes work better (cycle lanes, distances); judgement.

One topic; three essays. Only (c) needs evidence about car use, and only (b) needs private bicycles at all.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which opening best answers *How far do you agree that online shopping has harmed small local shops?*
- [ ] *Online shopping has grown quickly in the last twenty years, and many people now buy clothes, books and food online.*
- [ ] *There are many advantages and disadvantages of online shopping.*
- [x] *Online shopping has harmed small shops that sell standard goods, but it has helped some specialist shops to reach customers far beyond their town.*
- [ ] *I agree that online shopping is very convenient.*
::: solution
The third opening gives an answer of degree — harmed some kinds of small shop, helped others — and stays on the question's focus (*small local shops*). The first describes online shopping in general; the second announces a list without a position; the fourth answers a different question (convenience for shoppers).
:::
:::

::: warning Writing about the topic, not the question
✗ A question asks *Why do some students prefer to study abroad?* The essay describes the advantages and disadvantages of studying abroad.

✗ A question asks *Should governments spend more on space exploration or on problems on Earth?* The essay is a history of space travel.

The writer sees a familiar topic and writes what they know about it — knowledge telling ([[essay-writing/writing-process]]). A related trap is the **memorised essay**: a prepared answer on a topic, adapted at the last minute to whatever question appears. Markers recognise it quickly, because its paragraphs answer a question that was not asked. Every paragraph you write should pass the test: *How does this help to answer the question?*
:::

## Key terms and assumptions

::: theorem Define the key terms {#rule-define}
If a key term in the question is vague or disputed — *success*, *modern*, *effective*, *productive*, *a good education* — say in the introduction or the first body paragraph what you take it to mean, and keep to that meaning. Choose a definition that is reasonable and that the evidence can test. Do not copy a dictionary definition of a word everyone understands.
:::

::: proof
Many disagreements are about words: two people arguing whether a policy is *effective* may be measuring different things. A writer who defines the term controls the argument and saves the reader from guessing. Dictionary definitions rarely help, because they give the general meaning (*success: the achievement of an aim*) rather than the meaning that makes the question answerable (*success here means finding work related to one's degree within a year of graduating*). The opening of *The Oxford English Dictionary defines …* is a cliché that markers tend to dislike for this reason ([[essay-writing/thesis-introduction]]).
:::

Questions also carry assumptions. *Why have young people stopped reading books?* assumes that they have. You are allowed — often expected — to test an assumption: *It is not clear that young people have stopped reading books; what has changed is …* Do this openly and with evidence, and still answer the question in the form it was asked, as far as the evidence allows.

::: example Testing an assumption {#ex-assumption}
How could you begin an answer to *Since exams only test memory, should schools replace them with coursework?*
::: solution
1. **Find the assumption**: *exams only test memory*. It is stated as a fact, and the question depends on it.
2. **Test it**: many exams test more than memory — problem-solving in mathematics, analysing an unseen text, writing an argument under time limits. Some exams do mainly test recall.
3. **Decide how to use it**: the essay can accept part of the assumption and reject the rest, and then answer the actual question.

*Whether schools should replace exams with coursework depends partly on a premise the question takes for granted: that exams only test memory. Some exams, especially those built on recall of facts, largely do; but well-designed exams also test the ability to solve unfamiliar problems and to write under pressure, which coursework does not. The case for replacing exams is therefore strongest for the first kind …*

The opening challenges the assumption with reasons, and then turns back to the question.
:::
:::

## Narrowing a topic

When you choose your own topic or question — in coursework, in a research essay — the commonest mistake is a topic that is too wide. *Technology and education* could fill a library; a 1,500-word essay on it can only list.

::: definition Scope {#def-scope}
The **scope** of an essay is the range of material it covers: which people, places, periods and aspects of the topic. A **manageable scope** is one that the essay can treat with explanation and evidence in the space and time available.
:::

::: theorem Narrow the topic {#rule-narrow}
Narrow a broad topic by one or more of:

1. **group**: *students* → *first-year university students* → *first-year students studying in a second language*;
2. **place**: *cities* → *Chinese cities* → *one city*;
3. **time**: *the last twenty years*, *since 2020*;
4. **aspect**: *technology in education* → *recorded lectures* → *recorded lectures and attendance*;
5. **relation**: turn the topic into a question about a cause, an effect, a comparison or a solution.

Stop when the topic can be argued with specific evidence in the length you have. Then write it as a question.
:::

::: example Narrowing for a 1,500-word essay {#ex-narrow}
A student wants to write about *tourism*. Narrow the topic to a question suitable for a 1,500-word argumentative essay.
::: solution
1. **Aspect**: tourism has economic, environmental, cultural and social sides. Choose one: *the effect of tourism on local residents*.
2. **Place**: residents where? *historic town centres*.
3. **Relation**: what about the residents? Housing is a well-documented issue: *short-term holiday rentals and housing for residents*.
4. **Turn it into a question that needs a position**: *Should historic towns limit short-term holiday rentals to protect housing for residents?*

The final question names a group (residents), a place (historic towns), an aspect (housing) and a decision (should … limit). The student can now look for specific evidence — rental numbers, rents, the experience of towns that have introduced limits — and argue a position.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which question is best suited to a 1,000-word essay?
- [ ] *What are the effects of climate change?*
- [ ] *Is technology good or bad?*
- [x] *Should secondary schools in large cities provide free breakfast for all students?*
- [ ] *How has education changed in the last hundred years?*
::: solution
The third question has a clear group, place and decision, and can be argued with specific reasons and evidence in 1,000 words. The others are far too broad: each could be the subject of a book.

## From the question to ideas

Once the question is clear, ask it questions of your own. Useful ones:

- What would **someone who disagrees** say? (This gives you counter-arguments; see [[essay-writing/counter-arguments]].)
- **Who** is affected, and are they affected differently?
- **What causes** this, and what follows from it?
- **What evidence** would settle the question, and what do I actually know?
- **Does it depend** on something — the place, the period, the group, the definition?
- **What is the strongest case** for each possible answer?

The last two questions are especially useful for questions of degree: *it depends* is not an answer by itself, but *it depends on X, and in the cases that matter most, X is …* is the start of a good thesis.

::: example From a question to a list of ideas {#ex-ideas}
Use the questions above to generate ideas for *Should secondary schools in large cities provide free breakfast for all students?*
::: solution
1. **Someone who disagrees**: cost to schools or taxpayers; parents' responsibility; food waste if students don't eat it; why give it to families who can afford it?
2. **Who is affected**: students from low-income families (benefit most); all students (concentration, social mixing); staff (extra work); parents (time, money).
3. **Causes and effects**: why do students skip breakfast — cost, time, long journeys, habit? Effects of hunger on attention in morning lessons.
4. **Evidence**: studies of school breakfast programmes; attendance and punctuality data; the experience of schools that already do it.
5. **Does it depend**: on whether the breakfast is universal or targeted; on how it is organised (before lessons or in the first lesson); on the quality of the food.
6. **Strongest case for each answer**: *yes* — universal provision avoids stigma, so the students who need it actually come; *no* — the same money could fund targeted help for those in need.

The writer now has material for a position such as *yes, universally, because targeted schemes are underused*, with a counter-argument about cost to answer. Whatever position she chooses, she must check what the evidence actually shows before relying on it.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **审题 transfers well.** Chinese-educated writers are often trained to read essay prompts very carefully (审题), and that habit is exactly what this lesson asks for. What may need adjusting is the next step. In many Chinese school essays, 立意 means finding a central idea that is meaningful or uplifting, often a lesson about life or society. An English argumentative essay needs a **position on the question asked** — which may be *partly*, *it depends* or *no* — rather than a moral.
- **Task words and their Chinese equivalents.** 讨论 and 谈谈你的看法 are close to *discuss* and *what is your view*, but 评价 can mean ‘comment on’ more loosely than English *evaluate*, which expects a weighed judgement. 分析 matches *analyse* well. *To what extent* has no fixed Chinese equivalent (在多大程度上 is a translation, not a common prompt); learners sometimes answer it with a simple *yes*, which misses the question of degree.
- **"Both sides" essays.** Many learners have practised a pattern of advantages, then disadvantages, then *in my opinion*. This fits *discuss both views* questions; it does not fit *to what extent*, *evaluate* or *should*, which want the writer's judgement to organise the whole essay.
- **Memorised material.** Memorising good sentences, examples and quotations is a common and useful way of building language. The risk is using them where they do not answer the question. Adapt memorised material to the question, or leave it out.
:::

::: history
Many of the task words used in essay questions became standard through work on the classification of educational objectives. In 1956 a group of American examiners led by Benjamin Bloom published the *Taxonomy of Educational Objectives, Handbook I: Cognitive Domain*, which arranged thinking skills from knowledge and comprehension through application and analysis to synthesis and evaluation. Teachers and examination boards took up the taxonomy as a way of writing questions at different levels, with verbs such as *describe* for the lower levels and *evaluate* for the higher. Lorin Anderson, David Krathwohl and colleagues published a revision in 2001 that renamed the levels as verbs (*remember*, *understand*, *apply*, *analyse*, *evaluate*, *create*). The taxonomy has been criticised for treating the levels as a strict hierarchy, but its influence on how essay questions are worded is still visible in the lists of task words given to students today.
:::

## Where this leads

With the question understood and the topic narrowed, the next step is a thesis: [[essay-writing/thesis-introduction]]. The different essay types that task words point to — discursive, compare-and-contrast, cause-and-effect, problem–solution — are set out in [[essay-writing/essay-types]], and the special demands of questions under exam conditions in [[essay-writing/timed-essays]]. Reading academic texts with a question in mind is practised in [[academic-reading/strategic-reading]], and forming a research question for a longer project in [[academic-writing/research-questions]].

::: summary
- An essay question has task words, a topic and a focus (limiting words); it may also contain key terms to define and assumptions to test ([[#def-question-parts]]).
- Mark up the question and rewrite it in your own words before planning.
- Answer the task, not the topic: judgement questions (*discuss*, *evaluate*, *to what extent*, *do you agree*) need a position stated early and supported throughout ([[#rule-task]]).
- *To what extent* and *how far* ask for an answer of degree, with reasons for both the extent and the limits ([[#rule-degree]]).
- Define vague or disputed key terms in a way the evidence can test, and test assumptions openly ([[#rule-define]]).
- Narrow a broad topic by group, place, time, aspect and relation until it can be argued with specific evidence in the space available ([[#def-scope]], [[#rule-narrow]]).
- Generate ideas by questioning the question: who disagrees, who is affected, what causes what, what the evidence is, and what the answer depends on.
:::

Write a one-sentence answer of degree to each question. (a) *To what extent do exams measure what students have learned?* (b) *How far has video calling replaced business travel?*
::: solution
Answers will vary. Models:

(a) *Exams measure well what students can recall and do alone under time pressure, but they capture less of what students can do with time, resources and other people, such as research and teamwork.*

(b) *Video calling has largely replaced short internal meetings that once required travel, but it has replaced much less of the travel needed to win new clients or to build trust with partners.*

Check: each sentence says *how much* and marks a limit, not just *yes* or *no*.

::: exercise Spot the off-question paragraph {level=2}
The question is *Why do many young adults in large cities rent rather than buy their homes?* Which of these paragraph topics are off the question? (a) high house prices compared with incomes; (b) the advantages of owning a home; (c) the need to move for work; (d) the history of house building in the twentieth century; (e) difficulty saving for a deposit; (f) how to choose a good landlord.
::: solution
Off the question: (b), (d) and (f). The question asks for causes of renting. (b) could be useful only if it is turned into a point about why, despite these advantages, young adults still rent; on its own it does not answer *why*.

::: exercise Test the assumption {level=3}
Each question contains an assumption. Identify it and write the first two sentences of an introduction that tests it and then turns back to the question. (a) *Why has the quality of television declined?* (b) *Since online friendships are shallow, should parents discourage them?*
::: hint
Ask: is the assumption always true, partly true, or true only for some cases? Then say which part of the question you can still answer.
:::
::: solution
Answers will vary. Models:

(a) Assumption: television quality has declined. *Whether television has declined depends on what is measured: there is more low-cost programming than ever, but also more expensive, carefully made drama, now often made for streaming services. This essay argues that the average has fallen while the best has improved, and asks why both have happened at once.*

(b) Assumption: online friendships are shallow. *Some online friendships are shallow, but many teenagers keep close friendships alive online, and some young people find support online that they cannot find nearby. Whether parents should discourage them therefore depends less on the fact that they are online than on what kind of friendships they are.*
:::

::: exercise From question to plan {level=3}
Take the question *To what extent is it the responsibility of individuals, rather than governments, to keep themselves healthy?* Mark it up, define one key term, list at least six ideas using the questions in this lesson, and write a one-sentence answer of degree.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

- **Task**: *to what extent* (degree). **Topic**: responsibility for health. **Focus**: *individuals* vs *governments*; *keep themselves healthy*.
- **Key term**: *keep themselves healthy* — here, preventing avoidable illness through diet, exercise and not smoking, not treating illness once it occurs.
- **Ideas**: individuals choose what they eat and whether they exercise; choices depend on income, time and what is available nearby; governments control food labelling, taxes and the safety of streets and parks; some health risks (air pollution, unsafe housing) are beyond individual control; health education by governments helps individuals choose; opponents would say government rules limit personal freedom.
- **Answer of degree**: *Individuals are responsible for the choices they can actually make, but governments are largely responsible for the conditions that decide which choices are realistic, so responsibility is shared, and weighs more heavily on governments for people with fewer resources.*
:::
