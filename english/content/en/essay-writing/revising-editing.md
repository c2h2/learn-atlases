Zhang Yu has finished a first draft of an essay on *Should cities replace some car parking in the centre with parks and seating?* Her teacher returns it with three comments in the margin: *Your second and fourth paragraphs make the same point.* — *Where is the counter-argument you mention in the introduction?* — *Your conclusion argues for more parking, not less.* Zhang Yu spends an evening on the draft. She replaces *good* with *beneficial* nine times, corrects every article, changes *a lot of* to *numerous*, and adds *Moreover* to the start of three paragraphs. The second draft is more correct than the first. It has the same three problems.

What she did was editing. What the draft needed was revision: merging two paragraphs, writing a missing one, and either changing the conclusion or changing the thesis. Her teacher's comments were about the argument and the structure, and no amount of work on words could answer them. This is the most common way in which writers in any language waste revision time, and it has been documented since the first studies of how people revise ([[essay-writing/writing-process]]).

This lesson gives methods for the two kinds of work: **revising** an essay for its argument and structure, and **editing** it for language. It also covers feedback — how to give it to others and how to use what you receive. The basic editing checklist, error codes and the error log are taught in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/editing]]; this lesson applies them to a whole essay and adds the larger passes that come before them.

## Two kinds of change

::: definition Revising and editing {#def-revising-editing}
- **Revising** changes what an essay says and how it is organised: its thesis, the points of its paragraphs, their order, what is included and left out, how objections are handled, and whether the conclusion follows. Revision changes can add, delete, move or rewrite whole paragraphs.
- **Editing** changes how the essay says it: sentence structure, grammar, word choice, register, concision, punctuation and spelling. **Proofreading** is the last, narrowest part of editing: the search for surface slips before the text is handed in.

The distinction is between changes that affect the meaning of the text and changes that do not.
:::

::: theorem Revise before you edit {#rule-revise-first}
1. **Read the whole draft first**, without a pen, as a reader would. Note problems of argument and structure; do not correct anything yet.
2. **Revise from the largest unit down**: the thesis and the answer to the question; then the order and points of the paragraphs; then the support within each paragraph.
3. **Edit only when the structure is settled**: sentences first, then grammar and words, then proofreading.
4. **Leave time between passes**, and between drafting and revising, whenever you can.
:::

::: proof
Faigley and Witte (1981) classified the revisions writers make into **surface changes**, which leave the meaning of the text as it was (spelling, tense, synonyms, punctuation), and **meaning changes**, which alter what the text says. In their study, inexperienced student writers made mostly surface changes, while expert adult writers made far more meaning changes — the same contrast Sommers (1980) had reported. Expert writers revise from the top down because a change at a higher level can make lower-level work unnecessary: there is no point editing the sentences of a paragraph that will be cut. Reading first without correcting is the hardest part of the rule, because errors catch the eye; but a writer who stops at every error never sees the whole. The pause before revising helps because a writer who has just finished a draft still reads what they meant to write; after a day, they read more of what is actually on the page.
:::

::: quiz
Which change is **revising**, not editing?
- [ ] replacing *a lot of* with *many* throughout the essay
- [ ] correcting *The number of cars have fallen* to *has fallen*
- [x] moving the counter-argument from the first body paragraph to just before the conclusion
- [ ] splitting a long sentence into two
::: solution
Moving a paragraph changes the organisation of the argument, so it is revising. The others change words, grammar or sentence boundaries without changing what the essay says — editing.
:::
:::

## Revising the argument and structure

### The question test and the thesis test

The first revision questions are the ones [[essay-writing/writing-process#rule-revise]] listed: does the essay answer the question, and is its position the same from start to finish? Two quick tests answer them.

::: theorem Two tests for the whole essay {#rule-two-tests}
1. **The question test.** Copy the question at the top of the draft. Read the thesis and the first sentence of the conclusion directly after it. Do they answer *this* question — its task word, its topic and its limits ([[essay-writing/understanding-the-question#def-question-parts]])?
2. **The thesis test.** Put the thesis and the restatement in the conclusion side by side. Do they give the same answer? If not, decide which one the body supports, and change the other.
:::

::: proof
These tests take two minutes and catch the problems that cost most. An essay that drifts from the question usually does so because the writer followed their ideas as they drafted, and the drift is often visible only when the opening and the ending are read together. A conclusion that disagrees with the thesis is especially common in learners' essays: the act of writing the body changes the writer's mind, which is a good thing, but the introduction written before it still states the old view. The cure is to revise the thesis to match what the essay has actually argued, not to force the body back into the old position ([[essay-writing/conclusions#rule-restate]]).
:::

### The reverse outline

::: definition Reverse outline {#def-reverse-outline}
A **reverse outline** is an outline made *from* a draft rather than before it. For each paragraph, the writer writes in the margin, in a few words, (1) what the paragraph **says** — its point — and (2) what it **does** — its job in the argument (gives a reason, gives an example, answers an objection, concludes). The list of points is then read on its own, as the skeleton of the essay.
:::

::: theorem Using a reverse outline {#rule-reverse-outline}
Read the list of paragraph points and ask:

1. **Does each paragraph have one point?** If you need two lines to say what it says, it may need to be split.
2. **Is any point repeated?** Two paragraphs with the same point should be merged, or one of them cut.
3. **Does every point support or test the thesis?** A point that does neither is off the question, however interesting.
4. **Is anything missing** that the thesis promised, or that a reader would expect — a reason, an objection, a definition?
5. **Is the order the best one?** Would the argument be clearer if two paragraphs changed places?
:::

::: proof
A draft hides its structure under its sentences; a reverse outline uncovers it. Writing what each paragraph *says* forces the writer to state its point, which is difficult if the paragraph has none. Writing what it *does* reveals the shape of the argument: three paragraphs that all *give a reason* and none that *answers an objection* is a visible gap. The technique is recommended in many university writing guides, and it is the reverse of the planning outline of [[essay-writing/writing-process#def-outline]]: the planning outline says what the essay should do; the reverse outline says what it does.
:::

::: example A reverse outline {#ex-reverse-outline}
Zhang Yu's draft has six paragraphs. Her reverse outline reads:

1. *Says*: cities are crowded; parking takes space; thesis — replace some parking with parks and seating. *Does*: introduction, thesis.
2. *Says*: parks make people healthier — walking, fresh air. *Does*: reason.
3. *Says*: seating lets older people rest and stay longer in the centre. *Does*: reason.
4. *Says*: green space is good for health, and trees clean the air. *Does*: reason.
5. *Says*: my town's new square is popular with families. *Does*: example.
6. *Says*: shops need customers who drive, so the city should keep parking. *Does*: conclusion.

Use the questions of [[#rule-reverse-outline]] to plan her revision.
::: solution
1. **One point each?** Yes, mostly. Paragraph 1 combines background and thesis, as an introduction should.
2. **Repetition**: paragraphs 2 and 4 both argue that green space improves health. Merge them into one stronger paragraph, keeping the point about air quality as part of it.
3. **Support for the thesis**: paragraph 5 is an example, not a point. It belongs inside a reason paragraph — probably the one about people staying longer in the centre (paragraph 3).
4. **Missing**: the counter-argument promised in the introduction. The objection about shops and drivers appears, but in the **conclusion**, where it reverses the thesis. It needs its own paragraph, answered ([[essay-writing/counter-arguments#rule-rebuttal]]): for example, keep short-stay spaces and loading bays, and point out that people who stay longer in a pleasant centre may spend more.
5. **Order and conclusion**: introduction → health (merged 2 + 4) → staying longer (3 + the example from 5) → the objection about shops and its answer → a new conclusion that restates the thesis.

The revised essay has five paragraphs instead of six and answers all three of the teacher's comments. Not one of these changes is an edit of language.
:::
:::

### Revising paragraphs

Once the structure is right, each paragraph can be checked against the shape of [[essay-writing/arguing-a-point#rule-paragraph-shape]]: a claim, an explanation, support, and a link back to the thesis.

::: theorem Revising a body paragraph {#rule-revise-paragraph}
For each body paragraph, check:

1. **The topic sentence** states the paragraph's point and shows how it relates to the thesis or the previous paragraph ([[essay-writing/signposting#rule-paragraph-links]]).
2. **The explanation** says why the point is true or how it works, not just that it is.
3. **The support** — an example, evidence, a reason — is specific, relevant and accurate. Mark any figure or fact you cannot source; remove it or find a source.
4. **Every sentence** is about the point. Move or cut sentences that are not.
5. **The last sentence** connects the point back to the question, if the connection is not already clear.
:::

::: example Revising a paragraph {#ex-revise-paragraph}
Revise this body paragraph from an essay arguing that libraries should lend tools. Do not edit the language yet: work only on its content and order.

*Libraries are very important in our society. Many people use libraries every week. Lending tools would reduce waste. Lots of things are thrown away. My neighbour bought a ladder and used it once. Libraries are also quiet places to study. If tools are shared, fewer are made.*
::: solution
1. **Find the point.** The paragraph's real claim is in sentence 3: *lending tools would reduce waste*. Sentences 1–2 are general statements about libraries; sentence 6 (quiet study) is off the point.
2. **Find the explanation.** Sentence 7 explains *how* lending reduces waste: shared tools mean fewer are made. It should come straight after the claim.
3. **Find the support.** Sentence 5 (the neighbour's ladder) is an anecdote. It can stay as an illustration, presented as such, if it shows the mechanism — a tool bought for one use.
4. **Cut** sentences 1, 2 and 6. Sentence 4 (*Lots of things are thrown away*) is too vague to support anything.
5. **Add a link** to the thesis.

Revised (content and order only; the language will be edited later):

*Lending tools would reduce waste. If a tool is shared, fewer need to be made, and fewer end up thrown away after a few uses. Many tools are bought for a single job — a ladder for one repair, a drill for one set of shelves — and then kept for years or discarded. A library that lends them turns one object into many uses. That is a strong reason for libraries, which already exist to share things, to share tools too.*
:::
:::

::: warning Revising only the surface
✗ Changing *good* to *beneficial*, adding *Moreover*, and correcting articles, when the teacher's comment was *This paragraph does not answer the question.*

Read every comment and ask whether it is about **what** the essay says or **how** it says it. A comment about content can only be answered by revision. Synonyms and connectives added to a paragraph without a point make it longer and more formal, but it still has no point ([[essay-writing/signposting#rule-connectives]]).
:::

## Editing for language

Editing begins when the structure is settled. The checklist of [[sentences-and-paragraphs/editing#rule-checklist]] — sentence boundaries, verb forms, agreement, articles, plurals, prepositions, word choice — applies to essays as to any text. Essays add four concerns of their own.

::: theorem Four editing passes for an essay {#rule-edit-passes}
1. **Register.** Check for features that are informal in an essay: contractions (*don't*), conversational words and phrasal verbs where a neutral equivalent exists (*a lot of*, *get*, *stuff*, *find out*), *you* for people in general, and exclamations. Keep the register consistent ([[collocation-and-register/register#rule-consistent]]).
2. **Concision.** Cut words that do no work: *It is a fact that*, *in today's modern world*, *the reason why … is because*, *in my personal opinion*, doubled words (*each and every*). Prefer a verb to a noun phrase built around a weak verb (*make a decision* → *decide*) where the verb is clearer ([[style-and-rhetoric/concision]]).
3. **Strength of claims.** Check each claim against its support: hedge claims the essay has not proved (*may*, *often*, *tends to*), and remove hedges from claims it has ([[modality/hedging-boosting#rule-what-to-hedge]]).
4. **Your own errors.** Search for the errors your error log shows you make most often ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/editing#rule-feedback]]) — for many writers, agreement, articles, plurals and comma splices — one type at a time.

Then **proofread** for spelling, punctuation, numbers and formatting, ideally on paper or after changing the font or size, so that the text looks unfamiliar.
:::

::: proof
These passes are separate because each needs a different kind of attention. Reading for register means listening to the tone of the text; reading for concision means asking of each phrase whether it earns its place; reading for the strength of claims means comparing what is said with what has been shown; reading for errors means slowing down to the level of the word. A reader who looks for all of them at once sees few of them. The search for personal errors is the most efficient pass for a second-language writer, because errors are not evenly spread: most writers make a small number of kinds of error repeatedly, and a targeted search finds them faster than a general reading.
:::

::: example Editing a paragraph {#ex-edit}
Edit this paragraph for register, concision and strength of claims. The content and order are already right.

*In today's modern world, it is a fact that lots of tools are bought for just one job and then they get thrown away. You don't need to own a drill that you'll use for ten minutes! Sharing tools definitely solves the problem of waste and will make our cities completely sustainable.*
::: solution
1. **Register**: *lots of* → *many*; *get thrown away* → *are thrown away*; *You don't need …* (second person, contraction, exclamation) → a statement about people in general.
2. **Concision**: *In today's modern world, it is a fact that* adds nothing → cut; *and then they* → *and then*.
3. **Strength of claims**: *definitely solves* and *completely sustainable* claim far more than one paragraph about tools can show → hedge and narrow.

> *Many tools are bought for a single job and then thrown away. Few households need to own a drill that will be used for ten minutes a year. Sharing tools would not solve the problem of waste, but it would reduce one part of it, at little cost.*

The edited paragraph is shorter, consistent in register, and claims only what it can support.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is best for a formal essay?
- [ ] *You can't expect people to pay for something they'll hardly ever use.*
- [ ] *It is a well-known fact that in today's society nobody wants to pay for tools.*
- [x] *Few people are willing to pay for something they will rarely use.*
- [ ] *Absolutely nobody would ever pay for a tool they don't need!*
::: solution
The third sentence is neutral in register, concise and modest in its claim. The first uses *you*, contractions and an informal tone; the second is wordy and overclaims (*nobody*); the fourth overclaims and ends with an exclamation.
:::
:::

## Feedback: giving and using it

::: definition Feedback and peer review {#def-feedback}
**Feedback** is a reader's response to a draft, given so that the writer can improve it: comments from a teacher, a tutor, a classmate or anyone else who reads the text. **Peer review** (or *peer response*, *peer feedback*) is feedback from other writers at the same stage — usually classmates — who read each other's drafts, often with a set of guiding questions.
:::

::: theorem Giving feedback on a draft {#rule-giving-feedback}
1. **Read the whole draft first** and respond as a reader: what is the thesis, as you understood it? What was the most convincing point? Where did you get lost?
2. **Comment on the large things first**: the answer to the question, the structure, the support. Leave grammar for later, unless errors make a passage hard to understand.
3. **Be specific**: point to a paragraph or a sentence. *Paragraph 3 seems to make the same point as paragraph 2* helps; *Good structure* does not.
4. **Describe the effect on you** rather than giving verdicts: *I wasn't sure here whether this was your view or the opposing view* is easier to use than *This is wrong*.
5. **Suggest, don't rewrite**: ask questions and offer options; the text belongs to the writer.
6. **Say what works**, specifically, so that the writer keeps it.
:::

::: proof
Writers need to know how their text reads to someone else, which is the one thing they cannot find out by reading it themselves. A peer reader may know less about grammar than a teacher, but can say exactly where they lost the thread or misunderstood the thesis, and that information is what revision needs. Describing effects rather than giving verdicts keeps the decision with the writer and avoids arguments about taste. Research on peer review suggests that reviewers learn from it as well: Lundstrom and Baker (2009) found that university students learning English who were trained to give peer feedback improved their own writing more over a semester than students who only received it.
:::

::: theorem Using feedback {#rule-using-feedback}
1. **Sort the comments** into revising (content, structure, support) and editing (language). Deal with the first group first.
2. **Look for patterns**: a comment that two readers make, or that a teacher makes on several essays, is the most important.
3. **Decide**: you are the author. Not every suggestion will improve your essay; if you disagree, be able to say why. But a comment that a reader was confused is a fact about the reader's experience, even if their suggested solution is not the right one.
4. **Ask** about any comment you do not understand.
5. **Correct marked errors yourself** and record their types in your error log ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/editing#rule-feedback]]).
:::

::: proof
The research on written feedback has been the subject of a long debate. Truscott (1996) argued that correcting the grammar of second-language writers was ineffective and could even be harmful; Ferris (1999) replied that the evidence did not support abandoning correction and that clear, selective feedback could help. Later research, reviewed by Bitchener and Ferris (2012), found evidence that focused written correction can improve accuracy, especially when it targets a limited number of error types, while also showing that what the writer does with the feedback matters as much as the feedback itself. For revision of content and structure, the main lesson is practical: comments that are specific, prioritised and acted on change drafts; comments that are read once and filed do not.
:::

::: example Responding to a peer's draft {#ex-peer}
A classmate's essay answers *Should secondary schools start later in the morning?* Her introduction ends: *There are advantages and disadvantages to starting school later.* Her three body paragraphs argue for a later start; her conclusion says that *schools should start at half past nine.* There are a dozen article errors. Write four comments that follow [[#rule-giving-feedback]].
::: solution
Answers will vary. Model comments:

1. **As a reader**: *I understood from your conclusion that you think schools should start later — at 9.30 — and your three paragraphs all argue for that. Your paragraph on sleep, with the explanation of teenagers' body clocks, convinced me most.*
2. **The large problem**: *Your introduction doesn't tell me your view yet — it says there are advantages and disadvantages. Could the last sentence say what your conclusion says, so that I know from the start where you stand?*
3. **A gap**: *I kept thinking about parents who start work early. Would a paragraph on that objection, and your answer to it, make the essay stronger?*
4. **Editing, kept for last**: *There are some article errors (I've underlined a few in paragraph 2) — maybe one to check when you edit.*

The comments begin with what works, name the largest problem first, suggest rather than rewrite, and leave grammar until last.
:::
:::

::: warning Accepting every suggestion — or none
Two opposite habits waste feedback. Some writers change everything a reader suggests, including suggestions that contradict each other or that come from a misreading; the result can be a patchwork. Others defend every sentence and change nothing. Treat each comment as information about how the text was read, decide what the problem is, and choose the solution yourself.
:::

### Software and automated tools

As of 2026, many writers use spelling and grammar checkers, translation tools and AI writing assistants. Spelling and grammar checkers catch many surface errors and miss others: they can flag a wrong verb form but not a word that is correctly spelled and wrong in context (*their* for *there*, *form* for *from*), and some of their suggestions are themselves wrong ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/editing]]). They say nothing useful about whether the essay answers the question. Tools that rewrite or generate whole sentences raise a different question, which is not about language at all: whether the work is still yours. Schools, universities and examinations set their own rules on what help is allowed, and these rules change; find out what they are before you use any tool, and never hand in as your own text that you did not write ([[academic-writing/academic-integrity]]). Even where a tool is permitted, the revision questions of this lesson — is the thesis clear, does each paragraph have a point, does the conclusion follow — have to be answered by the writer.

::: note English and Chinese
- **修改 and 润色.** Chinese 修改 covers both revising and editing, and 润色 ‘polishing’ suggests the language; it is worth keeping the English distinction, because a draft that needs revision cannot be fixed by polishing.
- **Teacher feedback and peer feedback.** Students who have been taught mainly through teacher correction may feel, at first, that a classmate's comments are less reliable than a teacher's, or may be reluctant to criticise a classmate's work. This is not peculiar to any culture, and it changes with practice. In a small study of Chinese students in an American peer-response class, Carson and Nelson (1996) found that concern for group harmony shaped what the students were willing to say; in Taiwan, Min (2006) found that after training in how to give feedback, students' comments became more useful and their partners incorporated more of them in revision. The guidelines of [[#rule-giving-feedback]] — describe effects, be specific, suggest — make critical comments easier to give and to receive.
- **Model essays (范文).** Learning from model essays is a long tradition in Chinese writing education and a useful one: a model shows what a finished text looks like. In revision, however, the question is not *How close is my essay to the model?* but *Does my essay answer this question?* A draft revised towards a memorised model often loses its own argument.
- **Translation in editing.** Writers who draft partly in Chinese, or check a sentence by translating it, should edit the result as English: translated sentences often keep Chinese word order, double connectives (虽然……但是) and full-width punctuation (，。). Search for these specifically.
:::

::: history
Revision is as old as writing, and the drafts of famous authors show how far it can go. William Wordsworth worked on his autobiographical poem *The Prelude* for most of his life: a two-part version of 1799, a thirteen-book version of 1805, and a fourteen-book version published after his death in 1850, which differ in many passages and in their structure. Systematic research on how ordinary writers revise began much later. Nancy Sommers (1980) compared student and experienced writers, and Lester Faigley and Stephen Witte (1981) proposed their distinction between surface and meaning changes. The use of peers as readers grew in the same years: Peter Elbow's *Writing Without Teachers* (1973) described groups of writers responding to each other's drafts without a teacher, and Kenneth Bruffee's work on collaborative learning in the 1970s and 1980s helped to make peer response a normal part of writing classes in the United States, from where it spread to second-language teaching.
:::

- Revising changes what an essay says and how it is organised; editing changes how it says it; proofreading is the final search for slips ([[#def-revising-editing]]).
- Revise before you edit, from the largest unit down, after reading the whole draft without correcting ([[#rule-revise-first]]).
- Check the whole essay with the question test and the thesis test ([[#rule-two-tests]]).
- A reverse outline — what each paragraph says and does — reveals repetition, gaps, off-topic paragraphs and a better order ([[#def-reverse-outline]], [[#rule-reverse-outline]]).
- Revise each paragraph for its topic sentence, explanation, support, unity and link to the thesis ([[#rule-revise-paragraph]]).
- Edit an essay in separate passes for register, concision, the strength of claims and your own frequent errors, then proofread ([[#rule-edit-passes]]).
- Give feedback as a reader: large things first, specific, describing effects, suggesting rather than rewriting ([[#rule-giving-feedback]]); use feedback by sorting, finding patterns and deciding as the author ([[#rule-using-feedback]]).
- Grammar checkers and other tools catch some surface errors but cannot revise an argument; follow the rules on which tools are allowed.

## Exercises

::: exercise Revising or editing? {level=1}
Is each change revising or editing? (a) Deleting a paragraph that does not answer the question. (b) Changing *don't* to *do not*. (c) Adding a paragraph that answers an objection. (d) Replacing *make an improvement* with *improve*. (e) Rewriting the thesis so that it matches the conclusion. (f) Correcting *informations* to *information*.
::: solution
Revising: (a), (c), (e). Editing: (b), (d), (f).
:::
:::

::: exercise Says and does {level=1}
For each paragraph summary, say what the paragraph *does*: gives a reason, gives an example, presents an objection, answers an objection, introduces, or concludes. (a) *Critics argue that free museums lose income.* (b) *Free entry brings in families who could not afford tickets.* (c) *A museum in the writer's own town became much busier after it stopped charging.* (d) *Other income — shops, donations, paid exhibitions — can replace ticket sales.* (e) *Free entry is a condition of a museum doing its public job.*
::: solution
(a) presents an objection. (b) gives a reason. (c) gives an example. (d) answers an objection. (e) concludes (or, if it comes first, introduces with the thesis).
:::
:::

::: exercise Register and concision {level=1}
Edit each sentence for a formal essay. (a) *Lots of people reckon that cycling is way better than driving.* (b) *The reason why this is important is because it saves money.* (c) *In my personal opinion, I think that schools should do something about it.* (d) *You can't really blame them for not wanting to pay.*
::: solution
Possible answers: (a) *Many people believe that cycling is much better than driving.* (b) *This is important because it saves money.* (c) *Schools should act on this.* (or *In my view, schools should …*) (d) *Their unwillingness to pay is understandable.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Write a reverse outline {level=2}
Write a reverse outline (says / does) of this short essay plan, and name two revision problems. *(1) Introduction: online shopping is growing; thesis — it is better for most people than shopping in person. (2) Online shopping saves time. (3) Online prices are often lower. (4) Shopping in person lets you see and touch goods before buying. (5) Online shopping saves money because prices are lower. (6) Conclusion: both kinds of shopping have advantages.*
::: solution
Reverse outline: (1) says online is better / introduces and states thesis; (2) says saves time / reason; (3) says lower prices / reason; (4) says in-person lets you see goods / objection, but unanswered; (5) says saves money / reason — the same as (3); (6) says both have advantages / concludes, but retreats from the thesis.

Problems: paragraphs 3 and 5 make the same point — merge them; paragraph 4 is an objection with no rebuttal — answer it (for example, return policies, reviews); the conclusion does not match the thesis — rewrite it to restate the thesis. Any two of these.
:::
:::

::: exercise Hedge or boost? {level=2}
Each claim comes from an essay that has given one or two examples but no systematic evidence. Rewrite each so that its strength matches the support. (a) *Remote working always makes employees happier.* (b) *Free buses will certainly end traffic congestion.* (c) *Street trees reduce summer temperatures.* (The body cited a measured study.)
::: solution
Possible answers: (a) *Remote working often makes employees happier*, or *can make many employees happier*. (b) *Free buses may reduce congestion slightly, but are unlikely to end it.* (c) The body cited evidence, so the claim can stand, perhaps with its scope: *Street trees reduce summer temperatures in the streets they shade.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Sort the feedback {level=2}
A student receives these comments. Sort them into revising and editing, and put the revising comments in order of importance. (a) *Your conclusion doesn't answer the question.* (b) *Check your use of "the" in paragraph 2.* (c) *Paragraph 3 needs an example.* (d) *Contractions are too informal for an essay.* (e) *I can't tell whether paragraph 4 is your view or the opposing view.* (f) *Spelling: "enviroment".*
::: solution
Revising: (a), (e), (c). Editing: (b), (d), (f).

Order: (a) first — an essay that does not answer the question fails at the top level; (e) next — a confusion about the position affects how the whole argument is read, and is solved by signposting the counter-argument ([[essay-writing/counter-arguments#rule-placing]]); (c) last — adding support within a paragraph.
:::
:::

::: exercise Revise and edit a paragraph {level=3}
This paragraph comes from an essay arguing that secondary schools should teach cooking. Revise it (content and order) and then edit it (language), and show both stages.

*Nowadays, with the development of society, cooking is a very important skill. Lots of young people can't cook. Fast food is popular. My cousin only eats instant noodles. If people can cook, they can eat more healthily, because they control what goes into their food. Cooking is also fun. Therefore, schools should definitely teach cooking to every single student.*
::: hint
What is the paragraph's real point? Which sentences explain it, which illustrate it, and which are off the point or empty?
:::
::: solution
**Revision.** The point is health: people who can cook control what goes into their food. The empty opening (*Nowadays, with the development of society*) goes; *Cooking is also fun* is a different point and goes (or moves to another paragraph); *Fast food is popular* and the cousin's noodles can become a brief illustration of what happens when young people cannot cook; the last sentence links to the thesis.

**Edited version:**

> *Teaching cooking at school would help young people to eat more healthily. A person who can cook controls what goes into a meal — how much salt, sugar and fat, and how many vegetables — while a person who cannot depends on ready-made food, whose contents someone else has chosen. Many young people leave home without knowing how to prepare a simple meal, and fall back on instant noodles and takeaways. A short practical course at secondary school would give every student the skill to choose otherwise, which is why cooking deserves a place in the curriculum.*

Check: one point; an explanation of the mechanism; an illustration in general terms (the cousin is replaced by a general pattern, which would need a source if stated as a fact); no informal language (*lots of*, *can't*); the overclaim (*definitely … every single student*) replaced by a recommendation.
:::
:::

::: exercise Give feedback {level=3}
Here is a short essay (about 180 words) written for this exercise. Write four comments on it, following [[#rule-giving-feedback]], and then say which comment the writer should act on first.

*Should cities ban fireworks?*

*Fireworks are very popular all over the world. They are used at many festivals. There are good and bad points about fireworks.*

*Firstly, fireworks are dangerous. Every year, people are injured, especially children. Moreover, they frighten pets and wild animals.*

*Secondly, fireworks cause noise and air pollution. Furthermore, the smoke can make the air unhealthy for hours.*

*On the other hand, fireworks are an important part of many traditions and celebrations. People enjoy watching them, and public displays bring communities together.*

*In conclusion, fireworks have advantages and disadvantages. Cities should think carefully about this issue.*
::: solution
Answers will vary. Model comments:

1. *Your second and third paragraphs gave me clear reasons — safety, and noise and pollution — and the point about pets was one I hadn't thought of.*
2. *I couldn't tell what your answer to the question was: the introduction and conclusion both say there are good and bad points. What do you think — ban all fireworks, ban private ones, or allow them? If your thesis said that, I'd read the body knowing where it was going.*
3. *Your fourth paragraph gives a strong argument against a ban, but nothing answers it. If you think cities should ban fireworks, how would you respond to people who value the traditions? (Could public displays be allowed while private fireworks are banned?)*
4. *The claim about injuries every year would be stronger with a source, or with "can be injured" if you don't have one.*

The writer should act on comment 2 first: without a position, the essay does not answer the question, and the position decides how paragraph 4 should be answered and what the conclusion should say.
:::
