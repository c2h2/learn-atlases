The question is the one from [[essay-writing/thesis-introduction]]: *Should cities make public transport free for all passengers?* The writer's thesis was that free transport is a poor use of a city's money, because drivers care more about speed and convenience than about the fare, and the body of the essay argued this in three paragraphs and answered one objection. Here are three possible last paragraphs.

> **A.** *In conclusion, free public transport is a poor use of a city's money: the people it most needs to attract — drivers — care more about speed and convenience than about the fare, so the same money would cut more car journeys if it were spent on faster and more frequent services.*

> **B.** *In conclusion, there are both advantages and disadvantages to free public transport. Another problem is that free buses may attract people who would otherwise have walked or cycled, which is bad for their health. Every city must decide for itself, and we should all try to use our cars less in order to protect the planet for future generations.*

> **C.** *Making buses and trains free would help the passengers who already use them, and for some of them, on low incomes, that help would matter. But a city that wants fewer cars on its roads needs to win over people who now drive, and for them the price of a ticket is rarely what decides. The money that free fares would cost is therefore better spent on what does decide — services that come often, run late and reach where people actually need to go. Free travel for those who cannot afford a ticket can be part of that policy; free travel for everyone should not be its centre.*

A is the thesis again, word for word; the reader gains nothing from it. B does three things that conclusions should not: it retreats from the thesis to *advantages and disadvantages*, adds a new argument (walking and cycling) that the body never discussed, and ends with a call to save the planet that has little to do with fares. C is a conclusion. It gives the same answer as the thesis in new words, it pulls the reasons of the body together into a single line of thought, it admits what the opposing side got right, and it ends by saying what follows — what the city should actually do.

This lesson is about the last paragraph of an essay: what it is for, how to restate a thesis without repeating it, how to end, and what to leave out. A conclusion can only draw together what the essay has already done, so it builds on [[essay-writing/thesis-introduction]] and [[essay-writing/counter-arguments]].

## What a conclusion does

::: definition Conclusion {#def-conclusion}
The **conclusion** of an essay is its final paragraph (or, in a long essay, its final section). It gives the essay's answer to the question in its final form, shows how the main points of the body support that answer, and says why the answer matters or what follows from it. It usually makes some or all of these moves:

1. a **restatement** of the thesis, in new words and in the light of the body;
2. a **synthesis** of the main points: how they fit together, not a list of them;
3. where relevant, a **qualification**: the limits of the answer, or what the opposing view got right;
4. a **closing move**: an implication, a recommendation, a consequence or a question that the answer opens up.
:::

The introduction moves from the general to the specific: from the topic to the thesis ([[essay-writing/thesis-introduction#def-intro]]). The conclusion usually moves the other way, from the specific answer back out to its wider meaning. Textbooks often draw the two as a funnel and an upside-down funnel; the image is useful as long as the conclusion widens the view without returning to the history of the world.

::: theorem The conclusion answers the question {#rule-conclusion-answer}
1. The conclusion must give a **clear answer** to the question that was asked — the same answer as the thesis, though it may now be more precise.
2. It must **follow from the body**: every claim in it should be supported by something the essay has already argued.
3. It must **not add new arguments or new evidence**. If a point is important enough to include, it belongs in a body paragraph.
4. It should be **short**: in most essays, about as long as the introduction — roughly one tenth of the total.
:::

::: proof
The conclusion is the last thing the reader reads, and often the part a marker rereads when deciding a grade. If it gives no clear answer, the reader may conclude that the essay had none. If it gives a different answer from the thesis, the reader cannot tell which is the writer's real position, and the body, which was written to support the thesis, now supports something else. New arguments arrive without explanation or evidence, at a point where the writer cannot develop them, and suggest that the body was incomplete. The length is a convention, not a law.
:::

The conclusion gives the answer in its final form. A new statistic and a new reason are new support, and belong in the body; an unanswered opposing view at the end leaves the reader with doubt ([[essay-writing/counter-arguments#rule-placing]]).

## Restating without repeating

Most conclusions begin by stating the answer again. This is not redundancy: the reader now knows the reasons. But the restatement should show that the writer has been somewhere since the introduction.

::: theorem Restate the thesis in new words {#rule-restate}
1. **Do not copy** the thesis sentence. Express the same position in different words and, usually, a different sentence structure.
2. **Use what the body has established.** The restatement can refer to the reasons as known (*because drivers rarely choose by price*), not as promises.
3. **Make it more precise if the body has shown how.** If a counter-argument led you to limit your position, the restatement should include the limit.
4. **Keep the same answer.** A restatement that is stronger, weaker or different from the thesis without explanation looks like a change of mind.
:::

::: proof
Repeating a sentence word for word tells the reader nothing new and invites them to skip; a paraphrase forces the writer to think about what the essay has actually shown, which is often slightly different from what the introduction promised. If the body has shown a narrower claim than the thesis, either revise the thesis to match ([[essay-writing/writing-process#rule-revise]]) or state the narrower claim here. Paraphrasing a thesis uses the same techniques as paraphrasing a source — changing the structure as well as the words, and keeping key terms where no good synonym exists ([[academic-reading/paraphrase-and-quotation]]).
:::

::: example Restating a thesis {#ex-restate}
The thesis of an essay is: *Universities should record most lectures, because recordings help students learn and widen access, but recording should exclude discussion and protect students' privacy.* Write a restatement for its conclusion, and say what you changed.
::: solution
Identify the parts of the thesis — the position (*record most lectures*), two reasons (*learning*, *access*) and a limit (*discussion*, *privacy*) — then change the structure, describe the reasons as the body did, and keep the same answer:

> *A recording lets a student hear a difficult explanation a second time, and lets a student who could not be there hear it at all. Those benefits are large enough to justify recording most lectures as a matter of course, provided that seminars and discussions are left unrecorded and that students can ask questions without being filmed.*

Changes: new sentence structure (two sentences, reasons first); reasons described concretely, as the body did; the limit kept and made specific (*seminars*, *ask questions without being filmed*). The answer is the same.
:::
:::

::: warning The thesis in disguise
Changing a few words is not restating:

✗ Thesis: *Schools should start later because teenagers need more sleep.* Conclusion: *To conclude, schools ought to begin later since adolescents require more sleep.*

This is the thesis with synonyms, and a reader recognises it at once. Restate by adding what the body has shown — the mechanism, the evidence, the limit: ✓ *Teenagers do not choose to stay up late; their body clocks shift during adolescence, and an early start takes away sleep they cannot recover at weekends. A later start for older students is the simplest way to give it back.*
:::

## Synthesis, not summary

::: definition Summary and synthesis {#def-synthesis}
A **summary** in a conclusion repeats the main points of the body, usually in the order they appeared: *Firstly …, secondly …, finally …*. A **synthesis** shows how the points are related — which matters most, how one depends on another, what they show together that none shows alone — and connects them to the answer.
:::

A brief summary can help the reader of a long essay or report; in a short essay, whose body the reader has just read, a list of its topic sentences is the least useful thing a conclusion can offer.

::: theorem Draw the reasons together {#rule-synthesis}
To turn a summary into a synthesis, ask one or more of these questions about the body's points and answer it in the conclusion:

1. **Which point matters most**, and why? (*The decisive reason is …*)
2. **How are the points connected?** Does one cause another, or are two of them aspects of one problem? (*Both problems come from the same source: …*)
3. **What do the points show together** that none shows alone? (*Taken together, these suggest that …*)
4. **How does the counter-argument change the picture?** (*The objection about cost does not reverse this conclusion, but it does mean that …*)
:::

::: proof
Bereiter and Scardamalia's distinction between knowledge telling and knowledge transforming ([[essay-writing/writing-process]]) applies to conclusions with special force. A summary conclusion tells the reader again what the writer knows, in the order it was written. A synthesis requires the writer to think about the argument as a whole — the thinking an essay question is designed to test, and which a marker can see in the conclusion more clearly than anywhere else. One of the four questions is usually enough.
:::

::: example From summary to synthesis {#ex-synthesis}
Rewrite this summary conclusion as a synthesis. The essay answered *Is it better to study alone or in a group?* and its thesis was that the answer depends on what is being learned.

*In conclusion, studying alone and studying in groups both have advantages. Studying alone is good for concentration. It is also good for memorising. Studying in a group is good for discussing difficult ideas. It is also more motivating. So both are good.*
::: solution
The paragraph lists four points and ends with *both are good*, weaker than the thesis. Ask how the points connect: concentration and memorising are the needs of practice, discussion the need of understanding — the thesis's own distinction. Motivation is secondary.

> *Whether to study alone or with others is less a matter of personal preference than of the task. Practice and memorisation need the long, uninterrupted concentration that only solitary study gives, while difficult ideas are often understood only when they have to be explained to someone else, or defended against their questions. A student who studies alone when she needs explanation, or in a group when she needs repetition, is choosing the wrong tool. The best plan for most courses is therefore a sequence: understand a topic with others, then fix it alone.*

The new conclusion restates the thesis (*a matter … of the task*), sorts the points into two kinds rather than listing them, and ends with a recommendation that follows from the distinction.
:::
:::

## Ending the essay

The final sentence is often the hardest, because the writer feels that something memorable is required. What is required is something that follows.

::: theorem Choose a closing move that follows from the argument {#rule-closing}
Close with one of these, chosen to fit the question and the essay:

1. **An implication**: what the answer means for a wider question. *If drivers do not choose by price, cities that want fewer cars must compete on speed.*
2. **A recommendation**: what should be done, when the question asks *should*. *Schools should move the start of the day for older students first.*
3. **A qualification**: the conditions under which the answer holds, if they are important. *This applies to cities with existing networks; a town without buses faces a different problem.*
4. **A return to the opening**: the situation or example of the introduction, seen again in the light of the argument. *The student who spends longer travelling than listening needs a faster bus more than a free one.*
5. **A question for further thought**: a genuine question that the essay has opened but could not answer. Use this sparingly, and only if the essay's own question has been answered first.

Whatever the closing move, it must not introduce a new argument for the thesis, and it must be no larger than the essay can support.
:::

::: proof
Each of these moves extends the answer without adding new support for it. An implication and a recommendation say what follows from an answer that has already been established; a qualification marks its limits; a return to the opening frames the essay as a whole, the device Chinese writing teachers call 首尾呼应 ‘beginning and end echoing each other’ (see the note below). The rule against new arguments does not forbid new *thoughts*: an implication is new, but it is a consequence of the argument, which the reader can accept from what they have read, not support that would need new evidence. The size limit is the one most often broken: an essay about bus fares can end with how cities should spend their transport budgets, but not with the future of the planet.
:::

::: example Which closing move? {#ex-closing}
For each essay, suggest a suitable closing move and write the final sentence. (a) *To what extent do food delivery apps change eating habits in cities?* (thesis: they change where and when people eat far more than what they eat). (b) *Should museums be free to enter?* (thesis: national museums should be free, with charges for special exhibitions).
::: solution
1. **(a) An implication.** A question of degree calls for an implication, not a recommendation: *If the apps have changed the setting of meals more than the meals themselves, their main effect may be on restaurants and on the evening routines of households, not on diet — a reason to look for their consequences in those places first.*
2. **(b) A recommendation**, since the question asks *should*: *Keeping the permanent collections free and charging for special exhibitions would protect both the museums' income and the principle that a public collection belongs to everyone.*
Each final sentence follows from the thesis and needs no new proof.
:::
:::

::: warning Endings to avoid
- **A new argument or new evidence.** ✗ *Another advantage of free entry is that it would help tourism.* — if it matters, move it to the body.
- **Retreating from the thesis.** ✗ *In conclusion, there are advantages and disadvantages, and it is hard to say which is better.* The essay was written to say which is better.
- **The apology.** ✗ *Of course, this is only my opinion, and others may disagree.* Every essay is the writer's view; readers know that others disagree, and the counter-argument paragraph has already dealt with them.
- **The sweeping call to action.** ✗ *We must all work together to build a better world for our children.* A call to action belongs in a speech or a campaign leaflet; in an essay it must be specific and follow from the argument.
- **The decorative quotation.** ✗ *As the saying goes, "Where there is a will, there is a way."* A quotation at the end, like one at the start, is decoration unless it says something the essay needs.
- **The cliffhanger.** ✗ *Only time will tell.* It is a way of not answering.
:::

## Conclusions for different types of essay

The general moves are the same for every essay, but the answer they restate differs with the type of question ([[essay-writing/essay-types]]).

| type of essay | the conclusion gives … | a typical closing move |
|---|---|---|
| argumentative (*Should …?*, *Do you agree?*) | the position and the decisive reason | a recommendation |
| discursive (*Discuss both views*) | a judgement between the views, with the reason one is stronger | a qualification: when the other view has force |
| question of degree (*To what extent …?*) | the extent: *largely*, *only partly*, *in one respect but not another* | an implication |
| compare and contrast | a verdict: which is better, for what, or what the comparison shows | a practical judgement |
| cause and effect | which causes or effects matter most, and how they are connected | an implication for what could change them |
| problem–solution | which solution is best and why, and what it depends on | the first step, or the condition for success |

::: theorem The conclusion of a discursive essay must judge {#rule-discursive-conclusion}
When an essay sets out two views, its conclusion must say which is stronger — or in what respects each is right — and **why**. Choosing a side without a reason (*In my opinion, the second view is better*) or declaring the views equal (*Both sides have a point*) is not a conclusion. Phrases that introduce a reasoned judgement include *On balance, …*, *Weighing these arguments, …*, *The stronger case is …, because …*, *While X is right that …, Y's argument is more convincing on …*.
:::

::: proof
A discursive question asks for two things, and the second is the judgement. If the judgement has no reason, the body looks like two lists placed side by side ([[essay-writing/essay-types#rule-discursive-form]]). *On balance* claims that a weighing has taken place, so it must be followed by the result of the weighing.
:::

::: example A discursive conclusion {#ex-discursive-conclusion}
An essay discusses the question *Some people think that children should start learning a foreign language at primary school; others think secondary school is early enough. Discuss both views and give your opinion.* The body argued that (1) young children learn pronunciation more easily and are less anxious about speaking, but (2) older children learn grammar and vocabulary faster in the classroom, and (3) early teaching helps only if lessons are frequent and teachers are well trained. Write the conclusion.
::: solution
> *On balance, the case for starting at primary school is the stronger one, but only under conditions that many schools do not meet. Young children's ease with pronunciation and their willingness to speak are real advantages, yet with a single short lesson a week they are easily outweighed by the faster progress older learners make once lessons begin. Where an early start means frequent lessons with teachers who speak the language well, it gives children a foundation that secondary school can build on; where it does not, those who argue for waiting have the better argument. The decision, then, should depend less on children's age than on what a school can actually provide.*

It judges, gives the reason, says when the other view is right, and ends with an implication.
:::
:::

## The language of conclusions

| function | language |
|---|---|
| marking the conclusion | *In conclusion, …*, *To conclude, …*, *To sum up, …* (more informal), *Overall, …*, *In short, …* — or no marker at all |
| restating the answer | *It is clear, then, that …*, *This essay has argued that …*, *The evidence considered here suggests that …*, *X is, therefore, …* |
| synthesising | *Taken together, …*, *What these … have in common is …*, *The decisive factor is …*, *Above all, …*, *Both … and … point to …* |
| weighing | *On balance, …*, *Weighing these arguments, …*, *The stronger case is …*, *While …, …* |
| qualifying | *This is not to say that …*, *At least in …*, *This applies mainly to …*, *Where …, however, …* |
| implication and recommendation | *It follows that …*, *This suggests that …*, *If this is right, …*, *… should therefore …*, *The first step would be …* |

::: theorem Signalling the conclusion {#rule-signal-conclusion}
1. *In conclusion* is correct and widely used to begin the last paragraph of an essay. Use it **once**, at the start of the conclusion, and not for the end of a paragraph.
2. A marker is often **unnecessary**: a paragraph that restates the answer in its first sentence is recognisably a conclusion. Many experienced writers leave the marker out or use *then* or *therefore* inside the first sentence (*It is clear, then, that …*).
3. *To sum up* and *All in all* are slightly less formal than *In conclusion*; *In a word* introduces a one-word summary and does not introduce a paragraph; *At last* means ‘after a long wait’ and is never a marker of conclusion ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/coherence]]).
4. *This essay has argued / shown / discussed …* is common in academic writing; prefer *argued* or *shown* to *discussed*, which describes the essay's activity rather than its answer.
:::

::: proof
A marker tells the reader where they are. In a short essay the reader knows that the last paragraph is the conclusion, so *In conclusion* tells them little; in a long essay a marker helps more. Teachers who discourage *In conclusion* usually object less to the phrase than to what tends to follow it — a repetition of the introduction. Overusing markers across a whole essay is the subject of [[essay-writing/signposting]].
:::

::: quiz
Which opening is best for the final paragraph of a formal essay?
- [ ] *At last, I want to say that free museums are a good idea.*
- [ ] *In a word, museums should be free because they help people, they are public and the cost can be covered.*
- [x] *Free entry, then, is not a gift to visitors but a condition of a museum doing its public job.*
- [ ] *All in all, there are good and bad sides to free museums.*
::: solution
The third sentence restates the thesis in new words, with *then* signalling that it follows from the body. The first misuses *At last* and announces rather than answers; the second misuses *In a word* for a list; the fourth is less formal and retreats from the thesis.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **首尾呼应.** Chinese composition teaching often recommends that the end of an essay echo its beginning (首尾呼应), returning to an image, a phrase or a question from the opening. This transfers well to English: a return to the opening situation is one of the closing moves of [[#rule-closing]]. The echo should frame the answer, not replace it.
- **升华 and the moral at the end.** Many Chinese school essays end by raising the theme to a higher level (升华主题) — from a personal experience to a lesson about life, or from a local problem to the good of society. English argumentative essays can widen the view at the end, but by stating an implication that follows from the argument, not a moral that could end any essay. *We should all cherish our time* or *Only in this way can our society develop better* will strike most English readers as general; a specific implication will not.
- **总之 and its translations.** 总之 and 总而言之 are often translated *In a word* or *In short*, and 最后 as *At last*. *In a word* introduces a summary of one or two words; *At last* means ‘after a long wait’. For the conclusion of an essay, *In conclusion*, *To conclude*, *Overall* or no marker at all are the usual choices.
:::

::: history
Ancient rhetoric gave the ending of a speech a name and a job. Aristotle's *Rhetoric* (fourth century BC, book III) said that the epilogue had four tasks: to make the audience well disposed to the speaker and ill disposed to the opponent, to magnify or play down the main facts, to rouse the right emotions, and to refresh the audience's memory of what had been said. The Latin handbooks called the ending the *peroratio* or *conclusio*, and the anonymous *Rhetorica ad Herennium* (first century BC) divided it into three parts: a summing-up (*enumeratio*), amplification, and an appeal to the audience's pity. The modern essay keeps the summing-up, as restatement and synthesis, and amplification in a calmer form, as the implication that shows why the answer matters; an appeal to emotion, at home in a law court or an assembly, is usually out of place before a reader who is judging the reasoning.
:::

## Where this leads

The conclusion is the last part of an essay to be revised, and checking that it answers the question and matches the thesis is part of [[essay-writing/revising-editing]]; writing a conclusion in the last minutes of an examination is covered in [[essay-writing/timed-essays]]. The markers that signal a conclusion, and why one is often enough, belong to [[essay-writing/signposting]]. Calibrating the strength of the final claim is taught in [[modality/hedging-boosting]]. In research writing, the conclusion becomes a discussion section that interprets results, states limitations and suggests further research ([[academic-writing/discussion-conclusions]]); summarising at the end of a talk is covered in [[presentations-and-discussion/signposting-a-talk]].

::: summary
- A conclusion gives the essay's answer in its final form, draws the body's reasons together, may state the limits of the answer, and ends with what follows ([[#def-conclusion]]).
- It must answer the question, follow from the body, add no new arguments or evidence, and be about as long as the introduction ([[#rule-conclusion-answer]]).
- Restate the thesis in new words and structure, using what the body has established; keep the same answer, made more precise if the body showed how ([[#rule-restate]]).
- Prefer synthesis to summary: say which point matters most, how the points connect, or what they show together ([[#def-synthesis]], [[#rule-synthesis]]).
- Close with an implication, a recommendation, a qualification, a return to the opening or a genuine question — never with an apology, a retreat, a decorative quotation or a call to save the world ([[#rule-closing]]).
- A discursive conclusion must judge between the views and say why ([[#rule-discursive-conclusion]]).
- *In conclusion* is correct once, at the start of the last paragraph, and is often unnecessary; *In a word* and *At last* do not mark a conclusion ([[#rule-signal-conclusion]]).
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Keep or cut? {level=1}
Which of these sentences belong in a conclusion to an essay arguing that cities should plant more street trees? Mark each *keep* or *cut* and say why. (a) *Street trees, then, repay their cost many times over in cooler streets and cleaner air.* (b) *Another benefit is that trees provide homes for birds.* (c) *Of course, this is only my personal opinion.* (d) *The decisive argument is the summer heat: in a warming climate, shade is no longer a luxury.* (e) *Only time will tell.*
::: solution
(a) *keep* — a restatement of the thesis that refers to the reasons established in the body. (b) *cut* — a new argument; move it to the body if it matters. (c) *cut* — an apology that weakens the essay. (d) *keep* — synthesis: it says which reason matters most. (e) *cut* — a cliffhanger that avoids answering.
:::
:::

::: exercise Correct the marker {level=1}
Correct or improve the marker at the start of each final paragraph. (a) *At last, schools should teach cooking.* (b) *In a word, online shopping saves time, saves money and offers more choice, so it is better than shopping in person.* (c) *In conclusion, in conclusion, the plan should go ahead.* (d) *Last but not least, I think the government should act.*
::: solution
(a) *At last* means ‘after a long wait’: *In conclusion, schools should teach cooking* (or no marker: *Schools, then, should teach cooking*). (b) *In a word* introduces a one-word summary, not a list: *Overall, …* or *In short, …*. (c) The repetition is a slip: *In conclusion, the plan should go ahead.* (d) *Last but not least* introduces the final item of a list, usually in a body paragraph or a speech, not a conclusion: *In conclusion, the government should act …*, followed by the specific action.
:::
:::

::: exercise Name the closing move {level=1}
Which closing move — implication, recommendation, qualification, return to the opening, or question — does each final sentence use? (a) *If the apps have changed where people eat more than what they eat, nutrition campaigns aimed at them may be looking in the wrong place.* (b) *Councils should therefore begin with the streets around schools.* (c) *This holds for large cities; in villages, where the nearest library may be an hour away, a mobile service raises different questions.* (d) *The commuter waiting at the stop in the opening paragraph does not need a free ticket; she needs a bus that comes.* (e) *Whether the same pattern holds for adult learners is a question this essay has not been able to answer.*
::: solution
(a) implication. (b) recommendation. (c) qualification. (d) return to the opening. (e) question for further thought.
:::

::: exercise Restate the thesis {level=2}
Restate each thesis for a conclusion, in new words and a new structure, keeping the same answer. (a) *Secondary schools should teach financial skills, because they are needed by every student, are learned unequally at home, and can be taught in existing mathematics lessons.* (b) *Working from home benefits employees more than employers, because the time saved on travel goes to the employee while the costs of coordination fall on the organisation.*
::: hint
Begin with what the body has shown, rather than with the position; and do not simply replace words with synonyms.
:::
::: solution
Answers will vary. Models:

(a) *Every student will one day sign a contract, borrow money or plan a budget, but not every student will learn how at home. Since mathematics lessons can already teach the skills involved, leaving them out of the curriculum makes an unequal situation worse for no good reason.*

(b) *The hours that working from home saves are saved by the employee; the difficulties it creates, in arranging meetings and training new staff, are borne by the organisation. That imbalance explains why employees tend to value the arrangement more than their employers do.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Find the problem {level=2}
Each conclusion has one main problem. Name it and rewrite the conclusion. (a) *To sum up, public libraries are important. Firstly, they lend books. Secondly, they offer free internet. Thirdly, they are quiet places to study. In addition, libraries could also lend tools, which would reduce waste.* (b) *In conclusion, although I have argued that cities should ban cars from their centres, it is true that many people need cars, so maybe a ban is not a good idea after all.*
::: solution
(a) A summary of the body followed by a **new argument** (lending tools). Rewrite: *Public libraries are among the few places where anyone can read, go online or study without paying or being asked to buy something. That combination — free, open to all, and useful in more than one way — is what makes them worth funding even as fewer people borrow printed books.*

(b) The conclusion **retreats from the thesis** and raises a counter-argument without answering it. Rewrite: *A ban on private cars would make the centre cleaner, safer and more pleasant. The people who depend on cars — disabled drivers, deliveries, night workers — are a reason to design exemptions carefully, not to keep the traffic.* If the writer has genuinely changed their mind, the thesis and body must be revised.
:::
:::

::: exercise Synthesise {level=2}
The body of an essay on *Why do many people stop exercising after a few weeks?* gave three causes: (1) they set goals that are too ambitious; (2) exercise competes with work and family for time; (3) they choose kinds of exercise they do not enjoy. Write a conclusion of 60–90 words that synthesises these causes rather than listing them.
::: hint
Is there something the three causes have in common? Which one matters most, or which could be changed most easily?
:::
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

> People rarely give up exercise because they lack willpower. More often, they have chosen a plan that does not fit their life: too much, at times they do not have, in a form they do not enjoy. All three causes are failures of fit rather than of character, which suggests a simple remedy. Someone starting to exercise should choose something small enough to do on a busy week and pleasant enough to look forward to, and add to it only once it has become a habit.

The synthesis finds what the causes share (*failures of fit*) and draws an implication from it.

::: exercise Write a discursive conclusion {level=3}
The body of an essay on *Some people think that universities should prepare students mainly for employment; others think their main purpose is education for its own sake. Discuss both views and give your opinion.* argued: (1) employers need graduates with practical skills, and many students go to university to improve their job prospects; (2) the habits of thinking that a broad education develops — analysis, argument, judgement — are themselves valuable at work; (3) narrow training for current jobs can go out of date. Write a conclusion of 100–140 words that judges between the views and gives a reason.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

> On balance, the two purposes are less opposed than the question suggests, and where they do conflict, education should come first. Students are right to expect that a degree will help them find work, and universities that ignore this fail many of the people they serve. But the qualities employers value most over a career — the ability to analyse a problem, argue a case and judge evidence — are the products of a broad education, not of training for a particular job, and they do not go out of date as specific skills do. A university that teaches students to think well is therefore preparing them for employment, including for jobs that do not yet exist; one that teaches only today's skills is preparing them for a shorter future.

Check: *On balance* followed by a judgement and its reason; what the other view gets right; synthesis of points 2 and 3; an implication at the end; no new argument.
:::

::: exercise Revise a conclusion {level=3}
Here is the introduction and the conclusion of a student's essay on *Should secondary school students be required to do voluntary work in their community?* Revise the conclusion so that it answers the question, matches the thesis and follows the advice of this lesson. Write about 100 words.

*Introduction (last sentence): Compulsory community work would benefit both students and their communities, as long as students can choose the kind of work they do.*

*Conclusion: In conclusion, there are many advantages and disadvantages of compulsory voluntary work. On the one hand, it helps students. On the other hand, some students do not want to do it. Also, it can be difficult for schools to organise. As a famous saying goes, "It is better to give than to receive." We should all help others in our society.*
::: hint
The conclusion retreats from the thesis, lists points, adds a new argument (organisation), and ends with a quotation and a general moral. Which closing move would suit a *should* question?
:::
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

> Requiring students to work in their communities can seem a contradiction — voluntary work that is not voluntary. Yet the benefits argued here, from practical skills to a better understanding of the people around them, do not depend on students having chosen to begin; they depend on the work being worth doing. That is why choice matters more than compulsion: a student who can choose between a library, a sports club and a care home is more likely to keep going after the requirement ends. Schools should therefore require a modest number of hours and offer a wide range of placements.

Changes: the answer matches the thesis; the objection about unwilling students is answered, not listed; the new point about organisation is gone; the quotation and moral are replaced by a recommendation.
