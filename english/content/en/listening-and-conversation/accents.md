Zhang Yu has studied English for ten years, mostly with recordings in General American. She starts a master's degree in Edinburgh, and in her first week four people speak to her.

Her flatmate from Glasgow says [ɪts ˈrenɪn ðə ˈde] — *It's raining the day*, 'it's raining today' — with a tapped /r/, a pure [e] where she expected the diphthong of *rain*, and *the day* for *today*. Her tutor from London says [ðə ˈwɔːʔə ˈbɒʔl̩] for *the water bottle*, with no /t/ at all, but a catch in the throat. A classmate from Bangalore says [ˈʋeri ˈɡʊɖ] for *very good*, with a sound between /v/ and /w/ and a [ɖ] made with the tongue curled back. And a lab partner from Sydney tells her the seminar is on [ˈmʌndæɪ], and for a moment Zhang Yu hears *Mondie*.

None of these people is speaking badly. Each speaks a systematic **accent** of English, with its own regular patterns, and each is easily understood by thousands of other people. What Zhang Yu lacks is not English but **experience** of these accents. After a month, she understands all four without thinking about it.

This chapter describes how accents differ, gives the main features of some common accents of English, and shows how listeners adapt to an unfamiliar accent — quickly, as research shows. It uses the transcription of [[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet]] and builds on [[connected-speech/elision-assimilation]], because many accent features are processes of connected speech used more, less or differently. Varieties of English and the history behind them are the subject of [[world-englishes/models]] and the course that follows it; this chapter is about **listening**.

## What an accent is

::: definition Accent {#def-accent}
An **accent** is a way of pronouncing a language that is shared by a group of speakers — from a region, a country, a social group, or with a particular first language. Everyone who speaks has an accent, including speakers of General British and General American, which are two accents among many. An accent is different from a **dialect**, which also includes differences of vocabulary and grammar (*Are you coming the night?* for *tonight* in Scotland; *I'm after eating* in Ireland for 'I've just eaten').
:::

The atlas teaches General British (GB) and General American (GA) as **models**: clear, widely understood accents with good descriptions and dictionaries ([[world-englishes/models]]). They are reference points, not the "correct" English. Most English speakers in Britain and America do not speak them exactly, and most speakers of English in the world speak it as a second language, with accents influenced by their first languages.

::: definition Intelligibility, comprehensibility, accentedness {#def-intelligibility}
Munro and Derwing (1995) separated three ideas that are often confused:

- **Intelligibility**: how much of what a speaker says the listener actually understands (for example, how many words a listener can write down correctly).
- **Comprehensibility**: how easy or hard the listener **feels** it is to understand.
- **Accentedness**: how different the speech sounds from the listener's own accent or expectation.
:::

::: theorem A strong accent is not the same as unclear speech {#rule-accent}
Speech can be strongly accented and still fully intelligible. Accentedness depends partly on the **listener**: an accent that is unfamiliar to one listener is familiar to another. What makes speech hard to understand is usually a small number of features — certain sounds, misplaced word stress, the wrong nucleus — not the accent as a whole.
:::

::: proof
Munro and Derwing (1995) asked native English listeners to transcribe sentences spoken by Mandarin-speaking learners of English and to rate them for accentedness and comprehensibility. Many sentences rated as strongly accented were transcribed perfectly: the listeners understood every word, even though they noticed the accent. Comprehensibility and intelligibility were related to accentedness, but only partly. Later work by Derwing and Munro and others has repeatedly found that some features — word stress and the placement of the nucleus, for example — matter much more for intelligibility than others. For a listener, this means that an unfamiliar accent is a temporary problem of experience; for a speaker, it means that the aim is to be **intelligible**, not to lose your accent ([[sounds-of-english/sounds-for-chinese-speakers]]).
:::

## How accents differ

::: theorem Where accents differ {#rule-differences}
Accents of English differ mainly in:

1. **Vowels** — which vowel is used in a set of words, and its quality. Wells's **lexical sets** name the sets: *bath* has the TRAP vowel /æ/ in GA and northern England but the PALM vowel /ɑː/ in GB; *cup* has the STRUT vowel /ʌ/ in GB and GA but the FOOT vowel /ʊ/ in much of northern England ([[sounds-of-english/long-vowels-diphthongs]]).
2. **/r/ after vowels** — **rhotic** accents pronounce /r/ in *car*, *hard*, *water*; **non-rhotic** accents do not.
3. **/t/** — between vowels it may be [t], a tap [ɾ] (*water* [ˈwɑːɾɚ]), or a glottal stop [ʔ] (*water* [ˈwɔːʔə]).
4. **Other consonants** — /θ/ and /ð/ (said as [t d], [f v] or [s z] in some accents), /h/ (pronounced or dropped), /l/ (clear or dark), /v/ and /w/.
5. **Rhythm and stress** — how much unstressed vowels are reduced, and which syllable of a word is stressed.
6. **Intonation** — for example, rising tones on statements in some accents.
:::

::: proof
These are the dimensions used in standard descriptions of accents, such as Wells's *Accents of English* (1982), and they are not random. Each accent's choices are consistent: a speaker who has a tap in *water* also has one in *butter*, *city* and *get up*; a speaker who uses /ʊ/ in *cup* also uses it in *bus*, *love* and *money*. This is why listeners can adapt: once you notice one correspondence, you can apply it to every word in the set. The list also shows that many accent features are processes you already know from connected speech — tapping, glottal stops, elision ([[connected-speech/elision-assimilation]]) — used more often or in more positions.
:::

## Features of some common accents

The table gives some features that listeners notice in a number of accents. Each describes many — not all — speakers of the accent, and every accent has regional and social variation within it. The features are given for **recognition**: to help you hear, not to label people.

| accent | some features you may hear |
|---|---|
| **General British** (GB) | non-rhotic (*car* /kɑː/); *bath*, *grass*, *can't* with /ɑː/; *lot* with rounded /ɒ/; /t/ often a glottal stop before consonants (*not now* [nɒʔ naʊ]) |
| **General American** (GA) | rhotic (*car* /kɑːr/); /t/ tapped between vowels (*city* [ˈsɪɾi]); *lot* and *father* with the same /ɑː/; *bath* with /æ/ |
| **London and south-east England** | non-rhotic; /t/ often a glottal stop between vowels too (*water* [ˈwɔːʔə]); /θ/ often [f] (*think* [fɪŋk]); final dark /l/ like a vowel [ʊ] (*milk* [mɪʊk]) |
| **North of England** | *cup*, *bus*, *love* with /ʊ/; *bath*, *grass* with short /a/; *face* and *goat* often single vowels [eː], [oː] |
| **Scotland** | rhotic, /r/ often tapped [ɾ]; *face* [e], *goat* [o] as single vowels; /x/ in *loch*; less difference between long and short vowels |
| **Ireland** | rhotic; *face*, *goat* often single vowels; /θ ð/ often dental stops [t̪ d̪] (*think* [t̪ɪŋk]) |
| **Australia and New Zealand** | non-rhotic; /t/ often tapped between vowels; *face* closer to [æɪ] (*day* [dæɪ]), *price* closer to [ɑɪ]; in New Zealand, *kit* often close to [ə] |
| **India** | /t d/ often retroflex [ʈ ɖ]; /v/ and /w/ often both [ʋ]; *face*, *goat* as single vowels [eː], [oː]; often rhotic; fewer reduced vowels, so rhythm is closer to syllable-timed |
| **Nigeria** | fewer reduced vowels and a more even rhythm; *face*, *goat* as single vowels; /θ ð/ often [t d] |
| **Singapore** | fewer reduced vowels and a more even rhythm; final stops often unreleased or glottal; /θ/ often [t] or [f]; discourse particles such as *lah* in informal speech |

These and many other varieties are described in [[world-englishes/british-american]], [[world-englishes/south-asian-englishes]], [[world-englishes/southeast-asian-englishes]] and [[world-englishes/african-caribbean-englishes]].

::: example One sentence, four accents {#ex-water}
The sentence *Can I get a bottle of water?* is said by four speakers. Their pronunciations of *get a bottle of water* are given. Name the feature that makes each different from careful GB [ɡet ə ˈbɒtl̩ əv ˈwɔːtə].

1. [ɡeɾə ˈbɑːɾl̩ əv ˈwɑːɾɚ]
2. [ɡeʔə ˈbɒʔl̩ ə ˈwɔːʔə]
3. [ɡeɾə ˈbɔɾl̩ ə ˈwoːɾə], with *I* in *Can I* closer to [ɑɪ]
4. [ɡeʈ ə ˈbɒʈl̩ ɒf ˈʋɔːʈər]
::: solution
1. **GA**: taps for every /t/ between vowels ([ɡeɾə], [ˈbɑːɾl̩], [ˈwɑːɾɚ]); /ɑː/ in *bottle* (the LOT vowel unrounded); rhotic *water* [ɚ].
2. **London**: glottal stops for /t/ ([ɡeʔə], [ˈbɒʔl̩], [ˈwɔːʔə]); non-rhotic; *of* reduced to [ə].
3. **Australian**: taps like GA, but non-rhotic (*water* [ˈwoːɾə]); a rounder vowel in *bottle*; *I* closer to [ɑɪ].
4. **Indian**: retroflex [ʈ] for /t/; [ʋ] for /w/; *of* with a full vowel [ɒf] rather than weak [əv]; rhotic *water*.

In every case the words are the same, and the differences are regular: the /t/ of *get a*, *bottle* and *water* is treated in the same way within each accent. Once you know that a speaker taps or glottalises /t/, you can expect it everywhere.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A speaker says [ˈbʊs] for *bus* and [ˈlʊv] for *love*. Which accent feature is this, and which other word will probably have the same vowel?
- [ ] A rhotic accent; *car*.
- [x] No FOOT–STRUT split, as in much of northern England; *cup*.
- [ ] T-glottalling; *water*.
- [ ] A monophthong in FACE; *day*.
::: solution
Using /ʊ/ in words of the STRUT set (*bus*, *love*, *cup*, *money*, *come*) is typical of much of northern England, where the FOOT and STRUT vowels did not split. The same speaker will probably say *cup* [kʊp]. The other features are real but concern other sounds.
:::
:::

::: example Where is the /r/? {#ex-rhotic}
Two speakers, one from Chicago speaking close to GA and one from Manchester speaking close to GB, say: *Where are your car keys? They're under the paper.* Where does each pronounce /r/?
::: solution
**GA (rhotic)**: every written *r* is pronounced: [wer ɚ jɚ ˈkɑːr kiːz | ðer ˈʌndɚ ðə ˈpeɪpɚ]. *Where*, *are*, *your*, *car*, *They're*, *under* and *paper* all have /r/ (often as the r-coloured vowel [ɚ]).

**GB (non-rhotic)**: /r/ is pronounced only before a vowel: [weər ə jɔː ˈkɑː kiːz | ðeər ˈʌndə ðə ˈpeɪpə]. *Where* and *They're* keep /r/ because the next word begins with a vowel (*are*, *under*) — this is **linking /r/** ([[connected-speech/linking#rule-r]]). *Car* before *keys* and *paper* at the end have no /r/.

For a listener, the important point is that both speakers say the same words: in GB, *car* [kɑː] and in GA *car* [kɑːr]. A listener used to GA should not wait for an /r/ that a non-rhotic speaker will not say, and should not mistake GB *paper* [ˈpeɪpə] for a different word.
:::
:::

## Listening to an unfamiliar accent

The good news from research is that listeners adapt to a new accent **quickly**, and that the more voices they hear, the better they adapt.

::: theorem Listeners adapt quickly {#rule-adapt}
1. **The first minute is the hardest.** Clarke and Garrett (2004) found that native English listeners who heard Spanish- or Chinese-accented English were slower to process it at first, but that the difference shrank sharply within about a minute of listening.
2. **Many voices help more than one.** Bradlow and Bent (2008) trained listeners on Chinese-accented English. Listeners who heard several different speakers with the accent understood a **new** speaker of the same accent better afterwards than listeners who had heard only one speaker.
3. **Shared first languages can help.** Bent and Bradlow (2003) found that non-native listeners often understood non-native speakers who shared their first language as well as, or better than, native speakers — an effect they called the *interlanguage speech intelligibility benefit*.
:::

::: proof
Adaptation works because accents are systematic ([[#rule-differences]]). After a few sentences, the listener has learned some correspondences — *this speaker's [e] is my /eɪ/*; *this speaker's [ʔ] between vowels is /t/* — and applies them to new words automatically. Hearing many speakers teaches the listener which features belong to the **accent** and which to an individual voice, so the learning transfers to new speakers. The benefit of a shared first language comes from shared patterns: a Chinese listener and a Chinese speaker of English may treat final consonants and vowel length in similar ways. For a learner, the practical message is: do not give up in the first minute, and listen to many speakers ([[listening-and-conversation/learning-from-media]]).
:::

::: theorem Strategies for an unfamiliar accent {#rule-strategies}
1. **Give it a minute.** Keep listening for the gist; your ear is adjusting ([[listening-and-conversation/gist-and-detail]]).
2. **Look for correspondences.** When you recognise a word, notice how it was pronounced, and expect the same in similar words.
3. **Rely on stress, context and prediction.** Accents change sounds more than they change the position of stress and nuclei; top-down knowledge fills the gaps ([[listening-and-conversation/how-listening-works]]).
4. **Ask specifically.** *Sorry — on Monday?* works with every accent ([[listening-and-conversation/clarification]]).
5. **Listen widely.** Hear many voices, from many places, not one favourite accent ([[listening-and-conversation/learning-from-media]]).
6. **Don't judge.** An accent you find hard is not a worse accent; it is one you have heard less.
:::

::: example Adapting to a speaker {#ex-adapt}
Zhang Yu's flatmate Mhairi, from Glasgow, tells her about the weekend. Some words are given in transcription. Find the correspondences that help Zhang Yu.

*We went up to [ðə ˈhɪlz] on [ˈsatərde], but it was [ˈfriːzɪn], so we came [ˈhem] early and had a [ˈkʌɾi] — it was [ˈbɾɪljənt].*
::: solution
1. *the hills* — [ðə ˈhɪlz]: as expected.
2. *Saturday* [ˈsatərde]: a short [a] where GB has /æ/ and GA /æ/; a pronounced /r/ (rhotic); final [e] for the FACE vowel /eɪ/, a single vowel. **Correspondence**: Mhairi's [e] = /eɪ/.
3. *freezing* [ˈfriːzɪn]: *-ing* as [ɪn], common in informal speech in many accents.
4. *home* [ˈhem]: this one is surprising — in Scots, the traditional language related to English spoken in Scotland, *hame* is 'home', and many Scottish speakers use it in casual English. Context (*so we came … early*) makes the meaning clear.
5. *curry* [ˈkʌɾi] and *brilliant* [ˈbɾɪljənt] — /r/ as a tap [ɾ], which can sound like a quick /d/; the stress and the context make both words easy.

Once Zhang Yu knows that Mhairi's [e] is her /eɪ/, she will recognise *day*, *rain* and *late* in later conversations. The unusual word *hame* is learned from context, like any new word.
:::
:::

::: warning Mocking or "correcting" an accent
✗ *Her English is bad — listen to her accent.* ✗ Imitating an accent to make people laugh. ✗ *That's not real English.*
✓ *I find her accent hard because I haven't heard it much.* ✓ *Sorry — could you say that again?*

Accents are systematic varieties, used by millions of people, and they are tied to people's identities and communities ([[world-englishes/accent-identity]]). Judging a speaker's intelligence, education or competence by their accent is a form of prejudice. If you find an accent hard, the problem is experience — which you can change.
:::

## Your own accent

Everything in this chapter applies to your own English too. A Chinese accent of English is a systematic accent with its own features ([[world-englishes/english-in-china]]), and many listeners around the world understand it easily. The aim is not to sound like a native speaker of GB or GA, but to be **intelligible** to the people you talk to — who, in most international settings, are themselves non-native speakers.

::: theorem What matters most for being understood {#rule-intelligibility}
In conversations between speakers of English from different countries, Jenkins (2000) found that misunderstandings came mostly from a few features, which she called the **Lingua Franca Core**. Among them:

1. most **consonants** (but /θ/ and /ð/ can be replaced by sounds such as [f v] or [t d] without much loss);
2. **aspiration** of /p t k/ at the start of stressed syllables, which distinguishes *pin* from *bin*;
3. not dropping consonants from **clusters** at the start of words (*street*, not *seet* or *sutreet*);
4. the difference between **long and short vowels** (*ship*–*sheep*, *full*–*fool*);
5. placing the **nucleus** correctly ([[connected-speech/sentence-stress]]).

Exact vowel qualities, weak forms and some features of intonation mattered less in these conversations.
:::

::: proof
Jenkins recorded and analysed conversations between learners of English from different first-language backgrounds and noted where communication broke down. Breakdowns were usually caused by pronunciation features in the list above — a missing consonant, a short vowel for a long one, a nucleus in the wrong place — and rarely by features such as /θ/ or the exact quality of a vowel. Her list is a proposal for teaching English as an international language, and later research has debated its details ([[world-englishes/lingua-franca]]); but it agrees with Munro and Derwing's finding that a few features matter far more than the accent as a whole. For Chinese speakers, the usual priorities are final consonants, consonant clusters, vowel length and the nucleus ([[sounds-of-english/sounds-for-chinese-speakers]]).
:::

::: example Setting priorities {#ex-priorities}
Chen Hao tells a new colleague where he lives: *I live on Third Street, near the big park.* He says [aɪ ˈliːv ɒn ˈsɜːd səˈtriː ˈnɪə ðə ˈbɪɡə ˈpɑː]. Which features are most likely to cause misunderstanding, and which matter less?
::: solution
Following [[#rule-intelligibility]]:

1. **Vowel length** — *live* with a long [iː] sounds like *leave*: *I leave on Third Street* is possible English with a different meaning. High priority.
2. **Initial cluster** — *street* [səˈtriː] has an extra vowel and has lost its final /t/: the listener hears three syllables, *su-tree*, and may not recognise the name. High priority.
3. **Final consonants** — *park* [pɑː] has lost its /k/, and *big* has an added [ə], so *the big park* may be heard as *the bigger par…*. High priority.
4. **/θ/** — *Third* [sɜːd] with [s]: the listener will probably still understand *Third Street* from context. Lower priority.

Chen Hao's accent will remain, and that is fine. Working on the first three features — vowel length, clusters, final consonants — will make it far more intelligible than working on /θ/.
:::
:::

::: quiz
According to the research in this chapter, which statement is true?
- [ ] Speech with a strong foreign accent is always hard to understand.
- [ ] Listeners need months of exposure before they can understand a new accent.
- [x] Listeners adapt to a new accent within minutes, and hearing several speakers of it helps most.

::: solution
Clarke and Garrett (2004) found rapid adaptation within about a minute, and Bradlow and Bent (2008) found that exposure to several speakers of an accent generalised best to new speakers. Munro and Derwing (1995) showed that strongly accented speech can be fully intelligible, and everyone who speaks has an accent ([[#def-accent]]).
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **An analogy from Chinese.** Mandarin is spoken with many accents: a speaker from Guangzhou, one from Chengdu and one from Shenyang may pronounce 是, 四 and 十 differently, and Putonghua speakers from the north may at first find some southern accents hard. After a few conversations, they adapt. English accents work in the same way.
- **One accent in the classroom.** Many Chinese learners have heard mostly GA or GB recordings. Start early to listen to other accents — British regional, Australian, Indian, Nigerian, Singaporean — and to non-native speakers from many countries, because those are the people you are most likely to talk to.
- **Features of Chinese-accented English** — final consonants dropped or followed by a vowel, /θ/ as [s], /v/ as [w] (for some speakers), /n/ and /l/ confused (for speakers from some regions), long and short vowels not distinguished, an even rhythm — are systematic, as are those of every accent. Some reduce intelligibility more than others ([[#rule-intelligibility]]).
- **No accent is a mistake.** You may meet the idea that "British English is correct" or "American English is standard". Both are models; neither is the property of one country, and international English is spoken by far more people with other accents.
:::

::: history
Systematic description of English accents began with the phoneticians of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, such as Henry Sweet and Daniel Jones, who described the speech of southern England. John Wells's three-volume *Accents of English* (1982) described accents across the English-speaking world and introduced the lexical sets now used to compare them. Braj Kachru's model of the "three circles" of English (1985) helped make it normal to describe Indian, Nigerian or Singaporean English as varieties in their own right. In the 1990s and 2000s, research on intelligibility changed the aims of pronunciation teaching: Munro and Derwing (1995) separated accent from intelligibility, Jenkins (2000) proposed a Lingua Franca Core for international communication, and studies such as Clarke and Garrett (2004) and Bradlow and Bent (2008) showed how quickly and how well listeners adapt to unfamiliar accents.
:::

::: summary
- Everyone has an **accent**; GB and GA are models, not the only correct English ([[#def-accent]]).
- **Accentedness** is not the same as **intelligibility**: strongly accented speech can be fully understood (Munro and Derwing 1995; [[#def-intelligibility]], [[#rule-accent]]).
- Accents differ systematically in vowels, post-vocalic /r/, /t/, other consonants, rhythm and intonation ([[#rule-differences]]); the /t/ of *water* can be [t], [ɾ], [ʔ] or [ʈ].
- Listeners adapt to a new accent within minutes, and hearing many speakers helps most (Clarke and Garrett 2004; Bradlow and Bent 2008; [[#rule-adapt]]).
- With an unfamiliar accent: give it a minute, look for correspondences, rely on stress and context, ask specifically, listen widely, and do not judge ([[#rule-strategies]]).
- For your own accent, aim at intelligibility: consonants, clusters, vowel length, aspiration and the nucleus matter most (Jenkins 2000; [[#rule-intelligibility]]).
:::

::: exercise Intelligibility priorities {level=2}
A Chinese learner says these words. For each, say which feature from [[#rule-intelligibility]] might cause a misunderstanding.

(a) *ship* said with a long [iː] (b) *street* said [səˈtriːt] (c) *bad* said [bæ] (d) *think* said [sɪŋk]
::: solution
(a) **Vowel length**: [ʃiːp] is heard as *sheep*. (b) **Initial cluster** broken by an extra vowel: *street* may be heard as two syllables, or not recognised. (c) **Final consonant** dropped: [bæ] could be *bad*, *bat*, *back* or *bag*. (d) /θ/ as [s]: *sink* — usually clear from context, and less important for intelligibility according to Jenkins (2000), though worth working on.
:::
:::

::: exercise Listening log {level=3}
For two weeks, listen to at least five different speakers of English with five different accents (for example, from interviews or talks). For each, write: where the speaker is from, two features you noticed (with an example word), and how long it took you to feel comfortable. At the end, write a short paragraph on what you learned.
::: hint
Choose speakers talking about things that interest you; use the table in this chapter as a guide, but write what you actually hear.
:::
::: solution
Answers vary. A good log is specific: *"Speaker 3, from Lagos: 'think' [t̪ɪŋk] with a [t]-like sound; even rhythm, few weak forms, so every word was clear but the stress was hard to follow at first. Comfortable after about two minutes."* A good final paragraph notices that (1) the first minute was always the hardest; (2) some accents were easier than expected, especially ones with fewer weak forms; and (3) context and stress helped more than individual sounds.
:::
:::

::: exercise Write a reply {level=3}
A classmate writes in a chat group: *"I can't understand our new lecturer from Scotland at all. Her English is so bad!"* Write a friendly reply (60–100 words) that disagrees politely, explains what is happening, and gives two practical tips.
::: solution
A model answer:

*I know what you mean — I found her hard in the first lecture too! But I don't think her English is bad: Scottish English is just an accent we haven't heard much. It's very regular — for example, she says "day" and "late" with a single vowel, like [de]. Two things helped me: sitting near the front for the first few lectures, and watching a couple of interviews with Scottish speakers online. After a week it got much easier. You'll be fine!*

The reply uses partial agreement (*I know what you mean*), a softened disagreement (*I don't think…*), an explanation (an accent we haven't heard much; it is regular), and two tips ([[listening-and-conversation/opinions#rule-disagree]]).
:::
