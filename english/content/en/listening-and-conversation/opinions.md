Four colleagues are deciding when to hold their weekly team meeting. Their manager, Hannah, suggests moving it from Thursday afternoon to Monday at nine.

**Hannah:** *I think Monday morning would be better. Then we can plan the whole week together.*

**Dmitri:** *No. Monday is a bad idea.*

**Lin Jie:** *Yes, yes.*

**Grace:** *Mm, I see what you mean about planning the week. I'm just not sure Monday at nine works for everyone — a few of us drop children at school first. Could we do ten instead?*

Dmitri disagreed, and he may well be right — but his *No* sounded like a door closing, and Hannah looked annoyed. Lin Jie said *yes*, but she actually starts work at half past nine on Mondays; later she wrote Hannah an email explaining that she couldn't come, and Hannah wondered why she hadn't said so in the meeting. Grace also disagreed — in fact she disagreed with the same detail as Dmitri — but she began by agreeing with part of Hannah's idea, gave a reason, and offered another time. Hannah said: *Oh — good point. Ten it is.*

This chapter teaches how to give and ask for opinions, how to agree, and how to disagree so that people listen to your reasons instead of reacting to your tone. It builds on [[listening-and-conversation/turn-taking]], because in English conversation agreement and disagreement have typical turn shapes, and on [[connected-speech/intonation-meaning]], because the tone of *Well…* or *I see what you mean* says as much as the words.

## Giving and asking for opinions

::: definition Opinion marker {#def-opinion-marker}
An **opinion marker** is a phrase that shows that what follows is the speaker's view rather than a fact: *I think…*, *In my opinion…*, *I'd say…*, *It seems to me that…*, *Personally, I…*, *If you ask me…*. Opinion markers also show **how sure** the speaker is.
:::

Opinion markers can be arranged roughly by how sure they sound:

| strength | examples |
|---|---|
| very sure | *I'm absolutely convinced that…*, *I really do think…*, *There's no doubt in my mind that…* |
| sure | *I'm sure…*, *I definitely think…*, *I really believe…* |
| neutral | *I think…*, *In my opinion…*, *I'd say…*, *For me, …*, *Personally, …* |
| tentative | *I suppose…*, *I tend to think…*, *It seems to me…*, *I'd have thought…* |
| unsure | *I'm not sure, but maybe…*, *I might be wrong, but…*, *I don't know — perhaps…* |

*I think* is by far the most common in conversation, and it is never wrong. *In my opinion* is a little more formal and is more common in discussions and writing than in casual chat. *If you ask me* and *Personally* are informal and slightly emphatic.

::: theorem Give an opinion with a reason {#rule-reason}
In discussion, an opinion is usually followed by a **reason** and often by an **example**: *I think Monday would be better, **because** we could plan the whole week together. **For example**, we'd know straight away if someone was overloaded.* An opinion without a reason gives others nothing to discuss except whether they like you.
:::

::: proof
A reason turns an opinion into an argument that others can respond to: they can accept the reason, question it, or offer a better one. This keeps the discussion on the topic rather than on the people. It also explains the strength of your view: *I think we should go by train, because it's cheaper* invites comparison of prices, which is easy to settle; *I think we should go by train* alone invites only *I don't*. Reasons are introduced with *because*, *since*, *as*, *the thing is…*, *the main reason is…*; examples with *for example*, *for instance*, *like*, *say* (informal: *say, if someone's ill…*).
:::

### Asking for opinions

- *What do you think?* / *What do you think about…?* / *What do you think of…?*
- *How do you feel about…?*
- *What's your view on…?* (more formal) / *What's your take on…?* (informal)
- *Do you think we should…?*
- *Any thoughts?* / *Anyone else?* (in a group)
- *Would you agree?*

Asking for opinions is an important part of a discussion, not just of a teacher's job: it brings quieter people in, and it shows that you are interested in views other than your own. *What do you think **of** the film?* asks for an evaluation; *What do you think **about** the plan?* is a little broader ('your thoughts on it').

::: example From a one-word answer to an opinion {#ex-opinion}
At lunch, a colleague asks Nguyen Van Minh: *What did you think of the new canteen menu?* He answers: *Good.* Build a fuller answer step by step.
::: solution
1. **Opinion marker and view, with strength**: *I thought it was pretty good, actually.* — *pretty* softens *good* a little; *actually* adds a friendly, conversational tone.
2. **Reason**: *There's a lot more choice for vegetarians now.*
3. **Example**: *The lentil curry yesterday was really nice.*
4. **A reservation (optional)**: *The portions are a bit small, though.* — *though* at the end adds a contrast lightly.
5. **Hand the turn back**: *What about you?*

Full answer: *I thought it was pretty good, actually — there's a lot more choice for vegetarians now. The lentil curry yesterday was really nice. The portions are a bit small, though. What about you?* The answer is five short sentences, none of them difficult, but together they give the colleague several things to respond to ([[listening-and-conversation/turn-taking#rule-building]]).
:::
:::

::: warning *I am agree*
✗ *I am agree with you.* ✗ *I am not agree.* ✗ *I'm agree.*
✓ *I agree with you.* ✓ *I don't agree.* ✓ *I disagree.*

*Agree* is a verb, not an adjective, so it does not follow *be*. The negative is formed with *do*: *I **don't** agree*, *Do you agree?* The mistake is very common among learners whose languages express agreement with an adjective-like word — Chinese 同意 can follow 不 directly, which may make *I'm not agree* feel natural. Note also *agree **with** someone*, *agree **with**/**on**/**about** something* (*We agree on the price*), and *agree **to** a proposal* ('accept it').
:::

::: quiz
Which is the most natural way to give a tentative opinion in a meeting?
- [ ] *I am agree that we need more time.*
- [ ] *It is a fact that we need more time.*
- [x] *I tend to think we need a bit more time, actually.*
- [ ] *In my opinion, I think that we need more time.*
::: solution
*I tend to think…, actually* is tentative and natural. The first is ungrammatical (*I agree…*). The second presents an opinion as a fact. The fourth uses two opinion markers for one opinion; *In my opinion, we need more time* or *I think we need more time* is enough.
:::
:::

## Agreeing

Agreement can be strong or partial:

| kind | examples |
|---|---|
| strong | *Absolutely.* / *Exactly.* / *Definitely.* / *I couldn't agree more.* / *That's exactly what I think.* / *You're so right.* |
| ordinary | *I agree.* / *Yes, I think so too.* / *True.* / *Good point.* / *That's right.* / *Fair enough.* |
| partial | *I suppose so.* / *I guess so.* / *Up to a point.* / *To some extent.* / *Yes, in a way.* / *Maybe.* |

::: theorem Agreeing with negative statements {#rule-agree-negative}
To agree with a **negative** statement, English uses a negative answer: *It isn't very warm today, is it? — **No, it isn't.*** / *That wasn't a great film. — **No**, it wasn't.* To agree with *I don't…* or *I can't…*, use *Me neither* (informal), *Neither do I*, *Nor can I*: *I don't like the new logo. — **Neither do I.*** *Me too* and *So do I* agree with positive statements only ([[everyday-english/talking-about-yourself#rule-me-too]]).
:::

::: proof
English *yes* and *no* answer about the situation, not about the speaker's statement: *No* means 'it is not warm', which here agrees with the speaker. Mandarin 对 and 是 work differently: they respond to the statement — 'what you said is right' — so 对 can agree with a negative statement (今天不太暖和吧？ — 对，不暖和). Translated directly, ✗ *Yes, it isn't* sounds confused, and *Yes* alone may be heard as disagreement ('yes, it is warm'). Add the auxiliary to make the answer clear: *No, it isn't.* The same applies to negative questions: *Didn't you like it? — **No**, I didn't* (agreeing: I didn't like it) / *Yes, I did!* (disagreeing: I did like it). See [[core-grammar/questions-and-negatives]].
:::

::: remark Quick agreement and slow agreement
There is one more feature of agreement worth knowing. Pomerantz (1984), analysing recorded conversations, found that when one speaker gives an assessment — *It's a beautiful day* — agreement usually comes **quickly** and is often **upgraded** to a stronger word: *Yes, it's gorgeous!* An agreement with only the **same** or a **weaker** word, after a slight pause — *Yes… it's nice* — often comes just before a disagreement: *…but it's a bit cold for a picnic.* Listeners hear this pattern, so a weak, slow *yes* can sound like hidden disagreement.
:::

## Disagreeing politely

Disagreeing is not impolite in English. In meetings, seminars and among friends, people are expected to say when they think differently, and a group in which nobody ever disagrees is usually a group in which some people are not saying what they think. But **how** you disagree matters.

::: theorem The shape of a polite disagreement {#rule-disagree}
A polite disagreement in English usually has several of these parts, in this order:

1. **A short delay or preface**: *Well…*, *Hmm*, *Mm*, *Actually…*, *Yes, but…*
2. **Partial agreement** — something in the other view that you accept: *I see what you mean, but…*, *That's a good point, but…*, *You're right that…, but…*, *I take your point, but…*
3. **A softened opinion**: *I'm not sure that…*, *I'm not convinced…*, *I don't really think…*, *I'd have thought…*, with softeners such as *a bit*, *maybe*, *quite*, *really*.
4. **A reason**: *…because…*, *The thing is…*
5. **An alternative or a question**: *Could we…instead?*, *What if we…?*, *How would that work if…?*
:::

::: proof
Pomerantz (1984) described disagreement as a **dispreferred** response: in recorded conversations, disagreements were typically delayed, prefaced with *well* or *uh*, introduced by token agreement, and softened, while agreements came quickly and plainly. These features are not decoration. The delay and the preface warn the listener that disagreement is coming; partial agreement shows that you have listened and that you are disagreeing with an idea, not attacking a person; softeners leave room for the other person to keep face ([[pragmatics/politeness-face]]); the reason moves the discussion to the substance; and an alternative turns criticism into a plan. Grace in the opening dialogue used all five.
:::

### Phrases for disagreeing, from soft to strong

| strength | examples |
|---|---|
| very soft | *I'm not sure I agree.* / *I see what you mean, but…* / *That's true, but on the other hand…* / *Maybe, but…* |
| soft | *I'm not convinced.* / *I don't really agree.* / *I'm not sure about that.* / *I see it a bit differently.* |
| clear | *I don't agree.* / *I disagree.* / *I don't think that's right.* |
| strong | *I completely disagree.* / *That's not true at all.* / *No way.* (informal) |

Strong disagreement is fine in some settings — a debate, a friendly argument about football, a safety problem — but with colleagues and new acquaintances, the softer forms are usual.

::: theorem Intonation in agreement and disagreement {#rule-intonation}
1. A **fall-rise** on a partial agreement — *I see what you ↘↗**mean*** or *↘↗**Some**times* — signals that a *but* is coming, even before it is said ([[connected-speech/intonation-meaning#rule-attitude]]).
2. A **wide fall** on agreement — *↘**Ab**solutely* — sounds warm and sure; a **low, narrow** fall — *Yes.* — can sound reluctant.
3. A **level** or flat *Well* followed by a pause predicts disagreement.
4. Strong, high falls on every word of a disagreement — *That's ↘**not** ↘**true*** — sound angry; keep the pitch moderate when you want to stay calm.
:::

::: example Softening a blunt disagreement {#ex-soften}
Rewrite each blunt disagreement so that it is polite but still clear.

1. *No. That's wrong.* (A colleague says that the project will be finished by May.)
2. *Your idea is bad.* (A friend suggests a beach holiday in August.)
3. *You don't understand the problem.* (In a seminar, a student says that cars are the main cause of air pollution in cities.)
::: solution
1. *Hmm, I'm not sure we'll make May, actually. The testing alone usually takes six weeks. Could we say mid-June to be safe?* — preface, softened opinion, reason, alternative.
2. *Mm, I love the beach — but August might be really crowded and expensive, don't you think? What about June?* — partial agreement, softened opinion with a question tag, reason, alternative.
3. *That's partly true — traffic's certainly a big factor. But I've read that in some cities heating and industry contribute just as much. Do we know the figures for our city?* — partial agreement, a reason with a source, a question that moves the discussion forward.

Each version still disagrees clearly. Softening is not the same as hiding: the listener must understand that you think differently.
:::
:::

::: warning Hiding disagreement
✗ Saying *Yes* or nodding in a meeting when you disagree, then doing something different later or raising the problem afterwards in private.
✓ Disagreeing politely at the time: *I see the advantage, but I'm not sure it'll work for my team — can I explain why?*

In many English-speaking workplaces, silence and *yes* in a meeting are taken as agreement. Disagreeing afterwards can seem worse than disagreeing at the time, because the others have already acted on your apparent agreement. Over-softening has the same effect: *Maybe… possibly… I don't know* may be heard as agreement. Soften the tone, but make the disagreement clear.
:::

::: example Partial agreement or hidden disagreement? {#ex-hidden}
Hannah asks: *So is everyone happy with Monday at nine?* What does each answer probably mean?

1. *Absolutely — that works great for me.*
2. *Yes… I suppose so.*
3. *Mm. It's… fine.*
4. *That suits me, but I'm not sure about Ade — doesn't he work from home on Mondays?*
::: solution
1. **Real, strong agreement**: quick, with an upgraded word (*absolutely*, *great*).
2. **Reluctant agreement**: a pause, then a weak verb (*suppose*). The speaker will probably come, but would prefer another time; Hannah might ask: *You don't sound sure — would another time be better?*
3. **Probably hidden disagreement**: a delay, a hesitation inside the answer, and the weak word *fine* with a narrow pitch. A good listener checks: *Is there a problem with it?*
4. **Agreement for herself, disagreement on behalf of someone else**, with a reason and a question — a clear and polite way to raise a problem.

Answers 2 and 3 show why the timing and strength of agreement matter: English listeners hear a slow, weak *yes* as a possible *no*.
:::
:::

## Keeping the discussion friendly

Some phrases help a discussion stay pleasant when people disagree:

- **Conceding a point**: *Fair point.* / *You've got a point there.* / *I hadn't thought of that.* / *OK, that's true.*
- **Changing your mind**: *Actually, you're right.* / *You've convinced me.* / *OK, I take it back.*
- **Asking for more**: *Why do you think that?* / *What makes you say that?* / *Can you give me an example?*
- **Finding common ground**: *So we both think we need to…; we just disagree about how.*
- **Agreeing to disagree**: *Let's agree to disagree.* / *I think we'll have to agree to disagree on that one.*
- **Bringing others in**: *What do the rest of you think?*

::: example A short discussion {#ex-discussion}
Three students, Ahmed, Zhou Ting and Clara, are deciding what to present on. Label the moves: opinions, reasons, agreement, disagreement, concessions and questions.

**Ahmed:** *I think we should do social media and teenagers. Everyone's interested in it, so people will listen.*

**Zhou Ting:** *Mm, that's true, everyone's interested. But I'm a bit worried it's been done a lot — the group last term did almost the same topic.*

**Ahmed:** *Did they? I didn't know that.*

**Clara:** *Fair point. What about something more local, like the new cycle lanes? We could interview people.*

**Ahmed:** *Hmm, I'm not sure we'd get enough interviews in two weeks.*

**Zhou Ting:** *We could do a short online survey instead — that's quicker.*

**Ahmed:** *OK, that could work. You've convinced me.*

**Clara:** *Great. So cycle lanes, with a survey. Everyone happy?*
::: solution
- Ahmed: **opinion** (*I think…*) + **reason** (*so people will listen*).
- Zhou Ting: **partial agreement** (*Mm, that's true*) + **softened disagreement** (*I'm a bit worried*) + **reason** (the topic has been done).
- Ahmed: **reactive question** (*Did they?*) — he accepts new information.
- Clara: **concession** (*Fair point*) + **alternative** as a question (*What about…?*) + a reason (*We could interview people*).
- Ahmed: **preface** (*Hmm*) + **softened disagreement** (*I'm not sure…*) with a reason (two weeks).
- Zhou Ting: a **solution** to the objection.
- Ahmed: **changing his mind** (*You've convinced me*).
- Clara: **summary** and **check** (*Everyone happy?*).

Nobody wins or loses: each disagreement produces a better plan. This is what good discussion looks like in English.
:::
:::

::: quiz
In a discussion, someone says: *Online classes are just as good as face-to-face ones.* You partly disagree. Which response is the most effective?
- [ ] *You're wrong.*
- [ ] *Yes.* (and say nothing more)
- [x] *For some subjects, maybe. But I find it much harder to ask questions online — don't you?*
- [ ] *I am not agree, because online is bad.*
::: solution
The third response gives partial agreement (*For some subjects, maybe*), a clear but softened disagreement with a personal reason, and a question that invites the other person to respond. *You're wrong* is blunt and gives no reason; *Yes* hides the disagreement; the fourth is ungrammatical (*I don't agree*) and its reason is too vague.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **对 and *no*.** Agreeing with a negative statement or question is a frequent source of confusion: 对 agrees with what was said, but English *yes*/*no* describe the situation. *You're not coming, are you? — **No**, I'm not* ([[#rule-agree-negative]]).
- **Indirectness differs, not politeness.** Every language has ways to disagree gently, and people in every culture vary a great deal. Some Chinese ways of avoiding open disagreement — 再研究研究, 这个可能有点难度, silence — may not be read as disagreement by English-speaking colleagues. The English softened forms (*I'm not sure that works*) are understood as real disagreement; use them.
- **Seniority.** Disagreeing with a teacher or a manager can feel especially uncomfortable. In many English-speaking universities and workplaces, polite disagreement with seniors is expected and even welcomed, especially when it comes with a reason: *Could I ask about…? I wondered whether…*
- ***I think* + negative.** English usually puts the negative in the main clause: *I **don't** think it's a good idea* is more natural than *I think it's not a good idea*, which is grammatical but sounds more direct. Chinese 我觉得不太好 puts it after 觉得, so learners often prefer the second.
- ***Agree* is a verb**: *I agree*, *I don't agree* — not *I'm agree*.
:::

::: history
The study of how people agree and disagree in conversation owes much to Anita Pomerantz's chapter "Agreeing and disagreeing with assessments: some features of preferred/dispreferred turn shapes" (1984), in the collection *Structures of Social Action* edited by J. Maxwell Atkinson and John Heritage. Working with recordings, she showed that agreements and disagreements are built differently: agreement is quick and often upgraded, disagreement delayed, prefaced and softened. Around the same time, Geoffrey Leech's *Principles of Pragmatics* (1983) proposed an "agreement maxim" — maximise agreement, minimise disagreement — as part of politeness, and Penelope Brown and Stephen Levinson's *Politeness: Some Universals in Language Usage* (1987) explained softened disagreement as a way of protecting the other person's face. Teachers of English for discussion and debate have drawn on these descriptions ever since.
:::

::: summary
- Use **opinion markers** to show that you are giving a view and how sure you are: *I'm sure…*, *I think…*, *I tend to think…*, *I might be wrong, but…* ([[#def-opinion-marker]]).
- Give opinions with **reasons** and examples; ask others for theirs ([[#rule-reason]]).
- *Agree* is a verb: *I agree*, *I don't agree*. To agree with a negative, answer with a negative: *It isn't…, is it? — No, it isn't* ([[#rule-agree-negative]]).
- Real agreement is quick and often stronger than the original; a slow, weak *yes* often comes before disagreement (Pomerantz 1984).
- Disagree politely with a preface, partial agreement, a softened opinion, a reason and an alternative ([[#rule-disagree]]); a fall-rise on *I see what you mean* signals the *but* ([[#rule-intonation]]).
- Soften the tone but make the disagreement clear; concede points, ask questions, and look for common ground.
:::

**Ravi and Tom:** *Yep.*

::: exercise Speaking: three opinions {level=3}
Choose one statement and prepare a one-minute spoken answer in which you give your opinion with two reasons and an example, then mention one point on the other side and say why you still hold your view. Record yourself, and check your intonation on partial agreements.

A good answer has a clear structure: opinion (*I tend to think…*), reason 1, reason 2 with an example, the other side (*Of course, some people would say… and that's a fair point, because…*), and a return to your view (*But on balance, I still think…, because…*). On the recording, check that the concession (*that's a fair point*) is said with a fall-rise or a light fall that leads to *but*, not a heavy final fall, and that *But on balance* starts a new tone unit.
