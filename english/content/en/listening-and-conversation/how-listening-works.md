Chen Jing has a bad cough, and a pharmacist is explaining how to take some medicine. Chen Jing listens hard and catches these words clearly: *two*, *day*, *empty stomach*. She thanks the pharmacist and goes home, and the next morning she takes the tablets before breakfast. That evening she reads the label: *Take two tablets a day with food. Do not take on an empty stomach.*

Chen Jing heard every important word. What went wrong was not her ears but her guess: she put the words she caught into the sentence she expected, and the small word *not* — said quickly, without stress — did not fit that sentence, so she did not hear it.

A week later, her colleague Ravi says something to her in the office kitchen: [ˈwɒnə ˈɡræb ə ˈbaɪt ˈleɪtə]. This time she has no idea at all. She knows every word of *Want to grab a bite later?* — but she could not find them in the stream of sound, so she had nothing to guess with.

These two failures are opposite, and together they show what this chapter is about. Listening is not a recording of sounds in the head. A listener works on two levels at once: from the **bottom up**, turning sounds into syllables, words and sentences, and from the **top down**, using knowledge of the situation, the topic and the speaker to expect what will come. Good listening needs both. Chen Jing's first failure was a top-down guess that the sounds did not correct; her second was a bottom-up failure that left her guessing nothing.

## Two directions of processing

::: definition Bottom-up processing {#def-bottom-up}
**Bottom-up processing** (also called **decoding**) is building meaning from the signal: the listener hears sounds, groups them into syllables, recognises words, puts the words into phrases and clauses, and so arrives at the literal meaning of what was said. It works from small units to large ones.
:::

::: definition Top-down processing {#def-top-down}
**Top-down processing** is using knowledge that is not in the signal to understand it: knowledge of the world, of the situation (a shop, a lecture, a phone call), of the speaker, of the topic, of what has already been said, and of how this kind of talk usually goes. The listener uses this knowledge to **predict** what is likely to come and to **interpret** what was heard. It works from large units to small ones.
:::

The two are not separate stages. A listener does not first decode a whole sentence and then think about it: knowledge from above guides recognition below while the sentence is still being spoken, and each new word changes the expectations. The figure shows the levels and the two directions.

::: theorem Listeners combine both directions {#rule-interactive}
Successful listening uses bottom-up and top-down processing **at the same time**. Bottom-up processing gives the evidence; top-down processing gives the expectations that make the evidence quick to interpret. When the signal is clear and the topic unfamiliar, listeners rely more on decoding; when the signal is poor (noise, a fast speaker, an unfamiliar accent) or the topic familiar, they rely more on knowledge. Either can fail, and each can correct the other.
:::

::: proof
Experiments show both directions at work, even in native listeners.

- **Words heard alone are hard.** Pollack and Pickett (1963) cut single words out of recordings of fluent conversation and played them to listeners. Out of context, only about half of the words were recognised, although the same words were understood without difficulty in the original conversation. The context — the words around them — had been doing much of the work.
- **Listeners hear what they expect.** Warren (1970) replaced one sound in the word *legislatures* in a recorded sentence with a cough. Listeners reported hearing the whole word, with the missing sound, and could not say where the cough had been. This is called **phonemic restoration**: the brain fills the gap from what the word should be.
- **Knowledge must be available while listening.** Bransford and Johnson (1972) gave people a short passage that described an everyday activity in general words, without naming it. Listeners who were told the topic *before* hearing it (it was washing clothes) understood and remembered much more than listeners who heard it with no title or were told the topic only afterwards.

Without decoding, there is nothing to interpret; without knowledge, decoding is slow and every unclear word is a dead end. The figure's two columns are always working together.
:::

::: quiz
Which of these is an example of **top-down** processing?
- [ ] Hearing that a word has three syllables and the stress on the first.
- [ ] Noticing that [dʒə] is a reduced form of *do you*.
- [x] Expecting to hear a price because you are standing at a ticket counter.
- [ ] Hearing that a sentence ends with a falling tone.
::: solution
Expecting a price comes from knowledge of the situation, not from the sound itself: it is top-down. Counting syllables, recognising a reduced form and hearing a fall are all ways of working with the signal: bottom-up.
:::
:::

## Bottom-up: from sounds to words

The first bottom-up task is to divide the stream of speech into words. Speech has no spaces, and in connected speech words are reduced, linked and changed. These processes and the strategies for dealing with them — listening for the strong syllables, learning reduced chunks such as [dʒə] and [ˈwɒnə] — are taught in [[connected-speech/hearing-fast-speech]]; this chapter builds on them.

Once a listener has a few sounds, word recognition is fast. Psycholinguists describe it as a **competition**: as the first sounds of a word arrive, all the words that begin in that way become possible candidates, and the candidates are reduced as more sound arrives (Marslen-Wilson and Welsh 1978 called this set of candidates the **cohort**). On hearing [ˈkæ…], an English listener has *cat*, *can*, *cab*, *candle*, *camera*, *captain* and many more in play; [ˈkæp…] leaves *captain*, *capital*, *cap*; context removes most of the rest. A native listener usually recognises a common word before it is finished.

::: theorem What makes decoding hard {#rule-decoding}
Bottom-up processing is hard for a learner when:

1. **the word is not known** — a word you have never met cannot be recognised however clearly it is said;
2. **the word is known only in its written or careful form** — *comfortable* said as [ˈkʌmftəbl], *probably* as [ˈprɒbli], *going to* as [ˈɡənə];
3. **word boundaries are unclear** — linked forms such as *an apple* [əˈnæpl] or *get it* [ˈɡetɪt] ([[connected-speech/linking]]);
4. **the speech is fast** — there is less time for each word, and less time to think;
5. **the accent is unfamiliar** — vowels and consonants are not where the listener expects them ([[listening-and-conversation/accents]]);
6. **the signal is poor** — background noise, a bad phone line, several people speaking.
:::

::: proof
Points 1 and 2 are about vocabulary. To follow speech comfortably, a listener needs to know most of the words in it. Nation (2006), counting the words in corpora of spoken English, estimated that 6,000–7,000 word families are needed to know 98% of the running words in conversation; van Zeeland and Schmitt (2013) found that listeners could often understand adequately with lower coverage, around 95%. Either way, vocabulary sets a ceiling on listening. But knowing a word on paper is not enough: learners regularly report that they "do not recognise words they know" when they hear them (Goh 2000 lists this among the most frequent problems reported by Chinese learners in Singapore), because they have stored the written form or a careful pronunciation, not the form heard in fast speech.

Point 4 is about time. Tauroza and Allison (1990), measuring recordings of British English, found average rates of about 210 words per minute in conversation, about 190 in interviews, about 160 in radio monologues, and about 140 in lectures to non-native listeners. Conversation is the fastest kind of speech, and it is the kind learners hear least in class.
:::

::: example Which words will be hard to hear? {#ex-hard-words}
A tour guide says (GB, natural speed): *We'll probably have about twenty minutes, so you've got time for a coffee and a quick look round the market.* Which parts are likely to be hard for a learner to decode, and why?
::: solution
1. *We'll* — a weak form [wɪl] or [wl], easily lost before *probably*.
2. *probably* — usually [ˈprɒbli] or even [ˈprɒli] in fast speech: the learner may know only [ˈprɒbəbli].
3. *have about* — weak *have* [əv] linked to *about* [əˈbaʊt]: [əvəˈbaʊt], three weak syllables before the stress.
4. *twenty* — clear, because it is stressed and important; in GA it may be [ˈtwʌni] or [ˈtweni], with no /t/ after /n/.
5. *so you've got* — [səʊ juv ˈɡɒt]: *you've* is weak and may sound like *you*.
6. *a quick look round the market* — clear content words *quick*, *look*, *round*, *market*; *a* and *the* are weak.

The stressed words — *probably*, *twenty*, *minutes*, *time*, *coffee*, *quick*, *look*, *market* — carry the message; the hard parts are mostly weak function words. A listener who catches the stressed words and knows the situation (a tour stop) can rebuild the rest: *about twenty minutes, time for coffee and the market*.
:::
:::

## Top-down: knowledge and expectation

::: definition Schema {#def-schema}
A **schema** (plural **schemata** or **schemas**) is an organised body of knowledge about a familiar kind of situation or topic: what usually happens, in what order, who says what, and which words are likely. A *restaurant* schema, for example, includes being shown to a table, being offered menus and drinks, ordering, eating, asking for the bill and paying. Listeners use schemata to predict and to fill gaps.
:::

Listeners bring several kinds of knowledge to what they hear:

- **The situation.** At a check-in desk you expect *passport*, *bags*, *window or aisle*. In a doctor's surgery you expect *How long have you had it?*
- **The speaker.** A colleague who always complains about the traffic will probably start with the traffic.
- **The topic.** If you know something about football, a match report is far easier to follow than one about a sport you do not know.
- **What was said before.** A pronoun such as *it* or *they* can only be understood if you remember what it refers to; a question limits what the answer can be.
- **The type of talk.** A news report gives the main point first; a joke keeps it until the end; a story begins with who, where and when ([[listening-and-conversation/storytelling]]).

::: example Predicting before you listen {#ex-predict}
Zhao Lin is about to phone a hotel to book a room. Before the call, list what she can expect the receptionist to ask, and the words she should be ready to hear.
::: solution
From her schema of hotel bookings:

1. **Dates** — *When would you like to arrive? How many nights?* Words: *arriving*, *checking out*, *nights*, dates such as *the twelfth*.
2. **People and room type** — *How many guests? A double or a twin? Single occupancy?*
3. **Price and conditions** — *That's £120 a night, including breakfast. Is that OK?* Words: *including*, *excluding*, *VAT*, *deposit*, *cancellation*.
4. **Name and details** — *Can I take a name? Could you spell that? And a contact number?* ([[everyday-english/personal-information]])
5. **Payment** — *Can I take a card to secure the booking?*

With this list, Zhao Lin does not have to decode every word from nothing. When she hears [ˈtwɪn ɔː ˈdʌbl], her expectation of a room-type question makes it easy to recognise *twin or double*. The list also shows where she must listen carefully — numbers, dates and prices are hard to predict and must be decoded ([[listening-and-conversation/gist-and-detail]]).
:::
:::

::: theorem Check your guesses {#rule-check-guesses}
Top-down expectations are guesses, and guesses can be wrong. A good listener **holds an interpretation lightly** and changes it when the signal does not fit. In particular, listen carefully to words that can reverse a meaning even when they are short and unstressed: *not* and *n't*, *no*, *never*, *only*, *except*, *unless*, *without*, *before* and *after*. When something important depends on them, check ([[listening-and-conversation/clarification]]).
:::

::: proof
Expectation makes listening fast, but it also makes mishearing likely when the speaker says something unexpected. Chen Jing's mistake at the start of the chapter is typical: she expected an instruction about *when* to take the tablets and fitted *empty stomach* into it, while the speaker had said that the tablets should *not* be taken that way. The negative was short and unstressed — *Do ˈnot take* would have been clear, but *Don't ˈtake them on an ˈempty ˈstomach* puts the stress on the content words. A listener cannot hear everything perfectly, but she can notice that an important instruction depends on a small word, and check: *Sorry — so I should take them with food, not before?*
:::

::: warning Guessing without checking
✗ Hearing a few words, deciding what the speaker probably means, and stopping listening.
✓ Making a guess, listening on to see whether it fits, and checking when the meaning matters.

✗ *The meeting's been moved — so it's tomorrow.* (The listener guessed the new day.)
✓ *The meeting's been moved? To when?*

Top-down processing is not "guessing instead of listening". It is guessing *while* listening. A listener who stops decoding once she has a guess will make confident mistakes.
:::

::: quiz
A friend says: *I'd love to come to the party, but I'm working on Saturday.* You catch *love to come* and *party* clearly, but not the rest. What is the best thing to do?
- [ ] Conclude that your friend is coming, because you heard *love to come*.
- [ ] Say nothing, because asking would be rude.
- [x] Notice that *but* introduced something after the part you heard, and ask: *Sorry — so can you come or not?*
- [ ] Ask your friend to repeat the whole sentence word by word.
::: solution
*I'd love to…, but…* is a common way of saying **no** politely; the important information comes after *but*. A listener who stops at *love to come* will get it wrong. Asking a short, specific question is quick and natural; asking for a word-by-word repetition is slow and unnecessary.
:::
:::

## Why listening breaks down

Learners often describe their listening problems in the same words: *It's too fast*, *I understand the words but not the meaning*, *I miss the beginning of the next sentence while I'm still thinking about the last one*. Goh (2000), who asked Chinese students in Singapore to keep listening diaries, found problems at every stage of processing: not recognising known words, missing the start of the next part while thinking about the last, understanding the words but not the message, and quickly forgetting what had been heard.

::: theorem Common causes of breakdown {#rule-breakdown}
When listening breaks down, the cause is usually one of these:

1. **Decoding** — the words were unknown, or known but not recognised in their spoken form ([[#rule-decoding]]).
2. **Speed and memory** — the listener was still working on one sentence when the next began, and lost both. Holding unfamiliar sounds in memory is tiring; the more effort decoding takes, the less attention is left for meaning.
3. **Getting stuck** — one unknown word stops the listener, who tries to work it out and misses what follows.
4. **Missing knowledge** — the listener did not know the topic, the situation or a cultural reference.
5. **Wrong expectation** — the listener built the wrong picture early and fitted everything into it ([[#rule-check-guesses]]).
6. **Translation** — the listener translated each sentence into their first language before understanding it, which takes time the speaker does not give.
7. **Anxiety** — worry about not understanding uses up attention and makes the other problems worse.
:::

::: proof
The causes connect. Slow decoding (1) uses up attention, so less remains for holding sentences in memory (2) and for noticing that an expectation is wrong (5). Translation (6) adds a step to every sentence, and anxiety (7) both results from the others and makes them worse. The practical lesson is that improving any one part helps the rest. Faster recognition of common words and chunks frees attention for meaning; good prediction makes decoding easier; a calm decision to let an unknown word go keeps the listener with the speaker.
:::

::: example Diagnosing a breakdown {#ex-diagnose}
Here are four learners' notes after a listening task. For each, say which cause from [[#rule-breakdown]] is the main problem and suggest one thing to do about it.

1. *"The speaker said a word like 'tenure' and I spent the next ten seconds trying to think what it meant. After that I was lost."*
2. *"I understood every sentence, but I didn't know why they were laughing about the 'M25'."*
3. *"I caught 'flight', 'cancelled' and 'tomorrow', so I thought my flight was cancelled. Actually they said the cancelled flights were yesterday's and tomorrow's were fine."*
4. *"When I read the transcript afterwards, I knew all the words. I just couldn't hear 'I should've asked her'."*
::: solution
1. **Getting stuck** (3). Let the unknown word go; keep listening for the next stressed words, and come back to the word later — the context may explain it, and if not, you can ask or look it up.
2. **Missing knowledge** (4). The M25 is the motorway around London, famous for its traffic jams: the joke needs that cultural fact. Nothing in the sound would have helped. Background reading on a topic before listening, and asking, are the solutions.
3. **Wrong expectation** (5). The listener built a picture from three words and did not notice that *yesterday* and *fine* did not fit it ([[#rule-check-guesses]]). Listen for words that change the time or reverse the meaning, and check before acting.
4. **Decoding a known form** (1). *Should've asked her* is [ˈʃʊdəv ˈɑːskt ə] or [ˈʃʊdə ˈɑːstə] in fast speech: weak *have*, elided /k/ and /t/, weak *her*. The learner knows the written form but not the spoken one; dictation and shadowing of such chunks help ([[connected-speech/hearing-fast-speech#rule-practice]]).
:::
:::

## Listening in a conversation

Much listening practice uses recordings, which you cannot stop or question. In conversation, listening is different: you can ask, and the speaker will usually adjust. This two-way listening is easier in one respect — you can ask for help — and harder in another: you must prepare your own answer while you listen.

::: example Rebuilding a turn in conversation {#ex-rebuild}
Kwame is visiting his friend Li Na's flat for the first time. She says something as they go in, and he catches only the stressed words:

**Li Na:** *… ˈshoes … ˈdoor … ˈslippers … ˈbasket …*

What does Kwame do with this?
::: solution
1. **Bottom-up**: four clear content words — *shoes*, *door*, *slippers*, *basket*.
2. **Top-down**: the situation (entering a home), the speaker (Li Na, who might keep the custom of taking shoes off at home), and the order of the words suggest an instruction or an offer: *You can leave your shoes by the door — there are some slippers in the basket.*
3. **Check**: the guess is very likely, but the action matters (he might be meant to put his shoes *in* the basket). A quick check with a small action is natural: *Here? Great — thanks!* while putting the shoes down by the door.

In a conversation, the listener does not have to understand everything before acting: he can show what he understood and let the speaker correct it. This is the subject of [[listening-and-conversation/clarification]].
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Syllables and words.** Mandarin has a small set of syllable types — about 400 without tones — and nearly every syllable keeps a full vowel and its tone, so boundaries between syllables are clear. English has thousands of possible syllables, unstressed syllables are reduced, and word boundaries are hidden by linking. A Chinese listener's habit of attending equally to every syllable does not work for English: attend to the stressed syllables ([[connected-speech/hearing-fast-speech#rule-strong]]).
- **Exam listening.** Many learners in China have practised listening mainly with exam recordings: clear, carefully read, often at moderate speed, with questions printed in advance. That is good training for decoding careful speech and for predicting from questions, but it gives little practice with conversation at 200 words per minute, with overlaps and reduced forms. Add natural speech to your practice ([[listening-and-conversation/learning-from-media]]).
- **Translating as you listen.** Readers can stop and translate; listeners cannot. If you notice that you are turning each sentence into Chinese, try to listen for a picture or a gist instead, and translate only key words if you must.
- **Reading strength, listening weakness.** Many Chinese learners can read texts far harder than the speech they can follow. The gap is usually not vocabulary but spoken forms: the words are known in their written form. This is good news — the knowledge is already there, and listening practice connects it to sound.
:::

::: history
For a long time, listening in language teaching was treated as a passive skill: learners heard a text and answered questions about it, which tested comprehension but did not teach it. In the 1970s and 1980s, research on how people understand speech changed this view. Psychologists showed that listeners use their knowledge actively: Richard Warren's phonemic restoration experiment (1970) and John Bransford and Marcia Johnson's work on context and comprehension (1972) are two famous examples. Gillian Brown and George Yule's *Teaching the Spoken Language* (1983) and Anne Anderson and Tony Lynch's *Listening* (1988) brought these ideas into teaching, describing listening as an active process of building an interpretation. In the 2000s, Christine Goh, Larry Vandergrift and John Field argued that learners should be taught the processes and strategies of listening directly — how to predict, monitor and repair their understanding — rather than only tested on the result.
:::

::: summary
- Listening combines **bottom-up** processing (from sounds to words to meaning) and **top-down** processing (from knowledge and expectation), at the same time ([[#def-bottom-up]], [[#def-top-down]], [[#rule-interactive]]).
- Even native listeners recognise only about half of words cut out of conversation (Pollack and Pickett 1963), and they "hear" sounds they expect (Warren 1970): context does much of the work.
- Decoding is hard when words are unknown, known only in written form, linked, fast, in an unfamiliar accent, or in noise ([[#rule-decoding]]); conversation, at about 210 words per minute, is the fastest kind of speech (Tauroza and Allison 1990).
- **Schemata** — knowledge of situations and topics — let you predict; use them before and during listening ([[#def-schema]]).
- Expectations are guesses: listen for short words that reverse the meaning, and check when it matters ([[#rule-check-guesses]]).
- Breakdowns usually come from decoding, speed and memory, getting stuck, missing knowledge, wrong expectations, translation or anxiety; improving one helps the others ([[#rule-breakdown]]).
:::

You are going to hear a short weather forecast for the weekend. (a) Write six words you expect. (b) Here is the forecast. Which of your words appeared, and which words in it were harder to predict?

::: solution
(a) Likely predictions: *sunny*, *rain*, *showers*, *cloud*, *wind*, *degrees*, *temperature*, *dry*, *cold*.
(b) Predictable words in the forecast: *dry*, *bright*, *cloud*, *showers*, *wetter*, *windier*, *degrees*. Harder to predict: *build from the west*, *coastal areas*, *highs of only eleven* — these are the details that make this forecast different from others: where, when and how much. They must be decoded, and they are often what you need ([[listening-and-conversation/gist-and-detail]]).

::: exercise Rebuild the message {level=3}
On a noisy train, you hear only these stressed words of an announcement: *… ˈapologise … ˈdelay … ˈsignal ˈproblem … ˈBirmingham … ˈtwenty ˈminutes … ˈconnecting ˈservices …* Write the most likely full announcement, then say which parts you would check with another passenger and why.
::: hint
Use your schema of train announcements: who is speaking, what they usually say first, and what passengers need to know.
:::
::: solution
A likely announcement: *We apologise for the delay to this service. This is due to a signal problem near Birmingham. We expect to be running about twenty minutes late. Passengers with connecting services should speak to a member of staff.* (Other wordings are possible.)

Parts to check: **twenty minutes** — is it *twenty* or *twenty-five*, *late* or *more*? **Birmingham** — is the problem near Birmingham, or does the train now stop at Birmingham? **connecting services** — what should passengers with connections do? These are the details that affect your actions, and they cannot be predicted from the schema. A short question to another passenger is natural: *Sorry, did they say twenty minutes?*
:::

At the end of the week, look for patterns and choose one thing to practise.

Answers vary. A useful diary is specific: not *"It was too fast"* but *"I lost the speaker after 'which is why we decided…' because I was still thinking about 'redundancy', which I didn't know"* (getting stuck). Common patterns are (1) known words not recognised in fast speech — practise with dictation and chunks ([[connected-speech/hearing-fast-speech]]); (2) getting stuck on unknown words — practise letting go and listening for the next stress; (3) missing background — read about a topic before listening. Choosing one target for the next week is more useful than trying to fix everything.
