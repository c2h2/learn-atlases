On Monday morning, a colleague asks Pedro: *Good weekend?* Here is his first answer.

**Pedro:** *Yes. On Saturday I went to my friend's wedding in the countryside. I took the train. The train stopped. I walked to the next station. I arrived late. Then there was dinner.*

Every sentence is correct, and something quite dramatic happened. But the colleague says *Oh, right* and turns back to her computer. Here is the same weekend told again.

**Pedro:** *Oh, you won't believe what happened. So on Saturday I was going to my friend's wedding — it was out in the countryside, in this tiny village. And I'm on the train, in my suit, and about twenty minutes before we get there, the train just stops. In the middle of a field. And the guard comes on and says there's a tree on the line and we'll be there "for at least two hours".*

**Colleague:** *Oh no! What did you do?*

**Pedro:** *Well, I looked at the map, and the next station was about three kilometres away, so I just got off and walked. In my wedding shoes! Across a field full of cows.*

**Colleague:** *No way!*

**Pedro:** *I got to the church just as they were coming out. Covered in mud. But honestly, the bride laughed so much she said it was the best present she got.*

**Colleague:** *Ha! That's brilliant.*

The facts are the same. The difference is in how the story is told: it announces that something worth hearing is coming, sets the scene, slows down at the problem, uses the speaker's feelings and other people's words, and ends with a point. And the listener takes part. This chapter teaches how to tell a short personal story — an **anecdote** — in conversation, and how to listen to one. It uses the narrative tenses taught in [[tense-and-aspect/narrative-tenses]] and the back-channels of [[listening-and-conversation/turn-taking]].

## The shape of a story

::: definition Anecdote {#def-anecdote}
An **anecdote** is a short spoken story about something that happened, usually to the speaker or to someone they know, told in conversation because it is funny, surprising, embarrassing, frightening or touching. It usually takes from a few sentences to two or three minutes.
:::

People tell anecdotes all the time — about a journey, a misunderstanding, a holiday, a strange customer — and they follow a surprisingly regular pattern.

::: theorem The parts of a spoken story {#rule-parts}
A well-told personal story usually has these parts:

1. **Abstract** — an announcement that a story is coming and what kind it is: *You'll never guess what happened on Saturday.*
2. **Orientation** — who, where, when, and what was going on: *I was going to my friend's wedding, out in the countryside.*
3. **Complicating action** — the events, in order, building to a problem or high point: *the train stops… the guard says… so I got off and walked.*
4. **Evaluation** — the speaker's feelings and comments that show why the story matters: *In my wedding shoes!*, *you won't believe…*
5. **Resolution** — how it ended: *I got to the church just as they were coming out.*
6. **Coda** — a final comment that brings the listener back to the present: *She said it was the best present she got.* / *I'll never take that train again!*

The abstract, orientation, action and resolution come roughly in order; evaluation can appear anywhere, but is strongest just before the high point.
:::

::: proof
This description comes from William Labov and Joshua Waletzky (1967), who recorded people telling stories about their own experiences and found the same structure again and again; Labov (1972) added the abstract. Each part answers a question that a listener might ask. The abstract answers *What is this about, and should I listen?*; the orientation answers *Who? When? Where?*; the complicating action answers *Then what happened?*; the evaluation answers *So what? Why are you telling me this?*; the resolution answers *What finally happened?*; and the coda tells the listener that the story is over and it is their turn. Not every story has every part — a two-sentence story may have only action and evaluation — but stories that leave out the orientation confuse their listeners, and stories without evaluation leave them asking *So what?*
:::

::: example Labelling a story {#ex-labels}
Label the parts of this anecdote, told by Asha to a friend.

*Did I ever tell you about my first day as a waitress? Oh, it was a disaster. I was nineteen, and I'd got a summer job in this really posh restaurant by the river. And on my very first evening, I was carrying three bowls of soup to a table of businessmen — and I tripped over a bag on the floor. The soup went everywhere. All over the table, all over one of the men's shirts. I just wanted to die. But he was really nice about it — he said, "Don't worry, I hated this shirt anyway," and gave me a huge tip at the end. So I learned two things that night: always look at the floor, and some people are lovely.*
::: solution
- **Abstract**: *Did I ever tell you about my first day as a waitress? Oh, it was a disaster.* — announces the story and its kind (a disaster).
- **Orientation**: *I was nineteen, and I'd got a summer job in this really posh restaurant by the river.* — who, when, where; past progressive and past perfect for background.
- **Complicating action**: *on my very first evening, I was carrying three bowls of soup… I tripped over a bag… The soup went everywhere.*
- **Evaluation**: *it was a disaster*, *my very first evening*, *All over the table, all over one of the men's shirts*, *I just wanted to die*.
- **Resolution**: *But he was really nice about it — he said, "…," and gave me a huge tip at the end.*
- **Coda**: *So I learned two things that night: always look at the floor, and some people are lovely.*
:::
:::

## Starting a story

A story needs a long turn, and in conversation long turns have to be negotiated ([[listening-and-conversation/turn-taking]]). The abstract does this: it asks the listeners for the floor, and they usually agree.

- *You'll never guess what happened to me yesterday.*
- *Did I tell you about my trip to Kunming?*
- *The funniest thing happened at work today.*
- *I had a nightmare of a journey this morning.*
- *That reminds me of something that happened when I was a student.*

The listener answers — *No, what?*, *Go on*, *Oh no, what happened?* — and the story can begin. If the listener has heard it before, they can say so now (*Oh, the one about the cows? Yes, you told me!*).

The orientation then sets the scene. It uses the **past progressive** for what was going on (*I was going to a wedding*), the **past perfect** for what had happened before (*I'd got a summer job*), and words for time and place (*last summer*, *on my first evening*, *in this tiny village*). In informal stories, *this* often introduces a new person or thing that the listener does not know: *this tiny village*, *this man came up to me*.

## Telling the events

::: theorem Tenses and links in a spoken story {#rule-tenses}
1. **Past simple** for the main events, in order: *The train stopped. I got off and walked.*
2. **Past progressive** for background and for an action interrupted by an event: *I was carrying three bowls of soup when I tripped.*
3. **Past perfect** for an event earlier than the story's main time: *I'd never been to that village before.*
4. **Time links** to move the story forward: *then*, *after that*, *so*, *suddenly*, *all of a sudden*, *just as*, *as soon as*, *by the time*, *in the end*, *eventually*.

The forms and meanings of these tenses are taught in [[tense-and-aspect/narrative-tenses]]; in a spoken story, getting the main events in clear order matters more than using every tense.
:::

### The present tense in stories

In informal spoken stories, English speakers often switch to the **present** tense for the most exciting part: *So I'm on the train, and the train just stops. And the guard comes on and says…* This is called the **historical present** (or *narrative present*).

::: theorem The historical present {#rule-present}
In informal storytelling, speakers may tell the main events in the present simple and present progressive, especially at the high point, to make the listener feel that it is happening now. They usually begin in the past (in the abstract and orientation), switch to the present for the action, and return to the past or present perfect for the ending. In formal or written narrative — a report, an essay, an exam answer — keep to the past tenses.
:::

::: proof
Studies of recorded conversational stories found that the historical present is common in informal English storytelling and that speakers move between past and present within the same story. Nessa Wolfson (1979) argued that the **switch** itself is what matters: a change of tense marks the boundaries between events and highlights the part the speaker wants to dramatise. Deborah Schiffrin (1981) found that the present was used mostly in the complicating action, not in the orientation or the coda. For learners, this means the historical present is something to **recognise** when listening (*So she goes to the manager and says…* is about the past) and something you can use in casual stories, but not something you must use.
:::

### Reporting what people said

Stories are often built around what people said. In conversation, speakers usually use **direct speech** — they "perform" the words — rather than reported speech:

- *And he said, "Don't worry, I hated this shirt anyway."*
- *And she goes, "Are you sure you're in the right place?"* (informal: *go* = 'say')
- *And I was like, "What? Two hours?"* (informal: *be like* introduces words or a reaction)

*Say* is neutral in every register. *Go* and *be like* are informal and common in younger speakers' stories: Tagliamonte and D'Arcy (2004), studying the speech of young people in Toronto, found that *be like* had become the most frequent way of introducing quoted speech in the youngest age groups. Use *said* in anything formal; recognise *goes* and *was like* when you hear them. Reported speech (*He said that he had hated the shirt*) is more common in writing and in formal retelling ([[complex-sentences/reported-speech]]).

::: example Present or past? {#ex-tenses}
Here is part of a story told by Hamid. (1) Which tense does he use for the action, and why? (2) Retell it in the past for a written account.

*So last week I'm at the airport, right, and I'm about to go through security, and this woman taps me on the shoulder and says, "Excuse me, is this yours?" And she's holding my passport. I'd dropped it at the check-in desk!*
::: solution
(1) Hamid begins with a past time (*last week*) but tells the action in the **historical present** (*I'm at the airport*, *I'm about to go*, *taps*, *says*, *she's holding*), which makes the moment vivid. He switches back to the **past perfect** (*I'd dropped it*) for the earlier event that explains everything — the high point of the story.

(2) *Last week, I was at the airport and was about to go through security when a woman tapped me on the shoulder and asked whether it was mine. She was holding my passport. I had dropped it at the check-in desk.*

The written version uses the past simple for events, the past progressive for background, the past perfect for the earlier event, and reported speech. It is accurate, but much less dramatic.
:::
:::

## Making it worth hearing: evaluation

The difference between Pedro's two versions is mostly **evaluation**: everything that shows the listener why the story is interesting.

::: theorem Ways of evaluating {#rule-evaluation}
Speakers show what makes a story worth telling by:

1. **Saying how they felt**: *I was terrified*, *I just wanted to die*, *I couldn't stop laughing*.
2. **Strong and extreme words**: *absolutely*, *completely*, *totally*, *a nightmare*, *a disaster*, *brilliant*, *freezing*, *packed*.
3. **Exaggeration** that both speakers know is exaggeration: *about a million people*, *I waited for ever*.
4. **Direct speech**, performed with the speaker's voice: *And he goes, "At least two hours."*
5. **Comments to the listener**: *Can you imagine?*, *You know what I mean?*, *Honestly!*, *I'm not joking.*
6. **Details that matter**: *in my wedding shoes*, *across a field full of cows* — chosen for their effect, not because every detail must be told.
7. **Slowing down and pausing** just before the high point, and changing the voice (pitch, speed, loudness).
:::

::: proof
Labov (1972) argued that evaluation is the most important part of a personal story, because it answers the listener's unspoken question *So what?* A story that is only a list of events — like Pedro's first version — leaves the listener unsure how to react: is this funny, sad, annoying? Evaluation tells them, and so it also tells them what back-channels to give (*No way!*, *Oh no!*). The devices above are found in stories told in many languages. In English, intonation does much of the work: a wide pitch range on *a disaster* or *a field full of cows* is itself evaluation ([[connected-speech/intonation-meaning#rule-range]]), while a narrow range can make even an exciting story sound flat.
:::

::: example Bringing a story to life {#ex-improve}
Wang Fang's story is accurate but flat. Rewrite it with an abstract, a clearer orientation, evaluation and a coda.

*Last month I went to a job interview. I took the wrong bus. I phoned the company. They said it was OK. I arrived at 3.30, not 3.00. I got the job.*
::: solution
A model rewrite:

*You know my new job? Well, I almost didn't get it. So last month I had the interview — it was at three o'clock, on the other side of the city, and I was so nervous I'd hardly slept. I got on the bus, and I was reading my notes, going over my answers — and after about twenty minutes I looked up, and I had absolutely no idea where I was. Wrong bus. Completely the wrong direction! I just thought, "That's it. It's over." But I phoned the company, really embarrassed, and the woman was so kind. She said, "Don't worry — come when you can." I got there at half three, red in the face — and they offered me the job the next day! So now, whenever I'm nervous, I check the bus number twice.*

Added: an **abstract** (*I almost didn't get it*), **orientation** with background (*on the other side of the city*, *I'd hardly slept*, *I was reading my notes*), **evaluation** (feelings, *absolutely no idea*, *Completely the wrong direction!*, her own thought and the woman's words in direct speech), a clearer **resolution** (*they offered me the job*) and a **coda** (*whenever I'm nervous, I check the bus number twice*).
:::
:::

::: warning Telling a story in the present by mistake
✗ *Yesterday I go to the station and I see my old teacher.*
✓ *Yesterday I went to the station and I saw my old teacher.*
✓ (informal, deliberate) *So yesterday I'm at the station, and who do I see? My old teacher!*

The historical present is a choice, used for effect in the action of an informal story, usually after a past-tense start and with the present continuous (*I'm at the station*). Present verbs used throughout because the past forms are forgotten sound like a grammar error, and listeners may be unsure when the story happened. Learners whose first language does not mark tense on the verb (Chinese uses words such as 昨天 and particles such as 了) often drop the past ending in fast speech. If you are not sure, use the past.
:::

::: quiz
Which opening is the best **abstract** for a funny story about getting lost?
- [ ] *On Tuesday at 9.15 I left my house.*
- [x] *Did I tell you about the time I got lost in my own office building?*
- [ ] *I got lost.*
- [ ] *Getting lost is a common experience.*
::: solution
*Did I tell you about the time…?* announces a story, tells the listener what kind it is (funny — lost in *my own office building*), and invites a reply (*No, what happened?*). The first sentence starts with orientation details before the listener knows why to listen; *I got lost* gives away the point; the last sounds like the start of an essay.
:::

## Endings

A story needs a clear ending, so that the listener knows when to react and when it is their turn. The **resolution** says how things turned out; the **coda** closes the story:

- *So that was my weekend!*
- *Anyway, that's why I never take that bus any more.*
- *I'll never do that again, I can tell you.*
- *And we still laugh about it.*
- *So yeah. Never again.*

The coda is often said with a falling tone and a lower pitch: a signal that the turn is ending ([[listening-and-conversation/turn-taking#rule-ending]]).

## Listening to stories

Stories are told **with** listeners. A listener who says nothing makes the teller unsure; a listener who reacts at the right moments helps the story along.

::: theorem Reacting to a story {#rule-react}
1. **After the abstract**: invite the story — *No, what happened?* / *Go on!*
2. **During the orientation**: continuers — *Mm*, *Right*, *Yeah*.
3. **At the problem**: news markers and questions — *Oh no!*, *Really?*, *What did you do?*
4. **At the high point**: strong assessments — *No way!*, *You're joking!*, *How embarrassing!*
5. **At the end**: an assessment that matches the story — *What a nightmare!*, *That's hilarious*, *Oh, that's lovely* — and perhaps a question (*Did you ever see him again?*) or a short **second story** of your own (*That reminds me of when I…*), keeping the attention on the first story before moving to yours.
:::

::: example Listening well {#ex-react}
Write suitable listener reactions at each point in this story by Yuki.

*You'll never guess who I met on the plane.* (1) *So I was flying back from Singapore, and I was sitting next to this older man, and we started chatting.* (2) *And after about an hour he asks me where I went to school, and I tell him, and he says, "I taught there for thirty years."* (3) *It turns out he was my mum's English teacher!* (4) *So now they're having lunch together next week.* (5)
::: solution
1. *No — who?* / *Go on!* — inviting the story.
2. *Mm.* / *Right.* — continuers during the orientation.
3. *No!* / *Really?* — a news marker at the surprise.
4. *You're joking! That's amazing!* — a strong assessment at the high point.
5. *Oh, that's lovely. What a small world!* — a matching assessment at the end, perhaps followed by *Did your mum remember him?*
:::
:::

::: quiz
A friend finishes a story: *…and that's how I ended up sleeping at the airport. Never again!* What is the best response?
- [ ] *OK.*
- [ ] *I slept at an airport too. It was in Dubai, and the chairs were very uncomfortable, and then in the morning I…*
- [x] *Oh no, what a nightmare! Did you get any sleep at all?*
- [ ] *Why did you not book a hotel?*
::: solution
The third response gives an assessment that matches the story and a sympathetic follow-up question. *OK* is too weak. The second is a long **shift response** that takes the attention away ([[listening-and-conversation/turn-taking#rule-building]]). The fourth sounds like criticism, and *Why didn't you…?* would be the natural form.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Tense.** Chinese marks time with words (昨天, 那时候) and aspect with particles (了, 过, 着), not with verb endings, so learners often lose the past ending in fast speech: ✗ *Yesterday I go…* Practise telling the same short story several times, paying attention to the verbs ([[tense-and-aspect/tense-and-chinese]]).
- **然后 and *and then*.** A Chinese story often links events with 然后; translated, this becomes *and then… and then… and then…*. Vary the links: *so*, *after that*, *suddenly*, *just as*, *in the end*.
- **我跟你说 and *You know what?*** Chinese abstracts such as 我跟你说, 你猜怎么着 work exactly like English *You'll never guess…*, *You know what?*, *Guess what?* — use them.
- **Modesty and evaluation.** Some learners feel that dramatic evaluation (*It was absolutely amazing!*) sounds like showing off. In English anecdotes it is expected and usually heard as enthusiasm, not boasting — especially when the story is at your own expense. Laughing at yourself is one of the most common kinds of English anecdote.
- **Names in stories.** If you mention Chinese friends, help the listener: *my friend Xiaoyu — she's the one who lives in Shanghai*. New names are hard to catch; one extra phrase makes the orientation clear.
:::

::: history
The modern study of everyday storytelling began with William Labov and Joshua Waletzky's paper "Narrative analysis: oral versions of personal experience" (1967), based on stories recorded in interviews, often in answer to the question whether the speaker had ever been in danger of death. Labov developed the analysis in *Language in the Inner City* (1972), adding the abstract and arguing that evaluation is what makes a story "reportable". In the late 1970s and early 1980s, Nessa Wolfson and Deborah Schiffrin studied how speakers switch between past and present tense in conversational stories. Conversation analysts, starting with Harvey Sacks's lectures in the 1960s and 1970s, showed how stories are fitted into conversation: how tellers get a long turn, and how listeners take part. Neal Norrick's *Conversational Narrative* (2000) brought these strands together.
:::

::: summary
- An **anecdote** is a short personal story told in conversation ([[#def-anecdote]]).
- Stories usually have an abstract, orientation, complicating action, evaluation, resolution and coda (Labov and Waletzky 1967; Labov 1972; [[#rule-parts]]).
- Start with an abstract to get the floor (*You'll never guess…*), and set the scene with the past progressive and past perfect.
- Tell events in the past simple with clear time links ([[#rule-tenses]]); informal stories may switch to the **historical present** at the high point ([[#rule-present]]).
- Report speech directly — *he said*, informally *she goes* or *I was like* — and **evaluate**: feelings, strong words, exaggeration, details, comments, pauses ([[#rule-evaluation]]).
- End clearly with a resolution and a coda; as a listener, react at each stage, with an assessment at the end ([[#rule-react]]).

::: exercise Analyse a conversation {level=3}
Read this exchange. Identify the parts of Laila's story and the moves of the listener, Ben. Then say how Ben could have been a better listener at two points.

**Laila:** *Have I told you about my driving test?*

**Ben:** *No.*

**Laila:** *So it was raining really hard, and the examiner was this very serious man. Ten minutes in, I'm doing a perfect three-point turn, and a cat runs out in front of me. I brake so hard that his clipboard flies out of his hands.*

**Ben:** *OK.*

**Laila:** *And I think, that's it, I've failed. But he just picks up the clipboard and says, "Good reactions." And I passed!*

**Ben:** *I passed first time too.*
::: solution
**Story parts.** Abstract: *Have I told you about my driving test?* Orientation: *it was raining really hard, and the examiner was this very serious man.* Action, in the historical present: *I'm doing…, a cat runs out…, I brake…, his clipboard flies…*. Evaluation: *really hard*, *very serious*, *perfect*, *I think, that's it, I've failed* (her thought as direct speech). Resolution: *he… says, "Good reactions." And I passed!* There is no separate coda — *And I passed!* ends the story.

**Ben's moves.** *No.* — a minimal answer to the abstract; *No — what happened?* would invite the story. *OK.* — a weak acknowledgement at the high point (the flying clipboard), where an assessment (*No way!*, *Oh no!*) was needed. *I passed first time too.* — a shift response that turns attention to himself and ignores the story's point.

**Better**: at the high point, *Oh no! What did he say?*; at the end, *Ha! "Good reactions" — that's brilliant. You must have been so relieved!*
