Wu Jie has watched hundreds of hours of English-language television series with Chinese subtitles. He knows the characters, the jokes and the endings. But when he tries to watch an episode without subtitles, he understands much less than he expected, and in conversation with his English-speaking colleagues his listening has not improved much. His classmate Nadia has watched far less, but differently: short videos on topics she knows well, first with English subtitles, then without, often several times, sometimes repeating the speakers' sentences aloud. After a year, her listening is clearly better than his.

The difference is not the amount of English but **how** it was used. Recordings, podcasts, films, television and online talks are the richest source of spoken English most learners have — far more voices, accents and situations than any classroom. But listening to media is not automatically learning. This chapter shows how to choose material at the right level, how to use subtitles in stages, how to combine **extensive** listening for quantity with **intensive** listening for detail, how to practise **shadowing**, and how words you have studied become words you can hear. It puts into practice the ideas of [[listening-and-conversation/how-listening-works]] and [[connected-speech/hearing-fast-speech#rule-practice]], and it is the listening partner of [[reading-skills/extensive-reading]].

## Choosing material

::: theorem Choose material you can mostly follow {#rule-choose}
For regular listening practice, choose recordings that:

1. **you can mostly understand** — the gist without effort and most of the details with some effort;
2. **are about topics you know or care about**, so that your background knowledge helps ([[listening-and-conversation/how-listening-works#def-schema]]);
3. **have good sound** and, at first, one or two clear speakers rather than many people talking at once;
4. **are short enough to repeat** — for intensive work, two to five minutes;
5. **come from many speakers and accents** over time ([[listening-and-conversation/accents#rule-adapt]]).
:::

::: proof
Studies of vocabulary coverage explain the first point. Webb and Rodgers (2009) analysed the language of a large collection of films and television programmes and estimated that a viewer needs to know about the 3,000 most frequent word families, together with proper nouns and marginal words such as *um*, to know 95% of the words, and roughly twice as many for 98%. Below that level, too many words are unknown for the listener to guess the rest from context ([[core-vocabulary/frequency-and-coverage]]). Familiar topics help because top-down knowledge fills gaps that the sound alone leaves. Clear sound and few speakers make decoding possible while it is still slow. Short recordings can be heard again and again — the only way to work on details. And many voices teach the listener what belongs to the language rather than to one speaker.
:::

Different kinds of material suit different stages:

| material | why it helps | difficulty |
|---|---|---|
| **graded audio** for learners (audio versions of graded readers, learner podcasts) | controlled vocabulary, clear speech, often with a script | easiest |
| **talks and lectures** on familiar topics | one speaker, planned speech, often a transcript or captions | medium |
| **news** reports | standard accents, clear structure, but dense information and fast speech | medium to hard |
| **interviews and conversational podcasts** | real conversation, back-channels, hesitations, several speakers | medium to hard |
| **documentaries** | clear narration, pictures that support the meaning | medium |
| **films and television drama** | many speakers, slang, emotion, noise, fast overlapping speech | hardest |

The order in this table is a rough guide, not a rule. A comedy series about a workplace you know may be easier than a news report on economics. The test is always [[#rule-choose]]: can you mostly follow it?

::: example Choosing for a learner {#ex-choose}
Three learners ask for advice. For each, suggest material and explain why.

1. Li Na understands slow, clear speech but gets lost in normal conversation. She loves cooking.
2. Rafael can follow the news but finds films very hard. He is a nurse.
3. Kim Ji-woo understands most American speakers but almost nothing in a British crime drama.
::: solution
1. **Li Na**: cooking videos and podcasts, starting with one presenter who explains as they cook. The topic is familiar and the pictures support the meaning ([[#rule-choose]], points 1 and 2). Later, cooking competitions with several speakers and natural conversation.
2. **Rafael**: interviews and conversational podcasts about health and nursing — real conversation on a familiar topic — before moving to hospital dramas, where the topic still helps with the faster, emotional speech of films.
3. **Kim Ji-woo**: short interviews and talks with British speakers from different regions, then the drama itself with English subtitles at first ([[#rule-subtitles]]). The problem is experience of accents, not English in general ([[listening-and-conversation/accents#rule-strategies]]).
:::
:::

## Subtitles

Subtitles are the most common question about learning from media, and research gives a fairly clear answer.

::: definition Subtitles and captions {#def-subtitles}
**Subtitles** in the viewer's own language (here, Chinese) translate what is said. **Captions** (also called *same-language subtitles* or *English subtitles* when the programme is in English) show the words that are actually spoken, in the same language. A **transcript** is the full text of a recording, read before or after listening.
:::

::: theorem Use subtitles in stages {#rule-subtitles}
1. **Chinese subtitles** help you enjoy and understand the story, but you are mainly **reading** Chinese, so they do little for your English listening. Use them for entertainment, or for a first viewing of hard material.
2. **English captions** connect the sound to the written words. They are the most useful stage for learning: you see where one word ends and the next begins, and you notice how written words sound in fast speech.
3. **No subtitles** is the goal. Move to it with material you have already watched with captions, then with new material at an easier level.

A good routine for one short clip: watch with **no subtitles** for the gist → watch with **English captions** to check and notice → watch again with **no subtitles**.
:::

::: proof
Mitterer and McQueen (2009) had Dutch listeners watch an episode of a television programme with an unfamiliar Scottish or Australian accent, with English captions, Dutch subtitles or no subtitles, and then tested how well they recognised new sentences from the same accent. Listeners who had watched with **English** captions did best; those who had watched with **Dutch** subtitles did worse than those with no subtitles at all, because the subtitles told them what was meant and so they did not need to learn how the speaker's sounds mapped onto words. Reviews of research on captioned video (for example Montero Perez, Van Den Noortgate and Desmet 2013) have also found that captions help comprehension and vocabulary learning. Watching first without subtitles makes you listen actively; captions then let you check your understanding and notice what you missed — the "noticing" step of [[listening-and-conversation/gist-and-detail#rule-cycle]].
:::

::: warning Subtitles in your own language
✗ Watching every programme with Chinese subtitles and counting it as listening practice.
✓ Using Chinese subtitles for entertainment, and English captions — or none — for practice.

Your eyes read faster than your ears understand, so with subtitles in your own language you will read the story and hardly listen. This is why hundreds of hours of viewing can leave listening almost unchanged, as it did for Wu Jie. There is nothing wrong with relaxing in front of a film with Chinese subtitles — but do not expect it to train your English ears.
:::

## Extensive and intensive listening

::: definition Extensive and intensive listening {#def-intensive}
**Extensive listening** means listening to a lot of material that is easy enough to follow, for meaning and enjoyment, without stopping to study it: a podcast on the way to work, a series in the evening. **Intensive listening** means working closely on a short recording — listening several times, checking with captions or a transcript, writing down what you hear, and studying the forms of fast speech.
:::

::: theorem Combine both {#rule-combine}
Extensive listening builds **speed, automatic decoding and familiarity** with many voices; intensive listening builds **accuracy** and teaches you what you are not hearing. Both are needed. A practical balance for many learners is a large amount of extensive listening every week, with one or two short intensive sessions.
:::

::: proof
The two kinds of practice train different things. Fast, automatic word recognition — the decoding of [[listening-and-conversation/how-listening-works#rule-decoding]] — develops only with a large amount of practice, just as reading speed does ([[reading-skills/extensive-reading]]); extensive listening provides it. But extensive listening alone lets the listener ignore what they cannot hear, using context to get by; intensive work brings those gaps into view. Field (2008) argued that classroom listening has concentrated too much on testing comprehension and too little on training decoding, and recommended short, focused work on exactly the stretches of speech that learners misheard. Intensive listening with captions and dictation does this.
:::

::: example Planning a week {#ex-week}
Zhao Ming has about 30 minutes a day for English listening, and his goal is to understand work meetings better. Plan his week.
::: solution
A possible plan:

- **Monday to Friday, extensive (20 minutes)**: an interview podcast about his industry on the way to work, without stopping. He listens for the gist and does not worry about details.
- **Monday, Wednesday, Friday, intensive (10 minutes)**: one 2–3 minute clip from the same podcast. No subtitles → captions or transcript → again without; he writes down three phrases he did not catch at first ([[#rule-subtitles]]).
- **Tuesday and Thursday (10 minutes)**: shadowing one minute of the clip he studied the day before ([[#rule-shadowing]]).
- **Weekend**: a film or series for pleasure, with English captions.

The plan matches the material to his goal (talk about work, several speakers), combines extensive and intensive listening ([[#rule-combine]]), and repeats the same clip across days.
:::
:::

::: example Dictation from a clip {#ex-dictation}
Ana listens to a ten-second stretch of an interview four times and writes:

*I think the ___ thing is ___ you're ___ to ask for help.*

The transcript says: *I think the main thing is that you're not afraid to ask for help.* What did she miss, and why?
::: solution
She missed *main*, *that* and *not afraid*.

- *that* [ðət] is a **weak form**, very short and unstressed ([[connected-speech/weak-forms]]).
- *not afraid* was probably said [nɒʔəˈfreɪd] — linked, with a glottal stop for /t/ and a weak first syllable of *afraid* ([[connected-speech/linking]]).
- *main* may have been lost because *the main* [ðə ˈmeɪn] was spoken quickly before *thing*; but it is stressed, so a fifth listening, focused on that gap, would probably reveal it.

The value of the dictation is diagnostic: Ana now knows that her problem is weak forms and linking, not vocabulary — she knew every word. This is exactly the kind of gap that extensive listening alone does not show ([[listening-and-conversation/how-listening-works#ex-diagnose]]).
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which activity is **intensive** listening?
- [ ] Listening to an audiobook on a long train journey.
- [ ] Watching a whole series at the weekend with English captions.
- [x] Listening to a two-minute clip five times and writing down every word.
- [ ] Having a podcast on in the background while cooking.
::: solution
Intensive listening is close, repeated work on a short recording. The other activities are extensive (the audiobook, the series) or hardly listening at all (the podcast in the background, if you are not following it).
:::
:::

## Shadowing

::: definition Shadowing {#def-shadowing}
**Shadowing** means repeating what a speaker says aloud, almost at the same time — a fraction of a second behind, like a shadow — while you listen. It is different from **repeating** after the recording has stopped.
:::

::: theorem How to shadow {#rule-shadowing}
1. **Choose a short recording** (30–60 seconds) that you have already understood, ideally with a transcript.
2. **Listen and read** the transcript a few times first.
3. **Mumble along**: speak quietly with the recording, following the rhythm and melody even if you miss some words.
4. **Shadow with the transcript**: speak clearly, matching the speaker's stress, linking and intonation.
5. **Shadow without the transcript.**
6. **Record yourself** and compare with the original: where did you fall behind? Which weak forms or links did you not make?
:::

::: proof
Shadowing forces you to process the speech as fast as it comes, without time to translate, and to reproduce exactly the features of connected speech — weak forms, linking, the rhythm of stressed syllables — that make fast speech hard to hear ([[connected-speech/rhythm]]). Working on your own production of these features is widely believed to make them easier to recognise. There is some research support for shadowing: Hamada (2016), for example, found that Japanese learners of English who practised shadowing improved their listening, and that learners at a lower level improved more. But the research is still limited, and the size of the benefit varies between studies. Shadowing is best treated as one useful technique, not a complete method.
:::

::: example A shadowing session {#ex-shadowing}
Mehmet shadows this sentence from a talk: *What I want to talk about today is how we can make our cities a bit greener.* In his recording he says each word fully: [wɒt aɪ wɒnt tuː tɔːk əˈbaʊt təˈdeɪ ɪz haʊ wiː kæn meɪk ˈaʊə ˈsɪtiz eɪ bɪt ˈɡriːnə], and falls a second behind. What should he work on?
::: solution
The speaker probably said something like [wɒʔaɪ ˈwɒnə ˈtɔːk əˌbaʊʔ təˈdeɪ | z ˈhaʊ wi kən ˈmeɪk ɑː ˈsɪtiz ə bɪʔ ˈɡriːnə], with a nucleus on *greener*.

1. **Weak forms**: *to* [tə], *can* [kən], *a* [ə], *our* [ɑː] — Mehmet used strong forms, which take longer ([[connected-speech/weak-forms]]).
2. ***want to*** → [ˈwɒnə] in fast, informal speech.
3. **Linking and glottal stops**: *What I* [wɒʔaɪ], *about today*, *bit greener*.
4. **Rhythm**: only *want*, *talk*, *today*, *how*, *make*, *cities*, *greener* are strong; the rest are squeezed between them ([[connected-speech/rhythm]]).

Once he makes these changes, he will keep up with the speaker — and he will also find it easier to hear the same forms in other people's speech.
:::
:::

## Meeting words again

Words learned from a list or a reading text are often hard to recognise in speech. The written form *comfortable* does not prepare you for [ˈkʌmftəbl̩]; *probably* may be [ˈprɒbli]; *going to* may be [ˈɡənə].

::: theorem Turn known words into heard words {#rule-hear-words}
1. **Learn the sound with the spelling**: when you learn a word, check its pronunciation and stress ([[core-vocabulary/using-a-dictionary]]).
2. **Listen for words you have learned**: after studying a set of words, choose listening on the same topic, so that you meet them again in speech.
3. **Notice reduced forms**: when captions show a word you did not hear, listen to that moment again and note how it was said.
4. **Collect phrases, not only words**: *at the end of the day*, *to be honest*, *I was like* are heard as units ([[core-vocabulary/phrases-and-chunks]]).
5. **Space the meetings**: meeting a word again several times over days and weeks is what makes it stay ([[core-vocabulary/learning-and-remembering#rule-spacing]]).
:::

::: proof
Recognising a word in speech requires a memory of how it **sounds** in connected speech, not only how it is written; a word met only in print may be recognised slowly or not at all in fast speech. Repeated meetings in listening build that memory. Research on incidental learning from viewing supports this: Peters and Webb (2018), for example, found that learners picked up some new words just from watching a single documentary, and that words that occurred more often were more likely to be learned. Learning from viewing alone is slow, however, so it works best together with deliberate study: study a word, then meet it again in listening ([[core-vocabulary/learning-and-remembering]]).
:::

::: quiz
You have just learned twenty words about climate. What is the best next step for your listening?
- [ ] Learn twenty more words from a different topic.
- [x] Listen to a short talk about climate, first without and then with English captions.
- [ ] Watch a comedy film with Chinese subtitles.
- [ ] Read the word list aloud as fast as possible.
::: solution
Listening on the same topic gives you the chance to meet the new words again, in speech, with the help of context. Captions then show you which words you heard and which you missed ([[#rule-hear-words]], [[#rule-subtitles]]).
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Subtitles everywhere.** Chinese video platforms often show subtitles by default, and many learners are used to always having text on screen. That habit makes the move to listening without subtitles harder; practise it deliberately ([[#rule-subtitles]]).
- **Classroom recordings and real speech.** Listening tests and textbook recordings are usually slower and clearer than real speech, with few hesitations or overlaps. Media show what English sounds like in real use — including accents and the *um*s, *like*s and false starts of real conversation ([[listening-and-conversation/how-listening-works]]).
- **Repeating aloud is familiar.** Many Chinese learners have practised reading aloud (朗读) and reciting texts. Shadowing builds on this habit, but with a recording of natural speech, in real time, and with attention to linking and weak forms rather than to clear, separate words.
- **Using what you enjoy.** You already watch and listen to things you like in Chinese; find English-language material on the same topics — a sport, a hobby, a field of study. Enjoyment is what keeps extensive listening going for months.
:::

::: history
Recordings changed language learning soon after they became possible. Language courses on gramophone records were sold from the early twentieth century, and language laboratories — rooms with tape recorders where students listened and repeated — spread in schools and universities in the 1950s and 1960s, influenced by the audio-lingual method's emphasis on listening and repetition. In the 1980s, Stephen Krashen's input hypothesis argued that languages are acquired mainly by understanding messages, which encouraged large amounts of listening and reading at the right level. Teletext and DVD captions made same-language subtitles widely available, and from the 2000s internet video and podcasts gave learners almost unlimited recordings of real speech from many countries. Research on captions, such as Vanderplank's work from the 1980s onwards, and on incidental vocabulary learning from viewing has since given learners evidence about how to use this material well.
:::

::: summary
- Choose material you can **mostly follow**, on topics you know, with clear sound, short enough to repeat, and from many speakers over time ([[#rule-choose]]).
- Subtitles in your own language train reading, not listening; **English captions** help most; move towards none ([[#def-subtitles]], [[#rule-subtitles]]; Mitterer and McQueen 2009).
- **Extensive listening** builds speed and familiarity; **intensive listening** builds accuracy and shows what you are missing; combine both ([[#def-intensive]], [[#rule-combine]]).
- **Shadowing** — speaking with the recording, a fraction behind — trains rhythm, weak forms and linking ([[#def-shadowing]], [[#rule-shadowing]]).
- Turn known words into heard words: learn their sound, listen on the same topic, notice reduced forms, collect phrases, and space your meetings with them ([[#rule-hear-words]]).
:::
