Megan is telling her new colleague Huang Xin about her weekend. Huang Xin is listening carefully and politely — looking at Megan, saying nothing, waiting for her to finish.

**Megan:** *So on Saturday we drove up to the lake, and it was absolutely packed —*

*(pause; Huang Xin says nothing)*

**Megan:** *— erm, and we couldn't find anywhere to park, so we ended up —*

*(pause; Huang Xin still says nothing)*

**Megan:** *Sorry, am I boring you?*

**Huang Xin:** *No, no! Please continue.*

Huang Xin was not bored at all. In many settings he grew up with, a good listener is a quiet one, and speaking while someone else is talking is rude. But Megan, like most English speakers, expected small signals from her listener as she went: *Mm*, *Oh no!*, *Really?* Hearing nothing at the pauses where those signals should have come, she concluded that something was wrong.

The next day, in a team meeting, Huang Xin has an idea. He waits for a silence so that he will not interrupt — but the silence never comes. Each speaker starts the moment the last one finishes, sometimes a little before. The meeting moves on, and his idea is never heard.

Both problems are about **turn-taking**: the way speakers in a conversation share the talking, know when to start and stop, and show each other that they are listening. This chapter explains how it works in English and gives you the phrases and signals to take part. It continues from [[everyday-english/small-talk]], which introduced follow-up questions and response tokens.

## Turns and when they end

::: definition Turn {#def-turn}
A **turn** is a stretch of talk by one speaker, from the moment they start speaking to the moment another speaker starts. A turn can be one word (*Yeah.*) or a long story. **Turn-taking** is the system by which speakers decide who speaks next and when.
:::

::: definition Transition point {#def-trp}
A **transition point** (in conversation analysis, a *transition-relevance place*) is a moment at which a turn could be complete, so that another speaker may start without interrupting. It usually comes at the end of a sentence or a phrase that makes sense on its own, said with a final tone.
:::

The surprising fact about conversation is how smoothly turns change. Stivers and colleagues (2009) measured the gap between a question and its answer in recorded conversations in ten languages from five continents, including English, Japanese, Danish and the Mayan language Tzeltal. In all of them, the most common gap was very short — around 200 milliseconds, a fifth of a second — and the average differed between languages by only a few hundred milliseconds. Speakers do not wait for a silence to know that a turn is over: they **predict** its end from what they hear and begin preparing their reply before it comes.

::: theorem Signals that a turn is ending {#rule-ending}
Listeners recognise a coming transition point from several signals together:

1. **Grammar**: the sentence or phrase is complete (*We went to the lake.* — not *We went to the…*).
2. **Intonation**: a falling tone on the nucleus marks something finished; a rise or level tone usually marks something unfinished ([[connected-speech/intonation-basics#rule-unfinished]]).
3. **Questions**: a question, or a question tag, gives the turn to the person addressed (*…, didn't you?*, *What about you?*).
4. **Closing words**: *or something*, *you know*, *anyway*, *so yeah*, *but there you go* often end a turn.
5. **Voice and body**: the speaker slows down, lowers the voice, stops gesturing and looks at the listener.

Signals that the speaker is **keeping** the turn are the opposite: a rising or level tone, an unfinished structure, a linking word at the end (*and*, *but*, *so*, *because*), and filled pauses (*um*, *er*, *erm*).
:::

::: proof
Prediction is necessary because speech is fast: if listeners waited until they were sure that a speaker had finished and only then started to plan a reply, gaps would be much longer than 200 milliseconds, since even planning a single word takes longer than that. Listeners therefore combine the signals above to see the end coming. Grammar is the strongest clue — a sentence that is not complete is not finished — and intonation confirms it. Filled pauses work the other way: *erm* tells the listener "I'm still thinking; don't start yet", which is why Megan's *erm* in the opening dialogue was not an invitation to Huang Xin to speak — but her pauses after *packed* and *ended up* were moments for a short signal.
:::

::: example Finding the transition points {#ex-trp}
Mark the places in this turn where a listener could start speaking (/), and the places where the speaker is clearly keeping the turn (→).

**Javier:** *I finally finished the report last night — took me ages — and then this morning my boss said she wanted it shorter, so, erm, I've got to cut it in half by Friday. Can you believe it?*
::: solution
*I finally finished the report last night* / *— took me ages* / *— and then this morning my boss said she wanted it shorter,* → *so, erm,* → *I've got to cut it in half by Friday.* / *Can you believe it?* /

- After *last night* and *took me ages*: complete sentences with a fall — a listener can give a short signal (*Well done!*, *Oh, did it?*) without taking over.
- After *shorter* → the next word is *so*, and *erm* follows: Javier is keeping the turn.
- After *by Friday*: complete, with a fall; a reaction fits (*Oh no!*).
- After *Can you believe it?*: a question addressed to the listener — this is the clearest point to take a full turn: *No way! After all that work?*
:::
:::

## Taking the turn

In a conversation between two people, the turn comes to you often and naturally. In a group — a meeting, a dinner, a seminar — you may have to take it.

::: theorem Getting into the conversation {#rule-taking}
To take a turn in a group:

1. **Start at a transition point**, not in a silence after it. Begin as the other speaker finishes, with a short starter that claims the turn while you form your sentence: *Well…*, *Yeah, and…*, *Mm, I think…*, *So…*, *Actually…*.
2. **Link to what was said**: *That's a good point — and…*, *Going back to what Ana said…*, *Just on that…*.
3. **To come in before a transition point**, ask permission briefly: *Sorry, can I just come in here?*, *Can I just add something?*, *Sorry to interrupt, but…*.
4. **To keep the turn** when someone tries to come in: *Sorry — just let me finish this point*, *Hang on, one second*, *I'll be quick*.
5. **To give the turn**: ask a question, use a name, or look at someone: *What do you think, Kenji?*
:::

::: proof
A short starter solves a timing problem. The moment for taking a turn is very short, but a learner may need more time to prepare a sentence. Words such as *Well* or *Yeah, and…* take the turn at the right moment and give a second to plan. In English conversation, **short overlaps** at transition points — one speaker starting just as the other finishes — are normal and not usually felt as rude. An **interruption** is different: starting in the middle of someone's sentence, before a transition point, especially to change the topic. Asking permission (*Can I just…*) makes an interruption acceptable, and in meetings it is often necessary.
:::

::: warning Waiting for silence
✗ Waiting for a clear silence before you speak, so as not to interrupt.
✓ Starting at a transition point, as the other speaker finishes, with a starter such as *Yeah, I think…*.

In a lively English conversation, long silences between turns are rare, so a speaker who waits for one may never get a turn — as Huang Xin found in the meeting. Others may then think he has nothing to say. Starting at a transition point is not rude; starting in the middle of someone's sentence without asking usually is.
:::

## Back-channelling: showing that you are listening

::: definition Back-channel {#def-backchannel}
A **back-channel** (also called a **response token** or **listener response**) is a short signal from the listener — a sound, a word, a nod — that shows attention and understanding without taking the turn. The speaker continues. English back-channels include *mm*, *mhm*, *uh-huh*, *yeah*, *right*, *OK*, *I see*, *really?*, *oh*, *wow*, *no way!*, *oh no*.
:::

Back-channels are not all the same. They do different jobs:

| kind | examples | what it says |
|---|---|---|
| **continuers** | *mm*, *mhm*, *uh-huh*, *yeah* | "I'm listening — go on." |
| **acknowledgements** | *right*, *OK*, *I see*, *sure* | "I've understood that." |
| **news markers** | *oh*, *really?*, *did you?*, *is it?* | "That's new to me — tell me more." |
| **assessments** | *wow*, *great*, *lovely*, *oh no*, *how awful*, *that's ridiculous* | "Here is how I feel about it." |
| **collaborative completions** | finishing the speaker's sentence: *…so you had to walk!* | "I'm following so closely that I can see where you're going." |

::: theorem Using back-channels {#rule-backchannel}
1. **Give them at transition points and pauses**, especially while someone tells a story or explains something; in a long turn, a signal every few seconds is normal.
2. **Vary them**: the same *yeah, yeah, yeah* can sound impatient.
3. **Match the content**: *Oh no!* for bad news, *Wow!* or *Lovely!* for good news, *Really?* for surprise; an assessment shows more interest than a continuer.
4. **Mind the tone**. A rise-fall or wide rise on *Really?* or *Oh!* shows interest; a low, flat *right* or *really* can sound bored or doubtful ([[connected-speech/intonation-meaning#rule-attitude]]).
5. **On the phone, say them aloud**: the speaker cannot see you nod ([[listening-and-conversation/calls-online-meetings]]).
:::

::: proof
Back-channels tell the speaker that the conversation is working, and they also steer it. A continuer invites the speaker to go on; a news marker invites more detail; an assessment invites the speaker to move toward the point of the story. Without them, the speaker has no evidence that the listener is following, and may stop, repeat, or ask *Are you with me?* — or, like Megan, conclude that the listener is bored. The intonation of a back-channel is part of its meaning: *Really?* with a high rise sounds genuinely surprised, while a low *Really.* with a fall can mean "I don't believe you" or "I don't care".
:::

### Reactive questions

A common English back-channel is a short question made from the auxiliary of the speaker's sentence and a pronoun: *I've just got back from Lisbon. — **Have you?*** These **reactive questions** (also called *echo questions*) show interest and invite more; they are not real requests for information.

| speaker says | reactive question |
|---|---|
| *I've just got back from Lisbon.* | *Oh, have you?* |
| *We're moving to Leeds.* | *Are you? Why's that?* |
| *My sister works there.* | *Does she?* |
| *I didn't sleep at all last night.* | *Didn't you? What happened?* |
| *It was cancelled.* | *Was it? That's a shame.* |
| *I can't swim.* | *Can't you?* |

The auxiliary is the one in the speaker's sentence, or *do/does/did* when there is none, and it keeps the same polarity: positive after positive, negative after negative. A rising tone shows interest; often a comment or a follow-up question comes next.

::: example Adding back-channels {#ex-backchannel}
Ji-woo is telling Lukas about a job interview. Add a suitable back-channel or reactive question at each gap, and say what kind it is.

**Ji-woo:** *So I got to the building at half nine, and the interview was at ten —* (1) *— but then the receptionist said they'd moved it to another office across town.* (2) *So I had to get a taxi, and of course the traffic was terrible —* (3) *— and I got there at five past ten, totally out of breath.* (4) *But the funny thing is, they were running late too, so they hadn't even noticed!* (5)
::: solution
1. *Mhm* or *Right* — a continuer: nothing surprising yet.
2. *Oh no!* or *Really? Across town?* — a news marker or assessment: a problem has appeared.
3. *Oh no* / *Of course* (with a fall, sharing the irony) — an assessment.
4. *Oh, you poor thing!* or *Were they OK about it?* — an assessment, or a follow-up question at the point of tension.
5. *No way!* / *Ha! Lucky!* / *Hadn't they?* — an assessment or reactive question at the end of the story, followed by a full turn: *So how did it go?*

Lukas's signals get stronger as the story builds: continuers at the start, assessments at the problem and the end. That is what makes a listener sound involved.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A colleague says: *I've been offered a job in Singapore.* Which response best shows interest?
- [ ] *Right.* (low, flat)
- [ ] *OK.*
- [x] *Have you? That's amazing — are you going to take it?*
- [ ] *Did you?*
::: solution
*Have you?* is the correct reactive question for *I've been offered* (auxiliary *have*); an assessment (*That's amazing*) and a follow-up question complete it. A low, flat *Right* sounds uninterested; *OK* only acknowledges; *Did you?* uses the wrong auxiliary — a small slip that listeners usually forgive, but *Have you?* is the natural form.
:::
:::

## Keeping it going: building on what was said

[[everyday-english/small-talk]] introduced two ways of keeping a conversation alive: answering with a little more than the answer, and asking follow-up questions. In longer conversations, a few more moves help.

::: theorem Ways to build on a turn {#rule-building}
After someone's turn, you can:

1. **Ask a follow-up question** about something they said: *What was the best part?*, *How did you get into that?*
2. **Comment and ask**: *That sounds stressful — how did you manage?*
3. **Share a related experience — briefly — and hand back**: *I had something similar last year. Did they give you a reason?*
4. **Summarise or interpret**: *So basically you're doing two jobs at once.*
5. **Change the topic with a marker** when the topic is finished: *By the way…*, *Speaking of holidays…*, *That reminds me…*, *Anyway, how's your sister?*
:::

::: proof
These moves keep the conversation going because each one gives the other speaker something easy to respond to. The third move needs care. Sociologist Charles Derber (1979) distinguished a **support response**, which keeps attention on the speaker's topic (*Really? What happened then?*), from a **shift response**, which moves it to the listener (*Oh, that happened to me too. I was in Rome, and…*). Sharing an experience is friendly when it is short and hands the turn back; when it takes over the conversation, the first speaker may feel that their story has been taken away. Topic-change markers let a speaker move on without seeming to ignore what was said before.
:::

::: example Support or shift? {#ex-shift}
Aditi says: *I've just started learning to drive. I had my first lesson yesterday and I stalled the car about ten times!* Here are three replies. Which keep attention on Aditi, and which take it away? Improve the one that takes it away.

1. *Ten times? Ha! How did your instructor react?*
2. *Oh, I passed my test first time. I was really good at it, actually. My instructor said I was a natural, and…*
3. *Oh, I did that all the time when I started! Are you learning in a manual?*
::: solution
1. **Support**: a reactive repetition (*Ten times?*), a laugh, and a follow-up question about her experience.
2. **Shift**: the speaker moves to his own success, does not react to Aditi's story, and keeps the turn. It can also sound like boasting.
3. **Support with a short share**: one sentence about a similar experience, which makes Aditi feel normal, then a question that hands the turn back.

Improved version of 2: *Oh, don't worry — everyone does that at first. When's your next lesson?*
:::
:::

::: example A conversation that keeps going {#ex-dialogue}
Here is a conversation between neighbours, Mrs Okafor and Liu Yang. Label the moves: back-channels, reactive questions, follow-ups, shares, and topic changes.

**Mrs Okafor:** *Morning! You're up early.*

**Liu Yang:** *Morning! Yes, I've started going running before work.*

**Mrs Okafor:** *Have you? Good for you! Where do you go?*

**Liu Yang:** *Just round the park, usually. About five kilometres.*

**Mrs Okafor:** *Five! My goodness. I used to run, years ago — my knees won't let me now. Do you enjoy it?*

**Liu Yang:** *I do, actually. It's the only time I'm not looking at a screen.*

**Mrs Okafor:** *Mm, I know what you mean. Oh, by the way — did you get a letter about the parking?*

**Liu Yang:** *No, what parking?*
::: solution
- *Have you?* — a reactive question (*I've started* → *Have you?*); *Good for you!* — an assessment; *Where do you go?* — a follow-up.
- *Five! My goodness.* — a news marker by repetition and an assessment; *I used to run, years ago…* — a short share; *Do you enjoy it?* — hands the turn back.
- *I do, actually* — answer plus a detail (*the only time I'm not looking at a screen*), a hook.
- *Mm, I know what you mean* — a continuer and agreement that closes the topic; *Oh, by the way* — a topic-change marker.
- *No, what parking?* — a short answer and a question: a new topic has begun.
:::
:::

::: quiz
In a meeting, Paolo is explaining a plan, and you want to add an important point before he moves on. He has just said: *…so that's the timeline for phase one, and then for phase two —*. What do you say?
- [ ] Nothing — wait until the end of the meeting.
- [ ] *No. I have a point.*
- [x] *Sorry, can I just come in on phase one before you move on?*
- [ ] Start speaking about your point while he continues.
::: solution
Paolo is keeping the turn (*and then for phase two —*), so this is not a transition point. A brief request — *Sorry, can I just come in…?* — makes the interruption polite and explains why it cannot wait (*before you move on*). Waiting until the end may be too late; *No. I have a point.* is abrupt; talking over him is rude.

::: note English and Chinese
- **Quiet listening.** In many Chinese settings, especially with teachers, older people or seniors at work, listening quietly is a sign of respect. In English conversation, quiet listening without any signals can be taken as boredom, disagreement or failure to understand. Short back-channels are not interruptions: they are part of good listening.
- **嗯 and *mm*.** Mandarin has back-channels too — 嗯, 对, 是, 是吗, 真的啊 — and they work much like *mm*, *right*, *really?* Use your Chinese habits as a guide, but with English forms: 对对对 is like *yeah, yeah* or *right, right*; 是吗？ is like *Really?* or a reactive question (*Is it?*, *Did you?*). One study that recorded conversations in English, Japanese and Mandarin (Clancy et al. 1996) found that Mandarin speakers used reactive tokens less often than the English and Japanese speakers; but habits differ between people and settings in every language.
- **Head nods without sound.** Nodding is a good signal face to face, but on the phone and in online meetings with the camera off, it is invisible. Say *mm* or *right* aloud.
- **Waiting your turn.** Learners trained in classrooms where students speak one at a time, when asked, may wait to be invited. In English group discussions, you are often expected to come in yourself, at a transition point ([[#rule-taking]]).
- ***Did you?* and the auxiliary.** A reactive question must pick up the speaker's auxiliary and tense: *I've…* → *Have you?*; *I was…* → *Were you?*; *I can't…* → *Can't you?* Chinese 是吗 has one form for all of them, so this takes practice.
:::

::: history
The study of turn-taking began with recordings. In the 1960s, sociologist Harvey Sacks started to analyse tape recordings of real telephone calls, and with Emanuel Schegloff and Gail Jefferson he founded conversation analysis. Their article "A simplest systematics for the organization of turn-taking for conversation" (*Language*, 1974) set out the rules shown in the figure. At about the same time, psychologist Starkey Duncan (1972) studied the signals — grammar, intonation, gesture — that speakers use to yield or keep a turn, and linguist Victor Yngve (1970) introduced the term *back channel* for listeners' short signals. Later research measured turn-taking precisely: Stivers and colleagues' comparison of ten languages (2009) showed that the quick exchange of turns is common to human conversation, with small differences between languages.
:::

When you have not understood a turn, the next step is to ask: [[listening-and-conversation/clarification]]. Giving opinions, agreeing and disagreeing — and the turn shapes that soften disagreement — are in [[listening-and-conversation/opinions]]; reacting to a story in [[listening-and-conversation/storytelling]]. ::: summary
- A **turn** is one speaker's stretch of talk; turns change at **transition points**, where a turn could be complete ([[#def-turn]], [[#def-trp]]).
- Gaps between turns are typically about 200 milliseconds in many languages (Stivers et al. 2009): listeners predict the end of a turn from grammar, falling intonation, questions, closing words and body language ([[#rule-ending]]).
- To get in, start at a transition point with a starter (*Well…*, *Yeah, and…*); to come in earlier, ask (*Sorry, can I just…?*); don't wait for a silence ([[#rule-taking]]).
- **Back-channels** — continuers, acknowledgements, news markers, assessments — show attention without taking the turn; vary them, match the content and mind the tone ([[#def-backchannel]], [[#rule-backchannel]]).
- **Reactive questions** pick up the speaker's auxiliary: *I've…* → *Have you?*; *She was…* → *Was she?*
- Build on turns with follow-ups, comments, brief shares that hand back, summaries, and marked topic changes (*By the way…*) ([[#rule-building]]).
:::
