Sun Hao, a student in Manchester, comes back from class and finds a voicemail from his landlord. He presses play and tries to understand every word. The landlord speaks quickly; by the third sentence Sun Hao is still puzzling over a word in the first, and the message ends. He plays it again, and again the same thing happens.

His flatmate Amara listens once and says: *It's about the boiler. Someone's coming to fix it on Thursday morning, and he wants us to be in.* Then she listens a second time with a pen in her hand and writes down *Thurs, 8–10, call back if no — 07700 900 461*.

Amara did not hear better than Sun Hao. She listened differently: first for the **gist** — what the message was about and what it wanted — and then for the **details** she needed to act on it. This chapter teaches that way of listening. It builds on [[listening-and-conversation/how-listening-works]]: predicting uses top-down knowledge, and catching numbers and names is bottom-up decoding at its most demanding.

## Listening with a purpose

::: definition Gist {#def-gist}
The **gist** of a spoken text is its main idea: what it is about, what the speaker wants or thinks, and roughly what happens. **Listening for gist** means listening to the whole text without stopping, to understand its general meaning, and ignoring details and unknown words.
:::

::: definition Listening for detail {#def-detail}
**Listening for detail** (also called **listening for specific information**) means listening for particular items that you need — a time, a price, a name, an address, a reason, a step in a set of instructions — and paying less attention to the rest. You know what kind of information you are listening for before you hear it.
:::

The two ways of listening are the spoken equivalents of skimming and scanning in reading ([[reading-skills/reading-with-a-purpose]]). The difference is that a reader can choose where to look, while a listener must take the text in the order the speaker gives it, once, at the speaker's speed. That makes planning even more important.

::: theorem Decide why you are listening {#rule-purpose}
Before you listen, decide what you need from the text. Most real listening has a purpose — to know whether a train is late, to find out what a friend wants, to follow the argument of a lecture — and the purpose tells you how to listen: for the gist, for particular details, or both. When you can hear a text more than once, listen **first for gist and then for detail**; when you can hear it only once, keep the gist in mind and listen hardest at the places where the details you need are likely to come.
:::

::: proof
Listeners cannot attend to everything at once; attention is limited, especially in a second language ([[listening-and-conversation/how-listening-works#rule-breakdown]]). A purpose decides where to spend it. The gist comes first because it gives the frame: once you know that a voicemail is about a repair, the details (*Thursday*, *eight to ten*) have a place to go, and you can predict where they will come. Trying to catch every detail on the first listening usually means catching neither the details nor the gist, as Sun Hao found.
:::

## Before listening: predicting

::: theorem Prepare to listen {#rule-predict}
Before you listen, use everything you know:

1. **The situation.** Where is the text from — an announcement, a voicemail, a lecture, a conversation? Who is speaking to whom?
2. **The topic.** What do you already know about it? Which words are likely?
3. **The task.** If you have questions, read them first. Each question tells you what to listen for and roughly in what order — questions usually follow the order of the text.
4. **The kind of answer.** For each question, decide whether the answer will be a number, a time, a date, a name, a place, a reason or an opinion. Then you know what to listen for.
:::

::: example Predicting from questions {#ex-predict-questions}
Fatima is going to hear a short conversation in which a customer phones a bike shop. Her task has four questions. Predict the kind of answer for each, and some words she might hear.

1. What is wrong with the customer's bike?
2. When can the shop look at it?
3. How much will the basic service cost?
4. Why does the customer decide to wait?
::: solution
1. **A problem** — a noun phrase: *the brakes*, *a flat tyre* (AmE *tire*), *the chain keeps coming off*, *the gears*. Listen after *What's the problem?* or *What seems to be the trouble?*
2. **A time or day** — *tomorrow morning*, *on Friday*, *not until next week*, *after two*. Listen for weekdays and *-teen*/*-ty* numbers.
3. **A price** — a number with *pounds* / *dollars* / *euros*, maybe *including* or *plus parts*. Listen for *That'd be…*, *It's…*, *It comes to…*.
4. **A reason** — a clause after *because*, *since*, *so*, or a sentence such as *The thing is…*. The question also tells you that the customer first considered something else (not waiting) and then changed her mind.

Fatima now knows that the conversation will go in this order, roughly: problem → time → price → decision. She can follow the gist and catch each detail when its turn comes.
:::
:::

## First listening: the gist

On the first listening, do not stop and do not write much. Listen to the whole text and try to answer three questions: **What is it about? What does the speaker want or think? How does it end?**

::: theorem Where the gist is {#rule-gist}
The main idea of a spoken text is usually signalled in these ways:

1. **Beginnings and ends.** Speakers often say what they are talking about at the start (*I'm calling about…*, *I just wanted to let you know…*, *Today I'm going to talk about…*) and repeat the main point at the end (*So, anyway, …*, *So basically…*, *Just to sum up…*).
2. **Stressed words and nuclei.** The content words carry the stresses, and the most important new information carries the nucleus ([[connected-speech/sentence-stress#rule-last]]).
3. **Repetition.** Words that come back again and again — or words with similar meaning — show the topic.
4. **Signposts.** Phrases such as *The main thing is…*, *What I really wanted to say was…*, *The point is…*, *Basically…* announce the important part.
5. **Tone of voice.** Whether a speaker sounds pleased, worried, annoyed or apologetic is part of the gist ([[connected-speech/intonation-meaning]]).
:::

::: example The gist of a voicemail {#ex-gist}
Here is the landlord's message from the start of the chapter. The stressed words are marked. Read it once quickly, as if listening, and write the gist in one sentence.

*Hi, it's ˈDavid, your ˈlandlord. Erm, just ˈcalling about the ˈboiler — I know you said it's been making that ˈnoise again. ˈAnyway, I've ˈfinally got hold of the ˈplumber, and he can ˈcome on ˈThursday ˈmorning, ˈsomewhere between ˈeight and ˈten. He'll ˈneed someone to ˈlet him ˈin, ˈthough, so if ˈthat's a ˈproblem, ˈgive me a ˈcall ˈback and we'll ˈsort out ˈanother ˈday. My ˈnumber's oh seven seven double oh, nine double oh, four six ˈone. ˈThanks, ˈbye.*
::: solution
**Gist**: *The landlord has arranged for a plumber to repair the boiler on Thursday morning, and someone needs to be at home; if that's not possible, call him back.*

How to find it:

- The **beginning** gives the topic: *just calling about the boiler*.
- *Anyway* marks the move from background (the noise) to the main point (the plumber is coming).
- The **stressed words** in the middle — *plumber*, *come*, *Thursday*, *morning*, *let him in* — are the action.
- *if that's a problem, give me a call back* is the request at the end.

On the first listening, Sun Hao did not need *somewhere between eight and ten* or the phone number: those are details for the second listening.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A lecturer begins: *Last week we looked at how cities grew in the nineteenth century. Today I want to look at the problems that growth caused — housing, disease and transport — and then, in the second half, at how cities responded.* What is the gist of the lecture likely to be?
- [ ] How cities grew in the nineteenth century.
- [x] The problems caused by the growth of cities, and how cities dealt with them.
- [ ] Housing in the nineteenth century.
- [ ] Transport in modern cities.
::: solution
*Today I want to look at…* introduces the topic of this lecture: the problems caused by growth and the responses to them. The growth itself was last week's topic; housing is one of three problems (a detail); modern transport is not mentioned.
:::
:::

## Second listening: details

On the second listening, you know the frame, and you listen for the particular items you need. Some kinds of detail are especially hard to catch.

### Numbers

Numbers are hard for every listener: they cannot be guessed from context, they are often said quickly, and similar numbers sound alike. Prepare for them.

::: theorem Catching numbers {#rule-numbers}
1. **Listen for the stress in *-teen* and *-ty*.** *Fifˈteen* has the stress on *-teen* and a long /iː/ with a clear /n/; *ˈfifty* has the stress on the first syllable and a short final /i/ ([[everyday-english/numbers-in-conversation#rule-teen-ty]]). Before a stressed noun, *-teen* can lose its stress (*ˈfifteen ˈminutes*), so the vowel and the /n/ are safer guides than the stress.
2. **Know how numbers are grouped.** Phone numbers are said digit by digit, in groups, with *oh* (BrE, often AmE) or *zero* for 0 and *double* for a repeated digit in BrE (*double oh*). Large numbers are grouped in thousands, not ten-thousands ([[everyday-english/numbers-in-conversation#rule-big-numbers]]).
3. **Write numbers as digits immediately** — do not translate them into Chinese numbers first.
4. **Listen to what follows the number.** *Fifteen* what? *minutes*, *pounds*, *per cent*, *kilos*, *the fifteenth*?
5. **Listen for corrections.** Speakers often correct themselves: *It's on the fourth — sorry, the fifth.* The last version is the one that counts.
:::

::: proof
These rules follow from the way numbers are said. The stress contrast in *-teen*/*-ty* is real but not reliable, because of stress shift before a noun ([[connected-speech/word-stress-patterns#rule-shift]]), so vowel length and the final /n/ are better clues. Grouping matters because a listener who expects a 4-3-4 or 5-6 pattern for a phone number can hold the groups in memory one at a time. Writing digits at once saves the extra step of translation, which is often where a learner loses the next number. And corrections are very common in natural speech: listeners are used to taking the last version.
:::

::: example Numbers in an announcement {#ex-numbers}
At an airport gate, Bui Thi Lan hears this announcement (GB). Write down every number and what it refers to.

*Good afternoon, ladies and gentlemen. This is a boarding announcement for flight BA two-eight-one-six to Glasgow. We will begin boarding in approximately fifteen minutes, at sixteen forty, starting with passengers seated in rows thirty to forty-five. Please have your boarding pass ready, and note that we can accept only one cabin bag per passenger, with a maximum weight of seven kilos.*
::: solution
| number | as said | refers to |
|---|---|---|
| BA 2816 | *two-eight-one-six*, digit by digit | flight number |
| 15 | *fifˈteen ˈminutes* (stress may shift to *fif-*; the long /iː/ and the /n/ show *-teen*) | time until boarding |
| 16.40 | *sixteen forty* | the boarding time on the 24-hour clock (4.40 p.m.) |
| 30–45 | *thirty to forty-five* | rows that board first |
| 1 | *only one* | cabin bags per passenger |
| 7 | *seven kilos* | maximum weight |

Two checks: *fifteen minutes* and *sixteen forty* must agree with the current time (16.25 would be right), which helps to confirm both; and *thirty to forty-five* is a range of rows — the word *rows* tells you what kind of number it is.
:::
:::

### Names and spelling

Names of people, streets and places cannot be predicted; if they are new to you, they can only be decoded. In conversation, ask for the spelling ([[listening-and-conversation/clarification]]). In recordings, listen for the spelling that careful speakers often add (*That's Okafor — O-K-A-F-O-R*) and for familiar parts (*-ton*, *-ham*, *-field*, *-ford* in British place names; *Street*, *Road*, *Avenue*, *Lane*).

### Reasons, contrasts and changes

Many questions ask *why*, and many important details come after small words that change direction. These words are usually unstressed, so you need to expect them.

::: theorem Signals of detail {#rule-signals}
Listen especially carefully after:

- **reasons**: *because*, *'cause* [kəz], *since*, *as*, *so*, *that's why*, *the reason is*, *the thing is*;
- **contrasts**: *but*, *though* (at the end: *It's nice, though.*), *although*, *actually*, *in fact*, *the problem is*;
- **changes and corrections**: *sorry, I mean…*, *or rather*, *well, actually…*, *not … but …*;
- **conditions**: *if*, *unless*, *as long as*, *otherwise*;
- **sequence**: *first*, *then*, *after that*, *before you…*, *once you've…*, *finally*.

What follows these words is often the detail a question asks about — and often it changes what came before.
:::

::: example Finding the reason {#ex-reasons}
Here is the bike-shop conversation from [[#ex-predict-questions]]. Find the answers to the four questions.

**Shop:** *Hello, Wheel Works.*

**Rosa:** *Hi, I've got a problem with my bike — the gears keep slipping, and the back brake's not great either.*

**Shop:** *OK. We're pretty busy this week, I'm afraid. The earliest we could look at it is Friday.*

**Rosa:** *Friday? Hmm. I was hoping for sooner. How much is a service?*

**Shop:** *A basic service is forty-five pounds, and then parts on top, if it needs any.*

**Rosa:** *Right. I suppose I could try the place on Market Street…*

**Shop:** *You could, but they're closed till Monday — the owner's on holiday.*

**Rosa:** *Oh, are they? OK, well, Friday it is, then.*
::: solution
1. **The problem**: the gears keep slipping (and the back brake is not good). *The gears* come first and are the main problem; *either* adds the brake.
2. **When**: Friday — after *The earliest we could look at it is…*. Note *I'm afraid*, which signals bad news (the shop is busy).
3. **Price**: £45 for a basic service, *and then parts on top* — the full cost may be more. *Forty-five*: stress on *for-*, short final vowel — not *fourteen-five*, which is not a number.
4. **Why she waits**: the other shop is closed until Monday because the owner is on holiday. The signal is *but* in *You could, but they're closed…*; the decision follows: *Friday it is, then*. The reason is not given with *because* — it comes from the turn before her decision.
:::
:::

::: warning The first number is not always the answer
✗ Writing down the first time or price you hear and stopping there.
✓ Listening to the end of the sentence and the next turn, in case the speaker corrects, changes or rejects it.

In a natural conversation, people often mention several possibilities before they decide: *We could meet at six — no, wait, I've got a class till six thirty. Let's say seven.* The answer is *seven*. Exam recordings use this pattern on purpose, but it is not a trick: it is how real arrangements are made.
:::

::: quiz
A friend says: *The film starts at quarter to eight, but there are about twenty minutes of adverts, so we don't need to be there till eight.* What time do you need to arrive?
- [ ] 7.15
- [ ] 7.45
- [x] 8.00
- [ ] 8.20
::: solution
The film is listed at 7.45, but *but* and *so* introduce a change: adverts last twenty minutes, so arriving at 8.00 is enough (*we don't need to be there till eight*). The answer follows the last *so*.
:::
:::

## The listening cycle

::: widget graph
nodes: predict@0,4; listen for gist@3,4; check with a partner or notes@6,4; listen for detail@6,2; check the answers@3,2; reflect on what was hard@0,2
edges: predict>listen for gist; listen for gist>check with a partner or notes; check with a partner or notes>listen for detail; listen for detail>check the answers; check the answers>reflect on what was hard; reflect on what was hard>predict
title: A cycle for listening practice
caption: A practice cycle for a recording you can hear more than once: predict ([[#rule-predict]]), listen for the gist ([[#rule-gist]]), check your idea, listen again for the details ([[#rule-signals]]), check the answers against a transcript, and note what made the text hard, which prepares the next prediction. Vandergrift and Tafaghodtari (2010) taught a cycle of this kind to university learners of French; you can drag the boxes.
:::

::: theorem Listen more than once, with a different aim each time {#rule-cycle}
When you practise with a recording, use a cycle: **predict**, **listen for gist**, **check** your understanding (with a partner, or by writing a one-sentence summary), **listen for detail**, **check** against a transcript or the answers, and **reflect**: which part was hard, and why? Each listening has its own aim, so you hear the same text in a different way each time.
:::

::: proof
The cycle trains the processes of listening rather than only testing the result. Vandergrift and Tafaghodtari (2010) compared two groups of university students of French as a second language over a term. One group practised with a cycle of prediction, listening, verification with a partner and reflection; the other listened to the same texts the same number of times without it. The cycle group improved more in listening comprehension, and the difference was greatest for the less skilled listeners. The reflection step matters because it turns a failure into information: *I lost it at the numbers* tells you what to practise next ([[listening-and-conversation/how-listening-works#rule-breakdown]]).
:::

::: example One recording, three listenings {#ex-cycle}
Kim Soo-ah is practising with a two-minute podcast clip about a town that banned cars from its centre. Describe what she should do before and during each listening.
::: solution
**Before listening.** From the title she predicts: why the town did it (pollution, safety, noise), who was for and against (shops, residents), what happened afterwards (more walking, fewer customers or more?). Likely words: *traffic*, *pedestrian*, *pollution*, *businesses*, *residents*, *trial*.

**First listening — gist.** No pen. Afterwards she writes one sentence: *The town closed its centre to cars two years ago; shops were worried at first, but most now say business is better.*

**Check.** She compares with her prediction: the pollution idea was right; she did not expect the part about deliveries.

**Second listening — detail.** She listens for numbers (how many years, how much pollution fell, how many shops), the reasons people gave, and the one problem that remains (deliveries early in the morning).

**Check and reflect.** She reads the transcript and finds that she missed *a third fewer*, which she heard as *a third*, and *deliveries are only allowed before ten*, which went past while she was writing the previous number. Her note: *fractions; don't write during the next sentence — write in the pause.*
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Exam habits.** Listening sections in many Chinese exams (as of 2026) give questions in advance and play recordings once or twice. They train prediction from questions and listening for detail well, but some learners never practise listening for the gist of a long text without questions. In real life there are rarely questions: practise writing your own one-sentence gist.
- **Numbers.** Chinese groups large numbers in ten-thousands (万, 亿), English in thousands. Translating *fifteen thousand* into 一万五 while the speaker goes on to the next number is a common cause of lost details. Write digits, and convert later if you need to ([[everyday-english/numbers-in-conversation#rule-big-numbers]]).
- ***-teen* and *-ty*.** Mandarin has no stress contrast like *fifˈteen*/*ˈfifty*, and some learners do not hear it. Listen for the vowel (/iːn/ vs /i/) and, in GA, the tap in *forty* [ˈfɔːrɾi] — or ask (*Sorry, one-five or five-oh?*).
- **Names.** English names in recordings may be given first name first (*Maria Lopez*), and Chinese names may be given either way (*Wang Fang* or *Fang Wang*). When a name matters, check which is the family name.
:::

::: history
Listening for gist and for specific information became standard parts of language teaching with the communicative approach of the 1970s and 1980s. Penny Ur's *Teaching Listening Comprehension* (1984) argued that classroom listening should resemble real listening: learners should know before listening why they are listening, as people do in life, and tasks should ask them to do something with what they hear — fill in a timetable, follow a route, take a message. Textbooks of the 1980s and 1990s made the sequence of *pre-listening*, *while-listening* and *post-listening* activities familiar. Later, John Field (2008) criticised the habit of only asking comprehension questions, which tests whether listening succeeded but does not show learners how to listen better, and Larry Vandergrift and Christine Goh (2012) described the cycle of prediction, monitoring and reflection that this chapter uses.
:::

Each sentence is said at normal speed. Which number is meant? Give the clue.

(a) *The class has [θɜːˈtiːn] students.* (b) *It costs [fɔːˈtiːn] pounds.* (c) *The bus goes every [ˈfɪfti] minutes.*
::: solution
(a) **13**: stress on the second syllable, a long /iː/ and a final /n/. (b) **14**: the same clues — stress on *-teen*, long /iː/, final /n/. (c) **50**: stress on the first syllable, short final vowel, no /n/ — though *every fifty minutes* is unusual, so a listener might check (*Sorry — fifteen or fifty?*).

::: exercise The gist in one sentence {level=2}
Write the gist of this message in one sentence.

*Hi Mei, it's Tom. Listen, about tomorrow — I'm really sorry, but something's come up at work and I'm not going to be able to make lunch. I was thinking maybe we could do Thursday instead? Or Friday, if that's better for you. Anyway, let me know. Sorry again!*
::: solution
*Tom can't come to lunch tomorrow because of work, and he suggests meeting on Thursday or Friday instead.* The signals: *about tomorrow* (topic), *I'm really sorry, but…* (bad news follows), *instead* (a new suggestion), *let me know* (the request).

Listen (read) for detail and complete the notes.

Notes: Old date: ___. Reason: ___. New option 1: ___. New option 2: ___. Phone: ___.

Old date: the 23rd. Reason: Dr Patel is away that week. New option 1: the 30th at 10.15. New option 2: Monday the 4th at 2.40. Phone: 0161 496 0332. (*Double three* = 33; the groups are 0161 / 496 / 0332.)

::: exercise Signal words {level=2}
In each sentence, find the signal word and say what the detail after it tells you.

(a) *The hotel was lovely, though the breakfast was a bit disappointing.* (b) *We took the train, since the roads were closed.* (c) *You'll get a refund as long as you have the receipt.* (d) *It's on the second floor — actually, no, the third.*
::: solution
(a) *though* — a contrast: something negative about an otherwise good hotel. (b) *since* — the reason for taking the train. (c) *as long as* — the condition for a refund. (d) *actually, no* — a correction: the answer is the third floor.

::: exercise Plan a listening {level=3}
You are going to hear a ten-minute talk at a university open day called *Studying abroad: what nobody tells you*. Plan your listening: (a) predictions about content and vocabulary, (b) what you will do on the first listening, (c) three kinds of detail you will listen for if you could hear it again.
::: hint
Use the title: *what nobody tells you* suggests surprises and problems, probably with advice.
:::
::: solution
A model plan:

(a) **Predictions**: the speaker will talk about problems that students do not expect — loneliness, cost of living, different teaching styles, bureaucracy (visas, bank accounts), and perhaps positive surprises. Likely words: *homesick*, *budget*, *accommodation*, *independent study*, *seminar*, *deadline*, *culture shock*, *support services*. The talk will probably end with advice.

(b) **First listening**: no notes, or only one word per main point; at the end, write the gist in one sentence and list the main points the speaker made, in order.

(c) **Details**: numbers (costs, percentages of students who report a problem), the names of services or people to contact, and the specific advice for each problem (*what to do* after *so*, *the best thing is…*, *my advice would be…*).
:::

::: exercise Write a gist-and-detail task {level=3}
Write a short voicemail or announcement (60–90 words) for a fellow learner. Then write one gist question and three detail questions, with answers. Include at least one number, one name to be spelled, and one correction.
::: solution
A model answer:

*Hi Priya, it's Jonas from the climbing club. Just to let you know, Saturday's trip is still on, but we're meeting at the car park on Brook Lane — that's B-R-O-O-K — not at the station. We're leaving at half seven, sorry, quarter to eight. It's twelve pounds for the bus, and bring lunch, because there's no café there. Text me on oh seven seven double oh, nine double oh, one two three if you can't come. See you!*

Gist question: *What is the message about?* — *A change of meeting place for Saturday's climbing trip.*

Detail questions: *Where are they meeting?* — *The car park on Brook Lane.* *What time are they leaving?* — *7.45 (corrected from 7.30).* *Why should Priya bring lunch?* — *Because there's no café there.*
:::
