Liu Mei joins her first online meeting with a new international team. Seven faces appear on her screen. The chair, Sanjay, says something; Liu Mei catches *…introductions…* and *…go round…*. A colleague begins to speak, but her voice keeps cutting out: *…from the… team… and I… on the…*. Then there is a silence. Liu Mei realises that everyone is looking at the screen — at her. She starts to introduce herself. Nobody reacts. After ten seconds, Sanjay says, gently: *Mei, I think you're on mute.*

Everything in this scene is ordinary. The same English that works face to face — introductions, turn-taking, clarification — must work here without the help that a shared room gives: the faces, the glances that show who will speak next, the clear sound. On the phone even more is missing. This chapter teaches the language and the habits that make calls and online meetings work: opening and stating your purpose, dealing with sound and connection problems, taking turns when there is a delay, speaking up, and ending clearly. It builds on [[everyday-english/phone-messages]], which taught the basic shape of a phone call, and on [[listening-and-conversation/turn-taking]] and [[listening-and-conversation/clarification]].

## Why calls are harder

On the phone and in online meetings, listeners lose much of the help they have face to face.

::: theorem What is missing on a call {#rule-missing}
Compared with face-to-face conversation, calls and online meetings usually have:

1. **Fewer visual signals** — on the phone, none: no lip movements, gestures, nods or glances to show who will speak next. In video meetings, faces are small, eye contact is impossible, and cameras are often off.
2. **Poorer sound** — a narrower range of frequencies, compression, background noise and an echo. Fricatives such as /f/, /s/ and /θ/ are especially easy to confuse.
3. **Delay** — the sound arrives a fraction of a second late, so the normal timing of turns ([[listening-and-conversation/turn-taking#rule-ending]]) breaks down.
4. **More speakers, less order** — in a group call, several people may start at once, and it is harder to tell who is speaking.
:::

::: proof
Each point has a technical cause. Traditional telephone lines carried only the frequencies between about 300 and 3,400 hertz; that range is enough to understand speech but removes much of the high-frequency energy that distinguishes /f/ from /s/ and /θ/, which is why people spell names on the phone with words (*F for Freddie*). Internet calls can carry a wider range, but they compress the sound and lose pieces of it when the connection is poor — the "breaking up" Liu Mei heard. Delay matters because the gap between turns in conversation is usually about 200 milliseconds (Stivers et al. 2009): the International Telecommunication Union's recommendation G.114 treats a one-way delay of up to 150 milliseconds as acceptable for most uses, and longer delays make speakers overlap and then both stop. Losing visual signals removes the glances and gestures that tell people when a turn is ending. All of this means that on a call, the things that are usually automatic must be done **with words**.
:::

::: definition Latency {#def-latency}
**Latency** (or **lag**) is the delay between the moment a person speaks and the moment the listener hears them. In a video meeting with a delay of half a second, two people who both wait the normal fraction of a second and then start to speak will hear each other only after they have begun — and both stop. Speakers must leave slightly longer gaps and hand over turns explicitly.
:::

## Phone calls

[[everyday-english/phone-messages]] taught how a call opens (*Hello, this is…*), how to leave a message and how to close. In work calls and calls to organisations, a few more stages are common.

::: theorem A clear phone call {#rule-phone}
1. **Say who you are and why you are calling, early**: *Hi, this is Liu Mei from Hartley Design. I'm calling about the invoice you sent last week.* The listener cannot see you, so your name and purpose replace the face and the context.
2. **Back-channel aloud**: *mm*, *right*, *OK*, *I see* — silence on a phone line sounds like a lost connection.
3. **Spell with words** and read back numbers: *That's Liu — L for London, I, U*. *So that's oh two oh, seven nine four six…?* ([[everyday-english/numbers-in-conversation#rule-check]])
4. **Signal stages**: *Just a couple of questions…*, *One more thing…*, *So, to sum up…*
5. **Summarise and confirm** before closing: *So you'll send the corrected invoice by Friday, and I'll pay it next week. Is that right?*
:::

::: proof
Each step compensates for something missing ([[#rule-missing]]). Early identification lets the listener find the right file and the right frame of mind — top-down knowledge for understanding the rest ([[listening-and-conversation/how-listening-works]]). Audible back-channels replace nods. Spelling with words compensates for the narrow sound band. Signalling stages replaces the visual signs that a speaker is about to finish. And a summary turns a conversation that leaves no written record into a clear agreement, which is the most common way to avoid misunderstanding later.
:::

Calls to organisations often include being **put on hold** or **transferred**:

| you may hear | it means |
|---|---|
| *Can you hold, please?* / *Could you hold the line?* | wait; don't hang up |
| *I'll put you through.* / *I'll transfer you.* | I'll connect you to another person or department |
| *I'm afraid the line's busy.* / *She's on another call.* | the person is speaking to someone else |
| *Can I take a message?* / *Can I ask who's calling?* | they will pass on your name and message |
| *Sorry to keep you waiting.* | the person is back |
| *For bookings, press 1. For all other enquiries, press 2.* | an automated menu |

::: example A call with a transfer {#ex-call}
Read this call and label the stages: identification and purpose, hold, transfer, clarification, summary, closing.

**Receptionist:** *Good morning, Eastfield College, Grace speaking.*

**Ahmed:** *Hi, this is Ahmed Karimi. I'm calling about my application for the evening course in web design.*

**Receptionist:** *OK, let me see who can help with that. Can you hold for a moment?*

**Ahmed:** *Sure.*

**Receptionist:** *Thanks for holding. I'll put you through to Admissions.*

**Admissions:** *Admissions, Tom speaking. How can I help?*

**Ahmed:** *Hi, yes — I applied for the web design evening course, and I haven't heard anything yet.*

**Admissions:** *Can I take your reference number?*

**Ahmed:** *It's W-D, two four, seven, oh, nine.*

**Admissions:** *Sorry — was that W-B or W-D?*

**Ahmed:** *W-D — D for David.*

**Admissions:** *Got it. Yes, I can see your application. It's been accepted — you should get an email tomorrow. The course starts on the fourteenth.*

**Ahmed:** *Great. So I'll get an email tomorrow, and it starts on the fourteenth?*

**Admissions:** *That's right.*

**Ahmed:** *Perfect. Thanks very much for your help.*

**Admissions:** *You're welcome. Bye now.*
::: solution
1. **Identification and purpose**: *Hi, this is Ahmed Karimi. I'm calling about my application…* — both in the first turn.
2. **Hold**: *Can you hold for a moment? — Sure.* / *Thanks for holding.*
3. **Transfer**: *I'll put you through to Admissions.* Ahmed has to repeat his purpose to the new person — normal after a transfer.
4. **Clarification**: *Sorry — was that W-B or W-D?* — a specific request about two letters that sound alike on the phone; Ahmed answers with a word (*D for David*), as in [[#rule-phone]].
5. **Summary**: *So I'll get an email tomorrow, and it starts on the fourteenth?* — a confirmation check of both key facts.
6. **Closing**: thanks and goodbye ([[everyday-english/phone-messages#rule-closing]]).
:::
:::

## Online meetings: sound and connection

Most problems in online meetings are technical, and they are dealt with in a small set of phrases. Learn them well enough to say them without thinking.

| problem | you can say | you may hear |
|---|---|---|
| someone is muted | *I think you're on mute.* / *We can't hear you — you're still muted.* | *Sorry, I was on mute!* |
| sound cuts in and out | *You're breaking up a bit.* / *Your sound's a bit choppy.* | *Can you hear me now?* |
| you lost the connection | *Sorry, I dropped out for a moment — could you repeat the last part?* | *We lost you for a second there.* |
| echo or noise | *There's a bit of an echo — could everyone who isn't speaking mute?* | *Sorry, that's my neighbour's drill!* |
| video problem | *Your video's frozen.* / *I'll turn my camera off to help the connection.* | *Can you see me?* |
| screen sharing | *Can everyone see my screen?* / *You're still sharing your screen.* | *I can see your email, not the slides.* |
| you need a moment | *Sorry, give me one second — I'm just finding the file.* | *No rush.* |

::: warning *Can you hear me?* again and again
✗ *Hello? Hello? Can you hear me? Hello?* (repeated many times, while others are trying to answer)
✓ *Can you hear me OK?* — then wait two or three seconds for a reply, or check the chat.

With a delay, people's answers arrive late, and if you keep talking you speak over them. Ask once, then wait. If nobody can hear you, someone will usually write in the chat. Similarly, ✗ talking for a minute without checking whether you are muted — ✓ a quick *Can you hear me OK?* at the start of your first turn saves embarrassment.
:::

::: example Handling problems politely {#ex-tech}
In a project meeting, three problems happen. Write what you would say in each case.

1. Kemal starts speaking, but you can see his lips moving and hear nothing.
2. You were disconnected for about twenty seconds while Aiko was explaining the budget.
3. Your sound is poor, and two people say they can't follow you.
::: solution
1. *Kemal, sorry — I think you're on mute.* (Use his name so that he knows the message is for him; say it kindly, because it happens to everyone.)
2. *Sorry, Aiko — my connection dropped for a moment. Could you just repeat the last part about the budget?* (Apologise briefly, say what you missed, and ask specifically — [[listening-and-conversation/clarification#rule-specific]].)
3. *Sorry, my connection seems bad today. I'll turn my camera off — is that better? If not, I'll put my main points in the chat.* (Offer a solution rather than only apologising.)
:::
:::

## Taking turns online

Delay and missing visual signals change turn-taking. Two people start together, both stop, both say *Sorry, go ahead*, and both start again. Or nobody speaks, because nobody can see who is going to.

::: theorem Turn-taking in online meetings {#rule-online-turns}
1. **Hand over with a name**: *What do you think, Priya?*, *Over to you, Tom.*, *Kenji, did you want to add something?* A name does the work of a glance.
2. **End your turn clearly**: *That's all from me.*, *So that's my update.*, *Any questions?* A falling tone and a clear closing phrase replace the visual signs of finishing.
3. **Leave a slightly longer gap** before you start, to allow for delay.
4. **If you overlap**, sort it out quickly: *Sorry — go ahead.* / *No, you go.* / *After you.* The person who was speaking first usually continues.
5. **Use the meeting's tools**: a raised-hand button, or a short message in the chat (*I'd like to come in on this*), especially in large meetings.
6. **Back-channel without unmuting**: nod and smile on camera, or react in the chat (*Agreed*, *+1*, *Good point*). Unmuting just to say *mm* adds noise.
:::

::: proof
These habits all make explicit what is normally signalled by the body. In a room, a speaker looks at the person they want to hear next; on a screen, a look cannot be directed, so a name is needed. A clear closing phrase tells everyone that a transition point has come ([[listening-and-conversation/turn-taking#def-trp]]), which otherwise may be hard to hear through poor sound. Longer gaps prevent the double starts caused by latency ([[#def-latency]]). And because several people starting at once is more frequent online, short, polite phrases for sorting out overlaps are used much more than in face-to-face meetings.
:::

:::

::: example Fixing an awkward exchange {#ex-overlap}
Rewrite this exchange from an online meeting so that it runs smoothly.

**Chair:** *OK, any comments on the new schedule?*

*(silence, three seconds)*

**Ines and Wang Hao (together):** *I think — / Just a —*

**Ines:** *Sorry.*

**Wang Hao:** *Sorry.*

*(silence)*

**Ines and Wang Hao (together):** *So — / I —*
::: solution
**Chair:** *OK, any comments on the new schedule? Ines, you mentioned some concerns earlier — shall we start with you?*

**Ines:** *Thanks. Yes, I think the deadline for phase two is a bit tight. That's my main point.*

**Chair:** *Thanks, Ines. Wang Hao?*

**Wang Hao:** *Just a quick one — I agree with Ines, and I'd add that two people in my team are away in March.*

Or, if the overlap still happens:

**Ines and Wang Hao (together):** *I think — / Just a —*

**Wang Hao:** *Sorry — go ahead, Ines.*

**Ines:** *Thanks. … So that's my point. Over to you, Wang Hao.*

The chair uses names to choose speakers; Ines ends her turn clearly (*That's my main point*); in the second version, Wang Hao resolves the overlap at once with a name, and Ines hands the turn back by name.
:::
:::

::: quiz
In a video meeting, you have just finished giving a short update. What is the clearest way to end your turn?
- [ ] Stop talking and wait.
- [ ] *Hello? Can you hear me?*
- [x] *So that's where we are. Any questions, or shall I hand back to you, Sanjay?*
- [ ] Unmute and mute repeatedly.
::: solution
The third option signals the end with a summary phrase (*So that's where we are*), offers questions, and hands the turn to a named person. Simply stopping leaves everyone unsure whether you have finished or lost the connection.
:::
:::

## Speaking up and taking part

In online meetings, quieter participants can easily disappear. Taking part is a skill, and so is helping others take part.

- **Coming in**: *Can I just come in here?* / *Sorry to jump in — just a quick point on that.* / *I'd like to add something to what Ines said.*
- **When you have been waiting**: *I had my hand up earlier — just going back to the budget…*
- **When you need time**: *Good question — let me think about that for a second.* / *Can I come back to you on that by email?* (better than a long silence)
- **Bringing others in** (as chair or as a colleague): *We haven't heard from Mateus yet — Mateus, any thoughts?*
- **Keeping time** (chair): *We've got about five minutes left, so let's move on to the last item.*

::: theorem Ending an online meeting {#rule-ending-meeting}
Meetings end best with three steps:

1. **A summary of decisions and actions**: *So, to sum up: we're moving the deadline to the 20th, Ines will update the schedule, and I'll email the client.*
2. **A check**: *Have I missed anything?* / *Is everyone clear on what they're doing?*
3. **Thanks and a clear goodbye**: *Thanks, everyone — see you next week.* Then leave the meeting; staying silently can look strange.
:::

::: example Summarising actions {#ex-summary}
At the end of a meeting, the chair, Dong Xue, has these notes: *launch moved 3 May → 10 May; Pablo → new timeline Fri; Aiko → tell sales team; next meeting Thu 2 pm*. Write what she says to close the meeting.
::: solution
*OK, let's wrap up there. So, to sum up: we've agreed to move the launch from the third of May to the tenth. Pablo, you'll send round the new timeline by Friday. Aiko, you'll let the sales team know. And our next meeting is on Thursday at two. Have I missed anything? … Great. Thanks, everyone — see you on Thursday.*

The summary turns notes into sentences, names each person with their action (*Pablo, you'll…*), uses *will* for promised actions, and ends with a check and a clear goodbye ([[#rule-ending-meeting]]).
:::
:::

::: quiz
On a phone call, you are not sure whether the caller said *Mr Fox* or *Mr Fosse*. What is the best thing to say?
- [ ] *Pardon?*
- [x] *Sorry, could you spell that for me?*
- [ ] *Mr Fox, yes.* (and hope it is right)
- [ ] *Speak louder, please.*
::: solution
On the phone, /ks/ and /s/ are easy to confuse because high-frequency sounds are weakened ([[#rule-missing]]). Asking for the spelling solves the problem at once. *Pardon?* will probably bring the same unclear sound again; guessing risks an embarrassing mistake; *Speak louder* is impolite and does not help.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **喂 and *Hello?*** Answering the phone with 喂 corresponds to *Hello?* in English. At work, English speakers often add their name or organisation: *Hello, Liu Mei speaking* / *Hartley Design, this is Mei*. Avoid ✗ *Who are you?* to ask who is calling; say *Who's calling, please?* or *Who am I speaking to?*
- **Waiting to be invited.** Some learners wait in online meetings until the chair calls their name. Many English-speaking chairs expect people to come in themselves, and silence can be read as having nothing to say. Use the raised-hand button, the chat, or *Can I just come in here?* ([[listening-and-conversation/turn-taking#rule-taking]]).
- **Seniority online.** If the most senior person in a meeting has not spoken, Chinese participants may wait for them. In many international teams, the chair rather than the most senior person manages turns; follow the chair's lead.
- **Cameras.** Expectations about having cameras on differ between organisations and countries. When unsure, ask: *Do you prefer cameras on?*
- **The chat.** Writing a question in the chat can feel easier than speaking. It is a good way to take part, but in smaller meetings, speaking up is usually expected too.
:::

::: history
Alexander Graham Bell was granted the American patent for the telephone in 1876. The first users had no agreed way to answer: Bell himself is said to have preferred *Ahoy*, while Thomas Edison promoted *Hello*, which soon became the standard telephone greeting in English. Telephone conventions — identifying yourself, asking for a person, spelling with words — grew up over the following decades; the radiotelephony spelling alphabet that begins *Alpha, Bravo, Charlie* was adopted internationally in the 1950s. Video calls were demonstrated by AT&T with its Picturephone at the New York World's Fair in 1964, but they became part of everyday working life only with internet video meetings in the 2000s and, on a very large scale, during the COVID-19 pandemic from 2020, when many organisations moved their meetings online and new conventions — *You're on mute*, the raised-hand button, the chat — spread around the world in a few months.
:::
