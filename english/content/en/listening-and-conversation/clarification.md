Wu Hua is lost in an unfamiliar part of town and asks a man at a bus stop for the library.

**Man:** *The library? Oh, it's easy — go down here, past the lights, take the second right after the church, and it's just there on your left, opposite the car park.*

**Wu Hua:** *Pardon?*

**Man:** *Down here, past the lights, second right after the church, and it's on your left.*

**Wu Hua:** *Sorry… pardon?*

**Man:** *(more slowly, a little louder)* *Down. Here. Past. The. Lights…*

Wu Hua understood most of it the first time. What she missed was one part — *second right after the church* — but *Pardon?* asked for everything again, and the man had no way to know which part was the problem. He repeated it all, then repeated it louder, and both of them began to feel uncomfortable.

Here is the same moment with a different question:

**Wu Hua:** *Sorry — past the lights, and then which turning?*

**Man:** *The second right — just after the church. You'll see a big white church.*

**Wu Hua:** *Second right after the church, then it's on the left. Thank you!*

This chapter teaches how to ask for clarification so that the other person knows exactly what to say again, how to ask for a word to be explained or spelled, how to check that you have understood — and how to check that you have been understood. Asking is not a sign of weak English. Every speaker, native or not, does it many times a day.

## Repair: fixing problems in conversation

::: definition Repair {#def-repair}
**Repair** is the way speakers deal with problems of speaking, hearing or understanding in conversation. In **self-repair**, the speaker fixes their own talk (*It's on Tuesday — sorry, Wednesday*). In **other-initiated repair**, the listener shows that there is a problem (*Sorry, which day?*) and the speaker then fixes it. A turn that shows a problem is a **clarification request**.
:::

Repair is a normal, frequent part of every conversation. Dingemanse and colleagues (2015), studying recorded everyday conversations in twelve languages, found that listeners initiated repair on average roughly once every minute and a half. A word that signals "I didn't catch that" — English *huh?* — exists in very similar form in many unrelated languages: Dingemanse, Torreira and Enfield (2013) found a short questioning syllable like *huh?* used this way in all 31 languages they examined, from Icelandic to Mandarin (*a?*) and Siwu. Not catching something, and saying so, is part of how all humans talk.

## Saying that you have not understood

Clarification requests go from very general to very specific.

| kind | examples | what the speaker has to do |
|---|---|---|
| **open** | *Sorry?*, *Pardon?* (more formal, GB), *Sorry, what was that?*, *Excuse me?* (AmE), *What?* (informal), *Huh?* (very informal) | repeat the whole turn |
| **question word** | *Who?*, *Where?*, *When did you say?*, *Which one?*, *How many?* | repeat one part |
| **repeat + question word** | *Past the lights and then where?*, *You're meeting who?* | repeat one part, which is clearly located |
| **repeat with a rise** | *The ↗second right?*, *↗Fifteen?* | confirm or correct one word |
| **candidate understanding** | *You mean the white church?*, *So I turn right at the church?* | say *yes* or correct |

::: theorem Be as specific as you can {#rule-specific}
When you have understood part of what was said, use the **most specific** clarification request you can: repeat what you understood and ask about the rest (*Past the lights and then which turning?*), or give your understanding for the speaker to confirm (*So it's the second right after the church?*). Use open requests (*Sorry?*) only when you have understood nothing, or almost nothing.
:::

::: proof
A specific request does two things that an open one cannot. It shows the speaker **which part** caused the problem, so they repeat only that, and it shows **what you have understood**, so they can see that the rest got through. Schegloff, Jefferson and Sacks (1977) observed that listeners tend to use the strongest repair initiator they can — the one that locates the trouble most precisely — and the research since has confirmed this pattern in other languages. An open request after a long turn gives the speaker a problem: they do not know whether you misheard, did not know a word, or disagreed, and they usually just repeat the same words — which did not work the first time.
:::

### Tone matters

Clarification requests are short, and their tone carries much of their meaning ([[connected-speech/intonation-meaning]]).

- ***Sorry?*** with a rise is the most common polite request in British English, and is widely used elsewhere. ***Pardon?*** (rising) is also polite, slightly more formal. In American English, ***Excuse me?*** with a rise is common, but with a fall-rise or a sharp tone it can mean "I'm offended — did you really say that?"
- ***What?*** alone is fine among friends but abrupt with strangers or in formal situations; ***Sorry, what was that?*** is the polite version.
- **Echo with a rise** (*↗Fifteen?*) asks for confirmation. Echo with a high, wide rise (*↗↗Fifteen?!*) expresses surprise: "That's a lot!"

::: quiz
At a hotel reception, you did not hear what the receptionist just said. Which is the most suitable request?
- [ ] *What?* (falling)
- [x] *Sorry, what was that?* (rising)
- [ ] *Huh?*
- [ ] *Excuse me?* (sharp fall-rise)
::: solution
*Sorry, what was that?* with a rise is polite and clear. *What?* with a fall and *Huh?* are fine among friends but abrupt with a stranger at work. *Excuse me?* with a sharp fall-rise can sound offended, as if the receptionist had said something rude.
:::
:::

::: example From *Pardon?* to a specific request {#ex-specific}
In each situation, the learner said *Pardon?*. Write a more specific clarification request.

1. A colleague: *The meeting's been moved to [unclear] on Thursday.*
2. A waiter: *Would you like [unclear] or chips with that?*
3. A doctor: *Take one tablet three times a day for [unclear] days.*
4. A friend: *I bumped into [unclear name] at the station yesterday.*
5. A shop assistant: *That's twelve [unclear].*
::: solution
1. *Sorry — moved to when on Thursday?* or *Sorry, what time on Thursday?*
2. *Sorry — or chips, or what was the other one?* / *Would I like what, sorry?*
3. *Sorry — for how many days?* (Numbers in medical instructions are worth checking even if you think you heard them: *For seven days?*)
4. *Sorry, you bumped into who?* (In informal speech *who* is normal here; formal *whom* would sound odd.)
5. *Sorry — twelve pounds what?* / *Twelve-fifty?* (a candidate answer with a rise).

Each request repeats the part that was understood and puts the question word or a guess where the gap was, so the speaker repeats only the missing piece.
:::

## Asking for repetition, spelling and slower speech

Sometimes you need more than one part repeated, or the speaker is simply too fast. These requests are polite and normal:

- *Sorry, could you say that again?* / *Could you repeat that, please?*
- *Sorry, I didn't catch that.* / *Sorry, I didn't quite catch your name.*
- *Could you speak a bit more slowly, please?* / *Sorry, could you slow down a little?*
- *Could you spell that for me?* / *How do you spell that?*
- *Sorry, the line's not very good — could you say that again?* (on the phone; see [[listening-and-conversation/calls-online-meetings]])

When spelling is unclear — *M* and *N*, *B*, *D*, *P*, *T* and *V* are easily confused — speakers use words to identify letters: *M for Mike*, *N for November*, *B for Bravo*. The words of the international radiotelephony spelling alphabet (*Alpha, Bravo, Charlie, Delta…*) are widely understood, but any clear word works: *B as in boy*, *P as in Paris*.

::: warning *Repeat again* and *Again, please*
✗ *Please repeat again.* ✗ *Again, please.* ✗ *Speak slowly!*
✓ *Sorry, could you say that again?* ✓ *Sorry, could you repeat that?* ✓ *Could you speak a bit more slowly, please?*

*Repeat* already means 'say again', so *repeat again* means 'say a third time'. *Again, please* and a bare imperative such as *Speak slowly!* sound like orders. Requests in English are usually made with *Could you…?* and softened with *Sorry* and *a bit* ([[everyday-english/requests-offers#rule-request-forms]]). *Sorry* at the start of a clarification request is not a real apology: it is a polite signal that a request is coming.
:::

## Asking for explanation

::: theorem Asking about a word or a meaning {#rule-explain}
1. To ask about **a word**, use *What does X mean?* or *Sorry, what's X?*: *What does "tenure" mean?* / *Sorry, what's a "plumber"?*
2. To ask about **what the speaker intends**, use *What do you mean (by X)?*: *What do you mean by "soon"?* — 'how soon, exactly?'
3. To ask for **an example** or **another way of saying it**: *Could you give me an example?* / *Could you put that another way?* / *Sorry, I'm not familiar with that word.*
4. To show **what you understood so far**: *So is it a kind of…?*

Note the word order: *What does it **mean**?* (with *does*), but *What do **you** mean?* (*you* is the subject).
:::

::: proof
*What does X mean?* asks about the language: the dictionary meaning of a word. *What do you mean?* asks about the person: what they are trying to say, why, or how much. The two can be very different. *What does "flexible" mean?* asks for a definition; *What do you mean by "flexible"?* in a job interview asks whether the hours, the place or the duties are flexible. Note that *What do you mean?* with a fall and a strong stress on *mean* can sound like a challenge — 'that makes no sense' — while *Sorry, what do you mean by…?* with a rise is a genuine question. *Sorry, I'm not familiar with that word* is a useful formal phrase: it puts the problem in the word, not in the speaker or the listener.
:::

::: example A word or a meaning? {#ex-meaning}
Write a suitable question for each situation.

1. In a seminar, the tutor uses the word *tenure*, which you do not know.
2. A landlord says the flat is available *in the near future*, and you need to know when you can move in.
3. A colleague says your report needs *a bit more punch*. You know the words, but not what she wants you to change.
4. A friend says she is *knackered*.
::: solution
1. *Sorry, what does "tenure" mean?* (or, more formally, *Sorry, I'm not familiar with the word "tenure"*). The question is about a word.
2. *What do you mean by "the near future"? Would it be this month?* — about the speaker's intention, with a candidate answer to make it concrete.
3. *What do you mean by "more punch"? Could you give me an example of something I could change?* — about intention, plus a request for an example.
4. *Knackered? What does that mean?* or simply *Knackered?* with a rise. (It means 'very tired' in informal British English.) Among friends, an echo with a rise is enough.
:::
:::

## Checking that you have understood

::: definition Confirmation check and comprehension check {#def-checks}
A **confirmation check** is a turn in which the listener says what they understood and asks the speaker to confirm it: *So that's Thursday at eight?* A **comprehension check** is a turn in which the speaker asks whether the listener has understood: *Does that make sense?* Together with clarification requests, these are the main tools of what Long (1983) called the **negotiation of meaning**.
:::

::: theorem Confirm what matters {#rule-confirm}
When an instruction, arrangement or number matters, **say back your understanding** before you act on it:

- *So, just to check — I take two a day, with food?*
- *OK, so that's the second right after the church, and then it's on the left.*
- *Let me read that back: oh one six one, four nine six, oh three three two?* ([[everyday-english/numbers-in-conversation#rule-check]])
- *So what you're saying is that we need to finish phase one first?*

The speaker confirms (*That's right*, *Exactly*) or corrects (*Not quite — after food, not with*).
:::

::: proof
A confirmation check costs a few seconds and catches the mistakes that matter most: the ones you do not know you have made. Chen Jing in [[listening-and-conversation/how-listening-works]] would have caught her mistake with *So I take them before breakfast?* — the pharmacist would have said *No — with food!*. The check works because it puts your understanding into words the speaker can hear and compare with what they meant. It also signals care, not weakness: careful professionals — doctors, pilots, people taking orders on the phone — read back important information as a routine.
:::

::: example Checking instructions {#ex-check}
Lucía is at a pharmacy with a prescription. Read the conversation and find the clarification requests and checks.

**Pharmacist:** *These are antibiotics. You need to take one capsule every eight hours, and finish the whole course, even if you feel better.*

**Lucía:** *Every eight hours — so three a day?*

**Pharmacist:** *That's right. And avoid dairy within two hours of each dose.*

**Lucía:** *Sorry — avoid what?*

**Pharmacist:** *Dairy — milk, cheese, yoghurt. They stop the medicine working properly.*

**Lucía:** *Ah, OK. So no milk two hours before or after?*

**Pharmacist:** *Exactly. Does that all make sense?*

**Lucía:** *Yes. Three a day, finish them all, and no dairy two hours either side. Thank you.*
::: solution
- *Every eight hours — so three a day?* — a **confirmation check**: Lucía turns *every eight hours* into a number she can act on.
- *Sorry — avoid what?* — a **specific clarification request**: she repeats *avoid* and asks about the unknown word.
- The pharmacist **explains** rather than repeating: *milk, cheese, yoghurt* — examples.
- *So no milk two hours before or after?* — a **confirmation check** of her interpretation of *within two hours*.
- *Does that all make sense?* — a **comprehension check** from the speaker.
- Lucía's last turn is a **summary**: she says back all three instructions, giving the pharmacist a final chance to correct anything.
:::
:::

### When you are the speaker

Checking works in both directions. When you explain something, especially to someone whose English is weaker than yours or who seems confused, you can check their understanding:

- *Does that make sense?* / *Is that OK?* / *Am I making sense?* — friendly and common.
- *Do you know what I mean?* — informal.
- *Is that clear?* — fine from a teacher or in instructions; between equals it can sound a little like a teacher.
- ✗ *Do you understand?* — can sound as if you think the listener is slow, especially with a fall. *Does that make sense?* puts the question on your explanation instead.

And when someone asks you for clarification, **do not just repeat the same words louder**. Rephrase, simplify, give an example, spell, or slow down — as the pharmacist did with *dairy*.

::: quiz
A colleague says: *Could you send me the figures by close of play?* You don't know *close of play*. What is the best question?
- [ ] *Pardon?*
- [ ] *What do you mean?* (falling, with strong stress on *mean*)
- [x] *Sorry — by close of what? What time is that?*
- [ ] *Repeat again, please.*
::: solution
The third option repeats what was understood (*by close of*), asks about the unclear part, and asks for the practical meaning: *close of play* means 'the end of the working day' in informal British business English. *Pardon?* would probably get the same words again. A falling *What do you mean?* can sound like a challenge. *Repeat again, please* is not idiomatic and sounds like an order.
:::
:::

## When you are not understood

Sometimes the problem is on your side: the listener looks confused, asks *Sorry?*, or answers a different question. Then you need **self-repair**.

::: theorem Repairing your own talk {#rule-self-repair}
1. **Do not just repeat** the same sentence: rephrase it, more simply.
2. **Use a repair marker**: *Sorry, I mean…*, *What I'm trying to say is…*, *Let me put it another way…*, *In other words…*.
3. **If you don't know a word**, describe it: *It's a kind of…*, *It's a thing you use to…*, *It's like a…, but…*, *the opposite of…*.
4. **Spell or write** a name or a key word if the sound is the problem.
5. **Check** at the end: *Does that make sense?*
:::

::: example Finding a way round a missing word {#ex-paraphrase}
Ahmed wants to buy a colander in a shop but does not know the word. Write what he could say to the shop assistant.
::: solution
*Excuse me — I'm looking for a… sorry, I don't know the word. It's a kind of bowl with lots of small holes in it. You use it to drain pasta, or to wash vegetables.*

The assistant: *Oh, a colander! Aisle six.*

Ahmed uses a repair marker (*sorry, I don't know the word*), names the general type (*a kind of bowl*), gives its form (*with lots of small holes*) and its use (*to drain pasta*). Most objects can be identified with **category + form + use**. Now he knows the word, and is likely to remember it.
:::
:::

::: example Directions, repaired {#ex-directions}
Kofi is giving Li Ming directions over the phone. Rewrite Li Ming's turns so that every problem is repaired specifically.

**Kofi:** *Get off at Hartfield, come out of the station, turn left, and walk up to the roundabout — it's about five minutes.*

**Li Ming:** *Pardon?*

**Kofi:** *Get off at Hartfield…*

**Li Ming:** *What?*
::: solution
**Kofi:** *Get off at Hartfield, come out of the station, turn left, and walk up to the roundabout — it's about five minutes.*

**Li Ming:** *Sorry, get off at which station? Could you spell it?*

**Kofi:** *Hartfield — H-A-R-T, field.*

**Li Ming:** *OK, Hartfield. Then turn left and walk up to the… sorry, what's a roundabout?*

**Kofi:** *It's a place where several roads meet and the cars go round in a circle.*

**Li Ming:** *Oh, I see. So left out of the station, five minutes to the roundabout. And then?*

Each turn now repeats what was understood (*turn left and walk up to the…*), asks about one problem (the station name, an unknown word), and confirms (*So left out of the station…*). *And then?* asks Kofi to continue. Note *roundabout* (BrE) is called a *traffic circle* or *rotary* in parts of the US.
:::
:::

::: warning Pretending to understand
✗ Nodding and saying *Yes, yes* when you have not understood, to avoid seeming rude or slow.
✓ Asking a short, specific question at once: *Sorry — which form?*

Pretending causes bigger problems later — a missed appointment, a wrong order, a task done wrongly — and these are much more embarrassing than a question. It also teaches the speaker that you understand at their speed, so they do not adjust. A quick *Sorry, which one?* is a normal part of English conversation.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Asking feels like losing face.** Many Chinese learners feel that asking someone to repeat is a nuisance to them, or shows that one's English is poor. In English conversation, clarification requests are so frequent that they are hardly noticed. What is noticed is a wrong answer to a question you did not understand.
- **啊？ and 什么？** Mandarin listeners often say 啊？ or 什么？ when they have not heard. The English equivalents are *Huh?* and *What?* — fine with friends, but with strangers or at work, use *Sorry?* or *Sorry, what was that?*
- ***I don't understand* or *I didn't catch that*?** *I didn't catch that* or *I missed that* puts the problem in the hearing — the noise, the speed — and is the more comfortable phrase. *I don't understand* is fine for a meaning or an idea (*I don't understand how this works*), but after a simple sentence it can sound as if your English is the problem when it may only have been the noise.
- **Specific questions.** A Mandarin habit of repeating part of a turn with a question word (你说几点？) transfers well: *You said what time?* or *Sorry, what time did you say?* is exactly the kind of specific request English speakers use.
- **Names.** Chinese names are often hard for English speakers to catch. Spell yours, give the family name clearly, and say which is which: *It's Wu — W-U — that's my family name. My first name's Hua.*
:::

::: history
In 1977 Emanuel Schegloff, Gail Jefferson and Harvey Sacks published "The preference for self-correction in the organization of repair in conversation" in the journal *Language*. Using recordings of everyday talk, they showed that problems in conversation are handled by a regular system: speakers usually get a chance to fix their own trouble first, and when a listener initiates repair, they use a small set of forms, from *huh?* to repeating part of the turn. In second-language research, Michael Long (1983) showed that native speakers talking to learners use many confirmation checks, comprehension checks and clarification requests, and argued that this **negotiation of meaning** makes input understandable; Evangeline Varonis and Susan Gass (1985) found that conversations between two learners contain even more of it. More recently, Mark Dingemanse and colleagues compared repair in many languages (2013, 2015) and found that the basic options — and even the word *huh?* — are remarkably similar across languages.
:::

::: exercise Analyse a conversation {level=3}
Read the conversation between a hotel receptionist and a guest, Mr Tan. Identify every clarification request, confirmation check and comprehension check, and say which is most effective and why. Then rewrite the receptionist's turns to make them easier to follow.

**Receptionist:** *Breakfast is served in the Orchard Room on the mezzanine from half six till ten, but at weekends it's seven till half ten.*

**Mr Tan:** *Sorry, the what room?*

**Receptionist:** *The Orchard Room. On the mezzanine.*

**Mr Tan:** *Mezzanine?*

**Receptionist:** *Yes, the mezzanine. Do you understand?*

**Mr Tan:** *…Yes.*
::: hint

::: solution
**Requests and checks.** *Sorry, the what room?* — a specific clarification request (effective: it locates the problem exactly). *Mezzanine?* — an echo with a rise (a request to explain a word). *Do you understand?* — a comprehension check, but an unhelpful one: it may sound condescending, and Mr Tan's hesitant *…Yes* suggests he still doesn't understand.

**The main problem** is that the receptionist repeats instead of explaining. *The mezzanine* is repeated twice without explanation.

**Rewritten:**

**Receptionist:** *Breakfast is in the Orchard Room — that's on the mezzanine, the half-floor just above this one; you can take the stairs there. On weekdays it's from half six to ten, and at weekends from seven to half ten.*

**Mr Tan:** *Sorry, the what room?*

**Receptionist:** *The Orchard Room — O-R-C-H-A-R-D. It's up those stairs, on your left.*

::: exercise Role play {level=3}
With a partner, take turns giving each other directions from your school or workplace to a place nearby, at natural speed, with at least one place name and one word your partner may not know. The listener must not say *Pardon?* or *Sorry?* alone: use only specific requests, explanation requests and confirmation checks. At the end, the listener summarises the route. Then swap.
::: solution
Answers vary. A successful role play contains several specific requests (*Sorry, left at the what?*), at least one explanation request (*What's a zebra crossing?*), at least one confirmation check (*So it's the second left after the bank?*), and a final summary that the speaker confirms or corrects. A useful reflection afterwards: which requests did the speaker answer most easily? Usually the ones that repeated part of the route and placed a question word in the gap.
:::
:::
