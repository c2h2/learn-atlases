*Give* is an easy word, and so is *up*. But *She gave up* does not mean that she gave something upwards: it means that she stopped trying. English has thousands of combinations like this — *give up*, *find out*, *turn off*, *get on*, *put up with* — and they are everywhere in conversation, films and emails between friends. They also have a small grammar of their own:

- *Turn off the light.* ✓
- *Turn the light off.* ✓
- *Turn it off.* ✓
- ✗ *Turn off it.*

This lesson explains what phrasal verbs are, how particles such as *up* and *out* add meaning, where the object goes, and when a phrasal verb or a single verb is the better choice.

## What a phrasal verb is

::: definition Phrasal verb and particle {#def-phrasal}
A **phrasal verb** is a verb + a **particle** that together act as one unit of meaning: *get up*, *sit down*, *give up*, *find out*, *turn off*. The particle is a short adverb, usually one that also exists as a preposition: *about*, *around* (BrE also *round*), *away*, *back*, *down*, *in*, *off*, *on*, *out*, *over*, *through*, *up*.

Phrasal verbs can be **intransitive** (no object: *The plane **took off**.* *Please **sit down**.*) or **transitive** (with an object: ***Take off** your shoes.* *She **brought up** three children.*).
:::

Many grammars use **multi-word verb** for the whole family, which includes:

| type | form | examples |
|---|---|---|
| phrasal verb | verb + particle (adverb) | *give up*, *turn off*, *find out*, *grow up* |
| prepositional verb | verb + preposition + object | *look after* (the children), *depend on*, *listen to* |
| phrasal-prepositional verb | verb + particle + preposition | *look forward to*, *put up with*, *run out of* |

Learner's books and dictionaries often call all three "phrasal verbs". The difference matters mainly for where the object goes, and it is explained below; prepositional verbs have their own lesson, [[verb-patterns/prepositional-verbs]].

## How particles add meaning

The meaning of a phrasal verb is sometimes easy to see from its parts, and sometimes not.

::: theorem Three kinds of meaning {#rule-meanings}
1. **Literal**: verb and particle keep their ordinary meanings, often of movement and direction: *stand **up***, *sit **down***, *go **out***, *come **back***, *take **away***, *look **round*** (BrE) / *look **around***.
2. **Aspectual**: the particle adds an idea of completion, continuation, beginning or repetition to the verb's normal meaning: *eat **up*** (eat all of it), *drink **up***, *use **up***, *write **down*** (on paper), *carry **on*** (continue), *read **through*** (from start to end), *set **off*** (start a journey).
3. **Idiomatic**: the meaning cannot be worked out from the parts: *give **up*** (stop trying), *put **off*** (postpone), *turn **down*** (refuse), *bring **up*** (raise a child; mention a topic), *make **up*** (invent; become friends again), *get **over*** (recover from).
:::

::: proof
This three-way division is common in teaching grammars and corpus studies (Quirk et al. 1985 distinguish free combinations, semi-idiomatic and highly idiomatic combinations; Biber et al. 1999 make a similar point). The boundaries are not sharp, and one phrasal verb can have all three kinds of meaning: *take off* is literal in *Take off your coat*, idiomatic in *The plane took off* (left the ground) and in *Her career took off* (suddenly became successful). Even idiomatic phrasal verbs are often less random than they look: many particles have a few core meanings that run through many verbs, as the next table shows. Learning these core meanings makes new phrasal verbs easier to guess and to remember; some learner's books, such as Rudzka-Ostyn's *Word Power: Phrasal Verbs and Compounds* (2003), are organised around them.
:::

| particle | common meanings | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *up* | upwards; completely, to the end; increase; appear, approach | *stand up*; *eat up*, *tidy up*, *fill up*; *turn up the volume*, *prices went up*; *turn up* (arrive), *come up* (be mentioned) |
| *down* | downwards; decrease; on paper; stop working | *sit down*; *slow down*, *turn down the heat*; *write down*, *note down*; *break down* |
| *out* | out of a place; to the end; discovery; disappear; to many people | *go out*; *sell out*; *find out*, *work out*; *die out*, *run out*; *give out*, *hand out* |
| *off* | away, departure; disconnect; finish | *set off*, *take off*; *turn off*, *switch off*; *finish off*, *pay off* (a debt) |
| *on* | continue; wear; connect | *carry on*, *go on*, *keep on*; *put on*, *try on*; *turn on*, *switch on* |
| *back* | return; reply; in reserve | *come back*, *give back*; *call back*, *write back*; *hold back* |
| *over* | from one side or person to another; examine; again (AmE) | *hand over*, *take over*; *look over*, *think over*; *do over* (AmE) |

::: example Guessing from the particle {#ex-guess}
Use the meanings of the particles in the table to guess what the phrasal verbs in bold mean. (a) *Please **drink up** — we're leaving.* (b) *The dinosaurs **died out** millions of years ago.* (c) *Could you **slow down** a bit? I can't follow.* (d) *Let me **think it over** and I'll tell you tomorrow.* (e) *The teacher **handed out** the exam papers.*
::: solution
(a) *up* = completely: finish your drink.

(b) *out* = disappear: became extinct, until none were left.

(c) *down* = decrease: speak or move more slowly.

(d) *over* = examine: consider it carefully.

(e) *out* = to many people: gave one paper to each student.

In each case the verb keeps its own meaning, and the particle adds the direction or the end point. Always check a guess in context or in a dictionary: some phrasal verbs, like *give up*, cannot be guessed.
:::

## Where the object goes

::: theorem The object of a transitive phrasal verb {#rule-object-position}
With a transitive phrasal verb:

1. A **noun** object can go **before or after** the particle: *Turn off **the light**.* / *Turn **the light** off.* *She picked up **her bag**.* / *She picked **her bag** up.*
2. A **pronoun** object (*it*, *them*, *her* …) goes **between** the verb and the particle: *Turn **it** off.* *She picked **it** up.* (✗ *Turn off it.* ✗ *She picked up it.*)
3. A **long** object usually goes **after** the particle: *Turn off **all the lights in the building**.* *She brought up **the question of who would pay**.*
:::

::: proof
The particle of a phrasal verb is an adverb, and like other short adverbs it can follow the object. A pronoun is short, unstressed and refers to known information, so it is placed early, before the stressed particle; a long or new object is placed at the end, following the principles of end focus and end weight (see [[verb-patterns/two-objects#rule-end-focus]]). Biber et al. (1999) found that with noun objects both orders are common, with the particle-first order more frequent when the object is long. A small number of phrasal verbs are used mostly in one order: *take part in*, *carry out* (*carry out an experiment* is far more usual than *carry an experiment out*), and in some idioms the object is fixed in the middle: *cry your eyes out*, *talk somebody into something*.
:::

::: warning Pronoun after the particle
✗ *I wrote down it.* ✓ *I wrote it down.*

✗ *These shoes are dirty — take off them, please.* ✓ *Take them off, please.*

✗ *If you don't know the word, look up it.* ✓ *Look it up.*

But with **prepositional** verbs the pronoun follows the preposition: *She looks after **them*** (✗ *looks them after*). See the test below.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *I can't find my keys. Have you seen? I put down them somewhere.*
- [ ] *The music is too loud. Can you turn down it?*
- [x] *Here's the form. Please fill it in and give it back to me.*
- [ ] *That's a good idea. Let me write down it.*
::: solution
With a pronoun object, the pronoun goes between the verb and the particle: *fill **it** in*, *give **it** back*. The others should be *put them down*, *turn it down* and *write it down*.
:::
:::

### Phrasal verb or prepositional verb?

Some combinations look alike but behave differently: *look **up** a word* (phrasal) and *look **at** a picture* (prepositional).

::: theorem Telling phrasal and prepositional verbs apart {#rule-phrasal-test}
1. **Pronoun test**: with a phrasal verb, a pronoun goes before the particle (*look **it** up*); with a prepositional verb, it goes after the preposition (*look at **it***, *look after **them***).
2. **Noun test**: with a phrasal verb, a noun object can go before the particle (*look the word up*); with a prepositional verb, it cannot (✗ *look the picture at*).
3. **Stress**: in a phrasal verb the particle is usually stressed, especially at the end: *Turn it **OFF**.* *She gave **UP**.* In a prepositional verb, the preposition is normally unstressed: ***LOOK** at it.* ***LOOK** after them.*
:::

::: example Phrasal or prepositional? {#ex-test}
Use the pronoun test to decide whether each combination is a phrasal verb (P) or a prepositional verb (Pr), and replace the object with *it* or *them*. (a) *She takes after her mother.* (b) *He took off his jacket.* (c) *We're looking into the problem.* (d) *They called off the meeting.* (e) *I ran into an old friend.*
::: solution
Try both positions for the pronoun; only one will sound right.

(a) *She takes after **her*** (✗ *takes her after*): **Pr** — *take after* ‘resemble a parent’.

(b) *He took **it** off* (✗ *took off it*): **P**.

(c) *We're looking into **it*** (✗ *looking it into*): **Pr** — *look into* ‘investigate’.

(d) *They called **it** off* (✗ *called off it*): **P** — *call off* ‘cancel’.

(e) *I ran into **him** / **her*** (✗ *ran him into*): **Pr** — *run into* ‘meet by chance’.

A good learner's dictionary shows this difference: Oxford marks phrasal verbs with a separable object as *sth ↔ up* or *sb/sth up*, and prepositional verbs as *look after sb*.
:::
:::

## Three-word verbs

::: theorem Verb + particle + preposition {#rule-three-word}
Phrasal-prepositional verbs have a particle and then a preposition. The object comes **after the whole verb**, never between the parts, even when it is a pronoun:

- *I'm looking forward to **the weekend**.* / *I'm looking forward to **it**.*
- *How do you put up with **that noise**?* / *How do you put up with **it**?*
- *We've run out of **milk**.*
- *She came up with **a great idea**.*
- *I can't keep up with **you** — slow down!*
- *You should cut down on **sugar**.*
- *He got away with **cheating**.* (‘was not punished for’)
:::

::: warning Splitting a three-word verb
✗ *I'm looking forward it to.* ✓ *I'm looking forward to it.*

✗ *We've run out it of.* ✓ *We've run out of it.*

✗ *I look forward to see you.* ✓ *I look forward to seeing you.* (*to* is a preposition: [[verb-patterns/ing-or-infinitive#rule-after-prep]])
:::

::: example One phrasal verb, several meanings {#ex-pick-up}
A learner's dictionary gives these meanings of *pick up*, among others: (1) ‘lift something from a surface’, (2) ‘collect someone in a car’, (3) ‘learn something without studying’, (4) ‘get better, improve’. Which meaning is used in each sentence, and is the verb transitive or intransitive? (a) *Business usually picks up before the holidays.* (b) *She picked up a lot of Cantonese from her neighbours.* (c) *Can you pick me up from the station?* (d) *He picked the baby up and kissed her.*
::: solution
1. Check whether there is an object: if not, the verb is intransitive.
2. Use the object and the context to choose the meaning.

(a) No object: intransitive, meaning (4) — business improves.

(b) Object *a lot of Cantonese*: transitive, meaning (3) — she learned it informally.

(c) Object *me*, placed between verb and particle: transitive, meaning (2).

(d) Object *the baby*, before the particle: transitive, meaning (1).

One phrasal verb can have literal and idiomatic meanings, transitive and intransitive uses. Learn each meaning with an example sentence, as the dictionary gives it.
:::
:::

## Phrasal verbs and register

::: theorem Phrasal verbs and one-word verbs {#rule-register}
Many phrasal verbs have a one-word equivalent of Latin or French origin. The phrasal verb is usually more informal and is typical of conversation; the one-word verb is more formal:

| phrasal verb (informal, neutral) | one-word verb (more formal) |
|---|---|
| *find out* | *discover* |
| *put off* | *postpone* |
| *go up* | *increase*, *rise* |
| *look into* | *investigate* |
| *come up with* | *devise*, *propose* |
| *turn down* | *reject*, *refuse* |
| *set up* | *establish* |
| *put up with* | *tolerate* |

In formal writing, prefer the one-word verb where one exists. But many phrasal verbs are normal in all registers, including academic writing (*carry out* an experiment, *point out* a problem, *set up* a study), and some have no one-word equivalent at all (*get up*, *sit down*, *grow up*).
:::

::: proof
Biber et al. (1999) found phrasal verbs most frequent in fiction and conversation and least frequent in academic prose, but even there, a few — *carry out*, *point out*, *set up*, *find out* — are common. The pairs in the table are not perfect synonyms: *find out* suggests getting information by asking or looking, *discover* also suits scientific discoveries; *put up with* is everyday, *tolerate* is used of medicines and systems as well as people. A sentence full of Latinate verbs in conversation (*I shall postpone our meeting, as I must investigate the matter*) sounds stiff; a formal report full of phrasal verbs (*We looked into the problem and found out that costs had gone up*) sounds too casual. The topic is developed in [[collocation-and-register/phrasal-verbs-register]].
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is best for a formal written report?
- [ ] *We put off the survey because loads of people dropped out.*
- [x] *The survey was postponed because many participants withdrew.*
- [ ] *We put the survey off 'cause lots of people pulled out.*
- [ ] *The survey got put off because many people dropped out of it.*
::: solution
The second sentence uses one-word verbs (*postponed*, *withdrew*), a neutral subject and no informal words (*loads of*, *'cause*). The others are fine in conversation. Note that phrasal verbs are not wrong in reports — *carry out a survey* is normal — but here formal equivalents exist and fit better.
:::
:::

### British and American English

Most phrasal verbs are shared, but some differ:

- *fill in* a form (BrE) / *fill out* a form (AmE; also used in BrE)
- *ring* someone *up* (BrE, informal) / *call* someone *up* (both)
- *look round* (BrE) / *look around* (both)
- *do* something *over* ‘do it again’ (AmE)
- *meet up* (with) ‘meet socially’ is common in both; *meet with* ‘have a meeting with’ is mainly American

### Nouns from phrasal verbs

Many phrasal verbs have related nouns, written as one word or with a hyphen, and stressed on the **first** part: *a **BREAK**down* (from *break **DOWN***), *a **TAKE**off*, *a **WORK**out*, *a **CHECK**-in*, *a **SET**-up*, *a **TAKE**away* (BrE) / *a **TAKE**out* (AmE). The verb has the stress on the particle; the noun has it on the verb part.

::: example Making a message more formal {#ex-formal}
Rewrite this message to a manager in more formal English, replacing the phrasal verbs with one-word verbs where possible. *We've looked into the problem and found out that the price of paper has gone up. We'd like to put off the order until next month and set up a meeting with the supplier.*
::: solution
Replace each phrasal verb with a formal equivalent, and check that the grammar still fits (some one-word verbs take different objects).

- *looked into* → *investigated*
- *found out* → *discovered* (or *found*)
- *gone up* → *increased* (or *risen*)
- *put off* → *postpone*
- *set up* → *arrange* (*establish* is used for organisations, not meetings)

*We have investigated the problem and discovered that the price of paper has increased. We would like to postpone the order until next month and arrange a meeting with the supplier.*

The contractions (*We've*, *We'd*) are also expanded, as is usual in formal writing.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Chinese has similar verb + direction words.** Mandarin directional and resultative complements (趋向补语, 结果补语) work much like English particles: 站**起来** *stand up*, 坐**下** *sit down*, 写**下来** *write down*, 找**出来** *find out*, 吃**完** / 吃**掉** *eat up*, 关**掉** *turn off*, 拿**回来** *bring back*. Li & Thompson (1981) describe these as resultative and directional verb compounds. Thinking of an English particle as a direction or result added to a verb, as in Chinese, is a good way into phrasal verbs.
- **The order differs.** In Chinese the complement usually follows the verb directly, and a known object is often moved forward with 把: 把灯关掉. In English a pronoun object goes between verb and particle: *Turn **it** off.* (Chinese does have some freedom here too: in 拿出一本书来 the object stands inside the complement 出……来.)
- **Avoiding phrasal verbs.** A study of Chinese learners (Liao & Fukuya 2004) found that intermediate learners tended to avoid phrasal verbs, especially idiomatic ones, and to choose one-word verbs instead; advanced learners avoided them much less. The result is English that is correct but stiff in conversation (*I will return at six* for *I'll be back at six*). Learn the common phrasal verbs as whole expressions, with example sentences.
- **Particles are not optional.** Chinese learners sometimes leave the particle out (✗ *I'll pick you at six*, ✓ *I'll pick you **up** at six*) or add one where English has none (✗ *discuss about*; see [[verb-patterns/transitive-intransitive]]).
:::

::: history
English phrasal verbs grew in number in the Middle English period, at the same time as many Old English verbal prefixes (such as *a-*, *for-* and *to-*) fell out of use; historians of English (for example in *The Cambridge History of the English Language*) see the particles partly taking over the prefixes' work. Samuel Johnson already discussed them in the preface to his *Dictionary* (1755), noting that English changes the meaning of many verbs by adding a particle and that this causes foreign learners great difficulty. The name *phrasal verb* became established in the twentieth century: Logan Pearsall Smith used it in *Words and Idioms* (1925), saying that it had been suggested to him by Henry Bradley, one of the editors of the *Oxford English Dictionary*. Dictionaries devoted to phrasal verbs followed, among them Cowie and Mackin's *Oxford Dictionary of Current Idiomatic English*, vol. 1 (1975).
:::

## Where this leads

Prepositional verbs (*depend on*, *look after*) and the prepositions after adjectives and nouns are the topic of [[verb-patterns/prepositional-verbs]]. Phrasal verbs in the passive (*The meeting was called off*) appear in [[verb-patterns/the-passive]]. Choosing between phrasal verbs and their formal equivalents is developed in [[collocation-and-register/phrasal-verbs-register]], and the stress patterns of verbs and their nouns (*break DOWN*, *BREAKdown*) in [[connected-speech/sentence-stress]].

::: summary
- A phrasal verb is a verb + particle (*up*, *down*, *out*, *off*, *on* …) that acts as one unit: *give up*, *find out*, *turn off* ([[#def-phrasal]]).
- Meanings can be literal (*sit down*), aspectual (*eat up*, *carry on*) or idiomatic (*give up*, *put off*) ([[#rule-meanings]]); particles have core meanings that help with guessing.
- A noun object can go before or after the particle; a pronoun goes between verb and particle: *turn **it** off* ([[#rule-object-position]]).
- Prepositional verbs (*look after*, *look at*) keep the object after the preposition; the pronoun test tells the two kinds apart ([[#rule-phrasal-test]]).
- Three-word verbs (*look forward to*, *put up with*, *run out of*) take their object after the whole verb ([[#rule-three-word]]).
- Phrasal verbs are typical of conversation; formal writing often uses one-word equivalents, though some phrasal verbs (*carry out*, *point out*) are normal everywhere ([[#rule-register]]).
:::
