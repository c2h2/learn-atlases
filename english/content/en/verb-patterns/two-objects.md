You can say *Can you show me the word?* and *Can you give me the word?*, so why not ✗ *Can you explain me the word?* The meaning is clear, and Chinese says 给我解释一下这个词 without any difficulty. But *explain* belongs to a different group of verbs from *show* and *give*. English has two ways of saying who receives something:

- *She gave **Li Wei** a book.*
- *She gave a book **to Li Wei**.*

*Give* can use both. *Explain* can use only the second: ✓ *Can you explain the word **to me**?* This lesson shows the two patterns, which verbs take *to* and which take *for*, how to choose between the patterns in a real sentence, and which verbs allow only one of them.

## Two patterns

In [[core-grammar/sentence-patterns]] you met the pattern SVOO, with two objects.

::: definition Indirect and direct object {#def-two-objects}
With verbs such as *give*, *send*, *show* and *buy*, a clause can have two objects:

- the **direct object** (Od) is the thing that is given, sent, shown or bought;
- the **indirect object** (Oi) is usually the person who receives it or who benefits from the action.

The two objects can come in two orders:

| pattern | form | example |
|---|---|---|
| **double-object pattern** | V + Oi + Od | *I sent **my mother** (Oi) **some flowers** (Od).* |
| **prepositional pattern** | V + Od + *to* / *for* + person | *I sent **some flowers** (Od) **to my mother**.* |

In the second pattern the person is no longer an object: it is part of a prepositional phrase.
:::

Some more pairs with the same meaning:

- *Can you pass **me** the salt?* — *Can you pass the salt **to me**?*
- *He told **the children** a story.* — *He told a story **to the children**.*
- *I'll buy **you** a coffee.* — *I'll buy a coffee **for you**.*
- *She made **her son** a cake.* — *She made a cake **for her son**.*

### *To* or *for*?

::: theorem Verbs with *to* and verbs with *for* {#rule-to-for}
In the prepositional pattern, the preposition depends on the verb.

1. ***To*** is used when the thing **goes to** the person: it is handed, sent, told or shown to them. Verbs: *bring*, *give*, *hand*, *lend*, *offer*, *owe*, *pass*, *pay*, *promise*, *read*, *sell*, *send*, *show*, *take*, *teach*, *tell*, *throw*, *write*.
2. ***For*** is used when someone does something **for the benefit of** the person — gets, makes or keeps something for them. Verbs: *book*, *build*, *buy*, *choose*, *cook*, *find*, *get*, *keep*, *leave*, *make*, *order*, *pour*, *save*.
:::

::: proof
The two groups have different meanings. With *to*, the person is the receiver of a transfer: when I *give* or *send* something *to* you, it moves from me to you. With *for*, the person is the beneficiary: when I *buy* or *cook* something *for* you, I do it on your behalf, and you may not receive anything yet (*I bought a present for Wang Fang, but I haven't given it to her*). Reference grammars describe the two groups in this way (Quirk et al. 1985; Swan 2016), and Levin (1993) lists the verbs of each. A few verbs can take either preposition with a small change of meaning: *She wrote a letter **to** her aunt* (her aunt received it) but *She wrote a letter **for** her aunt* (her aunt could not write it herself). *Bring* and *take* normally take *to*, but *for* appears when the meaning is ‘as a present for’: *I've brought something **for** you.*
:::

::: example Finding the indirect object and the preposition {#ex-to-for}
Rewrite each sentence in the prepositional pattern, choosing *to* or *for*. (a) *Amina sent her brother a text message.* (b) *Can you get me a glass of water?* (c) *The teacher showed the class a short video.* (d) *My father cooked us dinner.* (e) *I owe my flatmate fifty pounds.*
::: solution
In each sentence the first object after the verb is the indirect object (the person); the second is the direct object (the thing). Move the person to the end with a preposition.

(a) *Amina sent a text message **to** her brother.* — the message went to him: *to*.

(b) *Can you get a glass of water **for** me?* — you are asked to fetch it on my behalf: *for*.

(c) *The teacher showed a short video **to** the class.* — *to*.

(d) *My father cooked dinner **for** us.* — he cooked for our benefit: *for*.

(e) *I owe fifty pounds **to** my flatmate.* — the money should go to the flatmate: *to*.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *She bought a new phone to her daughter.*
- [x] *She bought a new phone for her daughter.*
- [ ] *She lent her bike for me.*
- [ ] *Please send the file for me by Friday.*
::: solution
*Buy* takes *for*: it is done for the daughter's benefit. *Lend* and *send* take *to* when the person receives the thing: *She lent her bike **to** me*, *Please send the file **to** me*. (*Send the file for me* means ‘send it on my behalf, because I can't’ — a different meaning.)
:::
:::

## Choosing between the two patterns

When a verb allows both patterns, the meaning is the same; the choice depends on what is new and what is long.

::: theorem End focus: put the new information last {#rule-end-focus}
In English, the most important, new information usually comes at the end of the clause.

1. If the **thing** is the new information, use the double-object pattern: *What did you give your sister?* — *I gave her **a scarf**.*
2. If the **person** is the new information, use the prepositional pattern: *Who did you give the scarf to?* — *I gave it **to my sister**.*
3. A **long** object usually goes last: *I lent Tom **the book that you recommended last week**.* *I lent the book **to a friend of mine who is studying law**.*
:::

::: proof
This is a general principle of English word order, often called **end focus** and **end weight** (Quirk et al. 1985; Biber et al. 1999): the end of a clause is where listeners expect the news, and long phrases are easier to process at the end. In conversation the person is very often a pronoun that the listener already knows (*me*, *her*, *them*), so the double-object pattern is the more common one: *Give **me** a minute*, *Tell **me** your name*, *Send **us** a postcard*. The prepositional pattern is chosen when the person is new, contrasted or long.
:::

### When both objects are pronouns

::: theorem Two pronoun objects {#rule-pronouns}
When the thing is a pronoun (*it*, *them*), use the prepositional pattern:

- *Give **it to me**.* (? *Give me it* — see the explanation below.)
- *I sent **them to her** yesterday.*
- *Can you pass **it to Tom**?*

The double-object pattern with *it* or *them* last sounds unnatural to many speakers, especially in American English.
:::

::: proof
A pronoun such as *it* is old, known information and is unstressed, so end focus puts it early, not at the end (✗ *I gave the teacher it*). Swan (2016) notes that in informal British English two pronouns can also appear without *to* — *Give me it* and even *Give it me* are heard — but these are regional and informal; *Give it to me* is correct everywhere and is the form to use in writing.
:::

::: warning *It* at the end
✗ *Your bag? I gave Wang Fang it.* ✓ *I gave it to Wang Fang.*

✗ *The photos? Can you send me them?* ✓ *Can you send them to me?*

When the thing is *it* or *them*, put it right after the verb and the person after *to* or *for*.
:::

::: example Which order? {#ex-order}
Complete the replies with the words in brackets in the best order, adding *to* or *for* if necessary. (a) *What did you buy your mother for her birthday? — I bought ___ . (her / a silk scarf)* (b) *Who did you sell your old car to? — I sold ___ . (it / a student from my office)* (c) *Did you show Kenji the photos? — Yes, I showed ___ . (him / them)* (d) *What are you going to cook tonight? — I'm going to cook ___ . (dumplings / the whole family)*
::: solution
(a) *I bought **her a silk scarf**.* — the scarf is the new information, so it goes last; *her* is a known pronoun.

(b) *I sold **it to a student from my office**.* — *it* is a pronoun and known; the buyer is new and long, so it goes last with *to*.

(c) *I showed **them to him**.* — two pronouns: the thing first, then *to* + person.

(d) *I'm going to cook **dumplings for the whole family**.* — both are new, but the person is longer, so it goes last; *cook* takes *for*. (*I'm going to cook the whole family dumplings* is grammatical but sounds heavy.)
:::
:::

## Verbs that allow only one pattern

### Only the prepositional pattern

::: theorem Verbs that do not take two objects {#rule-to-only}
Many common verbs that seem to mean ‘give’ or ‘tell’ do **not** take the double-object pattern. The person must follow *to* (or *for*):

| verb | ✓ | ✗ |
|---|---|---|
| *explain* | *Can you explain the rule **to me**?* | ✗ *explain me the rule* |
| *describe* | *Describe the man **to the police**.* | ✗ *describe the police the man* |
| *say* | *What did she say **to you**?* | ✗ *What did she say you?* |
| *suggest* | *Can you suggest a hotel **to us**?* | ✗ *suggest us a hotel* |
| *introduce* | *Let me introduce my wife **to you**.* | ✗ *introduce you my wife* |
| *mention* | *I mentioned the problem **to my boss**.* | ✗ *mentioned my boss the problem* |
| *report* | *Report the accident **to the police**.* | ✗ *report the police the accident* |
| *return* | *Please return the form **to the office**.* | ✗ *return the office the form* |
| *donate* | *She donated her books **to the library**.* | ✗ *donated the library her books* |
| *deliver* | *We deliver food **to your door**.* | ✗ *deliver you food* |

Others: *announce*, *carry*, *confess*, *demonstrate*, *prove*, *push*, *repeat*, *whisper*.
:::

::: proof
Why can you *give* someone something but not *donate* them something, *tell* them something but not *explain* them something? Part of the answer is meaning: *push*, *carry* and *pull* describe how something moves, not a transfer of possession, and *say*, *whisper* and *shout* describe a manner of speaking. Another part is the history and form of the verbs. Studies of this pattern (Gropen et al. 1989; Levin 1993) noticed that the verbs which allow two objects are mostly short, native verbs stressed on the first syllable (*give*, *send*, *tell*, *show*, *offer*), while the longer verbs borrowed from Latin or French, stressed later (*explain*, *describe*, *donate*, *suggest*, *announce*, *deliver*), usually do not. This is a tendency, not a law: *offer*, *promise* and *assign* are Latin or French in origin and allow both patterns. The learner's dictionaries show the patterns of each verb. *Recommend* is a special case: *recommend a hotel to someone* is the safe form everywhere; *recommend someone a hotel* is used, especially in informal British English, and some learner's dictionaries record it.
:::

::: warning *Explain me*, *say me*, *suggest me*
✗ *Could you explain me how this machine works?* ✓ *Could you explain **to me** how this machine works?*

✗ *He said me that he was tired.* ✓ *He **told me** that he was tired.* ✓ *He said **(to me)** that he was tired.*

✗ *Can you suggest me a good restaurant?* ✓ *Can you suggest a good restaurant?* ✓ *Can you **recommend** a good restaurant?*

Notice the first correct sentence: when the thing explained is a long clause (*how this machine works*), *to me* comes before it, by end weight.
:::

*Say* and *tell* are a pair that deserves its own rule.

::: theorem *Say* and *tell* {#rule-say-tell}
- *Tell* normally has the person as its object: *tell someone something*, *tell someone that …*: *She **told me** the news.* *She **told me** that she was leaving.*
- *Say* does not take the person as an object. Either leave the person out or use *to*: *She **said** that she was leaving.* *What did he **say to** you?*
- *Tell* without a person is used in fixed phrases: *tell a story*, *tell a joke*, *tell the truth*, *tell a lie*, *tell the time*, *tell the difference*.
:::

::: example *Say*, *tell* or *explain*? {#ex-say-tell}
Choose the right verb and pattern for each idea. (a) Your friend gave you some news; report it. (b) Ask a classmate to make a grammar rule clear to you. (c) Ask a stranger for the time. (d) Report what a sign at the station says.
::: solution
1. Is the person the object? Only *tell* (and *show*, *give* …) allows that.
2. Is there a thing to make clear? Then *explain* + thing + *to* + person.

(a) *She **told me** some good news.* or *She **said** she had some good news.* — not ✗ *She said me*.

(b) *Could you **explain** this rule **to me**?* — *explain* needs *to* before the person.

(c) *Could you **tell me** the time, please?* — *tell* + person + thing. (*Could you say me the time* is a common error.)

(d) *The sign **says** that the last train leaves at 11.30.* — *say* with no person: the sign gives information to everyone.
:::
:::

### Only the double-object pattern

A few verbs work the other way round: they take two objects but have no natural *to* or *for* version.

::: theorem Verbs with two objects and no preposition {#rule-double-only}
*Cost*, *charge*, *fine*, *envy*, *forgive*, *bet*, *wish*, *take* (time) and *ask* (a question) are normally used in the double-object pattern:

- *The repair **cost me** 300 yuan.* (✗ *cost 300 yuan to me*)
- *They **charged us** too much.*
- *I **wish you** a happy new year.*
- *It **took me** two hours to get here.*
- *Can I **ask you** a question?* (✗ *ask a question to you*; formal English also has *ask a question **of** someone*.)
- *I **bet you** ten yuan he's late.*
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *She explained us the new timetable.*
- [ ] *The tickets cost 500 yuan to us.*
- [x] *He described the house to his friends.*
- [ ] *Can you say me your address?*
::: solution
*Describe* takes only the prepositional pattern: *described the house **to** his friends*. *Explain* needs *to* (*explained the new timetable to us*); *cost* takes two objects without *to* (*The tickets cost us 500 yuan*); and *say* cannot take the person as an object (*Can you **tell** me your address?*).
:::
:::

## The passive with two objects

A clause with two objects has two possible passives, because either object can become the subject.

- *They gave **Li Na** a prize.* → ***Li Na** was given a prize.*
- *They gave **a prize** to Li Na.* → ***A prize** was given to Li Na.*

::: theorem Passives of two-object verbs {#rule-two-object-passive}
With *give*, *send*, *show*, *offer*, *tell*, *teach*, *promise* and similar verbs, English very often makes the **person** the subject of the passive: *I **was offered** a job.* *We **were told** to wait.* *She **was sent** a letter.* The passive with the thing as subject is also correct, and the person then follows *to*: *A letter **was sent to** her.*

Verbs that take *for* (*buy*, *make*, *cook*) are rarely made passive with the person as subject: *A cake was made for her*, but ? *She was made a cake* sounds odd to most speakers.
:::

Chinese learners rarely use the first kind (*I was offered a job*), because Chinese would say 他们给了我一份工作 or 我得到了一份工作. It is very common in English, especially with *tell*, *ask*, *give*, *offer* and *show*. The passive in general is the subject of [[verb-patterns/the-passive]].

::: example Turning sentences into the passive {#ex-passive}
Rewrite each sentence in the passive, making the person the subject. (a) *The company offered Amina a better salary.* (b) *Somebody told us the wrong time.* (c) *The school will send all parents a letter.* (d) *They have given my grandfather a medal.*
::: solution
In each sentence, the indirect object (the person) becomes the subject; the direct object stays after the verb; *be* + past participle keeps the tense of the active verb.

(a) *Amina was offered a better salary.* (past simple → *was offered*)

(b) *We were told the wrong time.* (*us* → *we* as subject)

(c) *All parents will be sent a letter.* (*will send* → *will be sent*)

(d) *My grandfather has been given a medal.* (*have given* → *has been given*; the verb agrees with the new subject.)

The agent (*the company*, *somebody*, *the school*, *they*) is left out because it is obvious or unimportant.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **给 in three places.** Chinese can put 给 in several positions: 送他一本书, 送给他一本书, 送一本书给他, 给他送一本书. 朱德熙 (1982) discusses these patterns. English has fewer choices — *give him a book* or *give a book to him* — and the position of *to* is fixed after the thing.
- **给 can be *to* or *for*.** 给他写信 is *write **to** him* (he receives the letter), but 给他买书 is *buy books **for** him*, and 给他看病 is *see him* or *treat him* (as a doctor) — no preposition at all. Choose the English preposition from the English verb, not from 给.
- **给我解释一下.** Because 给我 appears before almost any verb in Chinese, learners produce ✗ *explain me*, ✗ *describe me*, ✗ *introduce me*. English says *explain it to me*, *describe it to me*, *introduce him to me*.
- **Taking away.** Chinese also has double-object verbs of taking: 偷了我一个钱包, 抢了他一百块钱 (朱德熙 1982 separates double-object verbs of giving from those of taking). English double objects are almost always about giving or doing something for someone: *steal me a wallet* would mean ‘steal a wallet **for** me’. English uses *from*: *Someone stole a wallet **from** me* or, more naturally, *Someone stole my wallet.*
- **借: *lend* or *borrow*.** 借 can mean both ‘lend’ and ‘borrow’; 借他一本书 is ambiguous. English separates them: *lend* (give for a time) takes two objects, *borrow* (take for a time) does not: *Can you **lend** me your pen?* — *Can I **borrow** your pen?* (✗ *Can you borrow me your pen?*)
:::

::: history
The indirect object is the descendant of a grammatical case. In Old English, the person who received something was marked by **dative** endings, and the thing by **accusative** endings, so the order of the objects was free. The modern pronouns *him* and *them* go back to old dative forms. As case endings were lost in the Middle English period, word order became fixed — person first, then thing — and the prepositional pattern with *to* became much more common, giving English its two patterns. Grammars of English still sometimes call the second pattern the "*to*-dative". The verb groups in this lesson are set out in A. S. Hornby's *A Guide to Patterns and Usage in English* (1954) and, with corpus evidence, in Francis, Hunston and Manning's *Collins COBUILD Grammar Patterns 1: Verbs* (1996).
:::

## Where this leads

The double-object pattern gives two passives, explained in [[verb-patterns/the-passive]]. Verbs whose second "object" is a verb or a clause — *tell someone to do*, *ask someone whether* — are in [[verb-patterns/object-and-verb]], and verbs with a fixed preposition (*provide someone with*, *remind someone of*) are in [[verb-patterns/prepositional-verbs]]. Reported speech with *say* and *tell* is treated fully in [[complex-sentences/reported-speech]].

::: summary
- Verbs such as *give* and *buy* can take two objects: the person (indirect object) and the thing (direct object) ([[#def-two-objects]]).
- They have two patterns: *give **her** a book* and *give a book **to her***; *buy **her** a book* and *buy a book **for her***.
- *To* is for receiving (*give*, *send*, *show*, *lend*, *tell*); *for* is for doing something on someone's behalf (*buy*, *make*, *cook*, *get*, *book*) ([[#rule-to-for]]).
- Put the new or longer information last; when the thing is a pronoun, say *give **it to** me* ([[#rule-end-focus]], [[#rule-pronouns]]).
- *Explain*, *describe*, *say*, *suggest*, *introduce*, *mention*, *report*, *return*, *donate* and *deliver* take only the pattern with *to* ([[#rule-to-only]]); *tell* takes the person as object, *say* does not.
- *Cost*, *charge*, *ask*, *wish* and *take* (time) take two objects with no preposition ([[#rule-double-only]]).
- In the passive, the person is often the subject: *I was offered a job* ([[#rule-two-object-passive]]).
:::
