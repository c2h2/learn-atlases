Compare four sentences:

- *Mei looked at **herself** in the mirror.* — Mei looked at Mei.
- *Mei looked at **her** in the mirror.* — Mei looked at another woman.
- *Mei and Tom looked at **each other**.* — Mei looked at Tom, and Tom looked at Mei.
- *Mei and Tom looked at **themselves** in the mirror.* — each of them looked at his or her own reflection.

English marks these differences with different pronouns: the **reflexive** *herself*, the personal pronoun *her*, the **reciprocal** *each other*. Chinese learners meet three problems. First, Chinese 自己 does not follow the same rules as *myself* and *herself*. Second, many verbs that take a reflexive in other languages, or seem to need one in Chinese (洗澡, 放松自己), do not take one in English: *I relaxed* (not ✗ *I relaxed myself*). Third, 互相 is an adverb before the verb, while *each other* is an object after it.

This lesson explains when English uses a reflexive pronoun, when it uses a personal pronoun, which verbs normally need neither, and how *each other* and *one another* work. The forms *myself*, *yourself*, *themselves* and so on were listed in [[core-grammar/nouns-pronouns]].

## Reflexive pronouns

::: definition Reflexive pronoun {#def-reflexive}
A **reflexive pronoun** ends in *-self* (singular) or *-selves* (plural): *myself*, *yourself*, *himself*, *herself*, *itself*, *oneself*, *ourselves*, *yourselves*, *themselves*. It is used as an object or complement that refers to the same person or thing as the subject of its clause: *I cut **myself**.* *She is proud of **herself**.* *The machine switches **itself** off.*

There is no ✗ *hisself* or ✗ *theirselves* in standard English, though both are heard in some dialects. *Yourself* is for one person and *yourselves* for more than one. *Themself* is used by some writers for one person of unspecified or non-binary gender (*Someone has hurt themself*); it is increasingly accepted, but many style guides still prefer *themselves*.
:::

::: theorem Reflexive or personal pronoun? {#rule-same-clause}
Use a reflexive pronoun when the object refers to the **subject of the same clause**. Use a personal pronoun (*me*, *him*, *her*, *them*) when it refers to someone else, or to the subject of a different clause.

| sentence | who is meant |
|---|---|
| *Tom blamed **himself**.* | Tom blamed Tom. |
| *Tom blamed **him**.* | Tom blamed another man. |
| *Tom asked me to help **him**.* | *him* = Tom, but it is in the clause *(me) to help him*, whose subject is *me*. |
| *Tom asked me to help **myself**.* | I should help myself (for example, to food). |
| *Mei says that Ana trusts **her**.* | *her* = Mei (or another woman). |
| *Mei says that Ana trusts **herself**.* | *herself* = Ana only. |

The reflexive looks for its partner inside its own clause.
:::

::: proof
This "same clause" rule is how all the reference grammars describe the basic use (Quirk et al. 1985; Huddleston & Pullum 2002; Swan 2016). Linguists have studied it closely because languages differ: the English reflexive must find its antecedent nearby, while in Chinese and many other languages the reflexive can reach further (see the note below). There are a few places where English allows either form — mainly after prepositions of place, explained below — and some uses where the reflexive does not refer to an object at all but adds emphasis.
:::

::: warning Reflexive or personal pronoun: common mistakes
✗ *I hurt me when I fell.* ✓ *I hurt **myself** when I fell.*

✗ *We really enjoyed at the party.* ✓ *We really enjoyed **ourselves** at the party.* (*enjoy* needs an object)

✗ *My parents asked me to look after themselves.* ✓ *My parents asked me to look after **them**.* (the subject of *to look after* is *me*)

✗ *He said his brother would visit himself.* ✓ *He said his brother would visit **him**.*
:::

::: example Choosing the pronoun {#ex-pronoun}
Choose the reflexive or the personal pronoun. (a) *Be careful with that knife — you'll cut (you / yourself).* (b) *Li Wei wanted his sister to drive (him / himself) to the airport.* (c) *The children made the cake (them / themselves).* (d) *Ana told Mei that she was proud of (her / herself).* (e) *We introduced (us / ourselves) to the new neighbours.*
::: solution
1. Find the clause the pronoun is in, and its subject.
2. Same person as that subject → reflexive; anyone else → personal pronoun.

(a) *yourself* — the subject is *you*.

(b) *him* — the clause is *(his sister) to drive him*; *him* = Li Wei, who is not that subject.

(c) *themselves* — emphatic use: ‘without help’ (see below).

(d) Either, with different meanings: *proud of herself* = Ana was proud of Ana (the subject of *was* is *she* = Ana); *proud of her* = Ana was proud of Mei. Most readers take *she* to mean Ana here.

(e) *ourselves*.
:::
:::

### After prepositions

After a preposition that belongs to a verb or adjective, the normal rule applies: *She talks to **herself**.* *He's angry with **himself**.* *Take care of **yourself**.*

After a preposition of **place**, English usually uses the personal pronoun, because there is no doubt who is meant:

- *She closed the door behind **her**.* (✗ *behind herself*)
- *He put the bag next to **him**.*
- *They had their whole lives in front of **them**.*

The reflexive is possible when it adds contrast or is needed for clarity: *She looked around **her*** and *She looked around **herself*** are both used. Swan (2016) and Huddleston & Pullum (2002) both describe this area as one where usage varies.

## Emphatic and idiomatic uses

::: theorem The emphatic reflexive and *by myself* {#rule-emphatic}
1. **Emphasis.** A reflexive placed after a noun or at the end of the clause stresses ‘this person and nobody else’: *The president **himself** opened the conference.* *I made this table **myself**.* (‘without help’) Here the reflexive is not an object, and it can be left out without making the sentence wrong.
2. ***By* + reflexive** = ‘alone’ or ‘without help’: *She lives **by herself**.* *Can you do it **by yourself**?* *On my own* means the same: *She lives on her own.*
3. **Fixed expressions** with a reflexive object:
   - *enjoy yourself* ‘have a good time’: *Did you enjoy yourselves?* (✗ *Did you enjoy?*)
   - *help yourself (to sth)* ‘take what you want’: *Help yourself to coffee.*
   - *behave yourself* ‘behave well’ (to children): *Behave yourself!*
   - *make yourself at home*
   - *introduce yourself*, *express yourself*, *teach yourself (to do sth)*, *hurt yourself*, *cut yourself*, *blame yourself*.
:::

::: proof
*Enjoy* needs an object ([[verb-patterns/transitive-intransitive#rule-object-needed]]), so a learner who wants to say 玩得开心 must say *enjoy myself* or *have a good time* — or name the thing enjoyed (*enjoy the party*). The emphatic use is described by Quirk et al. (1985) and Swan (2016); in speech the emphatic reflexive is usually stressed (*I made it mySELF*), while an object reflexive after a stressed verb often is not (*I CUT myself*). In the emphatic use the reflexive can stand next to the noun it emphasises (*The president himself*) or move to the end (*The president opened it himself*).
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence uses *herself* for emphasis rather than as an object?
- [ ] *She taught herself to play the guitar.*
- [ ] *She looked at herself in the mirror.*
- [x] *She painted the whole flat herself.*
- [ ] *She introduced herself to the class.*
::: solution
In *She painted the whole flat herself*, the object of *painted* is *the whole flat*; *herself* adds ‘without help’ and could be left out. In the other three, *herself* is the object of the verb.
:::
:::

## Verbs that normally need no reflexive

::: theorem Verbs used without a reflexive {#rule-no-reflexive}
Many verbs that take a reflexive in French, German or Spanish, or seem to need 自己 in Chinese, are used **without** one in English:

| verbs | example |
|---|---|
| *relax*, *concentrate*, *rest*, *calm down* | *Sit down and relax.* (✗ *relax yourself*) *I can't concentrate.* |
| *feel* + adjective | *I feel tired.* (✗ *I feel myself tired*) |
| *wash*, *dress*, *shave*, *shower* | *He washed, dressed and went out.* |
| *meet*, *get up*, *wake up*, *sit down*, *stand up*, *hurry* | *We met at university.* *Hurry up!* |
| *remember*, *complain*, *worry*, *change* (clothes) | *I remember her.* (✗ *I remember myself of her*) *I need to change.* |

The reflexive is possible with *wash*, *dress* and *shave* when the action is unusual or needs effort: *My grandfather is ninety but he still dresses **himself**.* *The cat was washing **itself**.*
:::

::: proof
Swan (2016) gives this list of verbs as a typical learners' problem, and the pattern is the one described in [[verb-patterns/transitive-intransitive#rule-object-omission]]: when the object is obviously the subject's own body, English leaves it out. With *relax* and *concentrate* the reflexive is not ungrammatical in every context (*Relax yourself* appears in some instructions), but it is unusual, and the plain verb is the normal choice. *Feel* is a linking verb ([[verb-patterns/transitive-intransitive#def-linking]]) and takes an adjective directly.
:::

::: warning Adding a reflexive that English does not use
✗ *I want to relax myself this weekend.* ✓ *I want to relax this weekend.*

✗ *We should concentrate ourselves on the exam.* ✓ *We should concentrate on the exam.*

✗ *I feel myself much better today.* ✓ *I feel much better today.*

✗ *When I woke myself up, it was raining.* ✓ *When I woke up, it was raining.*
:::

::: example Reflexive or nothing? {#ex-verbs}
Add a reflexive pronoun where it is needed, and cross out the ones that are not. (a) *Did you enjoy ___ at the party?* (b) *I usually shave ___ in the morning.* (c) *Please make ___ at home.* (d) *I can't concentrate ___ with this noise.* (e) *The little boy dressed ___ for the first time today.* (f) *Don't worry ___ — I'll be fine.*
::: solution
(a) *yourself* (one person) or *yourselves* — *enjoy* needs an object.

(b) Nothing — *I usually shave in the morning.*

(c) *yourself* — a fixed expression.

(d) Nothing — *I can't concentrate with this noise.*

(e) *himself* — it took effort; this is the point of the sentence.

(f) Nothing — *Don't worry.* (*Don't worry yourself* is heard in informal British English, but it is not the normal form.)
:::
:::

## *Each other* and *one another*

::: definition Reciprocal pronoun {#def-reciprocal}
*Each other* and *one another* are **reciprocal pronouns**. They show that two or more people do the same thing to the other(s): *Mei and Tom helped **each other*** means Mei helped Tom and Tom helped Mei. They work like one object pronoun and come in the object position, after the verb or preposition: *They often write to **each other**.*

Both have a possessive: *each other's*, *one another's*: *We borrowed **each other's** notes.* (✗ *each others'*)
:::

::: theorem *Each other*, *one another* and *themselves* {#rule-reciprocal}
1. *Each other* and *one another* mean the same. A traditional rule says that *each other* is for two people and *one another* for more than two, but educated writers and speakers do not follow it (Swan 2016; Huddleston & Pullum 2002). *Each other* is much more common; *one another* is slightly more formal.
2. A reflexive and a reciprocal have different meanings with a plural subject:
   - *They blamed **themselves*** — each blamed himself or herself (or the group blamed the group);
   - *They blamed **each other*** — each blamed the other.
3. *Each other* cannot be the subject of a clause: ✗ *We know that each other is honest.* ✓ *We each know that the other is honest.* / *We trust each other.*
:::

::: example *Themselves* or *each other*? {#ex-each-other}
Complete with *themselves* or *each other*, and say what each choice means. (a) *After the argument, the two brothers didn't speak to ___ for a week.* (b) *The students introduced ___ to the new teacher.* (c) *The students introduced ___ — they had never met before.* (d) *My parents have known ___ since primary school.* (e) *The players were angry with ___ after the defeat.*
::: solution
(a) *each other* — the brothers did not speak to the other brother. (*themselves* would mean that neither talked to himself.)

(b) *themselves* — each student told the teacher his or her own name.

(c) *each other* — each told the others their name.

(d) *each other* — Mum has known Dad and Dad has known Mum.

(e) Both are possible: *with themselves* = each was angry at his own play; *with each other* = they blamed one another.
:::
:::

### Reciprocal verbs

Some verbs already have a reciprocal meaning when their subject is plural, so *each other* is usually left out:

- *meet*, *marry*, *kiss*, *hug*, *fight*, *argue*, *agree*, *disagree*, *divorce*, *shake hands*: *We met in 2015 and married two years later.* *They hugged and said goodbye.* *Do you two always argue?*

*Each other* can be added for emphasis with verbs of physical contact (*They hugged each other*) but it sounds odd or unnecessary with *meet* and *marry*: *They met each other in 2015* is possible, but *They met in 2015* is usual, and *They got married* is more common than *They married each other*. With a singular subject, the other person becomes an object or follows *with*: *I met Ana.* *She married a doctor.* *He argued **with** his boss.* American English also uses *meet with* for a planned meeting (*The president met with union leaders*); British English uses it less ([[verb-patterns/prepositional-verbs]]).

::: example From Chinese 自己 and 互相 {#ex-chinese}
Translate into English, paying attention to 自己 and 互相. (a) 你要好好照顾自己。(b) 他们互相帮助。(c) 我自己做的。(d) 张老师说李明不相信自己。(e) 我们周末在家放松一下。
::: solution
(a) *You must take good care of **yourself**.* — 自己 as object of the same clause → reflexive.

(b) *They help **each other**.* — 互相 is an adverb before the verb; *each other* is the object after it. (✗ *They each other help.*)

(c) *I made it **myself**.* — emphatic: ‘without help’.

(d) This depends on who 自己 refers to. If it means Li Ming: *Mr Zhang says that Li Ming doesn't believe in **himself**.* If it means Mr Zhang: *Mr Zhang says that Li Ming doesn't believe **him**.* English must choose; the reflexive can only mean Li Ming.

(e) *We're going to relax at home this weekend.* — no reflexive with *relax*.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **自己 can reach further than *himself*.** In 张三认为李四不喜欢自己, 自己 can refer to 李四 (the nearest subject) or to 张三 (the subject of the main clause). This "long-distance" use of 自己 is well known in descriptions of Mandarin, and 自己 also prefers to refer to a subject. The English reflexive is strictly local: in *Zhang San thinks Li Si doesn't like himself*, *himself* can only be Li Si. To refer to Zhang San, English uses *him*: *Zhang San thinks Li Si doesn't like **him***.
- **自己 has more uses.** 自己 can stand before a verb as an adverb (我自己去 ‘I'll go myself / by myself’), combine with nouns (自己的房间 ‘my own room’) and stand alone as a subject (自己的事自己做). English uses *-self* forms for some of these, *own* for others (*my own room*, *on my own*), and a personal pronoun for the rest.
- **互相 and *each other*.** 互相 is an adverb that stands before the verb (我们互相学习); *each other* is a pronoun that takes the object position (*We learn from each other*). Learners sometimes write ✗ *We each other learn* or ✗ *We mutually help*; *mutually* exists (*mutually beneficial*) but is not used like 互相 with ordinary verbs.
- **Verbs with 自己 in Chinese but nothing in English.** 放松自己, 介绍一下自己 and 照顾好自己 have English equivalents with different patterns: *relax* (no reflexive), *introduce yourself* (reflexive), *take care of yourself* (reflexive after a prepositional verb). Learn the English verb, not a translation of 自己.
:::

::: warning *Myself* for *me*
In formal speech and business English, *myself* is often used where *me* or *I* is expected: *Please contact John or **myself**.* *My wife and **myself** were delighted.* Huddleston & Pullum (2002) note that reflexives are found in coordinations like these, but many usage guides criticise them, and examiners treat them as mistakes. Use the ordinary pronoun: *Please contact John or **me**.* *My wife and **I** were delighted.*
:::

::: history
Old English had no special reflexive pronouns. The ordinary object pronoun was used, so *he washed him* could mean that he washed himself; *self* was an emphatic word that could be added, rather as *own* can be added today. During the Middle English period, compounds of a pronoun with *self* became the regular way of marking a reflexive object, and the plain pronoun in that use became old-fashioned. Traces of the older system survive: Shakespeare and the King James Bible have forms like *Sit thee down* and *Get thee hence*, and some informal American English still says *I'm going to get me a coffee*. *Each other* also began as two separate words, with *each* as subject and *other* as object (*each loved the other*); the two words gradually came together into the single object pronoun we use today.
:::

## Where this leads

Transitive verbs and verbs whose object can be left out were covered in [[verb-patterns/transitive-intransitive]]; *teach yourself to* and *allow yourself to* follow the verb + object + *to* pattern in [[verb-patterns/object-and-verb]]. Pronouns that refer back across sentences, and how to follow them when reading, are covered in [[reading-skills/following-the-thread]].

::: summary
- Reflexive pronouns (*myself*, *themselves*) are used when the object is the same person as the subject of its own clause: *Tom blamed himself* ([[#def-reflexive]], [[#rule-same-clause]]).
- If the pronoun refers to someone in another clause, use *him*, *her*, *them*: *Tom asked me to help him*. After prepositions of place, use the personal pronoun: *She closed the door behind her*.
- Reflexives also add emphasis (*I made it myself*), and *by myself* means ‘alone’; *enjoy yourself*, *help yourself* and *behave yourself* are fixed ([[#rule-emphatic]]).
- *Relax*, *concentrate*, *feel*, *meet*, *wake up* and usually *wash*, *dress* and *shave* need no reflexive ([[#rule-no-reflexive]]).
- *Each other* and *one another* show that people do something to the other(s); *themselves* points back to the same people ([[#def-reciprocal]], [[#rule-reciprocal]]).
- *Meet*, *marry*, *kiss*, *hug*, *argue* and *agree* are reciprocal on their own: *We met in 2015*.
- Chinese 自己 can refer to a more distant subject, and 互相 is an adverb before the verb; English *himself* is local, and *each other* is an object.
:::
