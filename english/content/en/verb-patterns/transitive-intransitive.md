Here are three sentences that Chinese learners often write:

- ✗ *The accident was happened at night.*
- ✗ *We discussed about the problem.*
- ✗ *I really enjoyed.*

Each one breaks the pattern of its verb. *Happen* never takes an object, so it has no passive: ✓ *The accident happened at night.* *Discuss* takes its object directly, with no preposition: ✓ *We discussed the problem.* And *enjoy* must have an object: ✓ *I really enjoyed it.* The meaning of all three sentences is clear, and a Chinese speaker would say 事故发生在夜里, 我们讨论了这个问题 and 我玩得很开心 without thinking about objects at all. But in English, the first thing to know about a verb is what it needs after it.

This lesson is the start of the course. It sorts verbs into four groups: verbs that need an object, verbs that cannot take one, verbs that can do both, and linking verbs such as *be*, *seem* and *become*, which are followed by a description of the subject. It builds on the seven clause patterns of [[core-grammar/sentence-patterns]].

## Objects and verbs

Remember from [[core-grammar/sentence-patterns]] that an **object** is the person or thing that comes after the verb and that the action is done to: *She plays **the piano**.* Verbs are grouped by whether they take an object.

::: definition Transitive and intransitive verbs {#def-transitive}
A **transitive verb** is a verb that takes an object: *She **opened** the window.* *I **like** jazz.* An **intransitive verb** is a verb that takes no object: *The baby **slept**.* *Prices **rose**.* Many verbs can be used in both ways; dictionaries mark each use, for example [T] and [I] in the Cambridge and Longman dictionaries, or *VN* and *V* in Oxford.
:::

The words come from Latin *transire* ‘to go across’: with a transitive verb, the action "goes across" from the subject to the object.

Some typical verbs of each kind:

| group | verbs | examples |
|---|---|---|
| need an object | *bring*, *describe*, *discuss*, *enjoy*, *find*, *like*, *make*, *mention*, *need*, *put*, *reach*, *use* | *Please **bring** a pen.* *We **reached** the top.* |
| take no object | *appear*, *arrive*, *come*, *die*, *disappear*, *exist*, *fall*, *go*, *happen*, *lie*, *occur*, *rise*, *sleep* | *The bus **arrived**.* *Something strange **happened**.* |
| both | *break*, *change*, *close*, *drive*, *eat*, *grow*, *help*, *leave*, *open*, *read*, *start*, *stop*, *win*, *write* | *She **writes** novels.* / *She **writes** every morning.* |

::: theorem Verbs that need an object {#rule-object-needed}
A transitive verb that needs an object must have one in every sentence, even when the object is clear from the context. If there is no noun, use a pronoun (*it*, *them*, *one*):

- *Did you like the film?* — *Yes, I really **enjoyed it**.* (✗ *I really enjoyed.*)
- *Where's my bag?* — *I **put it** on your desk.* (✗ *I put on your desk.*)
- *Do you need a pen?* — *Yes, I **need one**.* (✗ *Yes, I need.*)
:::

::: proof
Chinese often leaves out an object that the listener already knows: 你喜欢那部电影吗？——很喜欢. English can drop the object only with verbs whose object is general or obvious (*eat*, *drink*, *read*, *write*, *cook* — see below), not with verbs such as *enjoy*, *like*, *find*, *need*, *put*, *bring* or *use*. These verbs are listed as transitive in all the learner's dictionaries (Oxford, Cambridge, Longman). A few have a fixed exception in informal speech: a waiter may say *Enjoy!* (‘enjoy your meal’), which began in American English and is now heard in Britain too, and *I like!* is a joke, not normal English.
:::

::: warning An object that is left out
✗ *This film is great. I'm sure you'll love.* ✓ *I'm sure you'll love **it**.*

✗ *I've lost my phone. Have you seen?* ✓ *Have you seen **it**?*

✗ *These shoes are cheap, so I bought.* ✓ *These shoes were cheap, so I bought **them**.*

When an English verb needs an object, an object must be there, even a short pronoun.
:::

::: theorem Verbs that take no object {#rule-no-object}
An intransitive verb cannot have an object, and it therefore has **no passive**. If something needs to follow it, it is an adverbial (a time, a place, a manner): *The train arrived **at six**.* To add a person or thing, you need a preposition: *Something happened **to** my laptop.* *She arrived **at** the station.*
:::

::: proof
The passive (*The window was opened*) turns an object into a subject, so a verb with no object has nothing to make a passive from ([[verb-patterns/the-passive]]). Verbs of happening, existing, appearing and moving — *happen*, *occur*, *exist*, *appear*, *disappear*, *arrive*, *go*, *come*, *fall*, *die* — are intransitive in English. Their meanings describe something that happens to the subject, not something the subject does to another thing, so learners sometimes feel that a passive is needed: ✗ *The accident was happened.* ✗ *My cat was died.* English uses the active: *The accident happened.* *My cat died.* (*Dead* is the adjective: *My cat is dead.*)
:::

::: warning Passives of intransitive verbs
✗ *A big change was occurred in 2010.* ✓ *A big change occurred in 2010.*

✗ *The problem is still existed.* ✓ *The problem still exists.*

✗ *When I came back, my bike was disappeared.* ✓ *When I came back, my bike had disappeared.* (or *… my bike was gone.*)

✗ *He was died in 1998.* ✓ *He died in 1998.* ✓ *He has been dead for years.*
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *What was happened to your phone?*
- [x] *What happened to your phone?*
- [ ] *What happened your phone?*
- [ ] *What did happen your phone?*
::: solution
*Happen* is intransitive: it has no passive (so not *was happened*), and the thing affected follows the preposition *to*. In a question about the subject, English does not use *did*: *What happened?* (see [[core-grammar/questions-and-negatives]]).
:::
:::

## Verbs with two uses

Many verbs are transitive in one sentence and intransitive in another. There are two common kinds.

::: theorem Leaving out a general object {#rule-object-omission}
With some verbs the object can be left out when it is general, unknown or obvious. The meaning of the verb stays the same: the subject still does something to something.

- *We **ate** at eight.* (we ate a meal)
- *Do you **drink**?* (alcohol)
- *She **reads** a lot.* (books, articles)
- *He **writes** for a newspaper.*
- *I'll **cook** tonight.*
- *Who **won**?* (the game)

Common verbs of this kind are *cook*, *draw*, *drink*, *drive*, *eat*, *help*, *paint*, *read*, *sing*, *study*, *teach*, *wash*, *win* and *write*.
:::

The second kind is different. Here the object of the transitive use becomes the subject of the intransitive use:

- *She **opened** the door.* — *The door **opened**.*
- *The heat **melted** the ice.* — *The ice **melted**.*
- *Someone **broke** the window.* — *The window **broke**.*
- *The company **increased** prices.* — *Prices **increased**.*

::: theorem Change-of-state verbs with two patterns {#rule-ergative}
Many verbs that describe a change in something — *begin*, *boil*, *break*, *change*, *close*, *cool*, *dry*, *grow*, *improve*, *increase*, *melt*, *move*, *open*, *shut*, *sink*, *start*, *stop*, *turn* — can be used in two ways:

1. **transitive**: someone or something causes the change (*The cook **boiled** the water.*);
2. **intransitive**: the thing that changes is the subject, and no cause is mentioned (*The water **boiled**.*).

The intransitive use is active in form but describes what happens to the subject.
:::

::: proof
Grammars call these verbs **ergative** (Collins COBUILD) or say that they take part in the **causative alternation** (Levin 1993); Quirk et al. (1985) treat them as verbs with both an intransitive and a transitive use. They are very common in English. Not every change-of-state verb works both ways: you can say *The vase broke*, but *disappear*, *die*, *fall* and *happen* are only intransitive (✗ *The magician disappeared the coin*; ✓ *made the coin disappear*), and *destroy*, *kill* and *cut* are only transitive in this meaning (✗ *The building destroyed in the fire*; ✓ *was destroyed*). The intransitive use is chosen when the cause is unknown or unimportant, much like a passive; compare *The door opened* (it just happened) with *The door was opened* (someone opened it). The second sentence implies a person who did it.
:::

| transitive only | both | intransitive only |
|---|---|---|
| *destroy*, *kill*, *raise*, *lay*, *cut* | *break*, *open*, *close*, *change*, *increase*, *melt*, *grow*, *stop* | *happen*, *die*, *fall*, *rise*, *lie*, *disappear*, *arrive* |

Two pairs in the table are famous for confusing native speakers as well as learners: *raise* / *rise* and *lay* / *lie*.

::: warning *Raise* or *rise*, *lay* or *lie*?
*Raise* (*raised*, *raised*) is transitive: *They **raised** prices.* *Raise your hand.* *Rise* (*rose*, *risen*) is intransitive: *Prices **rose**.* *The sun **rises** in the east.*

*Lay* (*laid*, *laid*) is transitive: ***Lay** the baby on the bed.* *Lie* (*lay*, *lain*) is intransitive: ***Lie** down.* *She **lay** on the beach all afternoon.*

✗ *The government rose taxes.* ✓ *The government raised taxes.*

✗ *I'm going to lay down for a while.* ✓ *I'm going to lie down for a while.*

The trouble is that *lay* is also the past tense of *lie*. In informal speech many native speakers say *lay down* for *lie down*; it is common, but it is marked as non-standard in learner's dictionaries and usage guides, so avoid it in writing.
:::

::: example Which use is it? {#ex-which-use}
Say whether each verb is transitive (T) or intransitive (I), and whether the subject causes the change or undergoes it. (a) *The meeting started late.* (b) *The chairman started the meeting.* (c) *My English has improved.* (d) *The new teacher improved my English.* (e) *The ice cream melted.*
::: solution
1. Look for an object after the verb.
2. If there is one, the verb is transitive and the subject is the cause.
3. If there is none, ask whether the subject is the thing that changes.

(a) No object: **I**. *The meeting* is what started — it undergoes the change.

(b) Object *the meeting*: **T**. *The chairman* caused the start.

(c) No object: **I**. *My English* is what changed.

(d) Object *my English*: **T**. *The new teacher* caused the change.

(e) No object: **I**. The ice cream underwent the change; the cause (the heat) is not mentioned.

Notice that (a) and (b), and (c) and (d), use the same verb: these are change-of-state verbs with two patterns ([[#rule-ergative]]).
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is **not** correct English?
- [ ] *The shop closes at nine.*
- [ ] *The wind closed the door.*
- [x] *The old house destroyed in the storm.*
- [ ] *The window broke in the storm.*
::: solution
*Destroy* is only transitive, so without a person or thing that destroys, it must be passive: *The old house **was destroyed** in the storm.* *Close* and *break* can be used intransitively: *The shop closes*, *The window broke*.
:::
:::

## Verb + object or verb + preposition?

Some English verbs take an object directly where Chinese, or the learner's feeling, suggests a preposition. Others need a preposition where Chinese has none. Only the dictionary can tell you which, but the commonest cases are worth learning now.

| take the object directly (no preposition) | need a preposition before the object |
|---|---|
| *discuss* a problem (✗ *discuss about*) | *listen **to*** music (✗ *listen music*) |
| *enter* a room (✗ *enter into the room*) | *look **at*** a picture |
| *reach* a place (✗ *reach to*) | *wait **for*** a bus |
| *marry* someone (✗ *marry with*) | *arrive **at** / **in*** a place |
| *answer* a question / a phone | *apologise **to*** someone |
| *contact* someone (✗ *contact with*) | *agree **with*** someone |
| *attend* a meeting (✗ *attend to*, which means ‘deal with’) | *depend **on*** something |
| *approach* the door | *complain **about*** something |

::: proof
Why does *discuss* take no preposition, while *talk* needs *about*? There is no logical reason; it is a fact about each verb, often going back to its history (*discuss*, *enter*, *approach* and *attend* came into English from French or Latin with direct objects). The meaning can be the same: *We discussed the plan* = *We talked about the plan*. The errors in the left-hand column often come from a related noun or a Chinese preposition: *a discussion **about*** is correct, but *discuss* is not; 和……结婚 contains 和 (*with*), but *marry* takes its object directly (*She married a doctor*; *She got married **to** a doctor*). Verbs with prepositions are the topic of [[verb-patterns/prepositional-verbs]].
:::

::: example Correcting verbs and objects {#ex-correct-objects}
Correct the sentences. (a) *Let's discuss about it tomorrow.* (b) *I listen music every morning.* (c) *When we reached to the hotel, it was dark.* (d) *She married with a teacher from Hangzhou.* (e) *I waited the bus for half an hour.*
::: solution
(a) *Discuss* takes a direct object: *Let's discuss it tomorrow.* (or *Let's talk about it tomorrow.*)

(b) *Listen* needs *to* before its object: *I listen to music every morning.*

(c) *Reach* takes a direct object: *When we reached the hotel, it was dark.* (or *When we got to the hotel …*, *When we arrived at the hotel …*)

(d) *Marry* takes a direct object: *She married a teacher from Hangzhou.* (or *She got married to a teacher …*)

(e) *Wait* needs *for* before the thing you wait for: *I waited for the bus for half an hour.* The second *for* is part of the time adverbial.
:::
:::

## Linking verbs

Some verbs are followed not by an object but by a description of the subject.

- *Mei is **a nurse**.* (Mei = a nurse)
- *The soup smells **good**.* (the soup is good, by its smell)
- *He became **famous**.* (at the end, he was famous)

::: definition Linking verb {#def-linking}
A **linking verb** (or copula) joins the subject to a **subject complement**: an adjective or noun phrase that describes or identifies the subject. The commonest linking verbs are:

- *be*;
- verbs of **seeming**: *seem*, *appear*, *look*, *sound*, *smell*, *taste*, *feel*;
- verbs of **becoming**: *become*, *get*, *go*, *turn*, *grow*;
- verbs of **staying**: *remain*, *stay*, *keep*.
:::

::: theorem Linking verbs take adjectives, not adverbs {#rule-linking-adj}
After a linking verb, use an **adjective**, because the word describes the subject, not the action:

- *You look **tired**.* (✗ *You look tiredly.*)
- *This sounds **interesting**.*
- *The milk tastes **sour**.*
- *Stay **calm**.*

The same verb used as an ordinary verb takes an adverb, because then the word describes the action: *She looked **carefully** at the map.* *He tasted the soup **slowly**.*
:::

::: proof
A test: if you can replace the verb with *is* or *was* and the sentence still makes sense, it is a linking verb and the word after it is an adjective. *The soup smells good* → *The soup is good*: linking. *She looked carefully at the map* → ✗ *She was carefully at the map*: not linking. *Feel* shows both uses: *I feel **cold*** (I am cold) but *She felt the wall **carefully*** (she touched it). *Good* and *well* need care: *I feel good* and *I feel well* are both correct after the linking verb *feel*, because *well* is also an adjective meaning ‘healthy’ (*I'm well, thanks*). *It sounds well*, however, is not used for *It sounds good*. This is the description of Swan (2016) and the learner's dictionaries.
:::

::: warning Adverb after a linking verb
✗ *The flowers smell beautifully.* ✓ *The flowers smell beautiful.*

✗ *Your idea sounds greatly.* ✓ *Your idea sounds great.*

✗ *Don't worry, everything will stay safely.* ✓ *… everything will stay safe.*
:::

### *Seem*, *look* and *sound* with nouns

With an adjective, these verbs are simple: *She seems happy*, *It looks easy*. With a noun, there is a choice, and a difference between British and American English.

::: theorem Linking verbs before a noun phrase {#rule-linking-noun}
1. *Look like*, *sound like*, *seem like*, *smell like*, *taste like* and *feel like* + noun mean ‘resemble’ or ‘give the impression of’: *She **looks like** her mother.* *That **sounds like** a good plan.* *It **feels like** a holiday.*
2. *Seem to be* + noun or adjective is possible everywhere: *He **seems to be** a nice person.* *She **seems to be** tired.* (With an adjective, *She seems tired* is more usual.)
3. In **British English**, *seem*, *look* and *sound* can also be followed directly by a noun phrase, especially one with an adjective: *It **seems** a good idea.* *You **look** a different person.* American English usually prefers *like* or *to be*: *It **seems like** a good idea.*
:::

::: proof
This difference is described in Swan (2016, entries on *seem* and *look*) and in the Cambridge and Oxford learner's dictionaries, which mark *seem* + noun as chiefly British. *Like* in pattern 1 is a preposition and is followed by a noun or a pronoun (*She looks like me*). In informal English, *like* is also used before a whole clause: *It looks like it's going to rain.* This is very common in speech in both varieties; in formal writing many writers prefer *It looks as if it's going to rain.*
:::

### Verbs of becoming

English has several verbs that mean ‘become’, and they are not all used in the same way.

| verb | used with | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *become* | most adjectives and nouns; neutral to formal | *She became a doctor.* *It became clear that …* |
| *get* | many adjectives; the usual verb in conversation | *It's **getting** dark.* *I **got** wet.* *Don't **get** angry.* |
| *go* | changes for the worse, and colours | *The milk has **gone** bad.* *He **went** pale.* *She's **going** grey.* |
| *turn* | colours and sudden changes; also *turn* + age | *The leaves **turned** red.* *She **turned** thirty last week.* |
| *grow* | slow changes; rather literary | *The children **grew** tired.* *It **grew** dark.* |

::: theorem Choosing a verb of becoming {#rule-becoming}
- *Get* is the most frequent in conversation, before adjectives: *get tired*, *get angry*, *get better*, *get married*. It is not used before a noun in this meaning: ✗ *She got a doctor* (that means she fetched one). Use *become*: *She became a doctor.*
- *Become* is neutral and goes with nouns and adjectives; in conversation it sounds a little formal before common adjectives (*I became hungry* — *I got hungry* is more natural).
- *Go* and *turn* are used in fixed combinations, mostly for changes of colour or for the worse: *go bad*, *go wrong*, *go deaf*, *go mad* (BrE also ‘get angry’), *turn red*, *turn sour*.
:::

::: proof
Biber et al. (1999) found *get* by far the most common copular verb of becoming in conversation, and *become* the most common in academic writing. The combinations with *go* and *turn* are collocations: you can learn them as phrases (*go bald*, *go blind*, *go missing* BrE), and the learner's dictionaries list them under the adjective. Chinese 变 covers much of this ground (变冷了 *It's getting cold*; 他的脸变红了 *His face went red* / *turned red*), but 变 + adjective has no single English equivalent.
:::

::: example Choosing the complement {#ex-complement}
Complete each sentence with the word in brackets in the right form (adjective or adverb), and say why. (a) *This cake tastes ___. (wonderful)* (b) *She tasted the sauce ___. (careful)* (c) *You sound ___ today. Are you OK? (strange)* (d) *Please read the instructions ___. (quick)* (e) *The room went ___ when he stood up. (quiet)*
::: solution
(a) *wonderful* — *taste* is a linking verb here (the cake is wonderful).

(b) *carefully* — *taste* is an ordinary transitive verb (she did something to the sauce), and the word describes how she did it.

(c) *strange* — linking verb (you seem strange).

(d) *quickly* — *read* is an ordinary verb; the word describes the action.

(e) *quiet* — *go* is a verb of becoming here (the room became quiet).

The test each time: can you say *… is / was + the word*? If yes, use the adjective.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *The music sounds beautifully.*
- [ ] *She seems a lot happily now.*
- [x] *The air feels warm today.*
- [ ] *He got a famous singer.*
::: solution
*Feel* is a linking verb here (the air is warm), so the adjective *warm* is right. *Sounds beautifully* and *seems happily* need adjectives (*beautiful*, *happier*). *He got a famous singer* means he fetched one; for the change, use *He became a famous singer*.
:::

## Verbs in more than one group

A verb is not "a transitive verb" once and for all: the same verb often has several meanings with different patterns, and a good learner's dictionary lists them separately.

- *grow*: *Tomatoes **grow** well here* (I) — *My father **grows** tomatoes* (T) — *It **grew** cold* (linking)
- *run*: *She **runs** every morning* (I) — *She **runs** a small hotel* (T, ‘manages’)
- *feel*: *I **feel** tired* (linking) — *I **felt** a hand on my shoulder* (T)
- *turn*: *The car **turned** left* (I) — ***Turn** the key* (T) — *The weather **turned** cold* (linking)

::: example Reading a dictionary entry {#ex-dictionary}
A learner's dictionary entry for *leave* lists these uses, among others. Write one sentence for each, and say whether the verb is transitive or intransitive. (1) ‘go away from a person or place’, (2) ‘go away’, without saying from where, (3) ‘allow something to remain in a place, often by mistake’.
::: solution
(1) Transitive: *She **leaves** home at seven.* The place is the object — no *from* (✗ *She leaves from home*). (*Leave from* is used for the place where a journey starts: *The train leaves from platform 4.*)

(2) Intransitive: *It's late — I have to **leave**.*

(3) Transitive: *I **left** my umbrella on the train.* (In this meaning, *leave* also needs a place: *I left it **at home**.* Compare *forget*, which is used without a place: *I forgot my umbrella.* ✗ *I forgot my umbrella at home* is common among learners; prefer *I left my umbrella at home*.)

The point: one verb, several patterns, each tied to a meaning. Learn the pattern together with the meaning.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Objects.** Chinese can leave out an object that is clear from context (你见过他吗？——见过), and so can a Chinese learner's English: ✗ *Yes, I have seen.* English verbs that need an object keep it, as a pronoun if necessary: *Yes, I have seen **him**.*
- **Change-of-state verbs.** Mandarin often expresses a change with a resultative compound: 打破 ‘hit-broken’, 关上 ‘close-up’, 杯子打破了. Li & Thompson (1981) describe these resultative verb compounds. English uses one verb, which may be transitive or intransitive: *She broke the cup* / *The cup broke*. Chinese 杯子破了 corresponds to *The cup broke* (or *is broken*), not to a passive.
- **Happening and existing.** Verbs such as 发生, 出现, 存在, 消失 are intransitive in Chinese too, but learners sometimes add *be* to their English equivalents, perhaps because the meaning is "something happened to the subject": ✗ *was happened*. Learn the rule: *happen*, *occur*, *exist*, *appear*, *disappear*, *die* have no passive.
- **Prepositions.** Chinese 和她结婚, 讨论一下关于……的问题 and 到达 suggest ✗ *marry with*, ✗ *discuss about* and ✗ *reach to*. English *marry*, *discuss* and *reach* take a direct object.
- **Adjectives as predicates.** Chinese adjectives can be predicates with no verb (这汤很香), and 看起来, 听起来, 闻起来 come before them. English needs a linking verb, and that verb takes an adjective, not an adverb: *This soup smells good.*
:::

::: history
The terms *transitive* and *intransitive* come from the Latin grammatical tradition: the idea of an action that "passes across" (*transitio*) from one person to another is found in the Latin grammarian Priscian, who wrote around 500 CE, and English grammars took the terms over from Latin. In the twentieth century, teachers of English as a foreign language began to list the patterns of each verb for learners: A. S. Hornby's numbered "verb patterns", first in his *Idiomatic and Syntactic English Dictionary* (1942) and then in *A Guide to Patterns and Usage in English* (1954), made the patterns part of every learner's dictionary. Today, dictionaries mark the patterns in each entry: [T] and [I] in Cambridge and Longman, and codes such as *VN* in Oxford.
:::

- *Discuss*, *enter*, *reach*, *marry* and *contact* take a direct object; *listen to*, *look at* and *wait for* need a preposition.
- Linking verbs (*be*, *seem*, *look*, *sound*, *become*, *get*, *stay*) are followed by an adjective or noun that describes the subject, not by an adverb ([[#rule-linking-adj]]).
- *Seem* + noun is mainly British; *seem like* and *seem to be* are used everywhere ([[#rule-linking-noun]]). In conversation, *get* is the usual verb for ‘become’ before adjectives.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Transitive or intransitive? {level=1}
Is the verb in each sentence transitive (T) or intransitive (I)? (a) *The plane landed safely.* (b) *The pilot landed the plane safely.* (c) *We arrived late.* (d) *I've found my glasses.* (e) *Her health is improving.* (f) *The children are sleeping.*
::: solution
(a) I; (b) T (object *the plane*); (c) I; (d) T (object *my glasses*); (e) I; (f) I.
:::
:::

::: exercise Add the missing object {level=1}
Complete each reply with a pronoun object. (a) *Do you like my new jacket? — Yes, I love ___.* (b) *Where are the tickets? — I put ___ in my bag.* (c) *Did you enjoy the concert? — Yes, we really enjoyed ___.* (d) *Have you got an umbrella? — No, I need ___.* (e) *Is that your bike? — Yes, I bought ___ last week.*
::: solution
(a) *it*; (b) *them*; (c) *it*; (d) *one*; (e) *it*.
:::
:::

::: exercise Adjective or adverb? {level=1}
Choose the correct word. (a) *This bread smells (fresh / freshly).* (b) *He shut the door (quiet / quietly).* (c) *You look (beautiful / beautifully) in that dress.* (d) *She sang (beautiful / beautifully).* (e) *The exam seemed (easy / easily).*
::: solution
(a) *fresh* (linking); (b) *quietly* (describes the action); (c) *beautiful* (linking); (d) *beautifully* (describes the singing); (e) *easy* (linking).
:::
:::

::: exercise Correct the verbs {level=2}
Each sentence has one mistake with the verb pattern. Correct it. (a) *The meeting was happened in room 302.* (b) *We should discuss about your plans.* (c) *He entered into the classroom without knocking.* (d) *The price of rice has risen by the government.* (e) *Can you contact with the hotel?* (f) *My grandfather was died two years ago.*
::: solution
(a) *The meeting took place in room 302.* or *The meeting was held in room 302.* (*happen* is used for unplanned events, and has no passive; for a planned event *take place* or *was held* is better.)

(b) *We should discuss your plans.* (or *talk about your plans*)

(c) *He entered the classroom without knocking.* (or *He went into the classroom …*)

(d) *The government has raised the price of rice.* (or *The price of rice has been raised by the government.* — *rise* is intransitive and has no passive; *raise* is transitive.)

(e) *Can you contact the hotel?*

(f) *My grandfather died two years ago.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Two uses of the same verb {level=2}
Rewrite each sentence with the same verb used intransitively, so that the thing that changes is the subject. (a) *The cold weather froze the lake.* (b) *Someone has changed the timetable.* (c) *The wind shut the door.* (d) *The new manager has increased sales.*
::: solution
(a) *The lake froze.* (b) *The timetable has changed.* (c) *The door shut.* (d) *Sales have increased.*

Each verb keeps its tense; the cause disappears from the sentence. If you want to show that someone did it without naming them, the passive is also possible: *The timetable has been changed.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Verbs of becoming {level=2}
Complete with *get*, *go*, *turn* or *become* in the right form. More than one answer may be possible. (a) *It's ___ dark — let's go home.* (b) *Leave the milk in the fridge or it will ___ sour.* (c) *After years of hard work, she ___ a successful lawyer.* (d) *The traffic lights ___ green.* (e) *Don't ___ angry — I was only joking.*
::: solution
(a) *getting* (*growing* is also possible, more literary). (b) *go* (also *turn*). (c) *became* (*got* is not used before a noun in this meaning). (d) *turned* (also *went*). (e) *get*.
:::
:::

::: exercise Correct a short email {level=3}
Find and correct the six mistakes with verb patterns.

*Hi Alex, Thanks for the photos — I really enjoyed. Something funny was happened yesterday. My sister married with her boyfriend last month, and yesterday they reached to our town for a visit. My mother cooked a big dinner and it smelled wonderfully. We discussed about everything.*
::: hint
Check each verb: does it need an object? Does it take a preposition? Is it a linking verb?
:::
::: solution
*Hi Alex, Thanks for the photos — I really enjoyed **them**. Something funny **happened** yesterday. My sister **married** her boyfriend last month, and yesterday they **reached** our town for a visit. My mother cooked a big dinner and it smelled **wonderful**. We **discussed** everything.*

Other good answers: *got married to her boyfriend*; *arrived in our town* or *came to our town*; *talked about everything*.

The six problems: (1) *enjoy* needs an object; (2) *happen* has no passive; (3) *marry* takes no *with*; (4) *reach* takes no *to*; (5) *smell* is a linking verb, so the adjective *wonderful*; (6) *discuss* takes no *about*.
:::
:::

::: exercise Write about changes {level=3}
Write five sentences about how your town, school or family has changed in the last ten years. Use at least three different change-of-state verbs intransitively (*grow*, *change*, *increase*, *improve*, *close*, *open* …) and at least two linking verbs (*become*, *get*, *look*, *seem* …). Label each verb I (intransitive) or L (linking).
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

- *My hometown has **grown** (I) a lot.*
- *Two old cinemas have **closed** (I), and a big shopping centre has **opened** (I).*
- *The roads have **become** (L) much busier.*
- *Housing prices have **increased** (I) every year.*
- *My grandparents' street **looks** (L) completely different now.*

Check that no intransitive verb has an object or a passive (✗ *was increased* for a change with no cause mentioned), and that each linking verb is followed by an adjective or a noun, not an adverb.
:::
