In Chinese, one short pattern covers a great deal: 我想让你来, 妈妈不让我出去, 老师叫我们安静, 我看见他进去了. The person in the middle is both the object of the first verb and the doer of the second. English has the same idea — *I want **you** to come* — but the form of the second verb changes from verb to verb, and some verbs do not allow the pattern at all. Three typical errors:

- ✗ *I want that you come with me.* ✓ *I want you to come with me.*
- ✗ *My parents don't let me to go out at night.* ✓ *My parents don't let me go out at night.*
- ✗ *She suggested me to apply for the job.* ✓ *She suggested (that) I apply for the job.*

This lesson builds on [[verb-patterns/ing-or-infinitive]]. It shows the four forms the second verb can take after an object — *to do*, *do*, *doing*, and a *that*-clause instead — and which verbs use which.

## Verb + object + *to*-infinitive

::: definition Verb + object + verb {#def-object-verb}
In the pattern **verb + object + verb**, the object of the first verb is also the person or thing that does the action of the second verb:

- *I want **Tom** to come.* (Tom will come.)
- *She asked **me** to wait.* (I should wait.)
- *They saw **the car** crash.* (The car crashed.)

The second verb is non-finite: a *to*-infinitive, a bare infinitive or an *-ing* form, depending on the first verb. A pronoun after the first verb is in the **object** form: *I want **him** to go* (✗ *I want he to go*).
:::

::: theorem Verbs followed by object + *to*-infinitive {#rule-obj-to}
The largest group takes object + *to*-infinitive:

| meaning | verbs | examples |
|---|---|---|
| wanting | *want*, *would like*, *would love*, *would prefer*, *need*, *expect* | *I'd like **you to meet** my sister.* *We expect **prices to rise**.* |
| asking and telling | *ask*, *tell*, *order*, *advise*, *remind*, *warn*, *invite*, *beg*, *urge* | *She told **us to sit** down.* *Remind **me to call** Dad.* |
| persuading and helping | *persuade*, *encourage*, *convince*, *teach*, *help* | *He persuaded **her to stay**.* *My father taught **me to swim**.* |
| allowing and forcing | *allow*, *permit*, *enable*, *force*, *require*, *cause* | *The rules don't allow **students to use** phones.* *The rain forced **us to stop**.* |

The negative puts *not* before *to*: *The doctor told **me not to** worry.* (✗ *told me don't worry*)
:::

::: proof
These verbs are about influencing or wanting the action of another person: the subject of *want*, *ask* or *persuade* is not the person who will do the second action. The *to*-infinitive, as in [[verb-patterns/ing-or-infinitive]], looks forward to an action that has not happened yet. The groups agree with Quirk et al. (1985) and Swan (2016), and learner's dictionaries list the pattern in each entry, often as *want sb to do sth*. Some verbs, such as *want*, *like*, *expect* and *need*, can also be used without an object, with a change in who does the action: *I want **to go*** (I will go) — *I want **you to go*** (you will go). Others, such as *tell*, *persuade*, *remind* and *force*, need the object: ✗ *She told to wait*.
:::

::: warning *Want that* and *want* + subject pronoun
✗ *I want that you help me.* ✓ *I want you to help me.*

✗ *My boss wants I finish the report today.* ✓ *My boss wants me to finish the report today.*

✗ *Do you want I open the window?* ✓ *Do you want me to open the window?*

In standard English, *want* is not followed by a *that*-clause. (Some American speakers say *I want for you to come*, but this is regional and not used in writing.)
:::

::: example Reporting requests and advice {#ex-report}
Rewrite each sentence with the verb in brackets + object + *to*-infinitive. (a) *"Please close the door," Mrs Chen said to me. (ask)* (b) *"Don't touch the screen," the guide said to the children. (warn)* (c) *"You should see a doctor," my friend said to me. (advise)* (d) *"Come to my party on Saturday," Wang Fang said to us. (invite)* (e) *"Don't forget your passport," Mr Okafor said to his son. (remind)*
::: solution
1. Choose the person: the one who must do the second action becomes the object.
2. Turn the instruction into a *to*-infinitive; for a negative, use *not to*.

(a) *Mrs Chen **asked me to close** the door.*

(b) *The guide **warned the children not to touch** the screen.*

(c) *My friend **advised me to see** a doctor.*

(d) *Wang Fang **invited us to come** to her party on Saturday.*

(e) *Mr Okafor **reminded his son to take** his passport.* — *don't forget* becomes *remind … to*, and *your* becomes *his*.

Reported speech with these verbs is treated fully in [[complex-sentences/reported-speech]].
:::
:::

## Verbs that do not take object + *to*

Several common verbs look as if they should fit the pattern but do not.

::: theorem *Suggest*, *recommend*, *insist*, *demand*, *hope* {#rule-that-clause}
*Suggest*, *propose*, *insist*, *demand*, *hope*, *say* and *explain* are **not** followed by object + *to*-infinitive. Use a ***that*-clause** instead (*that* can be left out in informal English, except after *insist* and *demand*, where it is usually kept):

- ✓ *She suggested **(that) I apply** for the job.* (✗ *suggested me to apply*)
- ✓ *They insisted **that we stay** for dinner.* (✗ *insisted us to stay*)
- ✓ *I hope **(that) you pass** your exam.* (✗ *I hope you to pass*)

After *suggest*, *recommend*, *propose*, *insist* and *demand*, the verb in the *that*-clause can take three forms:

1. the base form (the **subjunctive**): *She suggested that he **apply**.* — usual in American English, formal in British English;
2. ***should*** + base form: *She suggested that he **should apply**.* — mainly British;
3. an ordinary present or past form: *She suggested that he **applied**.* — informal British English.
:::

::: proof
These verbs report what someone said or wanted, and they take the whole proposal as a clause, with its own subject and verb. Swan (2016) describes the three forms of the verb in the *that*-clause and the difference between American and British use; corpus studies have found the subjunctive (*that he apply*) much more frequent in American than in British English, with British use growing. *Recommend* is between the two groups: *recommend that someone (should) do* is used everywhere, and British English also has *recommend someone **to** do* (*I'd recommend you to book early*), which the Oxford learner's dictionary marks as British. *Suggest* can also take *-ing* when the speaker is included or no person is named: *She suggested **going** by train* ([[verb-patterns/ing-or-infinitive#rule-ing-verbs]]).
:::

::: warning *Suggest* and *hope*
✗ *The teacher suggested us to read more.* ✓ *The teacher suggested (that) we read more.* ✓ *The teacher suggested reading more.*

✗ *I hope you to enjoy your stay.* ✓ *I hope (that) you enjoy your stay.* ✓ *I hope you'll enjoy your stay.*

✗ *He explained me to fill in the form.* ✓ *He explained how to fill in the form.* ✓ *He showed me how to fill in the form.*
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *My doctor suggested me to walk more.*
- [ ] *I hope you to have a good time.*
- [x] *My doctor advised me to walk more.*
- [ ] *She insisted me to stay.*
::: solution
*Advise* takes object + *to*-infinitive. *Suggest*, *hope* and *insist* do not: *My doctor suggested (that) I walk more*; *I hope you have a good time*; *She insisted that I stay* (or *should stay*), or *She insisted on my staying*.
:::
:::

## Verbs of perception: *see someone do* or *doing*

::: theorem Verb of perception + object + bare infinitive or *-ing* {#rule-perception}
*See*, *watch*, *hear*, *feel*, *notice*, *observe* and *listen to* take object + **bare infinitive** or object + ***-ing***, never object + *to*:

1. bare infinitive for the **whole** action, from beginning to end: *I saw her **cross** the road.* (I saw her get to the other side.)
2. *-ing* for part of an action **in progress**: *I saw her **crossing** the road.* (I saw her in the middle of it.)

✗ *I saw her to cross the road.*
:::

::: proof
The bare infinitive presents the event as complete; the *-ing* form, like the progressive aspect, presents it as going on. The difference often does not matter much (*I heard him come in* / *I heard him coming in*), but it is clear with long or repeated actions: *I watched them **play** a whole match* versus *I watched them **playing** for a few minutes*. With short actions that are over at once, the bare infinitive is usual: *I heard the door **slam***. In the **passive**, the bare infinitive becomes a *to*-infinitive: *He was seen **to enter** the building* (formal); *He was seen **entering** the building* is more common. Quirk et al. (1985) and Swan (2016) both describe this pattern. *Look at* and *listen to* are less usual with the bare infinitive, especially *look at*; *watch* and *listen to* + *-ing* are common.
:::

- *We heard the baby **cry** once and then go back to sleep.*
- *I can hear someone **singing** next door.*
- *Did you notice anyone **leave** early?*
- *I felt the ground **shake**.*

::: example Whole action or action in progress? {#ex-perception}
Choose the better form and explain. (a) *I saw the plane (land / landing) — it touched down smoothly and stopped.* (b) *When I looked out of the window, I saw the children (play / playing) in the snow.* (c) *I heard someone (knock / knocking) three times.* (d) *As I walked past, I could smell something (burn / burning).*
::: solution
(a) *land* — the whole action, from the air to stopping on the runway.

(b) *playing* — I saw only part of an activity that was going on before and after I looked.

(c) *knock* — three complete knocks, each one finished. (*knocking* is possible if the knocking went on and I heard part of it, but *three times* counts complete actions.)

(d) *burning* — *smell* is a verb of perception, and the burning was going on; with *smell*, only *-ing* is used.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is **not** correct?
- [ ] *I watched him fix the bike.*
- [ ] *I watched him fixing the bike.*
- [x] *I watched him to fix the bike.*
- [ ] *He was seen to leave at midnight.*
::: solution
Verbs of perception never take object + *to*-infinitive in the active. In the passive (*He was seen to leave*), *to* is used.
:::
:::

## *Help*, *let* and *make*

*Help* takes object + infinitive with or without *to*: *Can you help **me (to) carry** this?* Both forms are correct in British and American English; the form without *to* is more frequent in conversation and in American English, and has become more common in British English too (Biber et al. 1999). *Help* can also be used without an object: *She helped **(to) organise** the party.*

*Let* and *make* take object + **bare infinitive**: *My parents **let me stay** up late.* *The film **made me cry**.* These and the other causative verbs are the topic of the next lesson, [[verb-patterns/causatives]].

## Verb + object + *-ing*

::: theorem Verbs followed by object + *-ing* {#rule-obj-ing}
Some verbs take object + *-ing*:

- verbs of liking and minding: *mind*, *like*, *dislike*, *hate*, *can't stand*, *imagine*, *remember*: *Do you mind **me asking** a question?* *I can't imagine **him cooking** dinner.* *I remember **my grandmother singing** to me.*
- verbs of finding and leaving: *find*, *catch*, *keep*, *leave*: *Sorry to keep **you waiting**.* *I found **him sleeping** on the sofa.* *They left **the engine running**.*
- *stop* and *prevent* (+ *from*): *The noise prevented **me from sleeping**.* *Nothing can stop **her (from) singing**.* (*From* is often left out after *stop*, especially in British English, but not after *prevent* in careful writing.)

After *mind*, *like*, *imagine* and *remember*, a **possessive** can replace the object in formal English: *Do you mind **my** asking …?* *I don't remember **his** saying that.* The object form (*me*, *him*) is usual in conversation and is also correct.
:::

::: proof
Usage guides once insisted on the possessive (*my asking*, *his saying*), but both reference grammars and usage guides now describe the object form as normal, and the possessive as more formal (Swan 2016; Huddleston & Pullum 2002). With a long noun phrase, the possessive is impossible or awkward: *I remember **the people next door** complaining* (✗ *the people next door's complaining*). Note the difference between *like* + object + *-ing* and *would like* + object + *to*: *I don't like **you working** so late* (I'm unhappy that you do it) — *I'd like **you to work** less* (I want you to change).
:::

::: example Object + *-ing* or object + *to*? {#ex-obj-ing}
Complete each sentence with the verb in brackets, and say which pattern you used. (a) *Sorry to keep you ___ . (wait)* (b) *I'd like you ___ this form. (sign)* (c) *I don't like people ___ in the library. (shout)* (d) *The fog prevented the plane from ___ . (land)* (e) *Mum caught my brother ___ biscuits from the cupboard. (take)*
::: solution
1. Find the first verb and check its group.
2. *Keep*, *catch*, *prevent from* and *like* (for an attitude to something that happens) take object + *-ing*; *would like* takes object + *to*.

(a) *waiting* — *keep* + object + *-ing*.

(b) *to sign* — *would like* + object + *to*: a request.

(c) *shouting* — *like* + object + *-ing*: my feelings about something people do. (*I don't like people to shout in the library* is also possible, meaning ‘I don't want them to’.)

(d) *landing* — *prevent* + object + *from* + *-ing*.

(e) *taking* — *catch* + object + *-ing*: he was in the middle of it.
:::
:::

### *Tell someone what to do*

A question word + *to*-infinitive can follow an object with *tell*, *show*, *teach*, *ask*, *advise* and *remind*, and can follow *explain*, *know*, *learn*, *decide* and *wonder* without an object:

- *Can you **show me how to** use this machine?*
- *Nobody **told us where to** go.*
- *I don't **know what to** say.*
- *She **explained how to** get there.* (✗ *explained me how to*; see [[verb-patterns/two-objects]])

::: example Correcting a learner's message {#ex-message}
Correct the mistakes in verb + object + verb patterns. *Dear Ms Liu, My parents want that I study abroad, and they suggested me to ask you for advice. My English teacher always encouraged me read widely, and last year I saw some students to give a presentation about Australia. I hope you to help me.*
::: solution
1. *want that I study* → *want **me to study*** (*want* + object + *to*).
2. *suggested me to ask* → *suggested **(that) I ask*** (or *suggested asking*): *suggest* does not take object + *to*.
3. *encouraged me read* → *encouraged **me to read*** (*encourage* + object + *to*).
4. *saw some students to give* → *saw some students **give*** (whole action) or ***giving*** (part of it).
5. *I hope you to help* → *I hope **(that) you can help*** (*hope* takes a *that*-clause).

Corrected: *Dear Ms Liu, My parents want me to study abroad, and they suggested that I ask you for advice. My English teacher always encouraged me to read widely, and last year I saw some students give a presentation about Australia. I hope you can help me.*
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **The pivotal construction.** Chinese grammars call 我请他吃饭 and 老师叫我们安静 the 兼语式 (pivotal construction): the middle noun is the object of the first verb and the subject of the second (朱德熙 1982; Li & Thompson 1981). English verb + object + verb is the same idea, but English marks the second verb (*to come*, *come*, *coming*) and puts a pronoun in the object form (*me*, *him*, *them*).
- **让, 叫, 要.** 让 is the most frequent verb in this pattern, and it has several English equivalents: 我让他进来 can be *I **let** him come in* (allowed), *I **told** him to come in* (instructed), *I **asked** him to come in* (requested) or *I **had** him come in* (arranged). 妈妈要我早点回家 is *Mum **wants** me to come home early*. Choose the English verb by the meaning, then use its pattern.
- **希望 and 建议.** Chinese 希望你…… and 建议你…… have the pivotal form, but English *hope* and *suggest* take *that*-clauses: *I hope (that) you…*, *I suggest (that) you…*.
- **看见他进去.** Chinese 我看见他进去了 is close to *I saw him **go** in* — with no *to*. Chinese 我看见他在打电话 (with 在, progressive) is close to *I saw him **making** a phone call*.
:::

::: history
Traditional grammars of English borrowed a Latin name for the pattern *I want him to go*: the "accusative and infinitive" (Latin *accusativus cum infinitivo*), because in Latin the subject of the infinitive is in the accusative case — as English *him* is in the object form. A. S. Hornby's lists of verb patterns (1942, 1954) gave learners separate patterns for verb + object + *to*-infinitive, verb + object + bare infinitive and verb + object + *-ing*. Modern grammars analyse the pattern differently from one another: Quirk et al. (1985) treat the object as part of a complex object, while Huddleston and Pullum (2002) speak of "catenative" verbs and argue about whether the noun phrase belongs to the first verb or to the second. For learners the forms are the same whichever analysis is chosen.
:::

## Where this leads

[[verb-patterns/causatives]] looks at *make*, *let*, *have* and *get*. The passive of these patterns (*She was asked to wait*, *He was seen to leave*) is in [[verb-patterns/the-passive]]. *That*-clauses after *suggest* and other reporting verbs return in [[complex-sentences/noun-clauses]] and [[complex-sentences/reported-speech]].

::: summary
- In verb + object + verb, the object of the first verb does the second action; a pronoun takes the object form: *I want **him** to go* ([[#def-object-verb]]).
- *Want*, *would like*, *ask*, *tell*, *advise*, *remind*, *persuade*, *encourage*, *allow*, *force* and many others take object + *to*-infinitive; the negative is *not to* ([[#rule-obj-to]]).
- *Want* never takes a *that*-clause: ✗ *I want that you…*
- *Suggest*, *insist*, *demand*, *hope*, *say* and *explain* do not take object + *to*; use a *that*-clause (*She suggested that I (should) apply*) ([[#rule-that-clause]]).
- Verbs of perception take object + bare infinitive (whole action) or *-ing* (action in progress), never *to* in the active ([[#rule-perception]]).
- *Help* takes object + infinitive with or without *to*; *let* and *make* take the bare infinitive.
- *Mind*, *imagine*, *remember*, *keep*, *find*, *catch* and *stop* / *prevent (from)* take object + *-ing* ([[#rule-obj-ing]]).
:::
