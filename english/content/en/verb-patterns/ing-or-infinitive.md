Why do we say *I want **to learn** Spanish* but *I enjoy **learning** Spanish*? Both sentences put one verb after another, and Chinese uses the same form in both: 我想学西班牙语, 我喜欢学西班牙语. English chooses between two forms of the second verb, *to learn* and *learning*, and the choice depends on the first verb. Sometimes the choice even changes the meaning:

- *I stopped **smoking**.* (I don't smoke any more.)
- *I stopped **to smoke**.* (I stopped what I was doing so that I could smoke.)

This lesson sorts the commonest verbs into those followed by *-ing*, those followed by *to*, those that take either with little difference, and those whose meaning changes. It also gives the tendencies in meaning that make the lists easier to remember — and says honestly where a verb simply has to be learned.

## Two forms for the second verb

When one verb follows another, only the first verb shows tense (*I **want**ed*, *She **enjoy**s*). The second verb is **non-finite**: it has no tense and does not agree with the subject.

::: definition *-ing* form and *to*-infinitive {#def-forms}
The **-ing form** is the base of the verb + *-ing*: *learning*, *swimming*, *being*. When it is used like a noun, as here, school grammars call it a **gerund** (动名词).

The ***to*-infinitive** is *to* + the base form: *to learn*, *to swim*, *to be*. The **bare infinitive** is the base form without *to* (*learn*, *swim*), used after modal verbs (*can swim*) and a few other verbs ([[verb-patterns/object-and-verb]]).

Both have negative forms with *not* before them: *I decided **not to go**.* *I regret **not going**.*
:::

✗ *I want go.* ✗ *I enjoy swim.* — after an ordinary verb, the second verb is never the bare base form (except after *help*, *let*, *make* and verbs of perception, which come in later lessons).

## Verbs followed by *-ing*

::: theorem Verbs followed by the *-ing* form {#rule-ing-verbs}
These common verbs are followed by *-ing*, not by *to*:

| meaning | verbs | examples |
|---|---|---|
| liking and disliking | *enjoy*, *mind*, *can't stand*, *don't mind*, *dislike*, *fancy* (BrE) | *I **enjoy cooking**.* *Do you **mind waiting**?* |
| finishing, continuing, doing again | *finish*, *keep*, *keep on*, *carry on*, *give up*, *quit* | *She **kept talking**.* *He **gave up smoking**.* |
| avoiding and delaying | *avoid*, *put off*, *postpone*, *delay*, *risk*, *miss* | *Try to **avoid eating** late.* *Don't **put off calling** her.* |
| thinking and saying | *consider*, *imagine*, *suggest*, *recommend*, *admit*, *deny*, *mention* | *We **considered moving**.* *He **denied taking** the money.* |
| other | *practise* (AmE *practice*), *can't help*, *feel like*, *it's worth*, *it's no use* | *I **can't help laughing**.* *I **feel like going** out.* |
:::

::: proof
Many of these verbs look at an action that is real, already happening, or imagined as a whole: you can only *enjoy*, *finish* or *give up* something that you actually do, and *avoid*, *mind*, *imagine* and *consider* treat the action like a thing, a noun. This is why the *-ing* form, which behaves much like a noun, fits them. Swan (2016) and Biber et al. (1999) give lists like the one above; the meaning is a tendency that helps memory, not a test. *Consider* and *suggest* look forward, and still take *-ing*. *Suggest* is a frequent problem: ✓ *She **suggested going** by train* or ✓ *She suggested that we (should) go by train*, but not ✗ *She suggested to go* and not ✗ *She suggested us to go* (see [[verb-patterns/object-and-verb]]).
:::

::: warning *To* after an *-ing* verb
✗ *I enjoy to play tennis.* ✓ *I enjoy playing tennis.*

✗ *Have you finished to write your essay?* ✓ *Have you finished writing your essay?*

✗ *I suggest to take a taxi.* ✓ *I suggest taking a taxi.* ✓ *I suggest (that) we take a taxi.*

✗ *Would you mind to open the window?* ✓ *Would you mind opening the window?*
:::

## Verbs followed by *to*

::: theorem Verbs followed by the *to*-infinitive {#rule-to-verbs}
These common verbs are followed by *to* + base form:

| meaning | verbs | examples |
|---|---|---|
| wanting and hoping | *want*, *would like*, *hope*, *wish*, *expect*, *need* | *I **hope to see** you soon.* |
| deciding and planning | *decide*, *plan*, *choose*, *intend*, *arrange*, *aim* | *We **decided to stay**.* |
| promising, agreeing, refusing | *promise*, *agree*, *offer*, *refuse*, *threaten* | *He **refused to help**.* *She **offered to drive**.* |
| trying and succeeding | *try*, *manage*, *fail*, *learn*, *afford* | *Did you **manage to find** it?* *I can't **afford to buy** a car.* |
| seeming | *seem*, *appear*, *tend*, *pretend*, *happen* | *She **seems to know** everyone.* |
:::

::: proof
Many of these verbs look **forward** to an action that has not happened yet: you *want*, *hope*, *plan*, *decide* or *promise* to do something in the future. The *to* of the infinitive goes back historically to the preposition *to*, with a sense of direction towards something. Again this is a tendency: *manage* (*I managed to finish*) describes a past success, and *seem*, *tend* and *pretend* are not about the future. *Would like* is followed by *to* (*I'd like to go*), even though *like* alone can also take *-ing* (below). *Help* can be followed by *to* or by the bare infinitive: *She helped **(to) clean** the kitchen.*
:::

::: example Which form? {#ex-which-form}
Complete each sentence with the verb in brackets, and say which rule you used. (a) *I'm hoping ___ a job in Shanghai. (find)* (b) *Have you finished ___ the dishes? (wash)* (c) *We can't afford ___ a new car this year. (buy)* (d) *He denied ___ the window. (break)* (e) *They agreed ___ the price. (lower)*
::: solution
1. Find the first verb (the finite one).
2. Check which group it belongs to: *-ing* verbs ([[#rule-ing-verbs]]) or *to* verbs ([[#rule-to-verbs]]).

(a) *hope* → *to*: *I'm hoping **to find** a job in Shanghai.* (forward-looking)

(b) *finish* → *-ing*: *Have you finished **washing** the dishes?* (you can only finish something you are doing)

(c) *afford* → *to*: *We can't afford **to buy** a new car this year.*

(d) *deny* → *-ing*: *He denied **breaking** the window.* (also *He denied having broken* — more formal; the simple *-ing* is enough because *deny* already looks back.)

(e) *agree* → *to*: *They agreed **to lower** the price.*
:::

## After prepositions: always *-ing*

::: theorem *-ing* after prepositions {#rule-after-prep}
After a preposition, a verb takes the *-ing* form:

- *She's good **at drawing**.*
- *Thanks **for helping** me.*
- *I'm interested **in learning** Japanese.*
- *Wash your hands **before eating**.*
- ***Instead of taking** the bus, we walked.*

This includes the preposition ***to***, in expressions such as *look forward to*, *be used to*, *get used to*, *object to*, *be committed to*, *when it comes to* and *in addition to*:

- *I **look forward to hearing** from you.* (✗ *to hear*)
- *She's **used to working** at night.*
:::

::: proof
A preposition is followed by a noun phrase (*good at maths*), and the *-ing* form is the verb form that can stand in a noun's place (*good at drawing*). The difficulty is the word *to*, which is both the infinitive marker (*want to go*) and a preposition (*go to school*). A simple test: if you can put a noun after *to* in the same expression, *to* is a preposition, and a verb after it takes *-ing*. *I look forward to **the holiday*** is correct, so *I look forward to **seeing** you*. *I want to **the holiday*** is impossible, so *want to* is followed by the base form. The difference between *be used to doing* (‘be accustomed to’) and *used to do* (a past habit: *I used to live in Xi'an*) is explained in [[tense-and-aspect/past-habits]].
:::

::: warning *Look forward to* + base form
✗ *I look forward to hear from you.* ✓ *I look forward to hearing from you.*

✗ *I'm not used to get up so early.* ✓ *I'm not used to getting up so early.*

✗ *She is interested in to study medicine.* ✓ *She is interested in studying medicine.*

*I look forward to hearing from you* is a standard closing for a formal email; learn it as a whole.
:::

::: example Preposition *to* or infinitive *to*? {#ex-to-test}
Put the verb in brackets in the right form. (a) *We hope to ___ you in May. (visit)* (b) *We're looking forward to ___ you in May. (visit)* (c) *The company is committed to ___ its waste. (reduce)* (d) *I'd prefer not to ___ about it. (talk)* (e) *Many residents object to ___ for parking. (pay)*
::: solution
For each *to*, try a noun in its place. If a noun fits, *to* is a preposition and the verb takes *-ing*; if not, it is the infinitive marker and the verb stays in the base form.

(a) ✗ *We hope to your visit* — no noun possible: infinitive. *We hope to **visit** you in May.*

(b) ✓ *We're looking forward to your visit*: preposition. *We're looking forward to **visiting** you in May.*

(c) ✓ *committed to a cleaner future*: preposition. *The company is committed to **reducing** its waste.*

(d) ✗ *I'd prefer not to the talk* — no noun after *prefer not to*: infinitive. *I'd prefer not to **talk** about it.*

(e) ✓ *object to the new rules*: preposition. *Many residents object to **paying** for parking.*
:::
:::

## Verbs that take both with little change in meaning

::: theorem *Begin*, *start*, *continue*, *like*, *love*, *hate*, *prefer* {#rule-both-same}
1. *Begin*, *start*, *continue* and *intend* take *-ing* or *to* with little difference in meaning: *It **started raining** / **to rain**.* *She **continued working** / **to work**.* After a progressive form, use *to* to avoid two *-ing* forms together: *It's **starting to rain*** (✗ *It's starting raining*).
2. *Like*, *love*, *hate* and *prefer* also take both. In British English there is a small difference: *-ing* is used for enjoyment in general (*I **like swimming***), and *to* for choices and habits that you think are good or sensible (*I **like to get** to the airport early*). In American English *like to* is common in both meanings (*I like to swim*).
3. *Would like*, *would love*, *would hate* and *would prefer* take *to*: *I'**d like to** see that film.* (✗ *I'd like seeing*)
:::

::: proof
Swan (2016) describes the British contrast between *like doing* and *like to do*, and notes that it is a tendency: many British speakers use both forms for enjoyment as well. The difference between *like* and *would like* is important: *I like dancing* means I enjoy it in general; *I'd like to dance* means I want to dance now or on a particular occasion. Learners sometimes leave out the *'d*: ✗ *I like to have a coffee, please* (in a café). Say *I'd like a coffee, please* or *I'd like to have a coffee*.
:::

## Verbs that change their meaning

With a few very common verbs, *-ing* and *to* give different meanings.

::: theorem *Remember*, *forget*, *regret*, *stop*, *try*, *go on*, *mean*, *need* {#rule-meaning-change}
| verb | + *-ing* | + *to*-infinitive |
|---|---|---|
| *remember* | remember an earlier action: *I **remember locking** the door.* (I locked it, and now I remember that.) | remember an action you have to do: ***Remember to lock** the door.* (Don't forget; then lock it.) |
| *forget* | forget an earlier experience (mostly in *I'll never forget …*): *I'll never **forget seeing** the sea for the first time.* | forget to do something: *I **forgot to call** my mother.* (so I didn't call her) |
| *regret* | be sorry about something you did: *I **regret saying** that.* | be sorry about what you are going to say (formal): *We **regret to inform** you that …* |
| *stop* | end an activity: *He **stopped smoking** last year.* | stop one activity in order to do another: *He **stopped to buy** a newspaper.* |
| *try* | do something as an experiment, to see what happens: *If you can't sleep, **try drinking** warm milk.* | make an effort, perhaps without success: *I **tried to open** the window, but it was stuck.* |
| *go on* | continue the same activity: *She **went on talking** for an hour.* | move on to something new: *After school, she **went on to study** law.* |
| *mean* | involve, have as a result: *A new job **means moving** to Beijing.* | intend: *I **meant to call** you, but I forgot.* |
| *need* | (mainly BrE) needs to be done: *The car **needs washing**.* | it is necessary for someone to do something: *I **need to wash** the car.* |
:::

::: proof
For *remember*, *forget* and *regret* there is a useful pattern: the ***-ing* form looks back** to an action that happened before the remembering, forgetting or regretting, and the ***to*-infinitive looks forward** to an action that comes after it. When I *remember locking* the door, the locking came first; when I *remember to lock* the door, the remembering comes first and the locking follows. With *stop*, the *-ing* form is the object of *stop* (the thing that stops), while *to* expresses purpose, ‘in order to’, as in *I went out **to buy** bread*. *Try doing* means you do the thing and see whether it helps; *try to do* means you attempt it, and the attempt itself may fail. For *need* + *-ing* with a passive meaning (*The car needs washing* = ‘the car needs to be washed’), Swan (2016) notes that it is mainly British; American English usually says *The car needs to be washed*. These descriptions agree with the Oxford and Cambridge learner's dictionaries.
:::

::: example Remember, stop, try {#ex-meaning}
Explain the difference in meaning between the sentences in each pair. (a) *I remember meeting her.* / *I remembered to meet her.* (b) *She stopped looking at her phone.* / *She stopped to look at her phone.* (c) *Try turning it off and on again.* / *I tried to turn it off, but the button didn't work.*
::: solution
(a) *Remember meeting*: the meeting happened first; now I have a memory of it. *Remembered to meet*: I didn't forget my plan, so I went and met her. The order of events is reversed.

(b) *Stopped looking*: she was looking at her phone and then she stopped — the looking ended. *Stopped to look*: she was doing something else (walking, talking) and stopped **in order to** look at her phone — the looking began.

(c) *Try turning it off and on*: this is a suggestion for an experiment: do it and see if the problem goes away. *Tried to turn it off*: I made an effort, but I didn't succeed.
:::
:::

## Perfect and passive forms

Both forms have perfect and passive versions, used less often:

- *He denied **having taken** the money.* (perfect *-ing*: an earlier action; the simple *taking* is also correct)
- *She seems **to have forgotten** our meeting.* (perfect infinitive: earlier than *seems*)
- *I don't like **being told** what to do.* (passive *-ing*)
- *Everyone wants **to be loved**.* (passive infinitive)

The passive forms are part of [[verb-patterns/the-passive]].

::: example Correcting a paragraph {#ex-paragraph}
A student wrote this about learning to drive. Correct the five mistakes in verb forms. *Last year I decided learning to drive. At first I avoided to drive on busy roads, and I kept to forget the gears. My teacher suggested to practise in a car park. Now I'm used to drive in the city, and I enjoy it.*
::: solution
1. *decided learning* → *decided **to learn*** (*decide* takes *to*).
2. *avoided to drive* → *avoided **driving*** (*avoid* takes *-ing*).
3. *kept to forget* → *kept **forgetting*** (*keep* takes *-ing*).
4. *suggested to practise* → *suggested **practising*** (or *suggested that I practise / should practise*).
5. *used to drive* → *used to **driving*** (*be used to* + *-ing*, because *to* is a preposition here).

Corrected: *Last year I decided to learn to drive. At first I avoided driving on busy roads, and I kept forgetting the gears. My teacher suggested practising in a car park. Now I'm used to driving in the city, and I enjoy it.*
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **No form to choose in Chinese.** Mandarin verbs have no infinitive or *-ing* form: 我想**去** and 我喜欢**去** use the same 去. Li & Thompson (1981) describe such strings of verbs as serial verb constructions and verbs with verbal objects; in all of them the verbs keep one form. An English learner therefore has no help from Chinese and must learn the pattern of each English verb. The meaning tendencies of this lesson (*-ing* for real or ongoing actions, *to* for later ones) help, but the lists have to be practised.
- **忘了 and 记得.** Chinese 我忘了锁门 means ‘I forgot to lock the door’ (I didn't lock it); 我记得锁了门 means ‘I remember locking the door’. The Chinese difference is shown by 了 and word order, the English one by *to* and *-ing*.
- **建议.** 我建议去 and 他建议我去 often become ✗ *I suggest to go* and ✗ *He suggested me to go*. English *suggest* takes *-ing* or a *that*-clause.
- **停下来.** 他停下来抽烟 is *He stopped **to smoke***; 他戒烟了 is *He stopped **smoking*** or *He gave up smoking*. Chinese uses two different verbs where English uses one verb with two forms.
:::

::: history
The two forms have different histories. The *to*-infinitive comes from an Old English construction in which the preposition *tō* was followed by a noun-like form of the verb (*tō etanne* ‘for eating’), which is why it often carries a sense of purpose or direction. The *-ing* form of today merges two Old English endings: *-ung*/*-ing*, which made nouns from verbs, and the participle ending *-ende*. In Middle and Early Modern English the gerund gradually acquired verbal properties, such as taking a direct object (*reading books*), and many verbs came to take it as a complement; standard histories such as Baugh and Cable's describe these developments. The choice between *-ing* and *to* has continued to change: corpus studies, for example by Christian Mair, have found that *start* and *begin* + *-ing* became more frequent during the twentieth century.
:::

## Where this leads

The next lesson, [[verb-patterns/object-and-verb]], adds an object before the second verb: *I want **you** to come*, *I saw **him** leave*. [[verb-patterns/causatives]] deals with *make*, *let*, *have* and *get*. The *-ing* form and the infinitive also start clauses of their own (*Having finished, she left*; *To be honest, …*), which are the subject of [[complex-sentences/participle-infinitive-clauses]], and *be used to doing* is contrasted with *used to do* in [[tense-and-aspect/past-habits]].

::: summary
- After a verb, a second verb is non-finite: the *-ing* form or the *to*-infinitive, chosen by the first verb ([[#def-forms]]).
- *Enjoy*, *mind*, *finish*, *keep*, *avoid*, *consider*, *suggest*, *imagine*, *admit*, *deny*, *give up* and *practise* take *-ing* ([[#rule-ing-verbs]]).
- *Want*, *hope*, *decide*, *plan*, *agree*, *promise*, *offer*, *refuse*, *manage*, *fail*, *afford* and *seem* take *to* ([[#rule-to-verbs]]).
- After any preposition, including *to* in *look forward to* and *be used to*, use *-ing* ([[#rule-after-prep]]).
- *Begin*, *start*, *continue*, *like*, *love*, *hate* and *prefer* take both with little change; *would like* takes *to* ([[#rule-both-same]]).
- With *remember*, *forget* and *regret*, *-ing* looks back and *to* looks forward; *stop doing* ends an activity, *stop to do* gives a purpose; *try doing* is an experiment, *try to do* an effort ([[#rule-meaning-change]]).
- The lists show tendencies of meaning, but each verb's pattern must be learned; check it in a learner's dictionary.
:::
