*It depends **on** the weather.* *I'm waiting **for** a bus.* *She's interested **in** history.* *What's the reason **for** the delay?* Small words like *on*, *for* and *in* follow many English verbs, adjectives and nouns, and the right one is not always the one you would expect. Chinese speakers meet two problems here. First, Chinese often has the preposition **before** the verb (对历史感兴趣, 为迟到道歉) or no preposition at all (等车, 听音乐, 依靠父母), so learners leave the English preposition out: ✗ *I'm waiting a bus.* Second, the English preposition cannot be worked out from the meaning: why *depend **on***, *consist **of***, *succeed **in***?

This lesson lists the commonest combinations, groups them so that they are easier to learn, shows where the preposition goes in questions and the passive, and adds the prepositions after adjectives and nouns. It follows [[verb-patterns/phrasal-verbs]], which explained how to tell a prepositional verb (*look after them*) from a phrasal verb (*look them up*).

## Verbs with a fixed preposition

::: definition Prepositional verb {#def-prepositional}
A **prepositional verb** is a verb that is followed by a particular preposition before its object: *depend **on***, *listen **to***, *look **at***, *wait **for***, *consist **of***. The noun phrase after the preposition is called a **prepositional object**: in *It depends on **the weather***, *the weather* is the prepositional object of *depend on*.

Unlike a phrasal verb, a prepositional verb keeps its object after the preposition, even when the object is a pronoun: *Listen to **me**.* (✗ *Listen me to.*)
:::

The preposition belongs to the verb, and a dictionary gives it in the entry: *depend on sb/sth*, *apologize (to sb) (for sth)*.

::: theorem Common verb + preposition combinations {#rule-common}
| preposition | verbs | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *about* | *think*, *talk*, *worry*, *complain*, *hear*, *dream*, *know*, *care* | *Don't **worry about** it.* *What are you **talking about**?* |
| *at* | *look*, *laugh*, *smile*, *stare*, *point*, *aim* | *Why are you **laughing at** me?* |
| *for* | *wait*, *look* (‘search’), *ask*, *pay*, *apply*, *apologise*, *search*, *hope* | *We're **waiting for** a taxi.* *Who's **paying for** this?* |
| *from* | *suffer*, *recover*, *differ*, *escape*, *benefit* | *She's **recovering from** a cold.* |
| *in* | *believe*, *succeed*, *specialise*, *result*, *take part*, *invest* | *He **succeeded in** finding a job.* *Do you **believe in** ghosts?* |
| *of* | *consist*, *approve*, *think*, *dream*, *hear*, *die* (of an illness) | *The team **consists of** five people.* |
| *on* | *depend*, *rely*, *insist*, *concentrate*, *focus*, *comment*, *count*, *decide* | *You can **rely on** me.* *I can't **concentrate on** my work.* |
| *to* | *listen*, *belong*, *reply*, *respond*, *react*, *refer*, *happen*, *object*, *talk*, *apologise* | *This bag **belongs to** Li Wei.* *Please **reply to** this email.* |
| *with* | *agree*, *deal*, *cope*, *argue*, *disagree*, *cooperate*, *interfere* | *I **agree with** you.* *How do you **cope with** the stress?* |
:::

::: proof
Some prepositions have a core meaning that runs through their verbs: *about* introduces a topic (*think*, *talk*, *worry*, *complain about*), *at* a target or direction (*look*, *point*, *aim*, *laugh at*), *for* something wanted or sought (*wait*, *ask*, *look*, *apply for*), *from* a source or a starting point (*recover*, *escape*, *suffer from*). These tendencies make the lists easier to learn, and they are described in this way in teaching grammars (Swan 2016; Murphy 2019). But many combinations have no clear reason today: *depend **on*** and *rely **on*** come from the physical idea of resting on something, which is hard to feel in *It depends on the price*; *consist **of*** and *succeed **in*** simply have to be learned. Collins COBUILD's *Grammar Patterns 1: Verbs* (Francis, Hunston & Manning 1996) lists hundreds of verbs under each preposition, grouped by meaning.
:::

::: warning The wrong preposition, or none
✗ *It depends of the price.* ✓ *It depends on the price.*

✗ *The book consists from ten chapters.* ✓ *The book consists of ten chapters.*

✗ *I'm waiting the bus.* ✓ *I'm waiting for the bus.*

✗ *Please listen me.* ✓ *Please listen to me.*

✗ *She apologised me for being late.* ✓ *She apologised to me for being late.*
:::

## Same verb, different prepositions

Some verbs take different prepositions with different meanings.

::: theorem Verbs with more than one preposition {#rule-pairs}
| verb | preposition and meaning | example |
|---|---|---|
| *think* | *about* = consider, have in your mind; *of* = have an idea, an opinion, or remember | *I'm **thinking about** changing jobs.* *What do you **think of** my plan?* *I can't **think of** her name.* |
| *hear* | *about* = get news of; *of* = know that something exists; *from* = get a message from | *Did you **hear about** the accident?* *I've never **heard of** that singer.* *Have you **heard from** Amina?* |
| *agree* | *with* a person or opinion; *to* a request or plan; *on* a decision | *I **agree with** you.* *They **agreed to** our terms.* *We **agreed on** a price.* |
| *look* | *at* = direct your eyes; *for* = search; *after* = take care of; *into* = investigate | *She's **looking for** her keys.* *Who's **looking after** the baby?* |
| *care* | *about* = think something is important; *for* = look after (also ‘like’, formal) | *He really **cares about** the environment.* *She **cares for** her elderly father.* |
| *result* | *in* = have as a result; *from* = be the result of | *The storm **resulted in** floods.* *The floods **resulted from** the storm.* |
| *apologise* / *complain* | *to* a person, *for* / *about* a thing | *I **apologised to** her **for** the mistake.* *He **complained to** the manager **about** the noise.* |
:::

::: example *Think about* or *think of*? {#ex-think}
Complete with *about* or *of*, and explain the choice. (a) *What do you think ___ the new teacher?* (b) *I'm thinking ___ learning Japanese next year.* (c) *Can you think ___ a good name for the cat?* (d) *Don't think ___ it too much — just decide.* (e) *Whenever I hear this song, I think ___ my grandmother.*
::: solution
(a) *of* — asking for an opinion. (In speech *What do you think about…?* is also used.)

(b) *about* — considering a possible plan over time. (*thinking of learning* is also possible, with a similar meaning: ‘planning, considering’.)

(c) *of* — producing an idea.

(d) *about* — keeping something in your mind.

(e) *of* — remembering, an idea that comes to mind. (*about* is also possible.)

The two prepositions overlap; the clearest uses are *think of* for ideas, opinions and names, and *think about* for a period of thinking.
:::
:::

::: quiz
*Have you heard ___ Kenji since he moved to Osaka?* Which preposition means ‘received a message from’?
- [ ] *about*
- [ ] *of*
- [x] *from*
- [ ] *to*
::: solution
*Hear from* someone = get a letter, email or call from them. *Hear about* = get news about someone; *hear of* = know that someone exists.
:::
:::

## Verbs with no preposition

The opposite mistake is to add a preposition that the verb does not take. These verbs take their object directly ([[verb-patterns/transitive-intransitive]]):

- *discuss* (✗ *discuss about*), *enter* (✗ *enter into the room*), *reach* (✗ *reach to*), *marry* (✗ *marry with*), *contact* (✗ *contact with*), *approach*, *answer*, *attend*, *resemble*, *lack*, *emphasise* (✗ *emphasise on*), *mention* (✗ *mention about*), *request* (✗ *request for*; but *a request **for***), *meet* (in British English; American English also uses *meet with* for a formal meeting).

Often the related noun **does** take a preposition: *discuss a problem* but *a discussion **about** / **of** a problem*; *emphasise a point* but *put emphasis **on** a point*; *request information* but *a request **for** information*; *answer a question* but *the answer **to** a question*.

## Verb + object + preposition

::: theorem Verbs with an object and a preposition {#rule-vop}
Some verbs take a direct object and then a prepositional phrase:

| pattern | examples |
|---|---|
| *thank / blame / forgive sb **for** sth* | *Thank you **for** your help.* *They blamed me **for** the mistake.* |
| *blame sth **on** sb* | *They blamed the mistake **on** me.* |
| *accuse / remind / warn sb **of** sth* | *She accused him **of** lying.* *You remind me **of** my brother.* |
| *congratulate sb **on** sth* | *We congratulated her **on** her exam results.* |
| *protect / prevent / stop sb **from** sth* | *Sunscreen protects your skin **from** the sun.* |
| *provide sb **with** sth* / *provide sth **for** sb* | *The school provides students **with** books.* / *… provides books **for** students.* |
| *spend money / time **on** sth* | *I spend too much **on** clothes.* |
| *borrow sth **from** sb* | *Can I borrow a pen **from** you?* |
| *translate / divide / change sth **into** sth* | *Translate this **into** Chinese.* |
| *compare sth **with** / **to** sth* | *Compare this year's figures **with** last year's.* |
:::

::: proof
*Provide someone with something* and *provide something for someone* are standard everywhere; American English also often uses *provide something to someone*. *Compare with* and *compare to* overlap: traditionally *compare to* is used for a likeness (*She compared the city to a garden*) and *compare with* for a careful look at similarities and differences, a distinction found in usage guides but not always kept by writers. These patterns follow Swan (2016) and the learner's dictionaries.
:::

## Prepositions at the end

::: theorem The preposition in questions, relative clauses and the passive {#rule-stranding}
When the prepositional object is moved — to make a question, a relative clause or a passive — the preposition normally **stays with the verb**, at the end of the clause:

- Question: *Who are you waiting **for**?* *What are you looking **at**?* *What does it depend **on**?*
- Relative clause: *That's the man I was talking **to**.* *Is this the job you applied **for**?*
- Passive: *The children are well looked **after**.* *This bed has been slept **in**.*

In formal writing, the preposition can go before *whom* or *which* in questions and relative clauses: ***To whom** should I address the letter?* *the job **for which** you applied*. This is a choice of style, not a rule of correctness.
:::

::: proof
The idea that a sentence must not end with a preposition is not a rule of English grammar. It is usually traced to the poet John Dryden, who in 1672 criticised a line of Ben Jonson's for ending with one; eighteenth-century grammarians repeated the point, although Robert Lowth (1762) himself wrote that ending with a preposition was an idiom "which our language is strongly inclined to" and fitting in familiar style. All modern reference grammars (Quirk et al. 1985; Huddleston & Pullum 2002; Swan 2016) describe the preposition at the end as normal, and in conversation it is the only natural choice: ✗ *For what are you waiting?* sounds absurd. The fronted preposition (*to whom*, *in which*) is used in formal writing, especially in relative clauses.
:::

::: example Questions with prepositions {#ex-questions}
Make a question about the part in bold, keeping the preposition at the end. (a) *She's worried about **her exams**.* (b) *This phone belongs to **Wang Fang**.* (c) *They were laughing at **the dog**.* (d) *He applied for **a job in a bank**.* (e) *The success of the plan depends on **the weather**.*
::: solution
Replace the bold part with a question word (*what*, *who*), put it first, and leave the preposition after the verb.

(a) *What is she worried **about**?*

(b) *Who does this phone belong **to**?* (formal: *To whom does this phone belong?*)

(c) *What were they laughing **at**?*

(d) *What did he apply **for**?*

(e) *What does the success of the plan depend **on**?*
:::
:::

## Adjectives and nouns with prepositions

Prepositions also follow many adjectives and nouns, and these, too, are learned in pairs.

::: theorem Adjective + preposition {#rule-adj-prep}
| preposition | adjectives | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *about* | *worried*, *anxious*, *excited*, *happy*, *sorry*, *upset* | *I'm **excited about** the trip.* |
| *at* | *good*, *bad*, *brilliant*, *surprised* (also *by*) | *She's **good at** maths.* |
| *for* | *famous*, *responsible*, *late*, *ready*, *grateful* (for a thing) | *Hangzhou is **famous for** its lake.* |
| *in* | *interested*, *involved*, *experienced* | *Are you **interested in** art?* |
| *of* | *afraid*, *proud*, *fond*, *aware*, *capable*, *full*, *tired* | *I'm **proud of** you.* *Are you **aware of** the risks?* |
| *on* | *keen* (BrE), *dependent*, *based* | *He's **keen on** football.* |
| *to* | *similar*, *married*, *kind*, *polite*, *used*, *close* | *This is **similar to** that.* *She's **married to** a pilot.* |
| *with* | *angry* / *annoyed* (with a person), *satisfied*, *bored*, *familiar*, *pleased*, *busy* | *I was **angry with** him.* *Are you **satisfied with** the result?* |

Varieties and registers differ in a few cases:

- *different **from*** (BrE and AmE), *different **to*** (BrE, informal), *different **than*** (AmE). All three are standard in their variety; *different from* is accepted everywhere.
- *angry **with** someone* (BrE) / *angry **at** someone* (AmE, and increasingly BrE); *angry **about** something*.
- *bored **with*** (standard) and *bored **of*** (informal, increasingly common, especially in BrE).
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct in both British and American English?
- [ ] *My new school is very different to my old one.*
- [ ] *My new school is very different than my old one.*
- [x] *My new school is very different from my old one.*
- [ ] *My new school is very different with my old one.*
::: solution
*Different from* is standard in both varieties. *Different to* is common in British English and *different than* in American English; both are correct in their own variety but may be noticed in the other. *Different with* is not used.
:::
:::

::: theorem Noun + preposition {#rule-noun-prep}
| preposition | nouns | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *for* | *reason*, *demand*, *need*, *request*, *respect*, *cure* | *What's the **reason for** the delay?* |
| *of* | *cause*, *advantage*, *example*, *knowledge*, *lack* | *What's the **cause of** the problem?* |
| *on* | *effect*, *influence*, *impact*, *tax*, *ban* | *Social media has a big **effect on** young people.* |
| *in* | *increase*, *rise*, *fall*, *decrease*, *change*, *interest* | *There has been an **increase in** prices.* |
| *to* | *solution*, *answer*, *key*, *damage*, *access*, *invitation*, *reaction*, *attitude* | *Is there a **solution to** this problem?* |
| *with* / *between* | *relationship*, *contact*, *difference* (*between*), *problem* (*with*) | *What's the **difference between** them?* *There's a **problem with** my phone.* |
:::

::: proof
Nouns often keep the preposition of the related verb or adjective (*depend on* → *dependence on*; *interested in* → *interest in*; *reply to* → *a reply to*), but not always: *discuss* has none and *a discussion about* has one; *solve* has none and *a solution **to*** has one. Notice *an increase **in** prices* (the thing that increases) but *an increase **of** 10%* (the amount). These descriptions follow Swan (2016), Carter and McCarthy (2006) and the learner's dictionaries, which give the preposition in the entry for each noun.
:::

::: example Correcting a paragraph {#ex-paragraph}
Find and correct the six mistakes with prepositions in this paragraph from a student essay. *Many young people are interested about online shopping. It depends of the internet, and the reason of its popularity is convenience. However, it has a bad effect to small shops, and some people worry for this. We should discuss about how to protect them.*
::: solution
1. *interested about* → *interested **in*** (adjective + *in*).
2. *depends of* → *depends **on***.
3. *the reason of* → *the reason **for***.
4. *effect to* → *effect **on***.
5. *worry for this* → *worry **about** this*.
6. *discuss about* → *discuss* (no preposition).

Corrected: *Many young people are interested in online shopping. It depends on the internet, and the reason for its popularity is convenience. However, it has a bad effect on small shops, and some people worry about this. We should discuss how to protect them.*
:::
:::

::: example From Chinese prepositions to English ones {#ex-chinese}
Translate into English, paying attention to the position of the preposition. (a) 他对音乐很感兴趣。 (b) 我们为这次延误道歉。 (c) 这取决于天气。 (d) 我在等你。 (e) 她向经理抱怨了噪音。
::: solution
1. Find the English verb or adjective.
2. Put its own preposition **after** it, followed by the object; do not copy the Chinese preposition in front.

(a) *He's very interested **in** music.* — 对……感兴趣 → *interested in* (not ✗ *to music interested*).

(b) *We apologise **for** the delay.* — 为……道歉 → *apologise for*.

(c) *It depends **on** the weather.* — 取决于 has 于, but English needs *on*, not *of* or *at*.

(d) *I'm waiting **for** you.* — 等 takes a direct object in Chinese; *wait* needs *for*.

(e) *She complained **to** the manager **about** the noise.* — 向 + person → *to*; the thing complained about takes *about*.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Before or after the verb.** Chinese prepositions (介词, many of them developed from verbs — Li & Thompson 1981 call them coverbs) usually come **before** the verb: 对历史**感兴趣**, 跟他**结婚**, 为迟到**道歉**, 向经理**抱怨**. English prepositions come **after** the verb or adjective: *interested **in** history*, *apologise **for** being late*, *complain **to** the manager*. And sometimes there is no preposition in English where Chinese has one: 跟他结婚 → *marry him*.
- **Chinese verbs with direct objects, English verbs with prepositions.** 等车, 听音乐, 看我, 依靠父母, 回复邮件 have direct objects in Chinese; English says *wait **for** the bus*, *listen **to** music*, *look **at** me*, *depend **on** my parents*, *reply **to** an email*. These are among the most frequent errors of Chinese learners.
- **关于 and about.** 讨论关于环境的问题 and 提到关于…… lead to ✗ *discuss about* and ✗ *mention about*. The English verbs *discuss* and *mention* already contain the idea of ‘about’.
- **Particles at the end.** Chinese never leaves a preposition at the end of a sentence without its object (你在等**谁**？ keeps 谁 after 等). English questions like *Who are you waiting for?* therefore feel strange to learners at first, and they sometimes drop the preposition: ✗ *Who are you waiting?* Keep it.
:::

::: history
Prepositions at the end of a clause are old in English: they are found in Old English and in Chaucer, Shakespeare and the King James Bible. The objection to them is much younger. In 1672 John Dryden, in an essay on the dramatic poetry of the previous age, criticised Ben Jonson for ending a phrase with a preposition, apparently because Latin does not allow it; Dryden later revised his own prose to avoid the construction. In his *Short Introduction to English Grammar* (1762) Robert Lowth noted that the construction was common and suited to familiar speech and writing, but preferred the preposition before the relative pronoun in a solemn style. School grammars turned that preference into a rule. Modern reference grammars, from Quirk et al. (1985) to Huddleston and Pullum (2002), treat the end position as normal English.
:::

## Where this leads

Prepositional verbs in the passive (*The children were looked after*) are part of [[verb-patterns/the-passive]]. Relative clauses with prepositions (*the house in which I grew up* / *the house I grew up in*) are covered in [[complex-sentences/relative-clauses]]. Prepositions of time and place (*at six*, *in Beijing*) were introduced in [[core-grammar/prepositions-time-place]], and the collocations of verbs, adjectives and nouns more generally are the subject of [[collocation-and-register/verb-noun-adjective-noun]].

::: summary
- A prepositional verb takes a fixed preposition before its object: *depend on*, *listen to*, *wait for*, *consist of* ([[#def-prepositional]]).
- Some prepositions have core meanings (*about* a topic, *at* a target, *for* something sought, *from* a source), but many combinations must be learned with the verb ([[#rule-common]]).
- Some verbs change meaning with the preposition: *think about* / *of*, *hear about* / *of* / *from*, *agree with* / *to* / *on*, *look at* / *for* / *after* ([[#rule-pairs]]).
- *Discuss*, *enter*, *reach*, *marry*, *contact*, *emphasise* and *mention* take no preposition, though their nouns may (*a discussion about*).
- Verbs like *thank sb for*, *accuse sb of*, *remind sb of*, *provide sb with* take an object and a preposition ([[#rule-vop]]).
- In questions, relative clauses and the passive, the preposition usually stays at the end: *Who are you waiting for?* ([[#rule-stranding]]); "never end with a preposition" is not a rule.
- Adjectives and nouns take prepositions too: *interested in*, *proud of*, *the reason for*, *an effect on*; *different from* / *to* (BrE) / *than* (AmE) ([[#rule-adj-prep]], [[#rule-noun-prep]]).
:::
