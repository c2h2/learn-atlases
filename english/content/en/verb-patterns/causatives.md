A Chinese student comes to class with a new haircut and says, *I cut my hair yesterday.* Her teacher asks, surprised, *By yourself?* In English, *I cut my hair* means that I held the scissors. If someone else did it, English says:

- *I **had** my hair **cut** yesterday.*
- *I **got** my hair **cut** yesterday.*

Chinese 我昨天去剪头发了 makes no such difference, and that is only one of the meanings that English expresses with **causative** verbs — verbs for making someone do something, letting them do it, or arranging for it to be done. The four most common are *make*, *let*, *have* and *get*. They are short and frequent, and each has its own pattern:

| verb | pattern | example |
|---|---|---|
| *make* | *make* + object + **bare infinitive** | *She made me **wait**.* |
| *let* | *let* + object + **bare infinitive** | *She let me **go**.* |
| *have* | *have* + object + **bare infinitive** | *I'll have someone **call** you.* |
| *get* | *get* + object + ***to*-infinitive** | *I got him **to help** me.* |

This lesson builds on [[verb-patterns/object-and-verb]].

::: definition Causative verb {#def-causative}
A **causative verb** is a verb whose subject causes another person or thing to do something or to be in some state. In *The teacher made us rewrite the essay*, the teacher is the cause and *we* did the rewriting. In *I had my car repaired*, I am the cause and someone else did the repairing.
:::

## *Make*: forcing and causing

::: theorem *Make* + object + bare infinitive {#rule-make}
*Make* + object + bare infinitive means:

1. **force** someone to do something, often against their wishes: *My parents **made me practise** the piano every day.* *The police **made him open** his bag.*
2. **cause** something to happen, without any intention: *Onions **make my eyes water**.* *That film **made me cry**.* *What **makes the sky look** blue?*

In the **passive**, *make* is followed by a *to*-infinitive: *He **was made to open** his bag.*
:::

::: proof
The bare infinitive after *make* (*made me cry*, not ✗ *made me to cry*) is described in all the reference grammars (Quirk et al. 1985; Swan 2016). The *to* that appears in the passive (*was made to wait*) is one of the few places where English keeps an old pattern: in earlier English, *make* was often followed by *to* in the active as well (see the historical note below). *Make* can also be followed by an object and an **adjective** or **noun**: *The news **made her happy**.* *They **made him captain**.* This is the SVOC pattern of [[core-grammar/sentence-patterns]], and an adjective, not an adverb, is used there: ✗ *made her happily*.
:::

::: warning *To* after active *make* and *let*
✗ *The teacher made us to stand up.* ✓ *The teacher made us stand up.*

✗ *My mother doesn't let me to stay out late.* ✓ *My mother doesn't let me stay out late.*

✗ *This song makes me to feel relaxed.* ✓ *This song makes me feel relaxed.* ✓ *This song makes me relaxed.*
:::

## *Let*: allowing

::: theorem *Let* + object + bare infinitive {#rule-let}
*Let* + object + bare infinitive means ‘allow’: *My boss **let me leave** early.* *Don't **let the dog sleep** on the bed.*

1. *Let* has **no passive** in this meaning. Use *be allowed to*: *I **was allowed to leave** early.* (✗ *I was let to leave early.*)
2. *Let* is often used for offers and requests: ***Let me help** you.* ***Let me know** if you need anything.*
3. *Let's* (= *let us*) makes a suggestion that includes the speaker: ***Let's go** home.* The negative is *Let's not …* (in British English also *Don't let's …*, informal).
:::

::: proof
*Let* and *allow* have the same meaning but different patterns: *let* + object + bare infinitive, *allow* + object + *to*-infinitive (*They let us take photos* = *They allowed us to take photos*). *Let* is more informal; *allow* and *permit* are more formal, and *permit* is mostly used in rules and notices. In *let's* the object is fixed, and the meaning has changed from permission to suggestion: *Let's go* does not ask anyone for permission. *Let go of something* (‘stop holding’) and *let someone down* (‘disappoint’) are fixed phrases.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *We weren't let to use dictionaries in the exam.*
- [x] *We weren't allowed to use dictionaries in the exam.*
- [ ] *They didn't let us to use dictionaries.*
- [ ] *They didn't allow us use dictionaries.*
::: solution
*Let* has no passive, so the passive uses *be allowed to*. In the active, *let* takes the bare infinitive (*They didn't let us use*) and *allow* takes *to* (*They didn't allow us to use*).
:::
:::

::: example *Make* or *let*? {#ex-make-let}
Complete with *make* or *let* in the right form, and say which meaning you chose. (a) *The guard ___ us show our tickets before we went in.* (b) *Would you ___ me use your phone for a minute?* (c) *Cutting onions always ___ me cry.* (d) *Our teacher ___ us leave early because it was snowing.* (e) *Please don't ___ me eat that — I hate mushrooms!*
::: solution
Ask: did the person **want** to do it? If not, or if it just happened, use *make*; if they wanted to and were allowed, use *let*.

(a) *made* — we had to show them: forcing.

(b) *let* — I want to use it; I'm asking for permission.

(c) *makes* — causing, with no intention.

(d) *let* — we were allowed to leave.

(e) *make* — I don't want to eat it: forcing.
:::
:::

## *Have* and *get*: arranging and persuading

::: theorem *Have* + object + bare infinitive and *get* + object + *to*-infinitive {#rule-have-get}
1. ***Have* + object + bare infinitive** means ‘arrange for someone to do something’, usually someone whose job it is: *I'll **have the receptionist call** you a taxi.* *The doctor **had me lie down** on the bed.* This is common in American English and in formal or business English everywhere.
2. ***Get* + object + *to*-infinitive** means ‘persuade or manage to make someone do something’: *I finally **got my brother to help** me.* *Can you **get the printer to work**?* It is informal and very common in both British and American English.
:::

::: proof
The difference is one of effort. With *have*, the person is expected to do what is asked: a manager has an assistant do something, a customer has a waiter bring the bill. With *get*, there is some difficulty or persuasion: you *get* a child to eat vegetables, or *get* an old machine to start. Swan (2016) describes *have someone do* as more common in American than in British English; British speakers more often say *get someone to do* or *ask someone to do*. Notice that *get* is the only one of the four with *to*: ✗ *I got him help me*. *Have someone doing* is also possible, meaning ‘cause them to be doing’: *The teacher **had us all laughing**.* *In a month, I'll **have you speaking** basic English.*
:::

| verb | the person does the action because … | register |
|---|---|---|
| *make* (+ bare infinitive) | they are forced, or it just happens | neutral |
| *let* (+ bare infinitive) | they want to and are allowed | informal to neutral |
| *have* (+ bare infinitive) | it is arranged; often their job | neutral, more AmE |
| *get* (+ *to*) | they are persuaded | informal |

::: example Who does what in a dialogue {#ex-dialogue}
Read the dialogue and say, for each causative, who does the action and why that verb is used.

**Receptionist:** *Good morning. Can I help you?*

**Wang Fang:** *Yes, my laptop won't start. My son tried to fix it, but I made him stop before he broke it completely.*

**Receptionist:** *I'll have a technician look at it this afternoon. Let me take your phone number.*

**Wang Fang:** *Thanks. Could you get him to call me before he changes anything?*
::: solution
1. *made him stop* — the son stopped, because Wang Fang forced him: *make* + object + bare infinitive.
2. *I'll have a technician look at it* — the technician will look, because the receptionist arranges it and it is his job: *have* + object + bare infinitive.
3. *Let me take your phone number* — the receptionist will take it; *let me* is a polite offer, literally asking permission.
4. *get him to call me* — the technician should call; Wang Fang asks the receptionist to persuade or ask him: *get* + object + *to*-infinitive.
:::
:::

## *Have something done*

The most useful causative pattern for everyday life has a thing as its object and a past participle after it.

::: theorem *Have* / *get* + object + past participle {#rule-have-done}
***Have* + object + past participle** means that someone else does something to the object for you, usually a service that you pay for:

- *I **had my hair cut** yesterday.* (a hairdresser cut it)
- *We're **having the kitchen painted**.*
- *You should **have your eyes tested**.*

***Get* + object + past participle** has the same meaning and is more informal and more frequent in conversation: *I need to **get my phone repaired**.* *Where did you **get your photos printed**?*

The tense is shown on *have* or *get*: *I **have** my car **serviced** every year.* *I'**m having** my car **serviced** today.* *I'**ve** just **had** my car **serviced**.* *I'**ll get** it **done** tomorrow.*
:::

::: proof
The order matters. *I had my hair cut* (object before participle) means someone cut it for me; *I had cut my hair* (participle after *had*) is the past perfect and means that I cut it myself, before some other past time. The object is the thing affected, and the participle has a passive meaning: *my hair* was cut. If you need to say who did it, add *by*: *I had my portrait painted **by** a street artist.* The pattern is described in this way by Quirk et al. (1985) and Swan (2016), and it is taught in every learner's grammar (Murphy 2019).
:::

::: warning Doing it yourself or having it done
✗ *I cut my hair at the new hairdresser's.* (sounds as if you used their scissors yourself) ✓ *I had my hair cut at the new hairdresser's.*

✗ *I'm going to repair my car tomorrow.* (if a mechanic will do it) ✓ *I'm going to have my car repaired tomorrow.*

✗ *I had cut my hair.* (past perfect: you did it) ✓ *I had my hair cut.*

In informal speech, native speakers do sometimes say *I'm going to cut my hair* for a visit to the hairdresser, when the meaning is obvious; but *have* / *get* + object + past participle is the clear form.
:::

### Things that happen to us

The same pattern is used for something unpleasant that someone else does to the subject, with no arranging at all:

- *She **had her phone stolen** on the metro.*
- *He **had his licence taken away** for speeding.*
- *We **got our flight cancelled**.*

This is often the most natural English for Chinese 被 sentences about a person's possessions: 她的手机在地铁上被偷了 / 她在地铁上被偷了手机 → *She had her phone stolen on the metro.* (or *Her phone was stolen on the metro.*)

### *Get something done*: finishing

*Get* + object + past participle can also mean that the subject finishes a job, often by doing it themselves: *I need to **get this report finished** by Friday.* *Let's **get the dishes done** before the film starts.* The context shows which meaning is meant.

::: example At the hairdresser's and the garage {#ex-services}
Rewrite each sentence with *have* or *get* + object + past participle, keeping the tense. (a) *A mechanic is checking the brakes on my car.* (b) *Someone cleaned our windows last week.* (c) *A photographer will take our wedding photos.* (d) *A dentist has just taken out one of my teeth.* (e) *A thief stole Li Ming's bike from outside the library.*
::: solution
1. The subject is the person who benefits (or suffers).
2. Put the tense on *have* or *get*.
3. The thing affected is the object; the action becomes a past participle.

(a) *I'm having the brakes on my car checked.* (or *I'm getting the brakes checked*)

(b) *We had our windows cleaned last week.*

(c) *We'll have our wedding photos taken by a photographer.* (*by a photographer* can stay, because it is new information)

(d) *I've just had one of my teeth taken out.*

(e) *Li Ming had his bike stolen from outside the library.* — an unpleasant experience, not a service. *Li Ming's bike was stolen …* is also natural.
:::
:::

::: quiz
*My laptop is broken, so I'm going to ___ .* Which is correct?
- [ ] *have repaired it*
- [x] *have it repaired*
- [ ] *have it repair*
- [ ] *have repair it*
::: solution
*Have* + object + past participle: *have it repaired*. *Have repaired it* would be a perfect infinitive (‘to have repaired it myself’), and *have it repair* uses the base form, which would mean that the laptop does the repairing.
:::
:::

## Other ways to cause

| verb | pattern | use |
|---|---|---|
| *cause* | *cause* + object + *to*-infinitive | formal, often unexpected or unwanted results: *The storm **caused the river to flood**.* |
| *force*, *compel* | + object + *to*-infinitive | strong, often official pressure: *The strike **forced the company to close**.* |
| *allow*, *permit*, *enable* | + object + *to*-infinitive | formal permission or possibility: *The grant **enabled her to study** abroad.* |
| *keep* | + object + adjective / *-ing* | make someone stay in a state: *The noise **kept me awake**.* *Sorry to **keep you waiting**.* |
| *help* | + object + (*to*) infinitive | *She **helped me (to) find** a flat.* |

*Make* is the everyday verb for causing a feeling or reaction (*It made me laugh*); *cause* is used in more formal writing, especially for physical or technical results (*Heavy rain caused the road to collapse*). ✗ *It caused me to laugh* is grammatical but sounds odd in conversation.

::: example Choosing the verb for the meaning {#ex-choose}
Choose the best verb — *make*, *let*, *have*, *get* or *cause* — and complete each sentence correctly. (a) My little sister didn't want to go to bed, but I finally persuaded her: *I finally ___ her ___ to bed.* (b) In a hotel: *I'll ___ the porter ___ your bags up to your room.* (c) A scientific report: *The high temperature ___ the metal ___ .* (expand) (d) Your father allowed you to drive his car: *My father ___ me ___ his car.*
::: solution
(a) *got … to go* — persuasion: *I finally got her to go to bed.*

(b) *have … take* (or *bring*) — arranged; it is the porter's job: *I'll have the porter take your bags up to your room.* (*get the porter to take* is also possible, more informal.)

(c) *caused … to expand* — a formal, physical result: *The high temperature caused the metal to expand.* (*made the metal expand* is also correct, less formal.)

(d) *let … drive* — permission: *My father let me drive his car.*
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **让 and its English equivalents.** 让 covers permission (*let*), causing (*make*), arranging (*have*) and wanting (*want … to*). 他让我很生气 is *He **made** me very angry*, not ✗ *He let me very angry*; 这首歌让我想起家乡 is *This song **makes** me think of home* or *reminds me of home*; 妈妈不让我出去 is *Mum doesn't **let** me go out*. English *let* is only about permission.
- **使 and 令.** 使 and 令 (这个消息令人失望, 使他明白) are more formal than 让, and their usual English equivalents are *make* or an adjective: *The news was disappointing.* *It made him understand.* Writers sometimes translate 令人 literally with *make people*: ✗ *The result makes people disappointed.* An adjective is more natural: *The result was disappointing.*
- **Having things done.** Chinese usually does not mark whether you did something yourself or had it done: 我修了车 and 我去修车 can describe a visit to a garage. English needs *have* / *get something done* when someone else did it.
- **被 and possessions.** 我的钱包被偷了 and 我被偷了钱包 correspond to *My wallet was stolen* and *I had my wallet stolen*. Li & Thompson (1981) note that in 被 sentences of this kind the subject is the person affected and the stolen thing remains after the verb, much as in the English *have* pattern.
:::

::: history
In earlier English, *make* was often followed by *to* in the active as well as the passive. The King James Bible (1611) has *He maketh me to lie down in green pastures* (Psalm 23:2); a modern speaker would say *He makes me lie down*. The *to* disappeared from active *make* over the following centuries but survives in the passive (*I was made to wait*). *Let* has taken the bare infinitive since Old English. *Have* and *get* with a past participle (*have something done*, *get something done*) are described as a pair in modern grammars such as Quirk et al. (1985), and corpus grammars note that the *get* version is especially common in conversation (Biber et al. 1999).
:::

## Where this leads

The passive meaning of the past participle in *have something done* is explained in [[verb-patterns/the-passive]], which also looks at the *get*-passive (*My bike got stolen*). Verbs that take object + verb more generally are in [[verb-patterns/object-and-verb]], and the ways English makes polite requests (*Could you…?*, *Would you mind…?*) are part of [[pragmatics/requests-refusals]].

::: summary
- Causative verbs show that the subject causes someone to do something or something to happen ([[#def-causative]]).
- *Make* + object + bare infinitive = force or cause (*made me wait*, *made me laugh*); passive *be made **to** do* ([[#rule-make]]).
- *Let* + object + bare infinitive = allow (*let me go*); no passive: use *be allowed to* ([[#rule-let]]).
- *Have* + object + bare infinitive = arrange, often someone's job (*have him call you*); *get* + object + ***to*** = persuade (*got him to help*) ([[#rule-have-get]]).
- *Have* / *get* + object + past participle = someone else does it for you (*I had my hair cut*), or something unpleasant happens to you (*She had her phone stolen*) ([[#rule-have-done]]).
- English *let* is only permission; Chinese 让 can also mean *make*, *have* or *want … to*.
:::
