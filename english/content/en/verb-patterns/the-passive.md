Here are three English sentences and the most natural Chinese for each:

- *This bridge **was built** in 1957.* — 这座桥是1957年建的。
- *English **is spoken** in many countries.* — 很多国家都说英语。
- *My bike **was stolen** last night.* — 我的自行车昨晚被偷了。

All three English sentences are passive. Only one of the Chinese sentences uses 被. English uses the passive often and neutrally, for all kinds of events; Chinese uses 被 much less, traditionally for events that are unpleasant for someone, and expresses most English passives in other ways. For a Chinese learner this causes two opposite problems: avoiding the English passive where it is natural, and building wrong passives from verbs that do not have one (✗ *The accident was happened*).

This lesson shows how the passive is formed in every tense, when English chooses it, the *get*-passive, the reporting passive *It is said that…*, and how English passives relate to Chinese 被, 把 and sentences with no marker at all.

## Form

::: definition Active and passive {#def-passive}
In an **active** clause the subject is usually the doer (the **agent**) of the action: ***The company** built the bridge.* In a **passive** clause the subject is the person or thing that the action is done to, and the verb is ***be* + past participle**: ***The bridge** was built by the company.*

The object of the active clause becomes the subject of the passive. The agent can be added in a ***by*-phrase**, or, much more often, left out.
:::

::: theorem The passive in each tense {#rule-form}
The passive keeps the tense and aspect of the active verb. Only *be* changes; the main verb is always the past participle.

| tense or form | active | passive |
|---|---|---|
| present simple | *They **make** cars here.* | *Cars **are made** here.* |
| present progressive | *They **are repairing** the road.* | *The road **is being repaired**.* |
| present perfect | *Someone **has stolen** my bike.* | *My bike **has been stolen**.* |
| past simple | *They **built** it in 1957.* | *It **was built** in 1957.* |
| past progressive | *They **were interviewing** her.* | *She **was being interviewed**.* |
| past perfect | *They **had sold** the house.* | *The house **had been sold**.* |
| *will* | *They **will announce** the results.* | *The results **will be announced**.* |
| *be going to* | *They **are going to close** the shop.* | *The shop **is going to be closed**.* |
| modal | *You **must sign** the form.* | *The form **must be signed**.* |
| modal perfect | *Someone **should have told** us.* | *We **should have been told**.* |
| *to*-infinitive | *I want **someone to call** me.* | *I want **to be called**.* |
| *-ing* form | *I hate **people watching** me.* | *I hate **being watched**.* |

The perfect progressive passive (*has been being repaired*) is grammatical but rare; English usually avoids it.
:::

::: proof
*Be* carries everything that the active verb carried — tense (*is* / *was*), aspect (*being*, *been*) and agreement with the new subject (*The car **is** made* / *Cars **are** made*). The past participle carries the meaning of the verb. This is why two things go wrong most often: *be* is left out (✗ *The bridge built in 1957*), or the wrong form of the main verb is used (✗ *was build*, ✗ *has been stole*). For irregular past participles (*written*, *stolen*, *given*, *built*) see [[core-grammar/past-simple]]. The description follows Quirk et al. (1985) and every learner's grammar (Swan 2016; Murphy 2019).
:::

::: warning Missing *be* or the wrong verb form
✗ *The museum built in 1890.* ✓ *The museum **was** built in 1890.*

✗ *Rice is grow in the south.* ✓ *Rice is **grown** in the south.*

✗ *The letters have been wrote.* ✓ *The letters have been **written**.*

✗ *The road is repairing.* ✓ *The road is **being repaired**.*

The last one is a common error for 路正在修: English needs *being* + past participle.
:::

::: example Active to passive {#ex-form}
Rewrite in the passive, leaving out the agent if it is not needed. (a) *People speak Portuguese in Brazil.* (b) *The police are questioning two men.* (c) *Someone has cleaned the windows.* (d) *They will deliver the sofa on Monday.* (e) *You can't use this lift.*
::: solution
1. Find the object of the active verb: it becomes the subject.
2. Keep the tense; put it on *be*; use the past participle of the main verb.
3. Ask whether the agent is worth mentioning.

(a) *Portuguese is spoken in Brazil.* — *people* is obvious: leave it out.

(b) *Two men are being questioned (by the police).* — *by the police* can stay if it is new information.

(c) *The windows have been cleaned.* — *someone* is unknown: leave it out.

(d) *The sofa will be delivered on Monday.*

(e) *This lift can't be used.* — modal + *be* + past participle.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *The new hospital will be open by the mayor next week.*
- [x] *The new hospital will be opened by the mayor next week.*
- [ ] *The new hospital will opened by the mayor next week.*
- [ ] *The new hospital will be opening by the mayor next week.*
::: solution
*Will* + *be* + past participle: *will be opened*. *Open* is an adjective (*The shop is open*), *will opened* has no *be*, and *will be opening* is active progressive (the hospital would do the opening).
:::
:::

## Which verbs have a passive

::: theorem Verbs that can and cannot be passive {#rule-which}
1. Only verbs with an object can be passive. Intransitive verbs — *happen*, *occur*, *arrive*, *die*, *exist*, *appear*, *disappear*, *rise* — have **no passive** ([[verb-patterns/transitive-intransitive#rule-no-object]]).
2. A few transitive verbs that describe states are not used in the passive: *have* (‘own’), *lack*, *resemble*, *suit*, *fit* (‘be the right size for’), *cost*, *weigh* (‘have the weight’): *She has a car* (✗ *A car is had by her*). *This dress doesn't suit me* (✗ *I am not suited by this dress*).
3. Verbs with two objects usually make the **person** the subject: *I **was given** a key.* ([[verb-patterns/two-objects#rule-two-object-passive]])
4. Prepositional and phrasal verbs keep the preposition or particle after the verb: *The children **are looked after** by their grandmother.* *The meeting **has been called off**.*
5. Verbs with object + infinitive: the infinitive follows with *to*, even after *make*, *see* and *hear*: *She **was asked to wait**.* *He **was made to apologise**.* *They **were seen to leave**.* (*Let* has no passive: *be allowed to*.)
:::

::: warning Passives of intransitive verbs
✗ *A strange thing was happened.* ✓ *A strange thing happened.*

✗ *The price is risen by 10%.* ✓ *The price has risen by 10%.* ✓ *The price has been raised by 10%.* (*raise* is transitive)

✗ *The dinosaurs were disappeared.* ✓ *The dinosaurs disappeared.*
:::

::: example Passives of special verbs {#ex-special}
Rewrite in the passive where possible; if a sentence has no passive, say why. (a) *Their grandmother looked after the children.* (b) *The teacher made him rewrite the essay.* (c) *Someone saw her leave the building.* (d) *They have called off the match.* (e) *Li Na has a large flat.* (f) *Something strange happened.*
::: solution
(a) *The children were looked after by their grandmother.* — the preposition stays with the verb.

(b) *He was made to rewrite the essay.* — after passive *make*, the infinitive takes *to*.

(c) *She was seen to leave the building.* (formal) or *She was seen leaving the building.* — *to* again after a passive verb of perception.

(d) *The match has been called off.* — the particle stays after the participle.

(e) No passive: *have* meaning ‘own’ is a state verb that is not used in the passive.

(f) No passive: *happen* is intransitive.
:::
:::

## When English uses the passive

::: theorem Reasons for choosing the passive {#rule-use}
English uses the passive mainly when:

1. **the agent is unknown, obvious or unimportant**: *My phone **has been stolen**.* *The windows **are cleaned** every week.* *Rice **is grown** in the south.*
2. **the thing affected is the topic**, so it is natural to keep it as the subject, with new information at the end: *The Great Wall is very old. Parts of it **were built** more than 2,000 years ago.*
3. **the agent is new or important**, so it is put at the end in a *by*-phrase: *This painting **was painted by** Qi Baishi.*
4. **the style is formal or impersonal**: scientific writing, reports, news and instructions (*The mixture **is heated** to 80°C.* *Passengers **are requested** to remain seated.*).
5. **the writer prefers not to say who did something**: *Mistakes **were made**.*
:::

::: proof
Biber et al. (1999) found that the passive is a feature of writing much more than of speech: it is most common in academic prose, where roughly a quarter of finite verbs are passive, less common in news, and rare in conversation, where well under one verb in twenty is passive. In every register the great majority of passives have no *by*-phrase, which shows that reason 1 is the commonest: the passive is used above all to leave the agent out. Reason 2 is the principle of keeping known information first and new information last (see [[verb-patterns/two-objects#rule-end-focus]]). Style guides sometimes tell writers to "avoid the passive"; this is advice against vague or wordy passives, not a rule of grammar, and it does not fit scientific writing, where the passive is normal (Swan 2016; Huddleston & Pullum 2002).
:::

::: example Active or passive in a text? {#ex-choose}
Choose the better form (active or passive) for each verb in brackets, so that the text flows. *The Forbidden City is in the centre of Beijing. It (a) (build) between 1406 and 1420. For almost 500 years it (b) (be) the home of the emperors. Today it is a museum, and every year it (c) (visit) by millions of people. Visitors (d) (need) to book tickets in advance.*
::: solution
Look at what each sentence is about (its topic) and keep that as the subject.

(a) *was built* — the topic is the Forbidden City; the builders are not important. Passive, no *by*-phrase.

(b) *was* — *be* is not passive; active, past simple.

(c) *is visited* — the topic is still the Forbidden City; *by millions of people* is new and goes at the end. Passive with *by*. (*Millions of people visit it every year* is also correct, but it changes the topic of the sentence.)

(d) *need* — the topic changes to the visitors, who do the action. Active.
:::
:::

### The *by*-phrase

Most passives have no *by*-phrase. Include one only when the agent is new, important or surprising:

- ✓ *The window was broken by a ball from the football pitch.* (the cause is the point)
- ? *The window was broken by someone.* (adds nothing; *The window was broken* is enough)
- ? *English is spoken by people in many countries.* (*by people* is obvious)

::: quiz
Which *by*-phrase is most useful?
- [ ] *The thief was arrested by the police.*
- [ ] *The letters are delivered by a postman every morning.*
- [x] *The new library was designed by a 25-year-old student.*
- [ ] *Chinese is spoken by people in China.*
::: solution
That a student designed the library is new and surprising, so it belongs at the end of the sentence. In the others the agent is obvious (*the police* arrest thieves, a postman delivers letters, people in China speak Chinese) and the *by*-phrase can go.
:::
:::

## The *get*-passive

::: theorem *Get* + past participle {#rule-get}
In informal English, especially conversation, *get* can replace *be* in the passive:

- *My bike **got stolen** last week.*
- *Three people **got hurt** in the accident.*
- *She **got promoted** after a year.*
- *Did you **get paid**?*

The *get*-passive describes an **event**, not a state, and it is used especially for things that happen unexpectedly and are bad or good for the subject. It is not used with verbs of thinking or knowing: *It is known that …* (✗ *It gets known that …*).
:::

::: proof
Biber et al. (1999) found the *get*-passive almost only in conversation and very rarely in academic writing. Because *get* is dynamic, it can make the meaning clearer than *be*: *The door **was closed*** can describe a state (it wasn't open) or an event (someone closed it), but *The door **got closed*** can only be the event. Some *get* + participle combinations are fixed expressions rather than passives: *get married*, *get dressed*, *get lost*, *get changed*. Questions and negatives of the *get*-passive use *do*: *Did he get hurt?* *I didn't get invited.*
:::

## *It is said that …* and *He is said to …*

::: theorem The passive with reporting verbs {#rule-reporting}
With *say*, *think*, *believe*, *know*, *report*, *expect*, *consider* and similar verbs, English has two impersonal passive patterns, used in formal writing and news:

1. ***It* + passive + *that*-clause**: ***It is said that** the castle is haunted.* ***It is believed that** the fire started in the kitchen.*
2. **Subject + passive + *to*-infinitive**: *The castle **is said to be** haunted.* *The fire **is believed to have started** in the kitchen.* (*to have* + past participle for an earlier event)

Both mean ‘people say / believe …’ without naming who.
:::

::: proof
These patterns let a writer report an opinion or a piece of news without taking responsibility for it, which is why they are frequent in news reports and academic writing. In conversation, people more often say *They say …*, *People think …* or *Apparently, …*. Chinese uses 据说, 人们认为 and 一般认为 for the same purpose, so 据说这座城堡闹鬼 is naturally *The castle is said to be haunted* or *It is said that the castle is haunted*. This pattern is described in Swan (2016) and Murphy (2019); its use in academic hedging is taken further in [[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]].
:::

## The English passive and Chinese

English passives correspond to several different Chinese structures, and 被 is only one of them.

::: example Translating English passives into Chinese {#ex-chinese}
Give natural Chinese for each sentence, and say which Chinese structure you used. (a) *This bridge was built in 1957.* (b) *The letter has been sent.* (c) *My wallet was stolen on the bus.* (d) *The meeting is organised by the student union.* (e) *It is said that he speaks six languages.*
::: solution
(a) 这座桥是1957年建的。 — 是……的, which highlights the time of a past event. No 被.

(b) 信已经寄出去了。 — the thing affected is the subject, with no marker at all (a "notional passive"). No 被.

(c) 我的钱包在公共汽车上被偷了。 — 被, because the event is unfortunate for the speaker.

(d) 会议由学生会组织。 — 由 introduces the person responsible, in a neutral, formal style.

(e) 据说他会说六种语言。 — 据说, ‘it is said’.

Only one of the five uses 被. Going the other way, from Chinese to English, all five need an English passive.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **被 is not the English passive.** Mandarin 被 sentences traditionally describe events that are unfortunate or adverse for the subject (他被打了, 钱包被偷了); Li & Thompson (1981) describe this adversative meaning as basic. Under the influence of translation from European languages (what 王力 called 欧化, Europeanisation), 被 has spread to neutral and positive events (他被选为班长), but it is still used far less than the English passive. A Chinese sentence without 被 often corresponds to an English passive.
- **Sentences with no marker.** Chinese often makes the thing affected the subject with no marker at all: 信写好了, 饭做好了, 问题解决了, 房子卖了. 朱德熙 (1982) discusses these as sentences whose subject is the patient (受事主语). English needs a passive (*The letter has been written*, *The problem has been solved*, *The house has been sold*) or, for a few verbs, an intransitive use (*Dinner is ready*, *The door opened*).
- **是……的 and 由.** 这座桥是1957年建的 → *This bridge was built in 1957*. 由学校负责 → *is organised by the school*. Both are English passives.
- **把 is not a passive.** The 把 construction (他把杯子打破了) moves the object before the verb, but the agent is still the subject and the sentence is **active**: *He broke the cup*. Li & Thompson (1981) call it the disposal construction; it shows what the subject did to the object. Its English equivalent is an ordinary active SVO clause, with the object after the verb. Do not translate 把 sentences with English passives.
- **Learners' errors in both directions.** Because Chinese can describe what happened to a thing without 被, learners leave out *be*: ✗ *The problem solved*. Because some events feel "passive", they add *be* to intransitive verbs: ✗ *was happened*, ✗ *is disappeared*. And because 被 sounds negative, they avoid neutral English passives in formal writing where they would be natural.
:::

::: history
The passive with *be* is as old as English, but its progressive form is young. Until the late eighteenth century, English said *The house is building* to mean that it was being built. The form *is being built* appeared at the end of that century — an early example often quoted comes from a letter of the poet Robert Southey in the 1790s — and it spread during the nineteenth century, against the objections of some critics: the American writer Richard Grant White attacked it at length in *Words and Their Uses* (1870). Today *is being built* is the only normal form, and *The house is building* sounds old-fashioned. The *get*-passive is also relatively recent in its present frequency; corpus studies show it growing through the twentieth century, mainly in speech.
:::

## Where this leads

The passive with two-object verbs was introduced in [[verb-patterns/two-objects]], and *have something done*, which also has a passive meaning, in [[verb-patterns/causatives]]. Participle clauses with a passive meaning (*Built in 1957, the bridge…*) are part of [[complex-sentences/participle-infinitive-clauses]]. The passive in academic texts, and how to read it, is discussed in [[academic-reading/dense-sentences]], and the passive in English–Chinese translation in [[translation/structural-shifts]].

::: summary
- The passive is *be* + past participle; the active object becomes the subject, and the agent can follow *by* ([[#def-passive]]).
- Tense and aspect go on *be*: *is made*, *is being made*, *has been made*, *was made*, *will be made*, *must be made* ([[#rule-form]]).
- Only transitive verbs have a passive; *happen*, *occur*, *die*, *exist* and *disappear* do not, nor do *have* (‘own’), *resemble*, *suit* and *cost* ([[#rule-which]]).
- English uses the passive when the agent is unknown, obvious or unimportant, to keep the topic as subject, and in formal writing; most passives have no *by*-phrase ([[#rule-use]]).
- The *get*-passive (*got stolen*, *got hurt*) is informal and describes events ([[#rule-get]]).
- *It is said that …* and *X is said to …* report opinions impersonally ([[#rule-reporting]]).
- English passives often correspond to Chinese sentences without 被 (是……的, 由, patient-subject sentences, 据说); 被 is mainly adversative, and 把 sentences are active.
:::
