Here is a sentence from a textbook-style passage written for this lesson, followed by three attempts to use it in an essay:

> **Source:** *Because cities absorb and store the sun's heat in roads and buildings and release it slowly at night, urban areas often remain several degrees warmer than the surrounding countryside after dark.* (Okonkwo 2021, p. 47 — a fictional source)
>
> **A.** *Because cities take in and keep the sun's heat in streets and buildings and give it out slowly at night, urban areas frequently stay a few degrees hotter than the nearby countryside after dark (Okonkwo 2021).*
>
> **B.** *According to Okonkwo (2021), "urban areas often remain several degrees warmer than the surrounding countryside after dark" (p. 47).*
>
> **C.** *Night-time temperatures in cities are often several degrees higher than in nearby rural areas, Okonkwo (2021) explains, because heat taken in by roads and buildings during the day is only slowly released.*

Version A has a citation, and nearly every word has been changed. Yet it follows the source clause by clause and phrase by phrase, swapping words for synonyms: *absorb and store* → *take in and keep*, *remain* → *stay*, *often* → *frequently*. Most English-medium universities would treat it as **patchwriting**, a form of plagiarism, even though the student meant no harm. Version B quotes accurately, but there is no reason to quote: nothing about the exact wording matters. Version C is a **paraphrase**: the same meaning in a new structure and mostly new words, with the source acknowledged. It begins with the effect instead of the cause, uses a different main clause, and keeps only the technical vocabulary that cannot sensibly be changed.

This chapter explains the difference: what a paraphrase is, how to write one that is your own, when quotation is the better choice, how to quote accurately, and how to record sources as you read so that every idea can be traced. It completes the move from reading to writing begun in [[academic-reading/notes-and-summaries]].

## Paraphrase, quotation and patchwriting

::: definition Paraphrase and quotation {#def-paraphrase}
A **paraphrase** (释义, 转述) restates a specific passage of a source — usually a sentence or a short paragraph — in the writer's own words and sentence structure, at roughly the same level of detail, with the source cited. A **summary** restates a longer text more briefly ([[academic-reading/notes-and-summaries#def-summary]]). A **quotation** (直接引语, 引文) reproduces the source's exact words, marked with quotation marks (or set off as a block), with the source and usually the page number cited.
:::

::: definition Patchwriting {#def-patchwriting}
**Patchwriting** is writing that copies a source's sentence structure and much of its wording while changing some words, deleting some and substituting synonyms — "a paraphrase that stays too close to the source". The term was introduced by Rebecca Moore Howard (1993) and developed in later work (Howard 1995); Pecorari (2003) found it to be common in the writing of second-language postgraduate students, usually without any intention to deceive.
:::

Howard argued that patchwriting is a normal stage in learning to write in a new discourse: a writer who does not yet fully understand a source, or does not yet command the language of a field, leans on the source's words. That is a reason to treat it as something to learn out of, not as a moral failing. But it is still treated as plagiarism in English-medium academic settings when it appears in submitted work, because the reader is led to believe that the structure and wording are the writer's own. The cure is not better synonyms but a different method: understanding the passage well enough to write it from scratch.

::: warning Changing the words but keeping the structure
✗ *Because cities take in and keep the sun's heat in streets and buildings and give it out slowly at night, urban areas frequently stay a few degrees hotter …* (patchwriting: the source's sentence with synonyms)

✓ *Night-time temperatures in cities are often several degrees higher than in nearby rural areas, Okonkwo (2021) explains, because heat taken in by roads and buildings during the day is only slowly released.* (a paraphrase: new structure, new main clause, technical terms kept)

A citation does not make copied wording acceptable: it says where the **ideas** came from, not that the **words** are yours. And synonym swapping is risky in another way too: *hotter* is not quite *warmer*, and *a few* is not *several*. Substituting words one by one often changes the meaning slightly while keeping the structure — the worst of both.
:::

## How to paraphrase

::: theorem Writing a paraphrase {#rule-paraphrase}
1. **Understand** the passage: unpack its dense phrases ([[academic-reading/dense-sentences#rule-unpack]]), identify its claim and its stance, and look up anything unclear.
2. **Note** the key ideas in a few words, not in the source's sentences.
3. **Put the source away** and write from your notes, as if explaining the idea to someone.
4. **Change the structure**, not only the words: start from a different point, change the main clause, change word classes (noun ↔ verb), change active ↔ passive, split one sentence into two or combine two into one.
5. **Keep** technical terms, names, numbers and the source's degree of certainty.
6. **Cite** the source, with a page number if your style requires one for paraphrases.
7. **Compare** with the original: is the meaning the same, and is there no run of the source's words (more than a technical term or two) that you have kept by accident? If there is, rewrite it or quote it.
:::

::: proof
Step 3 is the heart of the method. A paraphrase written with the source in view tends to follow the source, because the eye goes back to it after each phrase; one written from notes, after a pause, has to be built from the writer's own understanding, and so takes the writer's own structure. Steps 1 and 2 make step 3 possible: you cannot write from understanding what you have not understood. The structural changes of step 4 are tools for revising a draft that is still too close, not a recipe for the first attempt — a paraphrase produced only by mechanical transformations of the source is still patchwriting. Step 5 matters because a paraphrase must be accurate: technical terms have no exact synonyms (*working memory* cannot become *operating recall*), and a paraphrase that loses the source's hedges misrepresents it ([[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]]).
:::

::: example Paraphrasing step by step {#ex-paraphrase}
Paraphrase this fictional source: *The introduction of free bus travel for people over sixty-five was followed by a marked increase in the number of trips made by older residents, particularly in areas with poor access to shops and health services.* (Lindqvist 2019, p. 112)
::: solution
**Step 1 — understand.** Unpack: *The introduction of free bus travel* = the city (probably) made buses free; *for people over sixty-five* = for older people; *was followed by* = afterwards (not necessarily caused by); *a marked increase in the number of trips made by older residents* = older people travelled much more; *particularly in areas with poor access …* = the increase was biggest where shops and doctors are hard to reach. Stance: *was followed by* is careful — sequence, not proven cause.

**Step 2 — notes.** *Free buses 65+ → older people's trips ↑ a lot; biggest ↑ where shops/health services far. (Sequence, not cause.)*

**Step 3 — write from notes.** *After buses became free for people over sixty-five, older residents travelled considerably more often, according to Lindqvist (2019), and the rise was greatest in neighbourhoods far from shops and health services.*

**Step 4 — check the structure.** The source has one sentence with a nominalised subject (*The introduction of free bus travel*) and a passive (*was followed by*). The paraphrase starts with a time clause (*After buses became free*), uses an active main clause with a human subject (*older residents travelled*), and adds a second main clause for the area effect.

**Step 5 — keep.** *over sixty-five* (a number), the stance (*After* keeps the sequence and does not claim a cause).

**Step 7 — compare.** Shared words: *over sixty-five*, *older residents*, *shops and health services* — a number and two plain descriptive phrases with no natural alternative. Acceptable; *older residents* could become *older people* to be safe.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which is the most acceptable paraphrase of *Regular physical activity has been shown to reduce the risk of developing type 2 diabetes* (a fictional source, Haddad 2020)?
- [ ] *Regular physical exercise has been demonstrated to lower the risk of getting type 2 diabetes (Haddad 2020).*
- [x] *People who exercise regularly are less likely to develop type 2 diabetes, as research summarised by Haddad (2020) shows.*
- [ ] *Physical activity prevents diabetes (Haddad 2020).*
- [ ] *Regular physical activity has been shown to reduce the risk of developing type 2 diabetes.*
::: solution
The second option has a new structure (people as subject, *less likely to*) and keeps the meaning, including the technical term *type 2 diabetes*. The first is patchwriting (synonyms in the source's structure). The third distorts the meaning (*prevents*, *diabetes* in general). The fourth copies the source without quotation marks or citation.
:::
:::

::: example Revising a paraphrase that is too close {#ex-revise}
A student wrote this paraphrase of the source in [[#ex-paraphrase]]. Revise it. *The start of free bus travel for over-65s was followed by a big rise in the number of journeys made by older people, especially in places with bad access to shops and health services (Lindqvist 2019).*
::: solution
**Diagnosis.** The sentence follows the source phrase by phrase: *The start of* ← *The introduction of*; *was followed by a big rise in the number of journeys made by* ← *was followed by a marked increase in the number of trips made by*; *especially in places with bad access to* ← *particularly in areas with poor access to*. Only single words have changed.

**Revision with structural changes** (step 4 of [[#rule-paraphrase]]): change the subject (people instead of the policy), change the verb (*travel more* instead of *a rise in the number of journeys*), and change the way the area effect is expressed (a comparison instead of *especially in …*).

*Older people made many more journeys by bus once travel became free for those over sixty-five, and the increase was largest where shops and health services were hardest to reach (Lindqvist 2019).*

**Check.** Same meaning, same caution (*once* = after; no causal verb), new structure. Remaining shared words — *shops and health services*, *sixty-five* — are plain terms with no better alternative.
:::
:::

## When to quote

::: theorem Quote only when the words matter {#rule-when-quote}
Quote when the **exact wording** is important:

1. a **definition** or a technical formulation that would lose precision if restated;
2. a phrase that is **memorable**, original or striking, where the way it is said is part of the point;
3. **language that you are analysing** — a text in literature or history, a politician's statement, a policy document, a survey question;
4. a claim you are going to **dispute**, where you must show that you have not misrepresented it;
5. a statement from an **authority** whose exact words carry weight (a law, an official guideline).

Otherwise paraphrase or summarise. Quote as little as possible — a phrase is often enough — and always explain what the quotation shows.
:::

::: proof
A paraphrase shows that the writer has understood the source and integrates it into the writer's own argument and style; a text that is mostly quotations reads as a collection of other people's voices with little of the writer's own. Conventions also differ between fields. Hyland (1999), comparing citation practices across disciplines, found direct quotation much more common in the humanities and social sciences than in the natural sciences and engineering, where sources are almost always summarised or paraphrased. In literary studies, history and law, by contrast, the exact words of the text are often the evidence, and quoting them is essential (point 3). The rule therefore asks not "is this important?" but "is the **wording** important?" — an important idea that could be stated in other words should usually be paraphrased.
:::

::: example Quote or paraphrase? {#ex-quote-or-paraphrase}
For each fictional source and purpose, say whether to quote or paraphrase. (a) A government guideline states: *Children under two should have no screen time except video calls.* You are writing about whether families follow the guideline. (b) A study reports that *students who slept less than six hours scored on average 8% lower*. You are using it as evidence in an essay on sleep. (c) An article defines *translanguaging* in a particular way that you will then criticise. (d) A historian writes that the reform was *a revolution disguised as an administrative adjustment*. You are writing about how historians have described the reform.
::: solution
(a) **Quote**: the exact wording of a guideline you are comparing behaviour against matters (*no screen time except video calls* is specific).

(b) **Paraphrase**: the finding matters, not its wording; give the figure accurately with a citation.

(c) **Quote** the definition: you will dispute it, so you must show exactly what it says.

(d) **Quote** the phrase: it is a striking formulation, and the way historians *describe* the reform is your topic. Integrate it into your own sentence and comment on it.
:::
:::

## Quoting accurately

A quotation must reproduce the source exactly, and any change must be visible.

- **Quotation marks.** Short quotations go inside quotation marks within your sentence. British publishers often use single marks (‘…’) and American ones double (“…”); follow your course's style and be consistent.
- **Block quotations.** Longer quotations are set off as an indented block without quotation marks; style guides set the threshold differently — APA style, for example, uses a block for quotations of 40 words or more.
- **Omissions.** Mark words left out with an ellipsis (…). Do not omit words that change the meaning (*not*, *may*, *in some cases*).
- **Changes and additions.** Put any word you change or add in square brackets: *"[The policy] was introduced without consultation"*, where the source had *It*. *[sic]* marks an error that is in the source.
- **Page numbers.** Give the page (or paragraph number for unpaginated sources) for every quotation.
- **Integration.** Introduce a quotation with your own words and a reporting verb, and make it fit the grammar of your sentence: *Okonkwo (2021) observes that urban areas "often remain several degrees warmer than the surrounding countryside after dark" (p. 47).*

::: example Integrating a quotation {#ex-integrate}
Source (fictional, Bauer 2018, p. 203): *In our view, the evidence that homework benefits pupils under the age of ten is, at best, weak and, at worst, non-existent, and schools should think carefully before setting it.* Write a sentence that quotes only the most important phrase, shortened with an ellipsis, and integrated grammatically.
::: solution
**Choose what to quote.** The striking part is the evaluation of the evidence: *at best, weak and, at worst, non-existent*. The rest can be paraphrased.

**Integrate it.** *Bauer (2018) judges the evidence that homework helps children under ten to be "at best, weak and, at worst, non-existent" (p. 203), and advises schools to be cautious about setting it.*

**With an ellipsis**, if you want the subject phrase in the quotation: *Bauer (2018) concludes that "the evidence that homework benefits pupils under the age of ten is … weak" (p. 203).* — but this omission removes *at best* and *at worst*, which changes the strength of the claim. Since an ellipsis must not change the meaning, the first version is better.

**Reporting verb.** *judges* and *concludes* fit an evaluation; the source marks it as opinion (*In our view*), so a verb such as *states as fact* would misrepresent it.
:::
:::

## Recording sources as you read

Paraphrases and quotations can only be cited correctly if the source was recorded correctly when it was read. Four habits prevent most problems:

1. **Record the full reference before anything else**, from the source itself, not from another text's reference list: authors, year, title, journal or publisher, volume, issue, pages, DOI. Reference-management software can store these, but check what it imports.
2. **Record the page number** next to every note, quotation and paraphrase.
3. **Mark secondary sources.** If you read about Smith's study in Lee's review, your note should say *Smith (2010), cited in Lee (2020, p. 14)*. If you cannot read Smith yourself, cite it as a secondary source in the form your style requires, and do not describe details that only Smith's text gives.
4. **Check every reference that you did not find yourself.** References copied from websites, lecture slides or a chatbot may be wrong or may not exist; confirm them in a library database or the publisher's site before you use them.

::: quiz
You read in a 2021 review that a 1998 study found an effect, but you cannot get the 1998 study. How should your note record it?
- [ ] as the 1998 study, with its own reference, as if you had read it
- [x] as the 1998 study cited in the 2021 review, with the review's page number
- [ ] as your own general knowledge, with no citation
- [ ] as the 2021 review's own finding
::: solution
You have read only the review's account of the 1998 study, so you must make that clear: *X (1998), cited in Y (2021, p. …)*. Citing the 1998 study directly would claim that you read it; citing nothing would present the idea as your own; and the finding is not the review's own.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Copying and culture.** It is sometimes said that Chinese students copy because their culture values memorising and reproducing authoritative texts. Researchers have questioned this explanation: Pennycook (1996) discussed how ideas of textual ownership differ and change, and studies such as Pecorari's (2003) found patchwriting to be a normal stage for second-language writers of any background, not a cultural trait. Chinese universities have their own rules against plagiarism (抄袭) and use text-matching software too. What does differ is the precise line: English-medium institutions usually count close paraphrase as plagiarism even with a citation, and students should know that line before they meet it.
- **Passives in paraphrase.** Changing active to passive (or the reverse) is a common way to restructure a paraphrase. English academic passives are neutral in meaning: *The samples were analysed* simply leaves out the actor. The Chinese 被 passive has traditionally been used mainly for unpleasant or adverse events (他被批评了), although its use has widened in modern written Chinese, partly under the influence of translation; Chinese academic prose more often expresses passive meaning without 被, through notional passives (样本已分析完毕), 由 (由两名研究者编码), 经 or 得到. So a Chinese reader should not read an English passive as adverse, and should not translate every English passive with 被.
- **Punctuation.** Chinese uses “ ” and ‘ ’ (and 「 」 in some regions) for quotations and 《 》 for titles of books and articles. English uses quotation marks for quotations and article titles, and *italics* for titles of books and journals (in most styles). The English ellipsis is three dots (…), the Chinese one six (……).
- **Translating a quotation.** If you quote a Chinese source in an English essay, you are quoting your own translation: say so (*my translation*), and, where the exact wording matters, give the Chinese original in a note.
:::

::: history
The modern rules of quotation and citation are relatively recent: in the twentieth century, style guides such as the American Psychological Association's *Publication Manual*, which began as a short set of instructions published in 1929, fixed the conventions of citation for whole disciplines. The research on paraphrase that informs teaching today is more recent still. Rebecca Moore Howard (1993, 1995) named *patchwriting* and argued that it should be treated as a stage of learning rather than as cheating; Alastair Pennycook (1996) questioned simple ideas of textual ownership in second-language writing; and Diane Pecorari (2003) studied patchwriting in the theses of second-language postgraduates and found it widespread and largely unintentional. Their work moved the discussion of plagiarism from punishment towards teaching — the approach this chapter takes.
:::

Using sources in your own writing — citation styles, reference lists and integrating sources into an argument — is taught in [[academic-writing/citation-referencing]], and the rules and values of academic honesty in [[academic-writing/academic-integrity]]. Paraphrase and summary together are the basis of a literature review ([[academic-writing/literature-review]]).

::: summary
- A **paraphrase** restates one passage in your own words **and structure**, cited; a **quotation** reproduces the exact words in quotation marks, cited with a page number ([[#def-paraphrase]]).
- **Patchwriting** — the source's structure with substituted words — counts as plagiarism in English-medium institutions, even with a citation ([[#def-patchwriting]]).
- To paraphrase: **understand**, **note**, **put the source away**, **write from notes**, change the **structure**, keep **terms, numbers and stance**, **cite**, and **compare** ([[#rule-paraphrase]]).
- **Quote** only when the wording matters — definitions, striking phrases, language you analyse or dispute, authoritative texts — and quote as little as possible ([[#rule-when-quote]]).
- Quote **exactly**: mark omissions with an ellipsis and changes with square brackets, never change the meaning, and integrate quotations into your own sentences.
- Record the **full reference**, **page numbers** and any **secondary citation** as you read, and check every reference you did not find yourself.
:::

::: exercise Paraphrase, quotation or patchwriting? {level=1}
Source (fictional): *Most of the world's fresh water is locked in ice sheets and glaciers.* (Moreau 2017, p. 5) Label each version P (acceptable paraphrase), Q (correct quotation) or PW (patchwriting). (a) *Most of the planet's fresh water is trapped in ice sheets and glaciers (Moreau 2017).* (b) *Moreau (2017) notes that "most of the world's fresh water is locked in ice sheets and glaciers" (p. 5).* (c) *Ice sheets and glaciers hold the greater part of the earth's fresh water (Moreau 2017).*
::: solution
(a) PW — the source's structure with two words changed; (b) Q; (c) P — the structure is reversed (ice as subject), with new verb and phrasing; *ice sheets and glaciers* and *fresh water* are terms that can stay.

Quote (Q) or paraphrase (P)? (a) a statistic about unemployment in a government report, used as evidence; (b) a poet's line you are analysing; (c) a researcher's definition of *code-switching* that you will compare with two other definitions; (d) a textbook's explanation of how vaccines work.

Source (fictional): *The survey, which was conducted online in March, received 1,200 responses from teachers in all twelve districts.* Write a quotation that (a) leaves out *which was conducted online in March* and (b) replaces *The survey* with *[the union's survey]*.
::: solution
(a) *"The survey … received 1,200 responses from teachers in all twelve districts."* (b) *"[The union's survey] … received 1,200 responses from teachers in all twelve districts."* (Version (b) also uses the omission from (a); either form is acceptable as long as the changes are marked.)

::: exercise Revise patchwriting {level=2}
Source (fictional): *Rising sea levels pose a serious threat to low-lying coastal communities, many of which lack the resources to build adequate defences.* (Tran 2022, p. 31) A student wrote: *Increasing sea levels present a grave danger to low coastal communities, a lot of which do not have the money to construct sufficient defences (Tran 2022).* Explain the problem and write an acceptable paraphrase.
::: solution
The student's sentence follows the source word by word with synonyms (*Rising* → *Increasing*, *pose a serious threat* → *present a grave danger*, *lack the resources* → *do not have the money*, *build adequate* → *construct sufficient*): patchwriting. *the money* also narrows *resources*. An acceptable paraphrase: *Many communities on low coasts cannot afford proper sea defences, Tran (2022) warns, which leaves them especially exposed as the sea rises.* (New order, new main clause, *cannot afford* restating *lack the resources*, technical sense kept.)
:::
:::

::: exercise Integrate a quotation {level=2}
Source (fictional, Ngata 2020, p. 88): *Language loss is rarely a choice; it is usually the result of pressures that make the community's language seem useless to its own children.* Write one sentence that quotes a short phrase from the source and paraphrases the rest, with a suitable reporting verb.
::: solution
A model: *Ngata (2020) argues that communities seldom choose to abandon their languages; rather, outside pressures make the language "seem useless to its own children" (p. 88).* Other good answers quote *rarely a choice*. Check that the quotation is exact, has a page number, fits the grammar of the sentence, and that the reporting verb (*argues*, *contends*) fits a claim of this kind.
:::

::: exercise A secondary source {level=2}
You read in Chen (2022, p. 19) that a study by Abara (2008) found that bilingual children were faster at a switching task. You have not read Abara. Write (a) the note you would make, and (b) a sentence for your essay. (Both studies are fictional.)
::: solution
(a) *Abara (2008), cited in Chen (2022, p. 19): bilingual children faster at a switching task. [Not read Abara — check if accessible.]* (b) *Abara (2008, cited in Chen 2022) found that bilingual children performed a task-switching test faster than monolingual children.* The exact form of a secondary citation depends on the style your course uses; the essential point is that the essay shows you read about Abara in Chen.
:::

::: exercise Paraphrase a paragraph {level=3}
Paraphrase this fictional paragraph (Ibrahim 2023, pp. 54–55) in about the same length, following [[#rule-paraphrase]]. Then list three structural changes you made and any shared words you kept on purpose.

> *Although open-plan offices were introduced partly to encourage collaboration, several studies have found that face-to-face interaction actually declines after companies remove walls between desks. Employees appear to protect their privacy by wearing headphones and communicating by email or messaging instead. The open office may therefore produce the opposite of the effect its designers intended.*
::: hint
Unpack first: what was the aim, what happened, why, and what is the writer's conclusion — and how certain is it? Then close the source and write from your notes.
:::
::: solution
A model: *Companies have often removed the walls between desks hoping that staff will work together more. According to Ibrahim (2023), however, a number of studies show that people talk to each other in person less, not more, once their office becomes open-plan, apparently because they put on headphones and turn to email and messaging to keep some privacy. Open offices, Ibrahim suggests, may achieve the reverse of what they were designed to do.*

Structural changes: (1) the concession *Although … introduced partly to encourage* became a separate opening sentence with *hoping that*; (2) the nominalised *face-to-face interaction actually declines* became a clause with a human subject (*people talk to each other in person less*); (3) the separate sentence about headphones was combined with the finding as a *because* clause. Kept on purpose: *open-plan*, *headphones*, *email*, *privacy* — plain terms without good alternatives. Stance kept: *appear* → *apparently*; *may therefore* → *may*, with *suggests*.
:::
:::

::: exercise From reading to writing {level=3}
Choose two short passages (100–150 words each) from academic sources on the same topic. For each, write notes, a paraphrase from the notes, and one sentence that integrates a short quotation. Then write a paragraph of about 120 words that uses both sources to make a point of your own, with full citations. Underline any word sequence of four or more words shared with a source and decide whether to keep it as a quotation, keep it as a technical term, or rewrite it.
::: solution
Answers will differ. A good answer has notes that are not copies of the sources; paraphrases with new structures that keep the sources' meaning and stance; quotations that are exact, short, marked and integrated; and a final paragraph in which the writer's own point is stated first and the sources support it (rather than a paragraph that simply reports one source and then the other). The underlining check should find only technical terms and marked quotations.
:::
:::
