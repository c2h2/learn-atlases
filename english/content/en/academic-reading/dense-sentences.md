Compare two ways of saying roughly the same thing:

> *Because universities admitted many more students in the 1990s, a smaller proportion of graduates found jobs that needed a degree.*
>
> *The rapid expansion of higher education in the 1990s led to a decline in the proportion of graduates entering graduate-level employment.*

The first is how a teacher might explain the point aloud. It has two clauses, each with a subject and a verb that says who did what: *universities admitted*, *graduates found*. The second is how an academic article would put it. It has one clause, and only one verb, *led to*, which does not describe an action at all: it links two things. Everything that happened — admitting, expanding, declining, entering — has become a noun or is packed inside a noun phrase. The second sentence is not longer, but it is **denser**, and a reader who does not know how to unpack it may understand every word and still not see who did what.

This chapter is about that density. It explains **nominalisation**, the turning of actions and qualities into nouns; the **long noun phrases** in which academic writing carries most of its information; the **relation verbs** that link them; and a method for restating a dense sentence in simpler words to check that you have understood it. It builds on [[reading-skills/long-sentences]], which showed how to find the core of a long sentence, and on the clause types of [[complex-sentences/participle-infinitive-clauses]] and [[complex-sentences/relative-clauses]].

## Nominalisation

::: definition Nominalisation {#def-nominalisation}
**Nominalisation** (名词化) is the expression of a process or a quality — something that would most directly be said with a verb or an adjective — as a noun. The noun may be formed with a suffix (*expand* → *expansion*, *fail* → *failure*, *develop* → *development*, *able* → *ability*, *accurate* → *accuracy*), have the same form as the verb (*increase*, *decline*, *use*, *change*), or be an *-ing* form (*the closing of the factory*). The term is also used for the noun itself: *expansion* is a nominalisation.
:::

Nominalisations are not a feature of bad writing; they are a resource that every academic discipline uses. Biber et al. (1999), in the *Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English*, found nouns formed with suffixes such as *-tion*, *-ment*, *-ness* and *-ity* to be far more frequent in academic prose than in conversation or fiction. Halliday (1993) called this use of nouns for processes **grammatical metaphor**: the grammar presents an action as if it were a thing.

::: theorem What nominalisation does for a writer {#rule-why-nominalise}
In academic English, writers turn processes into nouns because a noun can:

1. **pack** a whole clause into a phrase, so that several events fit into one sentence (*the expansion of higher education* = *higher education expanded*);
2. become the **subject** or **object** of another verb, so that one process can be said to cause, follow or explain another (*expansion led to a decline*);
3. be **counted, measured and evaluated** (*a 12% decline*, *a rapid expansion*, *a useful distinction*);
4. **refer back** to what was said in the previous sentence (*Universities admitted more students. This expansion …*);
5. **leave out** who did the action, when the writer thinks it is obvious, unknown or unimportant — or prefers not to say.
:::

::: proof
Points 1–3 explain why nominalisation is so frequent in science and the social sciences: these fields talk about processes as objects of study, and they need to relate one process to another and to measure them. Halliday (1993) argued that this way of writing developed together with modern science, because it lets an argument build step by step: what is reported as a clause in one sentence (*the metal expands when it is heated*) becomes a noun in the next (*this expansion*), and can then be used in a further claim. Point 4 is the same mechanism seen from the reader's side: a nominalisation often summarises the previous sentence, so it is a link to follow, like the *this* + noun of [[reading-skills/following-the-thread]].

Point 5 is the cost. A nominalisation does not have to say who acted or what was affected: *the reduction of costs* does not say who reduced them, or how. Williams and Bizup (*Style*) advise writers to prefer clauses in which the main characters are subjects and their actions are verbs; academic writing often does the opposite, and the reader has to restore the characters and actions — which is what the method below does. Teaching writers to use nominalisations well, and when to avoid them, is the subject of [[style-and-rhetoric/clarity]] and [[academic-writing/academic-style]].
:::

::: example Unpacking a nominalisation {#ex-unpack-one}
Restate this phrase as a clause with a subject and a verb: *the government's gradual withdrawal of support for small farms*.
::: solution
**Find the hidden verb.** *withdrawal* is the noun of *withdraw*.

**Find who did it.** The possessive *the government's* gives the actor: the government withdrew.

**Find what was affected.** *of support* is the object: the government withdrew support.

**Find the other details.** *gradual* (an adjective on the noun) becomes an adverb on the verb: *gradually*. *for small farms* says whose support.

**Clause:** *The government gradually withdrew the support it gave to small farms.* The noun phrase and the clause carry the same information; the clause makes the actor and the action visible.
:::
:::

::: theorem Unpacking a dense phrase {#rule-unpack}
To restate a nominalisation as a clause:

1. find the **verb or adjective** inside the noun (*expansion* → *expand*, *reliability* → *reliable*);
2. find the **actor** (who or what did it): in a possessive (*the government's*), a *by*-phrase (*by the committee*), an *of*-phrase with an intransitive process (*the growth of cities* = *cities grew*), a modifying noun or adjective (*student participation* = *students participated*), or nowhere — then ask whether you can tell from the context;
3. find the **affected** (what it was done to): usually an *of*-phrase (*the analysis of the data* = *someone analysed the data*);
4. turn **adjectives** on the noun into **adverbs** on the verb (*rapid growth* → *grew rapidly*);
5. write the **clause**, and check that it says the same thing.
:::

::: proof
The steps reverse what the writer did. When a clause is made into a noun phrase, its subject usually becomes a possessive, an *of*-phrase or a *by*-phrase, its object an *of*-phrase, and its adverbs adjectives ([[nouns-and-articles/noun-phrases]]; Quirk et al. 1985 describe these correspondences in detail). Because both subject and object can appear as *of*-phrases, step 2 and step 3 sometimes need the context to decide which is which — see the warning below. Writing the clause in step 5 is the test of understanding: if you cannot say who did what, you have not yet understood the phrase, however well you know its words.
:::

::: warning *Of*: actor or affected?
*the examination of the students* — did the students examine something, or were they examined?

*the criticism of the government* — did the government criticise, or was it criticised?

An *of*-phrase after a nominalisation can name the actor (*the arrival of the train* = *the train arrived*) or the affected (*the destruction of the forest* = *someone destroyed the forest*). With verbs that need an object, *of* usually marks the affected: ✓ *the examination of the students* most often means that someone examined the students. But do not decide from grammar alone. Check the context — *the criticism of the government by opposition parties* settles it — and if the text does not settle it, note the ambiguity rather than guessing.
:::

::: quiz
What does *a marked increase in the use of public transport by older residents* mean?
- [ ] Older residents increased the price of public transport.
- [x] Older residents used public transport much more than before.
- [ ] Public transport was used to carry more older residents to markets.
- [ ] The number of older residents increased because of public transport.
::: solution
Unpack from the inside: *the use of public transport by older residents* = *older residents used public transport* (*by* gives the actor). *an increase in* that use = they used it more. *marked* = noticeable, considerable. So older residents used public transport considerably more.
:::
:::

## Long noun phrases

Nominalisations rarely appear alone. They are the heads of long noun phrases, with modifiers before and after them, and those noun phrases are where academic writing carries most of its information.

::: definition The parts of a noun phrase {#def-np-parts}
A noun phrase has a **head** noun, which it is about. Words before the head are **premodifiers**: determiners (*the*, *this*, *these*), adjectives (*rapid*, *significant*) and other nouns used as modifiers (*student* in *student satisfaction*). Words after the head are **postmodifiers**: prepositional phrases (*of higher education*, *in rural areas*), relative clauses (*that were surveyed*), non-finite clauses (*entering graduate-level employment*, *to be measured*), and **noun complement clauses** that give the content of nouns such as *fact*, *idea*, *finding*, *claim* and *assumption* (*the finding that sleep improved*).
:::

Biber and Gray (2010, 2016) showed from corpus studies that this is where academic writing differs most from speech. Contrary to the stereotype, academic prose does not use more dependent clauses than conversation; it uses **fewer** of some kinds. What it uses much more are **phrasal** modifiers: nouns as premodifiers, attributive adjectives and, above all, prepositional phrases as postmodifiers. The result is a style they call **compressed**: the information is all there, but the relations between the parts — who did what to whom — are left for the reader to work out.

::: theorem Reading a long noun phrase {#rule-read-np}
1. Find the **head**: it is the **last** noun before the first postmodifier (in *the student satisfaction survey results of 2024*, the head is *results*, not *student* or *survey*); after the head, a preposition, a relative word or an *-ing*/*-ed* form usually begins a postmodifier.
2. Read a string of **nouns before the head from right to left**, adding *of*, *for*, *about* or *by* between them: *university student mental health services* = *services for the mental health of students at universities*.
3. Read **postmodifiers** as attached to the nearest suitable noun first, and then check whether the meaning makes sense; if not, attach them higher.
4. Name the **relation** each modifier adds: which one, what kind, whose, where, when, about what.
:::

::: proof
English premodifiers modify what follows them, so in a string of nouns the last noun is the head and each earlier noun narrows what comes after it: *a survey results* would be odd, but *survey results* are results of a survey. That is why the string is read from the right. Noun–noun sequences are especially compressed because the relation between the nouns is not expressed at all: *a heart attack* is an attack of the heart, *a heart surgeon* operates on hearts, *a heart patient* has a heart condition. The reader must supply the relation from knowledge of the field. Postmodifiers attach most often to the nearest noun, but not always (the figure above shows a phrase where two attachments are possible), which is why step 3 includes a check.
:::

::: example Untangling a noun string {#ex-noun-string}
What does *a low-income household energy efficiency improvement scheme* mean?
::: solution
**Head.** The last noun is *scheme*: it is a kind of scheme.

**Right to left.** *improvement scheme* — a scheme for improvement. *efficiency improvement* — improving efficiency. *energy efficiency* — the efficient use of energy. *household energy efficiency* — the energy efficiency of homes. *low-income household* — homes of people with low incomes (*low-income* is a compound adjective on *household*).

**Restated:** *a scheme to help families on low incomes use energy more efficiently in their homes* (for example by paying for insulation). Six words before the head became a full clause with an actor, a verb and a purpose. The restatement also shows what the phrase does not say: who runs the scheme and what it involves.
:::
:::

::: quiz
What is the head of the subject in this sentence? *The effects of air pollution on the respiratory health of children living near major roads have been studied extensively.*
- [ ] air pollution
- [ ] children
- [x] effects
- [ ] roads
::: solution
The subject begins *The effects*: *effects* is the first noun, and everything after it — *of air pollution*, *on the respiratory health*, *of children*, *living near major roads* — is postmodification. The plural verb *have been studied* agrees with *effects*. A check with [[reading-skills/long-sentences#rule-head]] confirms it.
:::
:::

## Relation verbs

When processes have become nouns, the main verb of the sentence often does not describe an action at all. It states a **relation** between two noun phrases.

::: theorem Find the two things and the relation {#rule-relation}
In a dense academic sentence, the main verb is often a **relation verb**, and the sentence has the shape *noun phrase A — relation — noun phrase B*. Common relations are:

1. **cause and effect**: *lead to*, *result in*, *cause*, *give rise to*, *contribute to*, *bring about*, *produce*; reversed, *result from*, *stem from*, *be due to*, *be attributed to*;
2. **association** (without claiming cause): *be associated with*, *be linked to*, *correlate with*, *be related to*;
3. **explanation and evidence**: *account for*, *explain*, *reflect*, *indicate*, *suggest*, *point to*;
4. **identity and composition**: *be*, *represent*, *constitute*, *consist of*, *involve*, *comprise*;
5. **comparison and change**: *exceed*, *differ from*, *be similar to*, *precede*, *follow*.

Read such a sentence by unpacking A, unpacking B, and stating the relation in plain words.
:::

::: proof
Nominalisation and relation verbs go together. Once *higher education expanded* has become *the expansion of higher education*, the sentence needs a verb to connect it to the next event, and that verb is a relation: *led to*. Halliday (1993) noted that in scientific English the logical connection that would be a conjunction in speech (*because*, *so*) often becomes a verb (*cause*, *lead to*) or even a noun (*the cause of*, *the reason for*). For a critical reader the relation verb is the most important word in the sentence, because it carries the claim: *led to* claims a cause, *is associated with* claims only that two things go together. The difference is the subject of [[academic-reading/evaluating-sources]].
:::

::: example A whole dense sentence {#ex-whole-sentence}
Restate in simpler words: *The rapid expansion of higher education in the 1990s led to a decline in the proportion of graduates entering graduate-level employment.*
::: solution
**Shape.** A = *the rapid expansion of higher education in the 1990s*; relation = *led to* (cause); B = *a decline in the proportion of graduates entering graduate-level employment*.

**Unpack A.** *expansion* → *expand*. What expanded? *higher education* (an *of*-phrase naming the actor of an intransitive process). *rapid* → *rapidly*. *in the 1990s* — when. → *Higher education expanded rapidly in the 1990s* (that is, universities took many more students).

**Unpack B.** Head: *decline* → *declined*. What declined? *the proportion of graduates entering graduate-level employment* — the share of graduates who got jobs that require a degree. → *The proportion of graduates who got graduate-level jobs declined.*

**Relation.** *led to* = A caused B.

**Restated:** *Because higher education expanded rapidly in the 1990s, a smaller share of graduates got jobs that needed a degree.*

**What a critical reader notices.** The original states a causal claim with a single verb. The restatement makes it easier to ask the right question: did the expansion cause this, or did something else — a change in the job market, for example — happen at the same time?
:::
:::

## Other sources of density

Three more features often make academic sentences hard to read.

**Noun complement clauses.** Nouns such as *fact*, *view*, *assumption*, *claim*, *finding*, *evidence*, *possibility* and *question* are often followed by a clause giving their content: *the assumption that learners transfer rules from their first language*; *the question of whether the effect lasts*; *the extent to which teachers follow the curriculum*. The noun says what kind of statement it is (an assumption, a finding, a possibility); the clause says what the statement is. Both matter: *the finding that X* and *the claim that X* report the same X with very different status.

**Agentless passives.** *Participants were interviewed twice. The data were coded and analysed.* The passive keeps the focus on the participants and the data, and in a methods section it is clear enough who acted (the researchers). Elsewhere, the reader may need to ask: *It is widely believed that …* — by whom?

**Abstract subjects with human verbs.** *This paper argues …*, *The results suggest …*, *Table 2 shows …*. These are conventional in English academic writing and mean *the authors argue in this paper*, *the authors interpret the results as suggesting*. They are not errors, but they also hide a person: when *the data show* something, it is still the authors who decided what the data show.

::: example Restating a methods sentence {#ex-methods}
Restate, making the actors explicit: *Following the completion of a consent procedure, interviews were conducted with 24 teachers, and transcription and coding of the recordings were carried out by two independent researchers, with disagreements resolved by discussion.*
::: solution
**Find the clauses and nominalisations.** *the completion of a consent procedure* → someone completed a consent procedure; *interviews were conducted* → someone interviewed; *transcription and coding of the recordings* → someone transcribed and coded the recordings, *by two independent researchers* gives the actors; *disagreements resolved by discussion* → the researchers discussed their disagreements and resolved them.

**Supply the missing actors from context.** In a methods section, the unnamed actor is normally the research team. The teachers completed the consent procedure (they agreed to take part).

**Restated:** *After the teachers had agreed to take part, the researchers interviewed 24 of them. Two researchers, working separately, transcribed and coded the recordings; where they disagreed, they discussed the difference until they agreed.*

Note that *independent* here means working separately, without consulting each other — a detail that matters, because it shows that the coding was checked.
:::
:::

::: quiz
In *the possibility that the effect was caused by the weather*, what is the status of the idea that the weather caused the effect?
- [ ] It is a fact the writer has proved.
- [x] It is an option the writer is considering.
- [ ] It is a claim the writer rejects.
- [ ] It is a finding of an earlier study.
::: solution
*possibility* tells you the status: the clause after *that* is presented as possible, not established and not rejected. Compare *the fact that …*, *the finding that …*, *the mistaken belief that …*: the noun before *that* carries the writer's stance ([[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]]).
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Nouns and verbs.** Chinese verbs can be used as nouns without any change of form: 发展, 研究, 分析 and 增长 can each be a verb or a noun, depending on the position. English marks the change with a suffix (*develop* → *development*, *analyse* → *analysis*) or uses the same form (*increase*). A Chinese reader therefore recognises a nominalisation easily as a concept but may not notice the suffix as a signal that a process has been packed into a noun.
- **A static and a dynamic style.** Comparative studies describe English, especially formal written English, as preferring nouns and a "static" style, and Chinese as preferring verbs and a "dynamic" one (连淑能《英汉对比研究》). Academic Chinese also nominalises — 对数据进行分析, 予以支持, 加以改进, where a light verb such as 进行 carries a verbal noun — but it more often keeps a sequence of verbs. When you translate a dense English sentence into Chinese, unpacking nominalisations into verbs, as in [[#rule-unpack]], often gives better Chinese ([[translation/english-into-chinese]]).
- **Modifiers before and after the head.** Chinese puts all modifiers before the head with 的: 九十年代高等教育的迅速扩张. English puts short ones before and long ones after: *the rapid expansion of higher education in the 1990s*. In an English noun phrase, meet the head early and read what follows as description of it.
- **Noun strings.** Strings of nouns before a head (*student mental health services*) resemble Chinese compounds (学生心理健康服务), and Chinese readers often read them easily. The difficulty is the opposite one: deciding where an English noun phrase ends and the verb begins, when the phrase is long and its postmodifiers contain verbs of their own.
:::

::: history
The dense, nominal style of academic English has a history. M. A. K. Halliday, in essays collected in *Writing Science* (Halliday & Martin 1993), traced grammatical metaphor in scientific writing back to the early modern period and argued that it developed with the needs of experimental science: a result stated as a clause could become a noun and enter the next step of an argument. Douglas Biber and Bethany Gray (2016), using historical corpora, found that the most compressed features — nouns modifying nouns, long chains of prepositional phrases — grew especially fast in the twentieth century, above all in science writing. At the same time, style manuals argued for the opposite: Joseph Williams's *Style* (first published in 1981) taught writers to turn nominalisations back into verbs whenever the reader would otherwise struggle. Readers of academic English need both views: the density is a real resource for experts, and an obstacle to everyone else.
:::

The relation verbs of this chapter carry the claims analysed in [[academic-reading/claims-and-evidence]], and the difference between *led to* and *is associated with* is taken up in [[academic-reading/evaluating-sources]]. Noun phrase structure in general is in [[nouns-and-articles/noun-phrases]]; the suffixes that form nominalisations are in [[word-formation/suffixes]]; and using nominalisation well in your own writing is taught in [[academic-writing/academic-style]] and [[style-and-rhetoric/clarity]].

::: summary
- **Nominalisation** turns processes and qualities into nouns (*expand* → *expansion*); it is far more frequent in academic prose than in conversation ([[#def-nominalisation]]).
- Writers nominalise to **pack** information, to make processes **subjects** of other verbs, to **measure** and **evaluate** them, to **refer back** — and sometimes to **leave out** the actor ([[#rule-why-nominalise]]).
- To **unpack**: find the hidden verb, its actor and what it affects, turn adjectives into adverbs, and write the clause ([[#rule-unpack]]). An *of*-phrase may name either the actor or the affected.
- Academic complexity lies mainly in **noun phrases** (Biber & Gray): find the head, read noun strings from the right, attach postmodifiers carefully ([[#rule-read-np]]).
- The main verb of a dense sentence is often a **relation** — cause, association, explanation, identity or comparison — and it carries the claim ([[#rule-relation]]).
- Nouns such as *finding*, *claim* and *possibility* before a *that*-clause tell you the **status** of the idea.
:::

Say what relation the main verb expresses (cause, association, explanation or evidence, identity, comparison). (a) *Poor diet contributes to the risk of heart disease.* (b) *Income was associated with participation in sport.* (c) *These results point to a role for social factors.* (d) *Rice constitutes the main part of the diet.* (e) *Costs in the first year exceeded expectations.*

Restate each phrase as a clause. (a) *the steady growth of online retail* (b) *the committee's rejection of the proposal* (c) *parental involvement in children's homework* (d) *the reliance of small businesses on bank loans* (e) *a gradual improvement in air quality*
::: solution
(a) *Online retail grew steadily* (or *People bought more and more online*). (b) *The committee rejected the proposal.* (c) *Parents were involved in (helped with) their children's homework.* (d) *Small businesses relied on bank loans.* (e) *Air quality gradually improved.* Other wordings are acceptable if they keep the actor, the action and the modifier.

Explain each noun string in a phrase or clause. (a) *teacher workload reduction measures* (b) *a coastal flood risk assessment* (c) *first-year student retention rates* (d) *mobile phone use restrictions*
::: solution
(a) *measures to reduce teachers' workload*; (b) *an assessment of the risk of flooding on the coast*; (c) *the rates at which first-year students stay at (do not leave) the university*; (d) *restrictions on using mobile phones*. In each case the head is the last noun, and the earlier nouns are read from right to left.

::: exercise Status nouns {level=2}
How does the noun before *that* change the status of the same idea? Rank from strongest to weakest support: (a) *the suggestion that bilingual children switch tasks faster* (b) *the finding that bilingual children switch tasks faster* (c) *the claim that bilingual children switch tasks faster* (d) *the myth that bilingual children switch tasks faster*
::: solution
From strongest to weakest: (b) *finding* — presented as a result of research; (c) *claim* — someone asserts it, and the writer does not commit to it; (a) *suggestion* — tentative; (d) *myth* — the writer rejects it. Claim and suggestion are close; some readers will rank them the other way. The point is that the writer's stance is carried by the noun ([[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Restate a paragraph {level=3}
Restate this fictional paragraph in plainer English, with clear actors and verbs, without losing information.

> *The introduction of congestion charging in the city centre in 2018 resulted in a substantial reduction in private car use during peak hours. However, the displacement of traffic to surrounding residential streets and the consequent deterioration of local air quality have been a source of considerable dissatisfaction among residents.*
::: hint
There are five nominalisations: *introduction*, *reduction*, *use*, *displacement*, *deterioration* (and *dissatisfaction*). Find the actor of each; one has none in the text.
:::
::: solution
A model answer: *In 2018 the city began charging drivers to enter the centre at busy times. As a result, people drove their own cars much less at peak hours. However, many drivers now use the residential streets around the centre instead, so the air in those streets has become worse, and the people who live there are very unhappy about it.*

Points to check: the actor of *introduction* is not given (probably the city authorities); *resulted in* is a causal claim, kept as *As a result*; *displacement of traffic* means traffic moved elsewhere; *consequent* means "caused by that", kept as *so*; *a source of considerable dissatisfaction among residents* = residents are very dissatisfied.
:::
:::

::: exercise Unpack and question {level=3}
Unpack this sentence, then write two questions a critical reader should ask about it. *Increased exposure to English-language media in early childhood is associated with higher vocabulary scores at age ten, which underlines the importance of parental media choices.*
::: solution
**Unpacked:** *Children who see and hear more English-language media (films, programmes, songs) when they are very young tend to have higher vocabulary scores when they are ten. The writer thinks this shows that it matters what media parents choose for their children.*

**Questions:** (1) *is associated with* claims only that the two go together, but *underlines the importance of parental media choices* assumes that the media cause the higher scores. Could something else explain both — for example, parents who choose English media may also have more education, money or English themselves? (2) How were *exposure* and *vocabulary* measured, and vocabulary in which language? Other good questions: How many children? In which country? Is the effect large or small?
:::
:::
