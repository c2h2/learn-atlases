Two weeks before an essay is due, Ahmed opens his notes on the fifteen articles he read for it. Some pages are full of sentences copied from the articles, with no page numbers and no sign of which article they came from. Others have a few highlighted words — *significant*, *mixed results*, *Table 3!* — that meant something at the time. One page, from a single article, has the full reference at the top, the article's claim in one line of his own words, the three main pieces of evidence, a quotation in quotation marks with its page number, and two lines marked *ME* with his own doubts about the sample. That is the only article he can use without reading it again.

Notes are where reading turns into writing. Good notes save rereading, keep sources apart, record the details needed for citation, and — most important — make the reader decide what a text says, because nothing can be written in your own words until it has been understood. This chapter describes methods of note-taking, then the **summary**, the most important reading-to-writing skill: restating a text's main ideas, at different lengths, in your own words. The next chapter, [[academic-reading/paraphrase-and-quotation]], deals with restating single passages and quoting them.

## Taking notes

::: definition Notes and note-making {#def-notes}
**Notes** are a reader's record of a text: its reference, its main ideas, selected details and quotations, and the reader's own responses. Some teachers distinguish **note-taking** (recording what a text or a lecture says) from **note-making** (reorganising and responding to it); good academic notes do both.
:::

The main formats are these:

- **Linear or outline notes**: headings and indented points that follow the order of the text. Quick to write, easy to read later; they can simply copy the text's structure without questioning it.
- **Cornell notes**: a page divided into a wide right-hand column for notes, a narrow left-hand column for **cues** (questions or key words, added after reading), and a strip at the bottom for a **summary** of the page, as described by Walter Pauk in *How to Study in College* (1962). The cue column is used for self-testing: cover the notes and answer the questions.
- **Concept maps** (or mind maps): the main idea in the centre or at the top, with related ideas linked by lines or arrows. Good for seeing the structure of an argument and the links between ideas; less good for detail.
- **Annotation**: marks and short notes in the margin of the text itself. Fast and close to the source, but scattered across many documents and easily reduced to highlighting.
- **Synthesis tables** (or matrices): one row per source and one column per question (aim, sample, method, main finding, limitations, my comments). The best format for comparing many studies on one question, as in a literature review.

::: theorem What good academic notes contain {#rule-good-notes}
1. **The full reference** first: author(s), year, title, journal or publisher, volume, pages, DOI or URL — everything needed to cite it.
2. **The text's claim** in one sentence, in your own words.
3. **The main support**: reasons, evidence, method and sample, in short points in your own words.
4. **Quotations** only when the exact wording matters, always in quotation marks, with the **page number**.
5. **Your own comments** clearly marked (for example with *ME:* or square brackets): questions, doubts, links to other sources.
6. **The stance** of the source: keep the hedges (*suggests*, *may*) and the reporting voice (*the authors argue*, *Smith (2010), cited in this article, found …*).

Each kind of content must be visibly different, so that later you can tell the source's words, the source's ideas and your own thoughts apart.
:::

::: proof
Points 1, 4 and 5 prevent the most serious problems of academic writing: a quotation that cannot be cited because the page number is missing, and — worse — the source's words or ideas presented later as your own because the notes did not show where they came from. Many cases of accidental plagiarism begin as notes without quotation marks ([[academic-writing/academic-integrity]]). Points 2 and 3 force understanding: copying a sentence requires only that you see it; restating it requires that you know what it means. There is some experimental evidence for this. Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014) found that students who took lecture notes on laptops tended to copy the lecturer's words more and did worse on conceptual questions than those who wrote by hand, and suggested that writing by hand forced students to rephrase. A later replication by Morehead, Dunlosky and Rawson (2019) found the differences smaller and less consistent, so the safer conclusion is not about pens and keyboards but about copying: notes in your own words are more useful than verbatim notes, whatever you write them with. Point 6 is the subject of [[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]].
:::

Here is a short passage written for this lesson, about a fictional study.

> *Urban green spaces are widely thought to benefit mental health, but most evidence comes from cross-sectional surveys, which cannot show whether green space improves well-being or whether people with better mental health choose to live near parks. Tanaka and Osei (2024) addressed this problem by following 1,400 adults who moved house in three cities over six years. People who moved to areas with more green space reported a small but lasting improvement in mental well-being, beginning in the first year after the move, while those who moved to less green areas showed no corresponding decline. The authors note that people who move are not typical of the whole population, and that "green space" was measured from satellite images, which show trees and grass but not whether the space is accessible or pleasant to use.*

::: example Cornell notes from a passage {#ex-cornell}
Make Cornell notes on the passage.
::: solution
**Reference** (top of the page): Tanaka, ?. & Osei, ?. (2024). [Full title, journal, pages — to be completed from the article itself; the passage is a secondary report.]

| cues | notes |
|---|---|
| Problem with earlier evidence? | Most studies cross-sectional → can't tell direction: green space → better MH, or healthier people → choose to live near parks? |
| Design? | Longitudinal: 1,400 adults who moved house, 3 cities, 6 yrs |
| Main finding? | Moved to greener area → small, lasting ↑ well-being (from year 1). Moved to less green → no matching ↓ |
| Limitations? | Movers not typical of population. Green space measured by satellite: trees/grass, not access or quality |
| ME | Why no decline for the other group? Asymmetry interesting — check discussion. Does "small" mean d ≈ 0.1? Look at results table. |

**Summary strip:** *A six-year study of 1,400 people who moved house found that moving to a greener area was followed by a small, lasting improvement in well-being, which gives stronger evidence of cause than earlier surveys; but movers are a special group and the measure of green space was crude.*

Notes in this form are in the reader's words, use symbols (→, ↑, ↓) to save time, separate the source from *ME*, and mark what is still missing (the full reference, which must come from the article itself, not from the passage that reports it).
:::
:::

::: warning Copying sentences into notes
✗ Notes: *people who move are not typical of the whole population, and green space was measured from satellite images which show trees and grass but not whether the space is accessible*

✓ Notes: *Limits: movers ≠ typical; satellite measure = trees/grass only, not access or quality.* or, if the wording matters: *"not whether the space is accessible or pleasant to use" (p. 12)*

The first note copies the source's sentence without quotation marks. Weeks later, it looks like the student's own words, and if it goes into an essay it is plagiarism, even if no deception was intended. Either restate in your own words (and shorter), or put the exact words in quotation marks with the page number. Nothing in between.
:::

- [ ] *Urban green spaces are widely thought to benefit mental health, but most evidence comes from cross-sectional surveys*
- [x] *Tanaka & Osei (2024): movers to greener areas → small lasting ↑ well-being; stronger causal evidence than earlier surveys. ME: movers atypical?*

## Summarising

::: definition Summary {#def-summary}
A **summary** (概要, 摘要) is a shortened restatement of the main ideas of a text, in the summariser's own words, attributed to its source. It keeps the text's main claim and main support, in proportion to their importance, and leaves out examples, details and repetition. A summary reports; it does not add the summariser's opinion, which belongs in a separate response or critique.
:::

Summaries are written at many lengths for different purposes:

- a **one-sentence summary** (sometimes called a **gist** statement): the author, the text, the main claim, and often the method — used in literature reviews and introductions;
- a **paragraph summary** (about 80–150 words): the claim, the main reasons and evidence, and any important limits;
- a **longer summary** (a page or more): the argument section by section, used in reports, critical reviews and examinations.

::: theorem Steps for writing a summary {#rule-summary-steps}
1. **Read** the text and identify its purpose, its main claim and the structure of its argument ([[academic-reading/claims-and-evidence]]); map it if it is long.
2. **Select**: mark the claim, the topic sentences of the main sections and the most important evidence.
3. **Delete** examples, repetitions, minor details, and background that is not part of the text's own argument.
4. **Generalise**: replace lists of particular items with a general term that covers them (*buses, trams and trains* → *public transport*).
5. **Write from your notes, not from the text**: put the text aside and write in your own words, beginning with the author and the claim (*Tanaka and Osei (2024) argue that …*).
6. **Check** against the text: is every point accurate, are the main points all there and in proportion, are the hedges kept, and are there no phrases copied from the source?
:::

::: proof
Steps 2–4 correspond to the **macrorules** that Kintsch and van Dijk (1978) proposed as the operations readers use to form the gist of a text: deleting propositions not needed to understand others, generalising a series of propositions into one that covers them, and constructing a statement that a series of propositions implies. Brown and Day (1983) studied how students learn to summarise and found that the simpler rules — deleting trivial and redundant material — are mastered earlier than selecting or inventing a topic sentence that covers a whole passage, which develops later and continues to challenge many university students. Step 5 is the most important for writing in your own words: if the text is in front of you, its sentences will shape yours ([[academic-reading/paraphrase-and-quotation]]). Step 6 protects the reader of your summary, who will rely on it as an accurate account of the source.
:::

::: example A one-sentence summary {#ex-one-sentence}
Write a one-sentence summary of the green space passage.
::: solution
**Identify the parts:** author and year — Tanaka and Osei (2024); method — a six-year study of 1,400 adults who moved house; claim — moving to greener areas was followed by a small, lasting improvement in well-being; significance — stronger evidence of cause than earlier surveys.

**Choose a reporting verb that fits the stance.** The finding is reported as a result, with limits: *found* fits better than *proved* or *showed beyond doubt*.

**Write it:** *Following 1,400 adults who moved house over six years, Tanaka and Osei (2024) found that moving to a greener neighbourhood was followed by a small but lasting improvement in mental well-being.*

**Check:** accurate (yes), the main point (yes), hedges kept (*small*), own words (no phrase of more than two or three words is copied, apart from technical terms such as *mental well-being*). The limitation is left out, which is acceptable at this length; a paragraph summary would include it.
:::
:::

::: example A paragraph summary {#ex-paragraph-summary}
Expand the one-sentence summary to a paragraph of about 80–100 words.
::: solution
*Most evidence that green space benefits mental health comes from surveys at a single point in time, which cannot show whether green space improves well-being or whether healthier people choose greener areas. Tanaka and Osei (2024) addressed this by following 1,400 adults in three cities who moved house over six years. Those who moved to greener areas reported a small improvement in well-being that began within a year and lasted, whereas those who moved to less green areas did not report a corresponding decline. The authors acknowledge that people who move house may not be typical, and that their satellite-based measure of green space does not capture access or quality.* (99 words)

**What was kept:** the problem the study addresses, the design, both findings, both limitations — in the proportions of the original.

**What was cut:** *widely thought* (generalised into *most evidence*), the details of satellite images (generalised).

**Voice:** the summary reports the authors' position with *addressed*, *reported* (for the participants) and *acknowledge* (for the limitations); it adds no opinion of the summariser's.
:::
:::

::: theorem What makes a good summary {#rule-good-summary}
A good summary is:

1. **accurate**: nothing in it contradicts or distorts the source;
2. **complete** in its main points, and **proportionate**: what the source treats as central is central in the summary;
3. **neutral**: the source's view, not the summariser's;
4. **in the summariser's own words and structure**, with technical terms kept;
5. **attributed**: it is clear throughout that these are the source's ideas (*the authors argue …*, *they also found …*);
6. **faithful to the stance**: hedges, limits and the source's own degree of commitment are kept.
:::

::: example Evaluating a summary {#ex-evaluate-summary}
Evaluate this student's summary of the green space passage using [[#rule-good-summary]]. *Green space is very good for mental health. Tanaka and Osei proved this with a big study of 1,400 people who moved house, which showed that moving to a greener area makes people happier. This shows that cities should build more parks. However, people who move are not typical of the whole population.*
::: solution
**Accuracy:** *proved* and *makes people happier* overstate a *small* improvement in *reported well-being*; *very good* is not in the source.

**Completeness and proportion:** the problem with earlier evidence — the reason the study matters — is missing; so is the second finding (no decline for those moving to less green areas) and the second limitation.

**Neutrality:** *This shows that cities should build more parks* is the student's own policy conclusion, not the source's.

**Own words:** *people who move are not typical of the whole population* is copied from the passage without quotation marks.

**Attribution:** the first sentence is not attributed, so it reads as the student's claim; the year is missing.

**Stance:** the hedge *small* is lost; *proved* is a booster the source never used.

**Verdict:** the summary needs rewriting from notes, as in [[#ex-paragraph-summary]].
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence belongs in a **summary** of an article, rather than in a response to it?
- [ ] *This is a convincing study, although the sample is rather small.*
- [x] *The authors conclude that early feedback may encourage more extensive revision.*
- [ ] *I agree with the authors that feedback is important.*
- [ ] *Teachers should therefore give feedback within a week.*
::: solution
A summary reports the source's ideas. The second option attributes a conclusion to the authors and keeps their hedge (*may*). The first and third give the summariser's evaluation, and the fourth draws the summariser's own recommendation.
:::
:::

## Summarising several sources

In essays and literature reviews, summaries rarely stand alone. Writers summarise several sources together, organised by the question rather than by the sources, so that the reader sees where they agree and disagree. This is **synthesis**, and a synthesis table is the natural way to prepare for it:

| source | design and sample | main finding | limitations | ME |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Survey A (2019) | cross-sectional, 8,000 adults | greener area ↔ better well-being | direction unclear | the classic result |
| Tanaka & Osei (2024) | longitudinal, 1,400 movers | moving to greener area → small lasting gain | movers atypical; crude measure | strongest causal evidence so far |
| Study C (2022) | experiment, 90 students; walks in park vs street | short-term mood gain after park walk | short-term only | different question: visits, not living nearby |

From such a table, a writer can say *Several studies have found an association between green space and well-being (Survey A 2019); longitudinal evidence suggests that at least part of this association may be causal (Tanaka & Osei 2024), and experimental studies show short-term benefits of visits (Study C 2022)*. The sources here are fictional; the technique is the one taught for literature reviews in [[academic-writing/literature-review]].

::: note English and Chinese
- **概括中心思想 and summary.** Chinese schools train students to 概括中心思想 (sum up the central idea) and to write 缩写 (shortened versions). Those skills transfer directly. Two conventions of English academic summary may be less familiar: summaries are **attributed** throughout (*the authors argue*, *they found*), and they keep the source's **hedges** — a summary that turns *may* into a definite statement is considered inaccurate.
- **Varying reporting verbs.** Chinese summaries often use 作者认为 for everything. English summaries choose verbs that show what the source is doing and how firmly: *argues*, *finds*, *reports*, *suggests*, *concludes*, *acknowledges*, *questions* ([[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging#rule-reporting-verbs]]).
- **Notes in which language?** Notes in Chinese can help understanding and make it impossible to copy English sentences by accident. But when you write in English from Chinese notes, you translate, and your English may become heavy or unidiomatic. Many students find a mixture works: Chinese for their own comments, English key terms and short English phrases for the content, and English quotations with page numbers.
- **Summary and abstract.** 摘要 is used for both *abstract* (the summary that the author writes before an article) and *summary* (one written by someone else). In English academic usage, an *abstract* is part of the article; a *summary* is your account of a text.
:::

::: history
Systematic methods of note-taking were taught as part of study skills in the mid-twentieth century; Walter Pauk, who directed a reading and study centre at Cornell University, described the divided page now known as Cornell notes in *How to Study in College* (1962). Cognitive psychology gave summarising a theory. Walter Kintsch and Teun van Dijk's model of text comprehension (1978) proposed that readers build a **macrostructure** of a text — its gist — by deleting, generalising and constructing propositions, and Ann Brown and Jeanne Day (1983) used these macrorules to study how summarising develops from childhood to adulthood. More recently, Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer's 2014 study of handwritten and laptop notes attracted wide attention, and the mixed results of later replications have become an example of why a single study should not settle a question ([[academic-reading/evaluating-sources]]).
:::

## Where this leads

Restating single passages and deciding when to quote them is the subject of [[academic-reading/paraphrase-and-quotation]]. Summaries are the raw material of literature reviews ([[academic-writing/literature-review]]) and of the background sections of essays ([[essay-writing/arguing-a-point]]); attribution and citation are taught in [[academic-writing/citation-referencing]].

::: summary
- Good notes start with the **full reference**, record the **claim** and **support** in your own words, put **quotations** in quotation marks with **page numbers**, and mark **your own comments** separately ([[#rule-good-notes]]).
- Formats include **outline**, **Cornell** (Pauk 1962), **concept maps**, **annotation** and **synthesis tables**; choose by purpose.
- Never copy sentences into notes without quotation marks: it is the commonest route to accidental plagiarism.
- A **summary** restates a text's main ideas, in proportion, in your own words, attributed and without your opinion ([[#def-summary]]).
- Summarise by **selecting**, **deleting** and **generalising** (Kintsch & van Dijk 1978), then **write from notes** and **check** ([[#rule-summary-steps]]).
- A good summary is accurate, complete, proportionate, neutral, attributed, in your own words and **faithful to the source's stance** ([[#rule-good-summary]]).
:::

Take notes on this fictional passage in a format of your choice, close the passage, and write a summary of 60–80 words from your notes. Then compare your summary with the passage and mark any phrase of four or more words that you copied.

> *Over the past two decades, many universities have replaced traditional lectures in introductory science courses with "active learning", in which students spend class time solving problems, discussing questions in groups and explaining ideas to one another.
