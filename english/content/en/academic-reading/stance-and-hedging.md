Here are four sentences that might end four different articles on the same question:

> *(a) Bilingualism protects against memory decline in old age.*
>
> *(b) Bilingualism appears to offer some protection against memory decline in old age.*
>
> *(c) These results suggest that bilingualism may offer some protection against memory decline, at least in this population.*
>
> *(d) It is possible that bilingualism plays a minor role in protecting against memory decline, although the evidence remains inconclusive.*

A reader who translates each into Chinese as roughly "bilingualism protects memory" has missed almost everything that differs between them. Sentence (a) states a general fact without reservation. Sentence (d) says little more than that the idea has not been ruled out. In between, (b) and (c) commit to the claim with care, (c) more carefully than (b) and with a limit on whom it applies to. In academic writing these differences are the meaning: they tell the reader how much the writer thinks the evidence shows.

This chapter is about how writers show their **stance** — their degree of certainty, their attitude, and their position towards other people's claims. It describes **hedges** and **boosters**, **attitude markers**, the **reporting verbs** that show how a writer regards a source, and how to read all of them accurately. The grammar of modal verbs and of hedging in general English is covered in [[modality/hedging-boosting]]; here the focus is on reading academic texts.

## Stance

::: definition Stance {#def-stance}
A writer's **stance** (立场, 态度) is the way the writer expresses a personal position in a text: how certain they are of a claim, how they feel about it, and how they present themselves. Hyland (2005) describes four main resources of stance in academic writing:

1. **hedges**, which reduce commitment to a claim (*may*, *possibly*, *suggest*);
2. **boosters**, which increase it (*clearly*, *demonstrate*, *in fact*);
3. **attitude markers**, which express the writer's feelings or evaluation (*surprisingly*, *unfortunately*, *important*, *remarkable*);
4. **self-mention**, the writer's reference to themselves (*I*, *we*, *our*, *the present author*).
:::

Stance is not the opposite of objectivity. Academic writing is often described as "objective", but every claim in it is made by someone with a degree of confidence, and the conventions of the genre require writers to show that degree. A text without any stance would be impossible to read critically, because the reader could not tell established findings from speculation.

## Hedges and boosters

::: definition Hedge and booster {#def-hedge}
A **hedge** (模糊限制语, 缓和语) is a word or phrase that signals that the writer is not fully committed to the truth of a claim, or that the claim holds only approximately or under conditions. A **booster** (增强语) signals strong commitment or certainty. The term *hedge* in this sense comes from Lakoff (1972), who used it for words that make meanings "fuzzier"; Hyland (1998) studied hedges in research articles in detail.
:::

| kind | hedges | boosters |
|---|---|---|
| modal verbs | *may*, *might*, *could*, *would* (*this would explain*) | *must* (*this must be due to*), *will* (in predictions), *cannot* |
| lexical verbs | *suggest*, *indicate*, *appear*, *seem*, *tend to*, *assume*, *propose* | *show*, *demonstrate*, *prove*, *establish*, *confirm* |
| adverbs | *possibly*, *perhaps*, *probably*, *likely*, *generally*, *largely*, *relatively* | *clearly*, *certainly*, *obviously*, *undoubtedly*, *definitely*, *indeed*, *in fact* |
| adjectives and nouns | *possible*, *likely*, *a tendency*, *a possibility* | *clear*, *evident*, *certain*, *well known*, *beyond doubt* |
| approximators | *about*, *approximately*, *around*, *roughly*, *somewhat*, *to some extent* | *exactly*, *precisely*, *always*, *never*, *all* |
| limiting phrases | *in this sample*, *under these conditions*, *to our knowledge*, *at least in part* | *in every case*, *without exception* |
| impersonal frames | *it is possible that*, *it may be that*, *there is some evidence that* | *it is clear that*, *it is well established that*, *there is no doubt that* |

::: theorem Why academic writers hedge {#rule-why-hedge}
Academic writers hedge for several reasons at once:

1. **accuracy**: to make the strength of a claim match the strength of the evidence (*may* when the result is from one small study);
2. **protection**: to avoid being shown wrong by later evidence, and to anticipate criticism from reviewers;
3. **politeness**: to leave room for readers to disagree and to avoid presenting claims as more important than other researchers' work (Myers 1989);
4. **convention**: because hedged claims are the expected style of the genre — so a hedge does not always mean that the writer has real doubts.

Boosters are used more sparingly, for claims the writer regards as established or wants to stress, and to show solidarity with readers who share background knowledge (*as is well known*).
:::

::: proof
Research findings are rarely certain: they come from samples, they depend on methods, and they may be overturned. A claim stated without hedges is a claim that the writer is prepared to defend in all conditions, and reviewers and readers will hold the writer to it. Hedging therefore makes claims more **precise**, not vaguer: *may reduce the risk in older adults* says exactly what the writer thinks is known. Myers (1989) argued that hedges in scientific articles are also a form of politeness towards the research community, because a strong new claim implicitly challenges others' work. In his study of research articles in biology, Hyland (1998) found hedges to be very frequent, and concentrated in the sections where results are interpreted rather than in the methods; his later comparisons across disciplines (Hyland 2005) found hedges more frequent in the "soft" fields (social sciences and humanities) than in the "hard" sciences. Point 4 is a caution for readers: in a genre where nearly every claim is hedged, *suggest* is the normal verb for a solid result, and only stronger hedging (*It is possible that … might …*) signals real uncertainty.
:::

::: theorem Reading a hedged claim {#rule-read-hedge}
1. Separate the **claim** (the proposition) from the **stance** (the hedges, boosters and limits around it).
2. Note the **degree of commitment** (strong, moderate, tentative) and any **limits** (to a sample, a condition, a part of the effect).
3. **Keep** the degree and the limits when you summarise or cite the claim. Do not turn *may reduce* into *reduces*, or *in older adults* into *in people*.
4. Compare the commitment with the **evidence**: is the writer overclaiming (strong words for weak evidence) or, less often, underclaiming?
5. Remember the **convention**: a single *may* or *suggest* in a discussion section is normal caution, not a sign that the writer doubts the result.
:::

::: example Separating claim and stance {#ex-claim-stance}
Separate the claim from the stance in this fictional sentence: *These findings suggest that, at least among younger learners, regular exposure to subtitled video may contribute to vocabulary growth, although its effect appears to be relatively small.*
::: solution
**Claim (proposition):** exposure to subtitled video contributes to vocabulary growth.

**Stance and limits:**
- *These findings suggest that* — the claim is the authors' interpretation of their results; moderate commitment.
- *at least among younger learners* — limit to a population; *at least* leaves open that it may hold more widely.
- *regular* — limit to a condition: frequent exposure, not occasional.
- *may* — a second hedge, adding caution.
- *contribute to* — a weak causal verb: one of several causes, not *cause*.
- *although its effect appears to be relatively small* — a concession; *appears* and *relatively* hedge even this.

**Summary that keeps the stance:** *The authors tentatively conclude that regular subtitled video may help younger learners' vocabulary a little.* A summary that dropped it — *Subtitled video improves vocabulary* — would claim far more than the authors did.
:::
:::

::: warning Dropping the hedges
✗ Source: *The intervention may reduce anxiety in some students.* Your note: *The intervention reduces anxiety.*

✓ Your note: *The intervention may reduce anxiety for some students (the authors are cautious).*

When readers take notes or summarise, hedges are the first words to disappear, because they seem to add no information. They add a great deal: the difference between *may reduce … in some* and *reduces* is the difference between a possibility in part of a group and a general fact. The opposite error also happens: readers who are not used to English hedging sometimes take *may* as meaning *probably not*. It means the writer thinks the claim is possible and worth stating, and does not want to say more than the evidence allows.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence makes the **strongest** claim?
- [ ] *The data indicate a possible link between noise and poor sleep.*
- [ ] *Noise may be one factor in poor sleep.*
- [x] *The data clearly demonstrate that noise disrupts sleep.*
- [ ] *It seems likely that noise affects sleep to some extent.*
::: solution
*clearly* and *demonstrate* are both boosters, and *disrupts* is an unhedged causal verb. The other three are hedged: *indicate a possible link*, *may be one factor*, *seems likely … to some extent*.
:::
:::

::: example Overclaiming {#ex-overclaim}
A fictional abstract reports: *In a pilot study with 14 participants, a mindfulness app was associated with lower self-reported stress after two weeks.* The conclusion says: *Mindfulness apps are clearly an effective treatment for stress and should be offered to all employees.* Identify the overclaiming.
::: solution
**Evidence:** a pilot (small, preliminary) study; 14 participants; *associated with* (no claim of cause in the results); self-reported stress; two weeks; one app.

**Conclusion's stance:** *clearly* (booster); *an effective treatment* (causal and clinical); *Mindfulness apps* (all apps, not this one); *should be offered to all employees* (a policy claim for a different, much larger population).

**Mismatch:** every element of the conclusion is stronger than the evidence. A fitting conclusion: *These preliminary results suggest that the app may be associated with lower stress; a larger controlled trial is needed.* When the conclusion of an abstract is stronger than its results, believe the results ([[academic-reading/academic-texts#rule-reading-abstract]]).
:::
:::

## Attitude and self-mention

Hedges and boosters concern how **certain** a writer is. **Attitude markers** show how the writer **evaluates** what is reported: *surprisingly*, *interestingly*, *unfortunately*, *importantly*, *it is striking that*, *a crucial step*, *a serious limitation*, *a promising approach*. They are less frequent than hedges, but they guide the reader's attention: *Surprisingly, the effect was larger in the older group* tells the reader that this result goes against expectations and deserves notice.

**Self-mention** — *I*, *we*, *our*, *my* — shows the writer taking responsibility for a choice or a claim: *We argue that …*, *In my view …*, *Our results show …*. Its frequency differs between disciplines: Hyland (2005) found self-mention much more common in philosophy, marketing and other "soft" fields than in physics, biology and engineering, where impersonal forms (*The results show …*, *It was found that …*) are more usual. For a reader, self-mention marks the places where the writer's own position is stated most directly — a useful signal when you need to separate the writer's claim from reported views.

::: example Finding the writer's own voice {#ex-voices}
This fictional paragraph mixes several voices. Say whose view each sentence gives, and what the writer's own position is. *(1) It is widely believed that students learn best when teaching matches their preferred "learning style". (2) Many teachers report adapting their lessons in this way. (3) Unfortunately, controlled studies have consistently failed to find that matching teaching to learning styles improves results. (4) We would argue that the time spent on such matching is better used elsewhere.*
::: solution
**Sentence 1:** a common belief, reported impersonally (*It is widely believed*). Not the writer's view — the frame signals a belief that may be about to be questioned.

**Sentence 2:** teachers' reports (*report*, a neutral reporting verb). A fact about what teachers say, not a claim that the method works.

**Sentence 3:** the evidence from research, presented as established (*consistently failed to find*), with the writer's attitude marked by *Unfortunately*: the writer regrets that the popular belief is not supported. *failed* is itself evaluative: the studies looked for the effect and did not find it.

**Sentence 4:** the writer's own position, marked by self-mention (*We*) and hedged (*would argue*): time spent on matching would be better used on other things.

**The writer's position:** matching teaching to learning styles is not supported by evidence, so teachers should spend their time on something else. The hedge in sentence 4 applies to the recommendation, not to the evidence in sentence 3, which the writer presents as firm.
:::
:::

## Reporting verbs: whose view, and how is it regarded?

Academic texts constantly report other people's claims. The verb used to report a claim shows not only **whose** it is but how the writer **regards** it.

::: theorem What reporting verbs signal {#rule-reporting-verbs}
1. **Neutral** verbs report without judging: *X states*, *describes*, *reports*, *notes*, *argues*, *proposes*, *examines*.
2. **Positive** (factive) verbs present the claim as true; the writer accepts it: *X shows*, *demonstrates*, *establishes*, *confirms*, *points out*, *recognises*.
3. **Tentative** verbs present the claim as cautious on the source's part: *X suggests*, *speculates*, *hypothesises*, *implies*.
4. **Distancing** and **critical** verbs show that the writer does not accept the claim or finds fault: *X claims*, *asserts*, *alleges*, *believes*; *X fails to consider*, *overlooks*, *exaggerates*, *ignores*.

Read every report of another source for two things: the claim, and the writer's stance towards it.
:::

::: proof
Thompson and Ye (1991), in a study of reporting verbs in academic papers, distinguished the stance of the cited author (whether they presented a claim as true, tentative or false) from the stance of the citing writer (whether the writer accepts the claim or not), and showed that verbs encode both. *X shows that …* commits the writer to the claim: you cannot write *X shows that the earth is flat* without seeming to agree. *X claims that …* reports the same proposition but leaves the writer free to reject it, and in academic contexts often suggests doubt. The categories overlap — *argues* is neutral in most contexts but can introduce a view the writer will reject — and the rest of the paragraph usually settles it. The choice of verbs in your own writing is taught in [[academic-writing/citation-referencing]].
:::

::: example Reading reporting verbs {#ex-reporting}
What is the writer's stance towards each source in this fictional literature review? *Li (2015) demonstrated that vocabulary size predicts reading comprehension in a second language. Brown (2018) claims that vocabulary matters less than grammar for advanced readers, but her study included only twelve participants. More recently, Osei and Park (2022) have suggested that the relative importance of the two may change with proficiency.*
::: solution
**Li (2015):** *demonstrated* — a positive verb: the writer accepts Li's finding as established.

**Brown (2018):** *claims* — a distancing verb, and the writer immediately adds a criticism (*but her study included only twelve participants*). The writer does not accept Brown's view, or at least thinks it is poorly supported.

**Osei and Park (2022):** *have suggested* — a tentative verb: Osei and Park present the idea cautiously, and the writer reports it as a possibility without either endorsing or rejecting it. *More recently* and the content (*may change with proficiency*) suggest that the writer sees this as a way to reconcile Li and Brown — a likely direction for the writer's own argument.
:::
:::

::: quiz
*Several studies have claimed that background music improves concentration.* What does *claimed* suggest about the writer's view?
- [ ] the writer accepts the studies' finding as established
- [x] the writer reports the finding without accepting it, and may go on to question it
- [ ] the writer did these studies
- [ ] the studies were never published
::: solution
*claim* is a distancing verb: it reports what the studies said while leaving the writer free to disagree. Compare *Several studies have shown*, which would commit the writer to the finding. Expect a *however* soon.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Hedges in both languages.** Chinese academic writing hedges too: 可能, 或许, 也许, 似乎, 大概, 在一定程度上, 一般来说, 初步表明. Comparative studies suggest that the conventions differ in frequency: Hu and Cao (2011), comparing abstracts of applied linguistics articles in English-medium and Chinese-medium journals, found that hedges were more frequent in the English-medium abstracts. Readers moving between the two should expect English academic texts to hedge more often, and should not read the hedges as weakness.
- ***Suggest* and 表明.** In translation, *show*, *indicate* and *suggest* are often all rendered as 表明, which loses the scale of [[#rule-read-hedge]]. When you take notes from English, keep the English verb, or use a Chinese equivalent that keeps the strength (*suggest* → 提示, 初步表明; *show* → 表明, 显示; *demonstrate* → 证明).
- **Self-mention.** Chinese academic articles more often refer to the writer as 笔者, 本文 or 本研究 than with 我 or 我们. English academic writing uses *we* widely in the sciences (even, in some fields, for a single author) and *I* in many humanities fields. Self-mention in English is not informality; it marks the writer's own position.
- **Disagreeing politely.** English reviews disagree with named sources through reporting verbs (*claims*, *overlooks*) and contrasts (*however*), often without any openly critical adjective. The criticism is in the verb: notice it.
:::

::: history
The linguist George Lakoff introduced *hedge* as a technical term in a 1972 paper on words such as *sort of* and *technically*, which make category membership a matter of degree. Studies of science soon showed that hedging was central to how knowledge is made. In *Laboratory Life* (1979), Bruno Latour and Steve Woolgar observed that a statement in a laboratory became a "fact" as the qualifications around it (*X has suggested that A may be …*) were dropped in later papers, and lost that status when qualifications were added. Greg Myers (1989) analysed hedges in biology articles as politeness towards the scientific community. Ken Hyland's *Hedging in Scientific Research Articles* (1998) and *Metadiscourse* (2005), based on large corpora, gave the descriptions of hedges, boosters, attitude markers and self-mention that are used in most teaching of academic English today.
:::

::: summary
- **Stance** is how a writer shows certainty, attitude and self: hedges, boosters, attitude markers and self-mention (Hyland 2005; [[#def-stance]]).
- **Hedges** (*may*, *suggest*, *possibly*, *to some extent*, *in this sample*) reduce commitment; **boosters** (*clearly*, *demonstrate*, *in fact*) increase it ([[#def-hedge]]).
- Writers hedge for **accuracy**, **protection**, **politeness** and **convention**; a single *suggest* or *may* is normal caution, not doubt ([[#rule-why-hedge]]).

- **Overclaiming** — strong words for weak evidence — is a warning sign, especially in conclusions and abstracts.
- **Reporting verbs** show whether the writer accepts (*shows*), reports neutrally (*argues*), finds tentative (*suggests*) or distances themselves from (*claims*) a source's claim ([[#rule-reporting-verbs]]).

::: exercise Find the stance words {level=1}
Underline (or list) the hedges, boosters and attitude markers. *Interestingly, the effect was considerably larger in rural schools. This may reflect the smaller class sizes there, although it is clear that other factors also played a part.*
::: solution
Attitude marker: *Interestingly*. Booster: *considerably* (it intensifies the size; some analyses treat it as an intensifier rather than a booster of certainty), *it is clear that*. Hedges: *may*, *reflect* (a cautious way of stating an explanation). *although* introduces a concession.

::: exercise Rank by strength {level=2}
Rank from strongest to weakest commitment. (a) *Diet may play a role in the condition.* (b) *Diet is the main cause of the condition.* (c) *Diet appears to be an important factor in the condition.* (d) *It cannot be ruled out that diet has some influence on the condition.*
::: solution
(b) — unhedged, *main cause*; (c) — *appears*, but *important factor*; (a) — *may*, *a role*; (d) — the weakest: the writer says only that an influence has not been excluded.
:::

::: exercise Keep the stance {level=2}
Write a one-sentence note on each source that keeps its stance. (a) *Our results provide some preliminary evidence that peer tutoring may improve attendance among first-year students.* (b) *There is now overwhelming evidence that smoking causes lung cancer.*
::: solution
(a) *The authors report preliminary evidence that peer tutoring may improve first-year students' attendance.* (Keep *preliminary*, *may*, *first-year*.) (b) *The writer states that the evidence that smoking causes lung cancer is overwhelming.* (Keep the booster; here the strong commitment is part of the content.)
:::

::: exercise Fix the summary {level=2}
A student summarised a fictional source as: *Wang (2021) proved that online homework improves maths scores in all secondary schools.* The source said: *In the two schools studied, pupils who completed online homework tended to score somewhat higher in maths, although the difference was not statistically significant.* What is wrong, and how should the summary read?
::: solution
The summary changes the stance (*proved* for a non-significant tendency), removes the limit (*two schools* → *all secondary schools*), drops *somewhat*, and turns *tended to score higher* into a causal claim (*improves*). A fair summary: *Wang (2021) found that, in two schools, pupils who did online homework tended to score slightly higher in maths, but the difference was not statistically significant.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Analyse a discussion paragraph {level=3}
Mark every stance feature in this fictional paragraph (hedges, boosters, attitude markers, self-mention, reporting verbs), then state in one sentence what the writers claim and how strongly.

> *Our findings clearly show that students who received written feedback within one week revised their essays more extensively than those who received it later. It is less clear why. One possibility is that early feedback arrives while the essay is still fresh in the writer's mind; another is that students simply had more time to revise. Ahmed (2019) argues that the timing of feedback matters less than its quality, but his study did not compare different timings directly. We would therefore suggest, tentatively, that timing deserves more attention than it has received.*
::: hint
The paragraph contains two different claims with two different degrees of commitment.
:::
::: solution
**Stance features:** *Our* (self-mention); *clearly show* (boosters); *It is less clear why* (a hedge on the explanation); *One possibility … another …* (hedging by presenting alternatives); *simply* (attitude: the second explanation is a plain, less interesting one); *argues* (neutral reporting verb) followed by *but his study did not compare …* (criticism); *We* (self-mention); *would*, *suggest*, *tentatively* (three hedges); *deserves more attention than it has received* (attitude: evaluation of the field).

**Claims and commitment:** The writers are **certain** that earlier feedback was followed by more extensive revision in their study, **uncertain** why, and **tentative** in recommending that researchers pay more attention to the timing of feedback. A good answer notices that the strong booster applies only to the finding, not to the explanation or the recommendation.
:::
:::

::: exercise Rewrite with appropriate stance {level=3}
Rewrite this overclaiming paragraph so that its stance fits the evidence described in it. *We have proved that learning music makes children better at maths. In our survey of 200 families, children who had music lessons had higher maths marks. This is obviously because music trains the brain to think mathematically. All schools must therefore teach music from the age of five.*
::: solution
A model: *In our survey of 200 families, children who had music lessons tended to have higher maths marks. This association may reflect an effect of music training on mathematical skills, but it could also be explained by other differences between families, such as income or parents' education, which our survey did not measure. If the association is confirmed in controlled studies, there may be a case for wider access to music lessons.* Points to check: *proved* and *obviously* removed; the causal verb *makes* replaced by an association; the explanation presented as one possibility with an alternative; the policy claim made conditional and moderated (*must* → *there may be a case*).
:::
