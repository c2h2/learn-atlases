In the first week of a master's course, Chen Yu receives a reading list for one module: three textbook chapters, two review articles and eleven research articles, about 350 pages in all, to be discussed over three weeks. Reading at the pace she uses for her English textbook — carefully, every sentence, looking up each unknown word — would take her far longer than three weeks. She is not expected to read it like that. Nobody on the course reads everything closely; the students who cope are the ones who decide, for each text, what they need from it and how much of it they must read to get that.

This chapter is about those decisions. It shows how to **survey** a text before reading it, how to turn its structure into **questions**, how to choose for each part whether to read closely, skim or skip, and how to manage a long reading list — which texts to read first, how to find more, and how to keep track. It builds on the three ways of reading of [[reading-skills/reading-with-a-purpose]] and the map of academic texts in [[academic-reading/academic-texts]]. None of it replaces close reading: its purpose is to make sure that close reading is spent on the pages that matter.

## Reading with a plan

::: definition Strategic reading {#def-strategic-reading}
**Strategic reading** is reading with a plan: the reader decides **why** they are reading a text, **what** they need from it, and therefore **which parts** to read and **how closely**, and checks during and after reading whether the need has been met. Its main stages are **surveying** the text, **setting questions**, **reading selectively** and **reviewing**.
:::

The purposes of academic reading are more varied than those of everyday reading. A student may read:

- to get an **overview** of a topic before a lecture or before choosing an essay question;
- to **answer a question**: what is known about X, how Y is usually measured, whether Z has been studied in China;
- to **understand a concept or method** in depth, because later work depends on it;
- to **evaluate** a study or an argument, for a seminar or a critical review;
- to find **evidence and sources** to cite in one's own writing.

Each purpose calls for a different kind of reading. An overview needs a review article skimmed from end to end; evaluating a study needs its methods and results read slowly; finding sources needs the reference lists scanned. Deciding the purpose first is the step that most inexperienced readers skip.

::: theorem Let the task decide the depth {#rule-depth}
For each text, and for each part of a long text, choose one of three depths:

1. **Read closely** the parts that answer your question or that you must evaluate or cite: every sentence, with notes.
2. **Skim** the parts that give context: first sentences of paragraphs, headings, figures and their captions.
3. **Skip** the parts you do not need for this task, and note that you skipped them, so that you can return if the task changes.

Decide before you start and adjust as you go. Most academic texts are read at all three depths at once, in different sections.
:::

::: proof
Reading time is limited, and comprehension costs time: research on reading speed shows a trade-off between speed and accuracy, and techniques that promise much faster reading with full comprehension have not been supported by evidence (Rayner et al. 2016). A reader therefore cannot read everything closely and must choose. The choice is safe because academic texts are organised so that their parts can be read separately ([[academic-reading/academic-texts#rule-imrad-questions]]): the methods of a study can be judged without the literature review, and the main finding can be found without the statistics. Mortimer Adler made the same point in *How to Read a Book* (revised edition with Charles Van Doren, 1972), where an **inspectional** reading — a quick, systematic look at the whole — comes before the slow **analytical** reading of what deserves it. The rule fails only when the reader skips before knowing what a part contains; that is why the survey comes first.
:::

## Surveying a text

::: definition Survey {#def-survey}
To **survey** (or **preview**) a text is to look over its whole structure quickly before reading it, in order to learn what it is about, how it is organised and where the information you need is likely to be. For an article, a survey usually takes five to fifteen minutes; for a book, a little longer.
:::

::: theorem What to look at in a survey {#rule-survey}
1. **Title and author details**: the topic, the field, the date — and whether the text is recent enough for your purpose.
2. **Abstract**, or a book's preface and contents page.
3. **Headings and subheadings**: the plan of the argument.
4. **Introduction**, especially its last paragraph, and the **conclusion**.
5. **Figures and tables**, with their captions: they often carry the main results.
6. The **first sentence of each paragraph** in the sections that look relevant.
7. The **reference list**: how long it is, how recent, and which names recur.

At the end, say in one or two sentences what the text claims and which parts you will read closely.
:::

Here is the start of a survey of a fictional textbook chapter. Its contents read:

> *Chapter 6: Language and Memory. 6.1 What is working memory? 6.2 Measuring working memory span. 6.3 Working memory and reading in a first language. 6.4 Working memory and second-language learning. 6.5 Can working memory be trained? Summary. Review questions. Further reading.*

The chapter's summary begins: *Working memory is a limited system for holding and manipulating information over short periods. Its capacity differs between individuals and is related to reading comprehension in both first and second languages. Training programmes improve performance on the tasks that are trained, but there is little evidence that the improvement transfers to reading or other skills.*

::: example Turning a survey into questions {#ex-survey-questions}
You are writing an essay on *What helps adults learn a second language?* Using only the contents and the summary above, (a) decide which sections of the chapter to read closely, skim or skip, and (b) write the questions you will read to answer.
::: solution
**Purpose first.** The essay is about second-language learning in adults, so the chapter matters only where working memory connects to that.

**(a) Depths.**
- 6.1 *What is working memory?* — **skim**: you need the definition (probably in a bold term or a box) but not every detail.
- 6.2 *Measuring working memory span* — **skim** or **skip**: useful only if you will discuss studies that measure it.
- 6.3 *Working memory and reading in a first language* — **skim**: background.
- 6.4 *Working memory and second-language learning* — **read closely**: this is the section that answers your question.
- 6.5 *Can working memory be trained?* — **read closely**: if training does not transfer (as the summary says), that is a useful point for an essay about what helps learners.
- Review questions and further reading — **scan** the further reading for sources on second-language learning.

**(b) Questions.** Turn headings into questions, then make them specific to the essay:
- *What is working memory, in one sentence I can cite?*
- *How strongly is working memory related to second-language learning, and in which skills?*
- *If working memory differs between people, what can learners with a smaller span do?*
- *What is the evidence that training working memory does or does not help language learning?*

These questions turn reading into a search for answers, and they show at once which parts of section 6.4 matter.
:::
:::

::: quiz
You are surveying a research article to decide whether it is relevant to your essay. Which of these is **least** useful at the survey stage?
- [ ] the abstract
- [ ] the headings and the figure captions
- [x] the detailed description of the statistical analysis
- [ ] the last paragraph of the introduction
::: solution
A survey looks at the parts that show what the text is about and how it is organised. The statistical details belong to a close reading of the methods, which comes later, and only if the article turns out to be relevant.
:::
:::

## Setting questions

Readers understand and remember more when they read to answer questions they have set themselves. The idea is at the centre of the oldest well-known study method.

::: definition SQ3R {#def-sq3r}
**SQ3R** is a method for studying a textbook described by Francis P. Robinson in *Effective Study* (1946). Its five steps are:

1. **Survey** the chapter: headings, summary, figures;
2. **Question**: turn each heading into a question;
3. **Read** each section to answer its question;
4. **Recite**: without looking, say or write the answer;
5. **Review**: at the end, go over the questions and answers again, and later return to them.
:::

The value of the method is not in the five letters but in two of its ideas. The first is reading **for answers**: a heading such as *Causes of the 1929 crash* becomes *What caused the 1929 crash?*, and the reader looks for the causes instead of reading every sentence with equal attention. The second is **recall without looking**. In a large review of study techniques, Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated **practice testing** — trying to recall information, as in the *recite* step — and **distributed practice** — reviewing at spaced intervals — as the techniques of highest utility, and rated **rereading** and **highlighting** as of low utility. A student who rereads a chapter twice may feel that it is familiar, but familiarity is not the same as being able to recall and use it.

::: warning Highlighting instead of reading
✗ Highlighting most of every page, then rereading the highlights before the seminar.

✓ Reading a section to answer a question, closing the text, and writing the answer in a few words of your own; marking only the sentence that states the claim and the key evidence.

Highlighting feels like work, but it postpones the decision about what matters: if half the page is yellow, nothing has been chosen. Dunlosky et al. (2013) found little evidence that highlighting improves learning in the way students use it. If you mark a text, mark sparingly and with a purpose — a symbol in the margin for *claim*, *evidence*, *definition*, *disagree* — and write a short note in your own words next to it. The method is developed in [[academic-reading/notes-and-summaries]].
:::

## Reading selectively

Selective reading is not careless reading. It is careful about **where** to be careful. Within one research article, a student evaluating the study and a student looking for background would read different sections at different depths:

| section | for an overview of the topic | to evaluate the study | to cite the main finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| abstract | read closely | read closely | read closely |
| introduction | skim; read the review of earlier work for sources | skim; read the gap and aim closely | skim; read the aim |
| methods | skip or skim | read closely | skim: design and sample |
| results | skim figures and tables | read closely | read the main result closely |
| discussion | read the first and last paragraphs | read closely, especially limitations | read the first paragraph and limitations |
| references | scan for useful sources | check key citations | — |

::: example Choosing depths for three tasks {#ex-three-tasks}
A (fictional) article reports a survey of 900 students at five universities about how they use generative AI tools in their studies. Say which sections you would read closely for each task: (a) you need one recent statistic for the introduction of your essay on AI in education; (b) you are presenting this article in a seminar and must comment on its strengths and weaknesses; (c) you are planning your own survey on the same topic.
::: solution
**(a) One statistic.** Read the abstract closely and **scan** the results for the figure you need; read the methods just enough to describe the sample correctly when you cite it (*a survey of 900 students at five universities*) and to check how the question was asked. Skip most of the rest.

**(b) Seminar evaluation.** Read the **methods** closely (how were the universities and students chosen? what exactly were they asked? were answers anonymous?), the **results** closely (do the numbers support the claims?), and the **discussion**, especially the limitations. Skim the introduction for the aim.

**(c) Planning your own survey.** Read the **methods** most closely of all — the questionnaire, the sampling, how the data were analysed — and look for the questionnaire itself in an appendix or supplementary material. The limitations paragraph tells you what the authors would do differently, which is valuable for your own design.

The same article, three different readings: the purpose decided the depth ([[#rule-depth]]).
:::
:::

## Managing a reading list

::: theorem Working through a reading list {#rule-reading-list}
1. **Triage**: survey every item briefly and sort them into *essential*, *useful* and *optional* for the task in hand.
2. **Read in order of generality**: textbook chapter or review first, then the research articles, so that the general picture makes the specific studies easier to read.
3. **Read recent before old** within each group, unless an older text is the classic everyone cites; recent texts summarise what came before.
4. **Follow the references**: from a useful text, look **backwards** at the sources it cites, and **forwards** at later texts that cite it (citation databases and search engines for scholarly literature show these).
5. **Keep a log**: for each text, the full reference, the date you read it, the depth, and two or three lines on what it claims and what you can use.
:::

::: proof
Triage turns an impossible list into a manageable one: the essential items get time, the optional ones get a survey and a line in the log. Reading from general to specific uses the review's summary of the field as background knowledge, which is what makes specialised research articles readable. Recent texts have the advantage of hindsight, and an older text that recent texts all cite is usually worth reading for that reason. Following references backwards and forwards — sometimes called **snowballing** — finds the texts most closely connected to the one you have, more reliably than a keyword search. The log matters because most students forget within weeks which article said what; a record made at the time saves rereading, and it holds the details needed for citation ([[academic-reading/paraphrase-and-quotation]]).
:::

::: example Triage of a reading list {#ex-triage}
For an essay on *Should primary schools teach handwriting or typing first?*, a student has found these (fictional) items. Sort them into essential, useful and optional, and give an order of reading. (1) A 2022 review of research on handwriting and early literacy. (2) A 1995 study of children's letter recognition after handwriting or typing practice, cited in most later work. (3) A 2020 experiment comparing note-taking by hand and on laptops among university students. (4) A national curriculum document describing what primary schools must teach. (5) A 2019 study of 7-year-olds' spelling after a year of typing lessons. (6) A blog post by a teacher arguing that handwriting is old-fashioned.
::: solution
**Essential:** (1), the review — it gives the overview and leads to other studies; (5), a direct study of the age group and the question; (4), because the essay is about what schools should teach, and the curriculum shows what they do now.

**Useful:** (2), the much-cited older study: read it after the review, which will explain why it matters and whether later work has confirmed it.

**Optional:** (3) — university students taking notes is a different question from young children learning to write; it can be mentioned with care, but it is not evidence about primary schools. (6) — a blog post is opinion, not research; it might give an example of a view to discuss, but it is not a source of evidence.

**Order:** (1) review → (5) → (2) → (4) → (3) and (6) if time allows. In the log, the student notes for (3) that its population differs from the essay's topic, so that the point is not forgotten.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A student has found a 2023 article that is exactly on her topic. What is the quickest reliable way to find other closely related texts?
- [ ] search for the same keywords again
- [x] look at the texts it cites and at later texts that cite it
- [ ] read the first ten results of a general web search
- [ ] ask a chatbot for a list of articles on the topic
::: solution
Following citations backwards and forwards finds the texts most closely connected to one you know is relevant. Keyword searches repeat what you have already done; general web searches mix academic and non-academic sources; and chatbots can produce references that look real but do not exist, so any reference from one must be checked in a library database before use.
:::
:::

## Vocabulary and speed

Second-language readers have an extra decision to make: what to do about unknown words. Academic texts contain three kinds of vocabulary beyond the most frequent words of English:

- **Academic vocabulary**: words common across many subjects but not frequent in everyday English — *analyse*, *concept*, *significant*, *derive*, *framework*, *hypothesis*. The best-known description is the **Academic Word List** (Coxhead 2000), 570 word families selected from a corpus of about 3.5 million words of academic texts from four broad areas (arts, commerce, law and science). Coxhead reported that these families made up about 10% of the running words in her academic corpus but far less in fiction, which is why they are worth learning early for academic reading. Academic vocabulary and its lists are taught in [[collocation-and-register/academic-vocabulary]].
- **Technical vocabulary**: the terms of one field — *working memory*, *ozone*, *heteroskedasticity* — usually defined in textbooks and glossaries, and learned with the subject itself.
- **Low-frequency words** that happen to appear in one text.

The strategy differs for each. Learn academic vocabulary as a set, because it will return in every subject. Build a glossary of technical terms for your field, with definitions from a textbook, because they will return in every text of that field. Treat low-frequency words as in [[reading-skills/words-from-context]]: guess from context when the word is not central, and look it up only when the meaning matters to your question.

Reading speed is the other constraint. Academic texts are read more slowly than fiction, and a dense methods section more slowly still; that is normal and not a sign of weak English. Two things make academic reading faster without losing comprehension: background knowledge (which is why reviews and textbooks come first) and familiarity with the vocabulary and structure of the genre, which grows with every article read.

::: example A reading plan for one week {#ex-week-plan}
Chen Yu, from the start of this chapter, has six hours this week for the module's first topic: two textbook chapters (about 30 pages each), one review article (18 pages) and four research articles (12–20 pages each). The seminar question is: *What explains the gap in reading achievement between children from richer and poorer families?* Write a plan.
::: solution
**Step 1 — triage and survey (1 hour).** Survey all seven texts for about eight minutes each: abstract or summary, headings, figures, conclusion. Sort them; decide that one research article is about a different age group (optional) and that the two textbook chapters overlap (read one closely, skim the other).

**Step 2 — overview (1½ hours).** Read the more relevant textbook chapter at mixed depths, turning headings into questions; read the review article's introduction, conclusion and section on explanations of the gap closely, and skim the rest.

**Step 3 — studies (2½ hours).** For the three relevant research articles, read abstract, introduction aim, results figures and discussion closely; read methods closely for the one that the review calls the strongest study, to be able to comment on it.

**Step 4 — review (1 hour).** Without looking, write the answer to the seminar question in five lines, with the sources for each point; check against the log; note two questions to raise in the seminar.

The plan spends most time where the seminar question is answered and leaves a record for later essays. It is a plan, not a timetable to be followed whatever happens: if the review shows that one of the articles is central, the plan changes.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **精读 and 泛读.** Many Chinese learners know two ways of reading from school: 精读 (intensive reading, sentence by sentence, with every word and structure explained) and 泛读 (extensive reading). Strategic reading uses both inside one text — close reading for the parts that matter, skimming for the rest. The habit to unlearn is reading a whole research article as a 精读 text.
- **Reading background in Chinese.** Reading a Chinese textbook or review to understand a new field quickly is a reasonable strategy, and it can make the English texts easier. But the terms you need for writing are the English ones, and they do not always match: keep a two-column glossary (English term, Chinese term, definition in English) as you read.
- **Translation as a last step.** Translating a passage into Chinese can help with one difficult sentence, but it is too slow as a way of reading a reading list, and it hides the English structure that signals what matters. Read for answers in English first; translate only what you still cannot understand.
- **Chinese sources on the list.** For many topics there is Chinese-language research that English reading lists ignore. It may be valuable, but check whether your course expects you to cite it, and how (a translated title with the original in brackets is usual).
:::

::: history
Methods for reading textbooks efficiently date from the first half of the twentieth century, when American universities grew fast and began to teach study skills. Francis P. Robinson, a psychologist at Ohio State University, described SQ3R in *Effective Study* (1946), drawing on wartime courses for military trainees who had to learn a great deal quickly. Mortimer Adler's *How to Read a Book* (1940) addressed general readers; the revised edition written with Charles Van Doren (1972) named four levels of reading, from elementary through inspectional and analytical to syntopical reading — reading several books on one subject together, which is close to what a literature review does. In 2013 John Dunlosky and colleagues reviewed the research on ten study techniques that students commonly use, and their conclusion that self-testing and spaced practice work far better than rereading and highlighting has influenced study advice since.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Read, skim or skip {level=1}
Your task is to find out how a (fictional) study measured students' stress. For each part of the article, say read closely, skim or skip: (a) abstract; (b) introduction; (c) methods, *Measures* subsection; (d) results; (e) acknowledgements.
::: solution
(a) read closely (it may name the measure); (b) skim (the aim only); (c) read closely — this is where the measure is described; (d) skim, or skip unless you need to know how the measure behaved; (e) skip.
:::
:::

::: exercise Headings into questions {level=1}
Turn each textbook heading into a question you could read to answer. (a) *The causes of urban flooding* (b) *Advantages and limitations of online surveys* (c) *Photosynthesis in low light* (d) *The 1978 economic reforms*
::: solution
Possible questions: (a) *What causes flooding in cities?* (b) *What are the advantages of online surveys, and what are their limitations?* (c) *How does photosynthesis change when there is little light?* (d) *What were the 1978 economic reforms, and what did they change?* Any question that the section is likely to answer is acceptable; questions that ask *why* and *how* lead to more useful reading than questions that ask only *what*.
:::
:::

::: exercise Which kind of vocabulary? {level=1}
In a geography article, classify each word as academic vocabulary (A), technical vocabulary of the field (T) or a high-frequency general word (G). (a) *analyse* (b) *permafrost* (c) *significant* (d) *river* (e) *precipitation* (f) *framework*
::: solution
(a) A; (b) T; (c) A; (d) G; (e) T (in geography and meteorology it is a technical term for rain, snow and similar; in general English it has other meanings); (f) A. Some words sit on the border between academic and technical — that is normal, and the point of the classification is the learning strategy, not the label.
:::

::: exercise Survey and summarise {level=2}
Here is the information from a survey of a fictional article. Write two sentences: what the article claims, and which parts you would read closely if your essay is about *how cities can reduce heat in summer*.

> *Title: Cool roofs and street temperatures: evidence from a summer field study. Abstract (last sentence): Roofs painted with reflective coatings lowered nearby street-level air temperatures by a small but measurable amount on the hottest afternoons. Headings: 1 Introduction 2 Study area and methods 3 Results 3.1 Roof surface temperature 3.2 Street air temperature 3.3 Indoor temperature 4 Discussion 5 Conclusions.*
::: solution
A good answer: *The article claims that reflective roof coatings slightly lowered air temperatures in nearby streets on the hottest afternoons. For an essay on reducing heat in cities, I would read sections 3.2 and 3.3 and the Discussion closely, and the methods enough to know where and for how long the study was done.* (3.1 can be skimmed: roof surface temperature is a step towards, not an answer to, the question about street heat.)
:::

::: exercise Order a reading list {level=2}
For an essay on *Does bilingualism affect children's attention?*, put these fictional items in a sensible reading order and say which is optional: (1) a 2017 research article reporting an experiment with 80 bilingual and 80 monolingual children; (2) a 2022 review of studies of bilingualism and attention, which reports that results are mixed; (3) a 2004 study often described as the start of this line of research; (4) a 2021 newspaper article reporting that "speaking two languages makes children smarter".
::: solution
(2) the review first — it gives the overview and explains why the results are mixed; (3) next, as the classic study the review will discuss; then (1), a specific recent study, read with the review's criticisms in mind. (4) is optional: it is not a research source, and its headline probably goes further than the evidence (*smarter* is not *better attention*). It might be used as an example of how research is reported in the media.
:::

::: exercise Recite, don't reread {level=2}
Read the section *Setting questions* in this chapter once. Close it, and write in three sentences: what SQ3R is, which two of its ideas the chapter says are most valuable, and what Dunlosky et al. (2013) found about rereading and highlighting. Then check your answer against the text.
::: solution
A model answer: *SQ3R is a method for studying textbooks described by Robinson in 1946: survey, question, read, recite, review. Its most valuable ideas are reading to answer questions and recalling the answers without looking. Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated practice testing and spaced practice as highly useful, but rereading and highlighting as of low utility.* The point of the exercise is the method: writing from memory, then checking, is itself the *recite* step.

::: exercise A reading plan {level=3}
Choose a topic you are studying, or use *the effects of remote work on productivity*. Find (in a library database or a search engine for scholarly literature) one review article and three research articles on it. For each, record the full reference, survey it, and write two lines on what it claims and which parts you would read closely. Then write a plan for reading them in five hours, as in [[#ex-week-plan]].
::: hint
Check every reference in the database itself; do not copy references from summaries or lists you have not verified.
:::
::: solution
Answers depend on the texts found. A good answer has complete and checked references, a one- or two-sentence claim for each text that is the text's own claim (not background from its introduction), a reasoned choice of depths, and a plan that puts the review first and leaves time for review at the end. A weak answer reads every text from start to finish, or lists texts whose claims it has not checked.
:::

::: exercise Snowballing {level=3}
Starting from one article you have found useful, (a) list three texts it cites that look relevant, and say why from the way they are cited (in the introduction? in the discussion? as a method?); (b) use a citation database or scholarly search engine to find two later texts that cite it, and say what each adds. Draw the five texts and the original as a small map, as in the figure of this chapter.
::: solution
Answers depend on the article. A good answer explains *why* each cited text looks relevant — for example, *cited three times in the discussion as the main study the authors disagree with*, or *cited in the methods as the source of the questionnaire* — and identifies whether the later texts confirm, extend or challenge the original. The map should show arrows from citing to cited texts. Noticing that two of your texts cite the same older study is a sign that it is central to the field.
:::
