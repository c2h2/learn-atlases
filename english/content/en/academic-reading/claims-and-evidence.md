Here is a paragraph of the kind found in the discussion section of an education article. It is written for this lesson, and the study it mentions is fictional.

> *Schools that ban mobile phones during the school day should not expect large gains in exam results. In our study, results in the 40 schools that introduced a ban rose by about the same amount over three years as results in comparable schools without one. It might be argued that bans take longer than three years to have an effect. However, if bans worked mainly by reducing distraction in class, as their supporters claim, their effect should appear quickly. A ban may still be worth having for other reasons, such as reducing online bullying, but improving results is not one of them.*

A reader who can repeat the paragraph has understood its English. A reader who can say *The writer claims that phone bans do not improve exam results much; the evidence is the comparison of 40 schools with similar schools; the writer answers one objection about time, and concedes that bans may have other benefits* has understood its **argument**. Academic texts are arguments from beginning to end: every research article claims something, and every review weighs claims against each other. Reading academically means reading for that structure.

This chapter shows how to find the **claim**, the **reasons** and the **evidence** in an academic text, the **assumptions** that link them, and the way writers handle **objections**; it also shows the language that signals each. The next chapters ask how good the evidence is ([[academic-reading/evaluating-sources]]) and how strongly the writer commits to the claim ([[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]]).

## Arguments and claims

::: definition Argument, claim, reason and evidence {#def-argument}
In academic writing an **argument** (论证) is not a quarrel: it is a set of statements in which some are offered as grounds for believing another.

- The **claim** (论点, also called the **conclusion** or, for a whole text, the **thesis**) is the statement the writer wants the reader to accept.
- A **reason** is a general statement offered in support of the claim (*because bans work by reducing distraction …*).
- **Evidence** (论据) consists of the specific facts, data, observations, examples or sources that support a reason or the claim directly (*in 40 schools, results rose by about the same amount …*).
:::

Arguments can be nested. A reason may itself be a claim that needs support, and the main claim of a paragraph is often one reason for the main claim of the whole article. This is the hierarchy of [[reading-skills/main-ideas]] seen as an argument: the main idea is a claim, the supporting details are reasons and evidence.

::: definition Kinds of claim {#def-claim-kinds}
Academic claims are of several kinds, and each needs a different kind of support:

1. **claims of fact** (descriptive claims): what is or was the case — *Rural school enrolment fell between 2010 and 2020*;
2. **causal claims**: what causes or explains something — *The fall was caused mainly by migration to cities*;
3. **evaluative claims** (claims of value): whether something is good, effective, important or justified — *The new curriculum is an improvement on the old one*;
4. **policy claims** (recommendations): what should be done — *Schools should delay the teaching of handwriting*;
5. **interpretive** and **theoretical claims**, common in the humanities and in theory: what a text, event or result means, or how it is best described — *The novel is less a love story than a study of class*.
:::

A useful habit is to label a claim before judging it. A claim of fact needs reliable observations; a causal claim needs evidence that rules out other causes; an evaluative claim needs criteria (good *for what*?); and a policy claim needs all of these plus an argument that the benefits outweigh the costs. Many weak arguments move from one kind to a stronger kind without saying so — from *X and Y go together* (fact) to *X causes Y* (cause) to *we should do X* (policy).

::: example A claim that changes kind {#ex-claim-slide}
Label the kind of claim in each sentence of this fictional conclusion, and say where the argument moves beyond its evidence. *(1) In our sample of 1,200 secondary pupils, those who slept less than seven hours on school nights had lower average maths scores. (2) Short sleep therefore impairs mathematical learning. (3) Later school start times would be a valuable reform. (4) Schools should start no earlier than 9 a.m.*
::: solution
**Sentence 1: a claim of fact.** It describes what was found in one sample: short sleep and lower scores go together. If the study was done properly, the evidence supports it directly.

**Sentence 2: a causal claim.** *impairs* says that short sleep causes poorer learning. The data in sentence 1 show only an association. Pupils who sleep less may differ in other ways — longer journeys, part-time jobs, more screen time, stress at home — and any of these could lower scores. The signal *therefore* presents the causal claim as if it followed from the fact; it does not, without further evidence.

**Sentence 3: an evaluative claim.** *valuable* judges the reform. It assumes both the causal claim and that later start times would actually lengthen sleep (they might simply move bedtimes later).

**Sentence 4: a policy claim.** It adds a precise recommendation (*9 a.m.*) that no data in the passage address, and ignores costs (transport, parents' working hours).

**Where it goes too far:** from sentence 2 onwards. Each step may be reasonable, but each needs its own evidence, and the passage gives evidence only for the first.
:::
:::

::: quiz
What kind of claim is this? *Smaller classes in the first two years of school raise children's later reading scores.*
- [ ] a claim of fact about what happened in one place
- [x] a causal claim
- [ ] an evaluative claim
- [ ] a policy claim
::: solution
*raise* says that smaller classes **cause** higher scores. It is not yet a policy claim — that would be *Schools should reduce class sizes* — although a policy claim might be built on it. Evidence for it must show not only that children in small classes read better, but that the class size, and not something else, is the reason.
:::
:::

## Signals of argument

::: theorem Words that signal the parts of an argument {#rule-signals}
1. **Claim** (conclusion): *therefore*, *thus*, *hence*, *consequently*, *so*, *it follows that*, *this suggests / shows / indicates that*, *we argue that*, *we conclude that*, *the evidence points to*, and modals of recommendation (*should*, *must*, *need to*).
2. **Reason**: *because*, *since*, *as*, *given that*, *for*, *on the grounds that*, *the reason is that*, *one explanation is that*.
3. **Evidence**: *for example*, *for instance*, *as shown in Table 2*, *according to*, *data from … show*, *X (2019) found that*, numbers, citations (*Lin and Okafor 2021*, *[14]*), and *in our study*.
4. **Counter-argument** and **concession**: *it might be argued that*, *critics claim*, *one objection is*, *admittedly*, *of course*, *although*, *while it is true that*.
5. **Rebuttal** (answer to an objection): *however*, *but*, *nevertheless*, *yet*, *even so*, *this overlooks*.
:::

::: proof
The connectives of English make relations between sentences explicit ([[reading-skills/following-the-thread]]), and in argument the most important relations are the logical ones: *because* points from a claim to its reason, *therefore* from reasons to a claim. Academic writers use them heavily because their readers expect to be able to trace the argument. The signals are not perfectly reliable: *so* can mean "very", *since* can mean time, and many claims are made with no signal at all — often in the first sentence of a paragraph, the topic-sentence position. But they are the fastest guide to the structure, and a reader who marks them can usually reconstruct an argument from them. Writers' use of these markers is taught in [[essay-writing/signposting]].
:::

::: example Finding the parts of an argument {#ex-phone-ban}
Analyse the paragraph on phone bans at the start of this chapter: find the claim, the evidence, the objection, the answer to it and the concession, and the words that signal each.
::: solution
**Claim** (sentence 1): *Schools that ban mobile phones during the school day should not expect large gains in exam results.* No signal word; its position as the first sentence and its general wording mark it. It is a causal claim (bans do not cause large gains), worded as advice (*should not expect*).

**Evidence** (sentence 2): *In our study, results in the 40 schools … rose by about the same amount … as results in comparable schools without one.* Signals: *In our study*, numbers, a comparison.

**Objection** (sentence 3): *It might be argued that bans take longer than three years to have an effect.* Signal: *It might be argued that* — a view the writer will answer, not the writer's own.

**Rebuttal** (sentence 4): *However, if bans worked mainly by reducing distraction in class, as their supporters claim, their effect should appear quickly.* Signal: *However*. This is a reason, not new evidence: it uses the supporters' own explanation against the objection.

**Concession and restated claim** (sentence 5): *A ban may still be worth having for other reasons … but improving results is not one of them.* The writer grants something (*may still be worth having*) and then, after *but*, restates the claim.

**In one sentence:** *Comparing schools with and without bans, the writer argues that phone bans do not raise results much, rejects the idea that the effect is simply slow, and accepts that bans may help in other ways.*
:::
:::

::: warning Taking an objection or a concession as the writer's claim
✗ *The writer thinks bans take more than three years to work.*

✓ *The writer mentions the view that bans take more than three years to work, and rejects it.*

Academic writers often state views they disagree with — introduced by *It might be argued*, *Some researchers claim*, *Critics point out*, *It is often assumed* — and then answer them. They also concede points (*Admittedly …*, *Of course …*, *Although …*) before returning to their own position. A reader who takes these sentences as the writer's claim gets the argument backwards. Always ask: *Whose view is this sentence?* The writer's position usually comes after *However*, *But* or *Nevertheless*.
:::

## Hidden assumptions

Every argument from evidence to a claim depends on a link that is often not stated. Stephen Toulmin's analysis of arguments made this link visible.

::: definition The Toulmin model {#def-toulmin}
In *The Uses of Argument* (1958), Stephen Toulmin analysed practical arguments into six parts:

1. the **claim**: what is argued for;
2. the **data** (or **grounds**): the facts offered in support;
3. the **warrant**: the general principle that licenses the step from data to claim, often unstated;
4. the **backing**: support for the warrant itself;
5. the **qualifier**: a word showing how strong the claim is (*probably*, *in most cases*);
6. the **rebuttal**: the conditions under which the claim would not hold.
:::

::: theorem Find the warrant {#rule-warrant}
To find the hidden assumption in an argument, ask: *What would have to be true, in general, for these data to support this claim?* State the answer as a general sentence, and then ask whether it is true. Many weak arguments have true data and a false or doubtful warrant.
:::

::: proof
Data are particular (*these 40 schools*) and claims are often general (*schools that ban phones*), so the move from one to the other needs a general principle: here, that the 40 schools are typical, and that three years is long enough for an effect to show. Writers leave warrants unstated because they seem obvious to them and their expert readers. That is exactly why they need checking: a reader from outside the field, or a critic within it, may not share the assumption. Toulmin's model was designed for arguments in law and everyday reasoning rather than formal logic, which is why it fits academic writing, where conclusions are rarely certain and are usually qualified.
:::

::: example Warrant, qualifier and rebuttal {#ex-toulmin}
Analyse this fictional argument with the Toulmin model: *In a survey of 2,000 adults, people who said they read books regularly reported higher life satisfaction than those who did not. Reading for pleasure therefore probably improves well-being, unless happier people simply have more free time to read.*
::: solution
**Claim:** reading for pleasure improves well-being.

**Data:** in a survey of 2,000 adults, regular readers reported higher life satisfaction.

**Warrant (unstated):** *if two things go together in a large survey, the first probably causes the second.* Stated like this, it is clearly doubtful: things that go together may be caused the other way round, or by a third thing.

**Backing:** none is given.

**Qualifier:** *probably*.

**Rebuttal:** *unless happier people simply have more free time to read* — the writer names one condition under which the claim fails (reverse causation or a shared cause: free time).

**Judgement.** The writer is honest enough to name a rebuttal, but the data cannot rule it out: a survey at one point in time shows an association, not a cause. A study that followed people over time, or that assigned some to a reading programme, would be needed to support the causal claim. These kinds of evidence are compared in [[academic-reading/evaluating-sources]].
:::
:::

## Valid steps and common gaps

Most academic arguments are not formal proofs, but some steps in them can be checked with simple logic. One of the most common errors in reading and in writing is to reverse a conditional.

::: widget truthtable
title: A conditional and its converse
formula: p -> q
compare: q -> p
caption: Let *p* = *the treatment works* and *q* = *symptoms improve*. *If p then q* (left) and *if q then p* (right) are not equivalent: the table shows rows where one is true and the other false. From *if a treatment works, symptoms improve* and *symptoms improved*, it does not follow that the treatment worked — symptoms may improve for other reasons. Change the second formula to *~q -> ~p* to see a statement that is equivalent (the contrapositive).
:::

Here are some other gaps that careful readers learn to notice. They are described in every textbook of critical thinking (for example Cottrell 2017), under various names.

| gap | pattern | example (fictional) |
|---|---|---|
| hasty generalisation | a few cases → all cases | *Two students in my class improved with this app, so it works for learners.* |
| after, therefore because of | B followed A → A caused B | *Crime fell after the new lights were installed, so the lights reduced crime.* |
| correlation as cause | A and B go together → A causes B | *Children who eat breakfast do better at school, so breakfast improves learning.* |
| false choice | only two options are presented | *Either we ban cars from the centre or the air will keep getting worse.* |
| appeal to authority | someone famous or senior said it | *A Nobel prize winner in physics says this diet works.* |
| straw man | an opposing view is misstated, then refuted | *Critics of testing want schools with no standards at all.* |
| circular argument | the claim is assumed in the reason | *The method is reliable because it gives dependable results.* |

These patterns are signs to look more closely, not automatic verdicts. A generalisation from a few cases is weak, but a generalisation from a large, well-chosen sample is the basis of science; an appeal to an expert in the relevant field is legitimate evidence, though not proof.

::: quiz
*Since the university introduced online lectures, attendance at the library has fallen. The online lectures have therefore made students less interested in reading.* What is the main gap in this argument?
- [ ] a straw man
- [x] treating a sequence (B came after A) as proof that A caused B
- [ ] a circular argument
- [ ] an appeal to authority
::: solution
The fall in library attendance followed the online lectures, but other changes could explain it — more books and journals online, for example, which would mean students read just as much but not in the library. The argument also jumps from *library attendance* to *interest in reading*, which are different things.
:::
:::

## Counter-arguments and concession

Academic writers rarely present only one side. They anticipate objections, grant what has to be granted, and show why their claim survives. This makes arguments more convincing and also more complicated to read.

::: theorem Reading a concession {#rule-concession}
In a sentence or paragraph of the pattern **concession + contrast** (*Although X, Y*; *X. However, Y*; *Admittedly X, but Y*; *While X, Y*):

1. **X** is a point the writer accepts but considers less important, or a view the writer reports in order to answer it;
2. **Y**, after the contrast signal, is the writer's position;
3. the writer's claim is **limited** by X: it is not "Y and X is false", but "Y, even though X".
:::

::: example Reading concession and rebuttal {#ex-concession}
State the writer's position in this fictional passage, and what the writer concedes. *Online courses have obvious advantages: they are cheap to scale, flexible for working students and available far from any campus. Completion rates for open online courses, however, have been consistently low, and the students who complete them tend to be those who already hold degrees. While online learning clearly has a place in higher education, it is not, on present evidence, the means of widening access that its early advocates hoped for.*
::: solution
**Concessions.** The writer grants three advantages (*cheap to scale*, *flexible*, *available far from any campus*) and, in the last sentence, that online learning *clearly has a place* (*While …*).

**Contrast and evidence.** *however* introduces the writer's evidence: low completion rates, and completers who already have degrees.

**Position.** After the concession in the last sentence: online learning is not the means of widening access that it was hoped to be.

**Limits of the claim.** The writer does not say online learning is bad or useless, and adds *on present evidence*, which allows that the evidence might change. A summary that said *The writer argues that online courses do not work* would distort the argument.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **论点, 论据, 论证.** Chinese school teaching of the argumentative essay (议论文) uses a similar framework: the 论点 (claim), the 论据 (evidence) and the 论证 (the reasoning that connects them). Readers who know it can transfer it directly. One difference is in what counts as evidence. School 议论文 often support a point with 事实论据 (facts and examples) and 道理论据, which include famous sayings and quotations. In English academic writing a quotation from a famous person is not evidence unless that person is an expert on the question, and even then it is weaker than data.
- **Explicit connections.** English argument marks logical relations with connectives (*because*, *therefore*, *however*) far more consistently than Chinese, which often leaves them to be inferred (the contrast often described as English 形合, hypotaxis, versus Chinese 意合, parataxis). Use the English signals: they are deliberate guides to the argument, and missing a *however* can reverse the meaning of a paragraph.
- **Arguing with sources.** In English academic texts, disagreeing with a published author is normal and expected, as long as it is done with reasons and evidence. *Smith (2018) argues X; however, this overlooks Y* is a respectful, ordinary sentence. Readers who expect sources to be presented as authorities may miss that the writer is criticising them.
:::

::: history
The analysis of argument is as old as rhetoric and logic. Aristotle distinguished the strict proofs of logic from the reasoning of public speech, which starts from what is generally accepted and leads to conclusions that are probable rather than certain. Formal logic later developed mainly as the study of valid deduction, which did not fit the arguments of law, science and daily life well. In *The Uses of Argument* (1958) Stephen Toulmin proposed a different model, taken from legal reasoning, in which a claim is supported by data through a warrant and is qualified and open to rebuttal. The model found its widest use not in philosophy but in the teaching of writing and critical thinking from the 1970s onwards, where it gave students a vocabulary for the step from evidence to claim that most arguments leave unstated.
:::

## Where this leads

The quality of evidence — what kind of study, how large a sample, whether it shows a cause — is the subject of [[academic-reading/evaluating-sources]], and the strength with which a claim is stated (*probably*, *may*, *clearly*) of [[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]]. Mapping an argument is the basis of a good summary ([[academic-reading/notes-and-summaries]]). On the writing side, [[essay-writing/arguing-a-point]] and [[essay-writing/counter-arguments]] show how to build arguments of your own, and [[presentations-and-discussion/debate-persuasion]] how to make them aloud.

::: summary
- An **argument** is a claim with grounds for believing it: **reasons** (general) and **evidence** (specific facts, data, sources) ([[#def-argument]]).
- Claims may be of **fact**, **cause**, **value**, **policy** or **interpretation**; each needs a different kind of support, and weak arguments slide from one kind to a stronger one ([[#def-claim-kinds]]).
- Connectives and reporting phrases **signal** claims, reasons, evidence, objections and rebuttals ([[#rule-signals]]).
- The **warrant** — the general principle linking data to claim — is often unstated; ask what would have to be true for the evidence to support the claim ([[#rule-warrant]]).
- Watch for common gaps: hasty generalisation, sequence or correlation taken as cause, false choice, misused authority, straw man, circularity; *if p then q* does not give *if q then p*.
- In **concession + contrast**, the writer's position comes after *however*, *but* or *nevertheless*, and is limited by what was conceded ([[#rule-concession]]).
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Claim, reason or evidence? {level=1}
Label each sentence claim (C), reason (R) or evidence (E). (a) *The city should extend its cycle lanes.* (b) *Separated lanes make cyclists feel safer.* (c) *After lanes were built on Park Road, the number of cyclists counted there rose from 400 to 1,100 a day.* (d) *More cycling reduces traffic and pollution.*
::: solution
(a) C (a policy claim); (b) R (a general reason); (c) E (specific data); (d) R (a general reason; it would need its own evidence). Some readers will treat (b) as a claim that (c) supports — that is also a reasonable analysis, because arguments are nested.
:::
:::

::: exercise Kinds of claim {level=1}
Label each claim fact, cause, value or policy. (a) *The number of international students in the country doubled between 2005 and 2015.* (b) *The rise was driven mainly by students from Asia.* (c) *This growth has enriched university life.* (d) *Universities should provide more language support for these students.*
::: solution
(a) fact; (b) cause (*driven by*); (c) value (*enriched* is an evaluation); (d) policy (*should*).
:::
:::

::: exercise Whose view? {level=1}
In each sentence, is the underlined idea the writer's own view (W) or a view the writer reports in order to answer it (R)? (a) *It is often assumed that older learners cannot acquire a native-like accent. The evidence, however, is more mixed.* — the assumption about older learners. (b) *Although the sample was small, the effect was consistent across all three schools.* — that the effect was consistent. (c) *Critics argue that the test measures only memory.* — that the test measures only memory.
::: solution
(a) R — *It is often assumed* reports a view, and *however* introduces the writer's answer. (b) W — the writer concedes the small sample and then states their own point after *Although …,*. (c) R — reported with *Critics argue*; we do not yet know whether the writer agrees.
:::
:::

::: exercise Find the warrant {level=2}
State the unstated warrant in each argument, and say whether it is convincing. (a) *This textbook is used by over 200 universities, so it must be accurate.* (b) *The new drug lowered blood pressure in the trial, so it will reduce heart attacks.* (c) *Students who attended more lectures got higher marks, so attending lectures improves marks.*
::: solution
(a) Warrant: *a book used by many universities is accurate.* Weak: popularity is not accuracy, although wide use by experts is some evidence. (b) Warrant: *lowering blood pressure with this drug reduces heart attacks.* Plausible, because high blood pressure is a known risk factor, but not guaranteed: a drug can change a measurement without changing the outcome that matters, which is why trials measure outcomes such as heart attacks directly. (c) Warrant: *when two things go together, the first causes the second.* Doubtful: motivated students may both attend more and study more.
:::
:::

::: exercise Name the gap {level=2}
Name the main weakness in each argument. (a) *Every student I interviewed preferred the new timetable, so students prefer it.* (b) *Either we raise fees or the university will close.* (c) *Professor Lin, a famous economist, says this vaccine is unsafe.* (d) *People who live near parks are healthier, so building parks will make people healthier.*
::: solution
(a) hasty generalisation — how many were interviewed, and how were they chosen? (b) false choice — other options (cutting costs, other funding) are ignored. (c) appeal to authority outside the person's field — an economist is not an expert on vaccine safety. (d) correlation taken as cause — people who can afford homes near parks may be healthier for other reasons (income, for example).
:::
:::

::: exercise A conditional {level=2 check="2"}
A writer states: *If a language is taught from age six, pupils will reach a good level by age sixteen.* Which of these conclusions follow from the statement alone? Give the number of statements that follow. (1) *Pupils taught from age six reached a good level by sixteen.* (2) *If pupils did not reach a good level by sixteen, they were not taught from age six.* (3) *Pupils who reached a good level by sixteen were taught from age six.* (4) *Pupils not taught from age six will not reach a good level.*
::: solution
**2** statements follow: (1), which applies the conditional directly, and (2), the contrapositive (*not q → not p*). (3) reverses the conditional (*q → p*), and (4) denies the condition (*not p → not q*); neither follows, because pupils could reach a good level in other ways. Whether the original statement is true is a separate question.
:::
:::

::: exercise Map an argument {level=3}
Draw an argument map (as in the figure of this chapter) of this fictional paragraph: claim, reasons, evidence, objection, rebuttal and any concession.

> *Universities should record lectures and make them available online. Recordings help students who miss a lecture through illness or work, and they allow non-native speakers to replay difficult passages; in a survey at one university, 70% of international students said they used recordings in this way. Some lecturers fear that recordings will empty their lecture halls. Studies of attendance, however, have generally found only small falls after recording was introduced. Recordings are no substitute for being present, of course, and students who rely on them entirely tend to do worse. But that is an argument for encouraging attendance, not for denying recordings to those who need them.*
::: solution
**Claim:** universities should record lectures and put them online (policy).

**Reasons:** (1) recordings help students who miss lectures through illness or work; (2) they let non-native speakers replay difficult passages.

**Evidence:** for reason 2, a survey at one university: 70% of international students used recordings this way. (Reason 1 has no evidence here.)

**Objection:** lecturers fear empty lecture halls.

**Rebuttal:** studies have generally found only small falls in attendance (*however*).

**Concession:** recordings are no substitute for attendance, and students who rely on them entirely do worse (*of course*).

**Answer to the concession:** this supports encouraging attendance, not refusing recordings (*But*).

A good map draws arrows from evidence to reason to claim, from the objection against the claim, from the rebuttal against the objection, and shows the concession as limiting the claim. A critical note: the survey is from one university, and *used recordings in this way* does not show that recordings improved learning.
:::
:::

::: exercise Write a reply {level=3}
Write a paragraph of about 120 words that argues against the claim of the previous exercise (or for a different policy), with a clear claim, at least two reasons, one piece of evidence (you may invent it, but label it as illustrative), one concession and one rebuttal. Then exchange with a partner and map each other's paragraphs.
::: solution
Answers will differ. A model: *Universities should not record every lecture by default. (Claim.) Lectures that are recorded tend to become more cautious, because lecturers avoid open discussion or unfinished ideas that will be stored permanently (reason 1); and students who know a recording exists are more likely to postpone listening and then cram (reason 2). In an illustrative example, a department might find that half of all views of its recordings happen in the week before the exam (evidence, labelled as illustrative). Admittedly, recordings help students who are ill or working (concession). However, these students can be supported by targeted recordings and notes on request, without recording every class (rebuttal).* The partner's map should find the same parts; if it does not, the signals may need to be clearer.
:::
