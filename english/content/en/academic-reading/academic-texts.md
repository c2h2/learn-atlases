On Monday your tutor sends a 24-page research article and asks you to come to Wednesday's seminar ready to say what the authors found and whether you believe it. One student starts on page one and reads every sentence in order; by Tuesday night she is on page 15, lost in a table of statistics, and still not sure what the main finding is. Another student reads the abstract, the last paragraph of the introduction, the first paragraph of the discussion and the conclusion, which takes twenty minutes. He now knows the question, the answer and the authors' own view of their study's weaknesses, and he spends the rest of his time reading the methods and results with those in mind.

The second student is not cleverer, and he did not skip the work: he used the **map** that academic texts carry with them. Research articles, textbooks and reviews are written to conventions that readers in every discipline learn, often without being told, and those conventions say where each kind of information will be. This chapter sets out the main kinds of academic text, the IMRaD structure of the research article, the parts of an abstract, the "moves" of an introduction, and the signposts that writers use to show readers the way. The next chapter, [[academic-reading/strategic-reading]], uses this map to plan reading; the chapter on text types in *Reading Skills* ([[reading-skills/text-types]]) showed the same idea for everyday texts.

## Kinds of academic text

::: definition Academic genres {#def-academic-genres}
A **genre** (体裁, 语类) is a type of text that a community uses for a recognised purpose and that therefore has a recognisable structure and style. The main genres a student reads are:

1. the **textbook**: a book that presents the established knowledge of a field to learners, organised for teaching;
2. the **research article** (研究论文), also called a **paper**: a report of new research — a study, an experiment, an analysis — published in a journal after review by other experts; it is **primary literature**, because it reports the research first-hand;
3. the **review article** (综述): a survey of the research on one question, which summarises, compares and evaluates many studies; it is **secondary literature**;
4. the **monograph** and the **edited book**: a book-length study by one author, or a collection of chapters by different authors on one theme, common in the humanities and social sciences;
5. **reports**, **theses** and **conference papers**, which have their own conventions but often borrow those of the research article.
:::

The genres differ in what they assume and what they offer.

| | textbook | research article | review article |
|---|---|---|---|
| written for | learners | experts in the field | experts and newcomers to a question |
| presents | agreed knowledge | one new study and its claim | the state of research on one question |
| tone | confident, explanatory | cautious, argued | evaluative |
| organised by | teaching sequence | IMRaD (in most sciences) | themes or questions |
| reader aids | objectives, bold terms, summaries, exercises | abstract, headings, figures | abstract, headings, tables of studies |
| best for | learning the basics of a field | finding out exactly what was done and found | getting an overview fast |

For most students, the most efficient order is the reverse of the order of prestige: a textbook chapter first, to learn the vocabulary of the field; then a recent review, to see the main questions and studies; and only then the research articles, read with a purpose. A research article read without that background is hard not because its English is difficult but because it assumes knowledge the reader does not yet have.

::: quiz
A student wants to know what research since 2010 has found about the effects of homework on primary-school pupils. Which source should she read first?
- [ ] a research article reporting one new experiment on homework
- [x] a recent review article on homework and achievement
- [ ] a monograph on the history of primary education
- [ ] a newspaper opinion piece about homework
::: solution
A review article surveys the studies on one question and is the quickest way to an overview. A single research article reports one study; the monograph is about a different question; and an opinion piece is not an academic source, although it may lead her to one.
:::
:::

## The research article: IMRaD

::: definition IMRaD {#def-imrad}
**IMRaD** (usually pronounced /ˈɪmræd/) is the standard structure of the empirical research article in the natural, medical and most social sciences: **Introduction**, **Methods**, **Results** and **Discussion**. In front of these come a **title** and an **abstract**; after them often a **conclusion** (sometimes part of the discussion), acknowledgements, **references** and appendices or supplementary material.
:::

::: theorem What each section answers {#rule-imrad-questions}
In an IMRaD article:

1. the **Introduction** answers *Why was this study done?* — what is known, what is not known (the *gap*), and what this study asks;
2. the **Methods** answer *How was it done?* — participants or materials, design, procedure and analysis;
3. the **Results** answer *What was found?* — the findings, usually with tables and figures, reported with little interpretation;
4. the **Discussion** answers *What does it mean?* — the main finding restated, its interpretation, comparison with earlier studies, **limitations**, implications and suggestions for further research.

The abstract answers all four questions in miniature, and the conclusion (if there is one) repeats the answer to the fourth.
:::

::: proof
The structure follows the logic of reporting an experiment: a problem, a procedure, an outcome and an interpretation. It also serves readers with different purposes. A specialist checking whether a result can be trusted goes to the Methods; a student wanting the answer goes to the abstract and the Discussion; someone repeating the study needs every detail of the procedure. Because each kind of information has its fixed place, all of them can find it without reading the rest. IMRaD was not always standard: Sollaci and Pereira (2004), surveying articles in four leading medical journals from the 1940s to 2000, found that it was adopted gradually from the 1940s and had become the dominant pattern by the 1970s and 1980s.

The rule has limits. In the humanities (history, literature, philosophy) articles are usually essays organised by the steps of an argument, under headings chosen by the author; theoretical papers in economics or physics often have a model in place of Methods; and some journals put the methods at the end. Even then the functions remain — the article still says why, how, what and so what — and the reader's task is to find where.
:::

The shape of an IMRaD article is often drawn as an **hourglass**. The introduction starts broad (the general field) and narrows to the specific question; the methods and results are narrow (this sample, this procedure, these numbers); the discussion starts narrow (the main finding) and widens again (what it means for the field). Knowing the shape tells you that the first sentences of an introduction and the last sentences of a discussion are the most general, and that the precise claim is at the narrow waist on either side: the end of the introduction and the start of the discussion.

::: example Where would you look? {#ex-where-to-look}
You are reading a (fictional) article, *Morning light and sleep timing in first-year university students*. In which section would you expect to find each piece of information? (a) how many students took part and how they were recruited; (b) that earlier studies had looked only at adults in full-time work; (c) the average time students fell asleep in each group; (d) that the authors think the effect might be smaller in summer; (e) the reason the authors chose to study first-year students; (f) the statistical test used to compare the groups.
::: solution
Ask which of the four questions each item answers.

(a) *How was it done?* — **Methods** (the participants subsection).

(b) What earlier research did and did not do — the gap — **Introduction**.

(c) A finding, given as numbers — **Results** (probably in a table as well as in the text).

(d) An interpretation and a limit on the finding — **Discussion** (very likely in the limitations paragraph).

(e) *Why?* — **Introduction**, usually near the end, where the authors state their aim.

(f) *How was the analysis done?* — **Methods** (a subsection often called *Statistical analysis* or *Data analysis*).
:::
:::

::: warning Mixing up what was known and what was found
✗ *The study found that screen use at night delays sleep.* — said of a sentence in the **introduction**: *Previous research has shown that screen use at night delays sleep (Okafor et al. 2019).* (The citation is invented for this example.)

✓ *The authors note that earlier studies have linked screen use at night with later sleep; their own study looks at morning light.*

The introduction of an article is full of other people's findings, each with a citation. They are the background, not the results of this study. Readers who skim an introduction and pick up a finding often report it as the article's own. Check two things: is there a citation (a name and year, or a number in brackets)? And is the verb in a pattern such as *has been shown*, *previous studies found*, *X (2019) reported*? If so, it is background. The article's own findings are in the Results and are usually introduced with *we found*, *the present study*, *our results*, or *in this study*.
:::

## The abstract

::: definition Abstract {#def-abstract}
An **abstract** (摘要) is a short, self-contained summary placed before an article, usually 150–300 words. Abstracts are **unstructured** (one paragraph) or **structured** (with headings such as *Background*, *Aim*, *Methods*, *Results* and *Conclusions*, common in medicine). Either kind normally contains some or all of five **moves** — parts with a function — which Hyland (2000) called Introduction, Purpose, Method, Product (the results) and Conclusion.
:::

The abstract is the most-read part of any article: databases show it, readers decide from it whether to read on, and many readers never go further. That is a reason to read it carefully, and also a reason to be careful with it, because an abstract is written to persuade a reader that the article matters.

Here is an abstract written for this lesson. The study is fictional.

> *Exposure to daylight in the morning is known to advance the timing of sleep in adults, but little is known about its effect on university students, whose sleep is often late and irregular. This study examined whether a simple morning-light routine shifted sleep timing in first-year students. Eighty-four students at one university were randomly assigned either to spend at least 20 minutes outdoors before 10 a.m. each day for four weeks or to continue their usual routine. Sleep was measured with wrist-worn activity monitors. Students in the morning-light group fell asleep on average 26 minutes earlier by the fourth week than those in the comparison group, and reported less daytime sleepiness. The difference in total sleep time was small and not statistically significant. These findings suggest that morning light may be a low-cost way to help students sleep earlier, although larger studies over a full academic year are needed.*

::: example The moves of an abstract {#ex-abstract-moves}
Divide the morning-light abstract into its moves and say what signals each.
::: solution
**Introduction (background and gap).** *Exposure to daylight in the morning is known to advance … but little is known about its effect on university students …* Signals: *is known to* (what is established), *but little is known* (the gap).

**Purpose.** *This study examined whether a simple morning-light routine shifted sleep timing in first-year students.* Signals: *This study* + *examined whether*.

**Method.** *Eighty-four students at one university were randomly assigned … Sleep was measured with wrist-worn activity monitors.* Signals: participants and numbers, *were randomly assigned*, *was measured*: passive verbs in the past tense, typical of method statements.

**Product (results).** *Students in the morning-light group fell asleep on average 26 minutes earlier … The difference in total sleep time was small and not statistically significant.* Signals: numbers, comparison, past tense.

**Conclusion.** *These findings suggest that morning light may be a low-cost way … although larger studies … are needed.* Signals: *These findings suggest* and *may* (a cautious interpretation), *are needed* (further research).

Note what a careful reader takes from the results move: the main effect was on the **timing** of sleep, not on its **amount** — the abstract says so honestly, but a hasty reader might report that morning light makes students "sleep more".
:::
:::

::: theorem Reading an abstract {#rule-reading-abstract}
When you read an abstract:

1. find the **question** (the purpose move) and the **answer** (the results move), and put them in one sentence of your own;
2. note the **design and the sample** from the method move: how many, who, how chosen, what was measured;
3. compare the **strength of the conclusion** with the results: does *suggest* or *may* become *show* or *will*? A conclusion stronger than the results is a warning sign;
4. decide what you still need from the full text, and go to that section.
:::

::: proof
An abstract compresses an article of several thousand words into a few hundred, so it leaves out most of what a critical reader needs — exactly how the sample was chosen, how large the effects were, what went wrong. It also selects: authors and journals want readers, and abstracts tend to present results in their best light. Steps 1 and 2 extract what the abstract does well; step 3 checks the most common distortion; step 4 treats the abstract as a guide to the article, not a substitute for it. Hedges such as *suggest* and *may* are the subject of [[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]].
:::

::: quiz
According to the morning-light abstract, which statement is accurate?
- [ ] Morning light made students sleep for longer.
- [x] Students who went outdoors in the morning fell asleep earlier than the comparison group.
- [ ] The study proved that morning light is the best treatment for student sleep problems.
- [ ] The study found no effect of morning light at all.
::: solution
The results move reports earlier sleep onset (26 minutes on average) and less daytime sleepiness, but no significant difference in total sleep. The first option confuses timing with amount; the third overstates the authors' cautious conclusion (*suggest*, *may*); the fourth ignores the main finding.
:::
:::

## Introductions: creating a research space

The introduction of a research article has a job beyond giving background: it must persuade readers that the study was worth doing. Swales (1990), studying many introductions across disciplines, described how they do this in his **CARS** model — *Create a Research Space*.

::: definition The CARS model {#def-cars}
In the **CARS model** (Swales 1990) an article introduction typically makes three **moves**:

1. **Establishing a territory**: showing that the general research area is important or interesting (*X is a central issue in …*, *Recent years have seen growing interest in …*), and reviewing previous research;
2. **Establishing a niche**: showing that something is missing or questionable in that research — a gap, a problem, a counter-claim, an unanswered question (*However, little attention has been paid to …*, *These studies have not considered …*);
3. **Occupying the niche**: announcing how the present study fills the gap — its purpose, sometimes its main findings and the structure of the article (*This paper examines …*, *The aim of the present study is …*).
:::

Swales revised the model in *Research Genres* (2004), noting for example that reviews of previous research are not confined to Move 1 but run through the whole introduction. The three-move outline remains the most widely taught description of research introductions, and it is a reader's tool as much as a writer's: Move 2 tells you what the authors think is wrong with earlier work, and Move 3 tells you exactly what they claim to add.

> *(1) Reading on screens has become a normal part of university study, and many courses now provide most of their materials in digital form. (2) A substantial body of research has compared comprehension of texts read on paper and on screen, and several reviews have concluded that comprehension of longer informational texts tends to be somewhat lower on screen. (3) However, almost all of this research has involved readers in their first language. (4) Whether the same difference holds for students reading in a second language, who may rely more on rereading and annotation, remains largely unexplored. (5) The present study therefore compares the comprehension of English academic texts read on paper and on tablets by Chinese undergraduates. (6) Section 2 describes the participants and materials, Section 3 reports the results, and Section 4 discusses their implications for course design.*

This introduction is written for the atlas, and the study it announces is fictional; the claims in sentence 2 are given here only as an example of how an introduction reviews earlier research.

::: example Finding the moves in an introduction {#ex-cars}
Label each sentence of the introduction above with its move, and say which words signal the change from one move to the next.
::: solution
**Sentences 1–2: Move 1, establishing a territory.** Sentence 1 claims the area matters (*has become a normal part of*, *many courses*). Sentence 2 reviews previous research (*A substantial body of research has compared*, *several reviews have concluded*). Note the present perfect, typical of reviewing earlier work.

**Sentences 3–4: Move 2, establishing a niche.** The signal is *However* at the start of sentence 3, followed by a limitation of earlier research (*almost all of this research has involved readers in their first language*). Sentence 4 states the gap as an open question (*Whether … remains largely unexplored*).

**Sentences 5–6: Move 3, occupying the niche.** *The present study therefore compares …* announces the purpose; *therefore* presents the study as the logical answer to the gap. Sentence 6 outlines the structure of the article.

**What a reader takes away:** the claimed contribution is narrow and precise — second-language readers, academic texts, paper versus tablets, Chinese undergraduates. A reader can now judge the rest of the article against that aim.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence most clearly establishes a niche (Move 2)?
- [ ] *Climate change is one of the most pressing problems of our time.*
- [ ] *This article reports a survey of 600 farmers in two provinces.*
- [x] *Few studies, however, have asked how small farmers themselves perceive these changes.*
- [ ] *Section 3 presents the results of the survey.*
::: solution
The third sentence points to a gap (*Few studies … have asked*), signalled by *however*. The first establishes a territory, the second occupies the niche (it announces the present study), and the fourth outlines the article's structure, which is also part of Move 3.
:::
:::

## Signposts

::: definition Signposts {#def-signposts}
**Signposts** are the words, phrases and features of layout that tell a reader where they are in a text and what is coming: headings and numbered sections; statements of the text's structure (*This chapter is organised as follows*, *In Section 4 we …*); sequencing words (*First*, *Second*, *Finally*); announcements of a stage (*We now turn to …*, *To summarise*, *In conclusion*); and references to other parts of the text (*as noted above*, *see Table 2*). In the terms of Hyland (2005) most of them are **interactive metadiscourse**: language about the text itself, used to guide the reader.
:::

::: theorem Where the key information usually is {#rule-key-places}
In an academic article or chapter, look first in these places:

1. the **title** and the **abstract** (or, in a textbook, the chapter objectives and summary);
2. the **last paragraph of the introduction**: the aim of the study and often an outline of the article;
3. the **first paragraph of the discussion**: the main finding restated in plain terms;
4. the paragraph that begins with a signal such as *This study has several limitations* or *Some limitations should be noted*, usually late in the discussion;
5. the **conclusion**, and in each section, the **first sentence of each paragraph**.

These are tendencies, strong in the sciences and weaker in the humanities.
:::

::: proof
Academic writing in English puts general statements before details at every level ([[reading-skills/main-ideas]]): the article states its aim before its methods, the section its point before its evidence, the paragraph its topic sentence before its support. The narrow waist of the hourglass — end of introduction, start of discussion — is where the article's own claim is stated most directly. Limitations are a required part of most discussions in empirical fields, so they are usually present and usually signalled. These places are not a substitute for reading; they are where to start, so that the rest is read with the main point in mind.
:::

::: example Following the signposts {#ex-signposts}
A (fictional) review article on urban trees has these headings: *1 Introduction; 2 Methods of the review; 3 Trees and air quality; 3.1 Particles; 3.2 Ozone; 4 Trees and temperature; 5 Trees and health; 6 Gaps in the evidence; 7 Conclusions*. The last paragraph of section 1 says: *We first describe how studies were selected (Section 2). Sections 3 to 5 review the evidence on air quality, temperature and health in turn. Section 6 identifies questions on which the evidence is weak or conflicting, and Section 7 draws out implications for city planning.* You need to know (a) whether trees reduce ozone, (b) which questions are still open, (c) what the review recommends for planners, and (d) whether the review included studies from Asian cities. Where do you go for each?
::: solution
Use the headings and the outline together.

(a) **Section 3.2**, *Ozone*. Read its first and last sentences first; the evidence is in between.

(b) **Section 6**, *Gaps in the evidence* — the outline says it covers questions where the evidence is *weak or conflicting*.

(c) **Section 7**, *Conclusions*: the outline says it *draws out implications for city planning*.

(d) **Section 2**, *Methods of the review*: a review states how studies were searched for and selected (databases, dates, languages, regions). If studies were limited to English-language journals, for example, Asian cities may be under-represented — information that affects how far the conclusions apply.

None of these answers required reading the whole review first. That is what signposts are for.
:::
:::

## Textbooks and reviews

Textbooks are the most signposted academic texts of all. They state learning objectives, print key terms in bold, put definitions in boxes, end chapters with summaries and review questions, and repeat ideas deliberately. A good textbook reader uses these: reads the objectives and summary first, turns the headings into questions, and checks understanding against the review questions. The method is set out in [[academic-reading/strategic-reading]].

Review articles are organised by themes or questions, not by IMRaD, although a **systematic review** — one that searches for all studies on a question by an explicit, repeatable method — has a methods section of its own, describing the search and the criteria for including studies. Reviews are evaluative: they say which findings are consistent, which conflict, and which rest on weak studies. For a newcomer, the most useful parts are the tables that list the studies reviewed and the section on gaps, which shows where the field's questions are.

::: note English and Chinese
- **The same map in both languages.** Chinese journals in the sciences and medicine use the same IMRaD structure (引言, 材料与方法 or 研究方法, 结果, 讨论, 结论), and most require a Chinese 摘要 and often an English abstract too. A reader who knows the structure in one language can use it in the other.
- **Move 2.** Comparative genre studies have suggested that introductions in Chinese-language journals establish a niche — point out a gap or a weakness in earlier work — less often or less directly than English-language introductions in the same fields (for example Loi 2010, comparing educational psychology articles). In English introductions, look for the *However …* sentence that names the gap: it is where the authors say what they think is new.
- **Reading the abstract in English.** Chinese readers often meet an English article through its abstract. The abstract is a summary written to attract readers, so do not decide that a study proved something from its abstract alone; check the results and the limitations.
- **Headings and signposting.** English academic texts often announce their own structure (*This paper is organised as follows*). Such sentences can seem redundant when translated into Chinese, but in English they are a normal courtesy to the reader and a quick guide to the text.
:::

::: history
The research article grew out of letters. The first scientific journals — the *Journal des sçavans* in Paris and the Royal Society's *Philosophical Transactions* in London, both founded in 1665 — printed reports that read like correspondence, often in the first person and in the order in which things happened. Over the next three centuries the article became more standardised, and in the twentieth century the IMRaD pattern spread through the experimental sciences and then to medicine and the social sciences; Sollaci and Pereira (2004) traced its spread in leading medical journals. In 1981 John Swales, then working in English for Academic Purposes at the University of Aston, published a study of article introductions, which he developed into the CARS model of *Genre Analysis* (1990). Genre analysis gave teachers a way to show students that the structure of academic texts is not arbitrary: it reflects what a research community needs from them.
:::

::: summary

::: exercise A conclusion stronger than the results {level=2}
Compare the results and conclusion of this fictional abstract. What is wrong, and how could the conclusion be rewritten?

> *Results: Pupils in the three schools that adopted a four-day week had attendance rates similar to those in comparison schools; test results were slightly lower but the difference was not statistically significant. ::: solution
The results show no **significant** difference in test scores in three schools — not that there is **no** effect. A non-significant difference in a small study is weak evidence either way, and the scores were in fact *slightly lower*. *should be adopted more widely* is a policy recommendation that goes far beyond three schools. A conclusion that fits the results: *In this small study, a four-day week was not associated with lower attendance, and differences in test results were small and not statistically significant; larger studies are needed before wider adoption can be recommended.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Analyse an introduction {level=3}
Label the moves and steps in this fictional introduction, and state in one sentence of your own what the authors claim to add.

> *Urban noise is one of the most widespread environmental exposures in modern cities, and its links with sleep disturbance and stress are well documented. Most studies, however, have measured noise outside people's homes, using maps produced by city authorities. Such maps say little about what people actually hear indoors, which depends on window type, room position and the time of day. Moreover, they cannot capture short loud events, such as passing motorcycles, which may matter more for sleep than average levels. In this study we placed small sound recorders in the bedrooms of 150 households in one city for two weeks and compared indoor noise events with participants' sleep diaries. We show that the number of loud events, rather than the average level, best predicts reported awakenings.*
::: hint
Look for *however*, *Moreover*, *In this study* and *We show*. The last sentence is a step of Move 3 that not every introduction has.
:::
::: solution
**Move 1** (territory): sentence 1 — the topic is important (*one of the most widespread*) and earlier research is summarised (*well documented*).

**Move 2** (niche): sentences 2–4. *however* introduces a limitation of earlier studies (outdoor maps); sentence 3 explains why it matters; *Moreover* adds a second limitation (short loud events are missed).

**Move 3** (occupying the niche): sentence 5 announces the present study and its method (*In this study we …*); sentence 6 announces the principal finding (*We show that …*), a step that is more common in some fields than others.

**Contribution in my own words:** *By recording noise inside bedrooms, the study claims to show that the number of loud noises, not the average noise level, is what best predicts how often people say they wake up.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Map an article you are reading {level=3}
Choose a research article from your own field or interest (a real one). Without reading it from start to finish, use the places in [[#rule-key-places]] to write: (a) the question the study asks; (b) its design and sample in one sentence; (c) the main finding; (d) one limitation the authors themselves mention; (e) one thing you would need to read the full methods or results to judge. Then read the article fully and check your answers. What did your first, selective reading get right, and what did it miss?
::: solution
Answers depend on the article. A good answer gives (a)–(d) in your own words, each traced to a place in the article (*end of the introduction*, *first paragraph of the discussion* …). For (e), typical points are how the sample was selected, how the main outcome was measured, or how large the effect was. In the comparison, readers usually find that the selective reading gave the question and the main finding correctly, but missed details that change how far the finding can be trusted or generalised — which is why the full reading still matters, and why it is easier after the map.
