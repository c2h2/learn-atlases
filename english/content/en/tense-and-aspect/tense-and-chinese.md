A teacher collected these sentences from the writing of a class of Chinese learners at intermediate level. Each one is understandable, and each one has a tense problem:

- ✗ *Yesterday I go to the library and borrow two books.*
- ✗ *I have seen this film last week.*
- ✗ *I live in Shenzhen for five years.*
- ✗ *When I will graduate, I want to work abroad.*
- ✗ *Look, the door is opening.* (meaning: 门开着)
- ✗ *Last summer I had gone to Guilin with my family.*

These mistakes are not random. Each one comes from a reasonable way of thinking about time that works in Chinese and fails in English. The first relies on the time word, as Chinese does. The second and sixth treat 了 or 过 as if they were English tenses. The third translates 了……了 with a present tense. The fourth adds *will* because the time is future, as 会 or 要 might. The fifth translates 着 as *-ing*.

The earlier lessons of this course have compared English and Chinese point by point in their notes. This lesson brings the comparison together. It describes how Mandarin marks time and aspect, following the standard descriptions of Li and Thompson (1981) and 朱德熙 (1982), shows where the Chinese markers match English forms and where they do not, and gives a procedure for choosing the English tense without translating the particles.

## How Chinese marks time

::: definition Time and aspect in Mandarin {#def-chinese-system}
Mandarin Chinese has **no tense**: verbs do not change for past, present or future. Time is shown by **time words** (昨天, 现在, 明年, 以前), by **adverbs** (已经, 正在, 刚, 曾经, 就要) and by **context**. Aspect is shown by **particles and auxiliary words**:

- **了** after the verb: a completed, **bounded** event (perfective aspect);
- **了** at the end of the sentence: a **new situation** that is relevant now;
- **过** after the verb: something has been **experienced** at least once;
- **在**/**正在** before the verb, and **着** after it: a situation that is **continuing** (durative aspect).

All of these are **optional** in a way that English tense is not: a Chinese sentence without any of them is often complete and correct.
:::

This is the description given in Li and Thompson's *Mandarin Chinese: A Functional Reference Grammar* (1981) and, in Chinese, in 朱德熙's 《语法讲义》 (1982), which treats 了, 着 and 过 as verb suffixes and sentence-final 了 as a modal particle (语气词). Chinese linguists often label the two kinds of 了 as **了₁** (after the verb) and **了₂** (at the end of the sentence); a sentence can have both.

The difference from English is not that Chinese "has no time". It is that Chinese expresses time **once**, where it is needed, and English expresses it **in every finite verb**, whether or not it is needed. In 昨天我去图书馆借了两本书, 昨天 places the whole story in the past, and only 借 has a marker. In English, both verbs must be past: *Yesterday I **went** to the library and **borrowed** two books.*

## 了 after the verb

::: theorem Verb + 了 (了₁) {#rule-le1}
了 after a verb presents the event as **complete and bounded** — seen as a whole, with an end (Li and Thompson's "perfective aspect"). It typically appears with a quantity or a definite object (我买了三本书, 他喝了那杯茶). It does **not** mark past time:

1. It can refer to the **future**: 明天下了课我去找你 → *I'll come and find you tomorrow **after class**.*
2. Many **past** sentences have no 了, especially habits and states: 他以前常常去 → *He often **went** / **used to go***; 我昨天很累 → *I **was** very tired yesterday.*
3. In the **negative**, 了 disappears: 我没买 → *I **didn't buy** it* / *I **haven't bought** it*, not ✗ 我没买了.

So verb + 了 can correspond to the **past simple**, the **present perfect**, the **past perfect**, or a **present tense** in a future time clause. The English form depends on time and viewpoint, not on 了.
:::

::: proof
Perfective aspect and past tense are different categories, as [[tense-and-aspect/time-tense-aspect#def-aspect]] explained: one concerns how an event is viewed, the other when it is placed. They often meet, because complete events are often in the past, but neither implies the other. Comrie (1976) discusses Mandarin among the languages that mark perfective aspect without marking tense. Li and Thompson (1981) show that 了 is used for bounded events whatever their time, and that it is avoided where an event is not seen as bounded — in habits, in states and in general statements.
:::

| Chinese | English | why |
|---|---|---|
| 我昨天买了一件外套。 | *I **bought** a coat yesterday.* | past time named → past simple |
| 我买了一件新外套，你看！ | *I'**ve bought** a new coat — look!* | result now → present perfect |
| 我到的时候，他们已经吃了饭。 | *When I arrived, they **had** already **eaten**.* | before a past point → past perfect |
| 你吃了饭再走吧。 | *Have something to eat **before** you go.* | future → no past form at all |

::: example Translating 了 {#ex-le1}
Translate into English and explain each choice of tense. (a) 她上个月生了一个女儿。 (b) 我刚洗了澡，现在很舒服。 (c) 等我看完了这本书就借给你。 (d) 他走了以后，我们才开始讨论。
::: solution
(a) *She **had** a baby girl last month.* — *last month*: a finished time, past simple. 了 shows completion.

(b) *I've just had a shower, and now I feel great.* — a recent event with a result now (*just*, *now*): present perfect. (AmE also *I just had a shower*.)

(c) *I'll lend you this book when I'**ve finished** it.* (or *when I **finish** it*) — the time is future; in a time clause English uses the present perfect or present, never a past tense and never *will*.

(d) *We didn't start the discussion until he **had left**.* (or *after he **left***) — 以后 and 才 show the order; *after* makes the past perfect optional ([[tense-and-aspect/narrative-tenses#rule-pp-optional]]).

Four sentences with 了, four different English tenses.
:::
:::

## 了 at the end of the sentence

::: theorem Sentence-final 了 (了₂) {#rule-le2}
了 at the end of a sentence shows that the situation is **new** or **changed**, and **relevant now** (Li and Thompson's "currently relevant state"; 朱德熙 calls it a 语气词 showing a new situation). English expresses this meaning in several ways:

1. the **present perfect**, when a past event has a present result: 他走了 → *He'**s gone*** / *He'**s left***;
2. a **present tense** with a word of change or time: 下雨了 → *It'**s raining** (now)* / *It'**s started** raining*; 我二十岁了 → *I'**m** twenty **now***;
3. ***not … any more*** for a changed plan or habit: 我不去了 → *I'**m not going** any more* / *I've decided not to go*; 他不抽烟了 → *He doesn't smoke **any more***;
4. ***already*** or ***now*** to show that something is earlier than expected: 都十点了 → *It's **already** ten o'clock!*
:::

::: proof
The common idea of sentence-final 了 is change: a situation that did not hold before holds now. English has no single form for change, so it uses the form that suits the kind of situation. For an event with a result, the present perfect (which also presents a past event as relevant now, [[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect#rule-pp-uses]]) is the closest match. For a state, English uses the plain present and adds a word such as *now*, *already* or *any more* to carry the idea of change. That is why 我饿了 is simply *I'm hungry*, not ✗ *I have been hungry*: the change is understood.
:::

::: warning Translating sentence-final 了 with a past tense
✗ *I was hungry.* (for 我饿了, said now) ✓ *I'm hungry.*

✗ *It rained!* (for 下雨了, said looking out of the window) ✓ *It's raining!* / *It's started to rain!*

✗ *I understood.* (for 我明白了, said now) ✓ *I see.* / *Now I understand.* / *Got it.*

Sentence-final 了 often describes the situation **now**. English needs a present form, sometimes with *now* or *already*.
:::

## 过

::: theorem Verb + 过 {#rule-guo}
过 after a verb shows that an event has been **experienced at least once** at some time in the past (Li and Thompson's "experiential aspect"). It often implies that the situation **no longer holds**.

1. With no time named, 过 usually corresponds to the **present perfect** of experience: 你去过西安吗？ → *Have you ever **been** to Xi'an?* 我没吃过榴莲 → *I'**ve never eaten** durian.*
2. With a finished time named, English needs the **past simple**: 我去年去过西安 → *I **went** to Xi'an last year*, not ✗ *I've been to Xi'an last year*.
3. Because 过 implies that the situation has ended, it often corresponds to ***used to*** or the past simple with *once*: 他当过兵 → *He **was** a soldier **once*** / *He **used to** be a soldier.*
:::

The contrast between 过 and 了 matches the contrast between *been* and *gone* ([[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect]]):

- 他去过美国。 → *He'**s been** to the United States.* (he came back; experience)
- 他去了美国。 → *He'**s gone** to the United States.* (he is there now) — or, in a story, *He **went** to the United States.*

::: example 过 or 了? {#ex-guo-le}
Translate and explain the difference in each pair. (a) 我学过日语。 / 我学了日语。 (b) 他结过婚。 / 他结婚了。 (c) 我看过这部电影。 / 我看了这部电影。
::: solution
(a) 我学过日语 → *I've studied Japanese* (at some time; perhaps I don't study it now; often implies I don't). 我学了日语 needs more context: *I studied Japanese* (for some time, in a story) or *I've started learning Japanese*. With a quantity, the meaning is clearer: 我学了两年日语 → *I studied Japanese for two years*.

(b) 他结过婚 → *He's been married before* (he is not married now — divorced or widowed). 他结婚了 → *He's got married* / *He's married now* (a new situation, sentence-final 了).

(c) 我看过这部电影 → *I've seen this film* (experience; I know the story). 我看了这部电影 → *I saw / watched this film* (an event, perhaps with a time: 昨天我看了这部电影 → *I watched this film yesterday*).
:::
:::

## 在, 正在 and 着

::: theorem 在, 正在 and 着 {#rule-zai-zhe}
1. **在** or **正在** before an **activity** verb shows that the action is in progress, like the English progressive: 他在打电话 → *He'**s talking** on the phone.* 在 has no tense: 昨天三点我在开会 → *At three yesterday I **was having** a meeting* (past progressive).
2. **着** after a verb usually describes a **continuing state**, often one that results from an action, or a **posture**: 门开着 → *The door **is open**.* 他站着 → *He'**s standing**.* 墙上挂着一幅画 → *There's a picture (hanging) on the wall.*
3. 着 in **V₁着 V₂** describes an accompanying action, the background to the main one: 他笑着说 → *he said **with a smile*** / *he said, **smiling***. 她哭着跑了出去 → *She ran out **in tears*** / ***crying***.
4. Neither 在 nor 着 combines with **state verbs** such as 是, 知道, 有 — just as English state verbs resist the progressive ([[tense-and-aspect/simple-and-progressive#rule-states]]).
:::

::: proof
Li and Thompson (1981) group 在 and 着 as markers of **durative** aspect, but they show that they divide the work: 在 goes with activities that can be carried out, 着 with states and with the ongoing results of actions (门开着, 灯亮着). English uses *be* + *-ing* for activities in progress and *be* + adjective or the simple form for states. So 在 + activity matches the English progressive quite closely, while 着 often matches *be* + adjective, a prepositional phrase or *there is*. The progressive does appear with posture verbs (*He's standing*, *She's sitting by the window*) and with clothes (*She's wearing a red coat*, 她穿着红外套), which is why learners extend it to 门开着 — wrongly, because *The door is opening* describes a movement in progress.
:::

::: example 着 and 在 {#ex-zhe-zai}
Translate and explain. (a) 灯还亮着。 (b) 他正在开灯。 (c) 桌子上放着几本书。 (d) 她戴着一副眼镜。 (e) 我们一直在等你。
::: solution
(a) *The light is still on.* — 着 describes a continuing state: *be* + adverb, not ✗ *The light is still shining* (possible, but it describes the light itself, not the lamp being switched on).

(b) *He's switching on the light.* — 在 with an activity in progress. (In fact *switch on* is an achievement; the progressive shows the moment just before or during the action.)

(c) *There are some books on the table.* — 着 in a sentence that describes where things are: *there is/are*.

(d) *She's wearing glasses* (now) or *She wears glasses* (always). — With clothes, 着 matches the progressive of *wear*.

(e) *We've been waiting for you* (all this time). — 一直在 up to now: present perfect progressive ([[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect-progressive]]).
:::
:::

## Choosing the English tense

Because the Chinese markers do not map one-to-one onto English forms, translating particle by particle does not work. A safer method starts from the meaning.

::: theorem Choosing the tense when translating from Chinese {#rule-procedure}
For each finite verb in the English sentence, ask:

1. **When?** Is the situation past, present or future? Use the time words and the context, not 了 or 过. If it is past, every verb in that part of the text needs a past form.
2. **From where?** Is a finished time named or meant (past simple), or is the past seen from now (present perfect), from a past point (past perfect) or from a future point (future perfect)?
3. **Whole or in progress?** Is the situation a complete event, an activity in progress (progressive), or a state (simple)?
4. **A time or condition clause?** After *when*, *if*, *as soon as*, *until*, use a present tense for the future.
5. **Check the result** against the rules of the earlier lessons — especially state verbs, *for* and *since*, and *will* in time clauses.
:::

::: example Correcting a paragraph {#ex-correct}
A learner wrote this paragraph. Identify each tense error, say which Chinese structure may have led to it, and correct it.

*I come to Shanghai three years ago. At first I am not used to the weather, and I have missed my family very much. Now I live here for three years and I have many friends. Last month I had gone back home for my mother's birthday. When I will finish my studies, I am going to stay here.*
::: solution
1. *come* → *came*. 三年前 already shows the past, so Chinese 来 needs no marker; English needs the past form.
2. *am not used to* → *wasn't used to*. 一开始我不习惯 has no marker; *at first* places it in the past.
3. *have missed* → *missed*. Probably 很想家 or 想了 translated with a perfect; the time (*at first*) is finished.
4. *live here for three years* → *have lived* (or *have been living*) *here for three years*. 在这里住了三年了: the double 了 means up to now.
5. *had gone* → *went*. 上个月我回了一趟家: 了 marks completion, but *last month* is the main time; no later past point.
6. *will finish* → *finish*. 等我毕业了 or 我会毕业: a time clause, so present tense.

Corrected: *I came to Shanghai three years ago. At first I wasn't used to the weather, and I missed my family very much. Now I've lived here for three years and I have many friends. Last month I went back home for my mother's birthday. When I finish my studies, I'm going to stay here.*
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which English sentence best translates 我昨天很忙？
- [ ] *Yesterday I very busy.*
- [ ] *I am very busy yesterday.*
- [x] *I was very busy yesterday.*
- [ ] *I have been very busy yesterday.*
::: solution
The Chinese has no verb before the adjective and no aspect marker, because 昨天 is enough. English needs a verb (*be*), in the past tense because *yesterday* is a finished time. The present perfect cannot go with *yesterday*.
:::
:::

::: quiz
*He's been to Japan.* Which Chinese sentence has the same meaning?
- [x] 他去过日本。
- [ ] 他去了日本。
- [ ] 他在去日本。
- [ ] 他要去日本。
::: solution
*Has been to* means ‘went and came back’, an experience, like 过. 他去了日本 is closer to *He's gone to Japan* (he is there now) or, in a story, *He went to Japan*. 他在去日本 would be *He's on his way to Japan*, and 他要去日本 *He's going to Japan* (a plan).
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Not all Chinese speakers use the same markers.** This lesson follows Mandarin. Cantonese has its own aspect markers — 咗 for completed events, 紧 for actions in progress, 住 for continuing states and 过 for experience — and Wu, Min and Hakka varieties have others. The systems differ in detail but share the main features: no tense on the verb, and aspect marked by optional particles. The transfer problems described here therefore affect speakers of all of them, though not always in the same way.
- **The difficulty is often production, not understanding.** Many learners understand English tenses well in reading, because the context makes the time clear. The problems appear when speaking and writing quickly, when the Chinese habit of marking time once takes over. Checking one form at a time in your own writing — first the past simple, then the present perfect, then *will* in time clauses — is more effective than trying to watch everything at once.
- **Chinese markers can help.** When you translate from English into Chinese, the English tenses can guide your choice of 了, 过, 着 and 在, but you will often need fewer markers than English has tenses. A Chinese translation that marks every verb (我昨天去了图书馆，借了两本书，然后回了家) sounds heavy; natural Chinese uses 了 where the completion matters.
:::

::: history
The aspect particles of Chinese were described long before the word *aspect* was used for them. Chinese grammarians of the twentieth century discussed 了, 着 and 过 under various names, and Yuen Ren Chao's *A Grammar of Spoken Chinese* (1968) treated them as aspect suffixes of the verb. Charles Li and Sandra Thompson's *Mandarin Chinese: A Functional Reference Grammar* (1981) gave the account most often cited in English: perfective 了 for bounded events, sentence-final 了 for a "currently relevant state", experiential 过, and durative 在 and 着. In the following year 朱德熙's 《语法讲义》 (1982) described the same particles in Chinese as verb suffixes, and sentence-final 了 as a modal particle. Both books stress the point of this lesson: Chinese aspect markers are not tense markers, and their use depends on how the speaker views the event.
:::

## Where this leads

The final lesson, [[tense-and-aspect/tenses-in-texts]], moves from single sentences to whole texts and shows how tenses are chosen and kept consistent in stories, news, emails and academic writing. Translation between English and Chinese, including the treatment of tense and aspect, is the subject of *Translation*: see [[translation/english-chinese-compared]] and [[translation/chinese-into-english]]. Transfer from the first language, of which these tense errors are an example, is discussed in [[linguistics/language-acquisition]].

::: summary
- Mandarin has no tense: time is shown by time words, adverbs and context; aspect by 了, 过, 着 and 在, all of them often optional ([[#def-chinese-system]]).
- Verb + 了 marks a complete, bounded event, not past time; it can correspond to the past simple, present perfect, past perfect or even a present tense in a future time clause ([[#rule-le1]]).
- Sentence-final 了 marks a new situation relevant now; English often uses the present perfect, or a present tense with *now*, *already* or *any more* ([[#rule-le2]]).
- 过 marks experience: the present perfect with no time named (*I've been to Xi'an*), the past simple with a finished time; 过 and 了 parallel *been* and *gone* ([[#rule-guo]]).
- 在 + activity is close to the progressive; 着 usually describes a continuing state (*The door is open*), and both refuse state verbs ([[#rule-zai-zhe]]).
- Choose the English tense from time and viewpoint, not from the particles: when? from where? whole or in progress? a time clause? ([[#rule-procedure]]).
- Cantonese and other Chinese varieties have different particles but similar systems, so similar transfer problems.
:::

::: solution
*I came to Beijing in 2020. When I first arrived, I didn't know anyone, and I often felt lonely. Later I joined a running club and made a lot of friends. I've now lived in Beijing for six years, and it has become my second home. Next month my sister is coming to Beijing to start university too.*

- *came*, *arrived*, *didn't know*, *felt* — past simple: finished times (*in 2020*, *when I first arrived*); 不认识 and 觉得 have no marker but are past states.
- *joined*, *made* — past simple: events in sequence (了 = completion).
- *'ve lived … for six years* — 已经住了六年了 (double 了): up to now, present perfect.
- *has become* — 成了 with a result now: present perfect.
- *is coming* — 要……了 in the future, an arrangement: present continuous (or *is going to come*).

::: exercise Explain to a classmate {level=3}
A classmate says: "了 is the past tense and 过 is the present perfect." Write a reply (100–140 words) in English explaining why this is not quite right. Give at least two Chinese examples with their English translations.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

*That's a useful first idea, but it isn't quite right. 了 shows that an action is complete, not that it is past. For example, 明天下了课我去找你 is about the future: "I'll come and find you tomorrow after class." And many past sentences have no 了 at all: 我昨天很忙 is "I was very busy yesterday." So in English we choose the tense from the time, not from 了. 过 is often like the present perfect: 我去过西安 is "I've been to Xi'an." But if we say when, English needs the past simple: 我去年去过西安 is "I went to Xi'an last year," not "I've been to Xi'an last year." The particles help, but the time and the speaker's viewpoint decide the English tense.*

Check: 了 explained as completion with one future example and one past example without 了; 过 matched to the present perfect, with the exception for a finished time.
