Chen Hao is standing at his front door, looking in all his pockets. His neighbour asks what is wrong.

**Chen Hao:** *I've lost my keys.*

The next day, he tells a friend about it.

**Chen Hao:** *I lost my keys yesterday, but my neighbour had a spare set.*

In Chinese both sentences could use the same words: 我把钥匙丢了. In English the first uses the **present perfect** (*'ve lost*), the second the past simple (*lost*). The event — losing the keys — is the same, and in both sentences it is in the past. What is different is where Chen Hao stands when he speaks about it. In the first sentence he is still at the door without his keys: he looks at the past event **from now**, because it matters now. In the second sentence the problem is over, and he looks at the event **in its own past time**, *yesterday*.

[[tense-and-aspect/time-tense-aspect]] showed this difference on a timeline: in the present perfect the reference time R is the moment of speaking. This lesson explains the form of the present perfect, its four main uses, the words that often go with it (*just*, *already*, *yet*, *ever*, *for*, *since*), and how to choose between it and the past simple — one of the hardest choices in English for learners whose language has no tenses.

## Form

::: definition Present perfect {#def-present-perfect}
The **present perfect** is formed with the present tense of ***have*** (*have*, *has*) + the **past participle** of the main verb: *I have worked*, *she has gone*. It is the present tense combined with the perfect aspect. It presents a past situation **as seen from now**: the reference time is the present.
:::

| | statement | negative | question | short answers |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *I*, *you*, *we*, *they* | *They've finished.* | *They haven't finished.* | *Have they finished?* | *Yes, they have.* / *No, they haven't.* |
| *he*, *she*, *it* | *She's finished.* | *She hasn't finished.* | *Has she finished?* | *Yes, she has.* / *No, she hasn't.* |

The contraction *'s* can mean *is* or *has*. The next word tells you which: *She's working* (*is* + *-ing*), *She's worked* (*has* + past participle), *She's tired* (*is* + adjective).

Regular verbs have the same past participle as the past simple: *work* → *worked*, *study* → *studied*. Many irregular verbs have a different form. These are the ones learners use most:

| base | past simple | past participle |
|---|---|---|
| *be* | *was/were* | *been* |
| *go* | *went* | *gone* |
| *do* | *did* | *done* |
| *see* | *saw* | *seen* |
| *eat* | *ate* | *eaten* |
| *take* | *took* | *taken* |
| *write* | *wrote* | *written* |
| *speak* | *spoke* | *spoken* |
| *break* | *broke* | *broken* |
| *forget* | *forgot* | *forgotten* |
| *begin* | *began* | *begun* |
| *drink* | *drank* | *drunk* |
| *fly* | *flew* | *flown* |
| *know* | *knew* | *known* |
| *come* | *came* | *come* |
| *get* | *got* | *got* (BrE), *gotten* (AmE) |

In American English, *have gotten* is the usual perfect of *get* when it means ‘obtain’ or ‘become’ (*She's gotten a new job*, *It's gotten colder*), while *have got* means ‘have, possess’ in both varieties (*I've got two sisters*). British English uses *got* in all these meanings.

## Meaning and use

The present perfect always connects a past situation to the present. There are four main kinds of connection.

::: theorem Uses of the present perfect {#rule-pp-uses}
Use the present perfect for:

1. **Experience** — something that has happened at some time in a person's life up to now; the exact time is not given or not important. Often with *ever*, *never*, *before*, *once*, *twice*, *several times*: *Have you **ever been** to Xinjiang?* *I'**ve never eaten** durian.* *She'**s read** that book three times.*
2. **Change and present result** — a past event whose result is still true now. Often with *just*, *already*, *yet*, *recently*: *I'**ve lost** my keys* (I don't have them now). *Prices **have gone** up* (they are higher now). *The rain **has stopped*** (it is dry now).
3. **A situation continuing up to now** — a state or habit that started in the past and is still true. Usually with *for*, *since* or *How long…?*: *I'**ve known** Aisha **for** ten years.* *They'**ve lived** here **since** 2018.* *How long **have** you **had** this car?*
4. **A period of time that is not finished**: *today*, *this week*, *this year*, *so far*, *in my life*: *I'**ve had** three coffees **this morning*** (it is still morning). *We **haven't seen** much rain **this year**.*
:::

::: proof
In all four uses the reference time is now (R = S), and the event time is before it or reaches up to it. In use 1 the period "my life up to now" is still open, so a past experience is part of what I am now. In use 2 the result of the event is part of the present situation. In use 3 the situation itself reaches the present. In use 4 the time period named includes now. If the period is over — *this morning* when it is already afternoon — the past simple is used instead: *I had three coffees this morning*. Swan (2016), Leech (2004) and Quirk et al. (1985) describe the uses in similar groups; the shared idea is often called **current relevance**: the past situation is presented as relevant now.
:::

Use 2 is also common in news, where it announces a new event before the details, which then follow in the past simple:

- *The city council **has approved** a new bus line. The vote **took** place on Monday evening, and work **will** start next spring.*

::: example Which use? {#ex-uses}
Name the use of the present perfect (experience, result, continuing situation, unfinished period). (a) *I've broken my glasses, so I can't read this.* (b) *Have you ever tried Sichuan hotpot?* (c) *My grandparents have been married for fifty years.* (d) *I haven't done much work this week.* (e) *Look — someone has opened the window.*
::: solution
(a) **Result**: the glasses are broken now, so I can't read.

(b) **Experience**: at any time in your life, up to now (*ever*).

(c) **Continuing situation**: they married fifty years ago and are still married (*for*).

(d) **Unfinished period**: the week is not over.

(e) **Result**: the window is open now. The speaker does not know or care when it was opened.
:::
:::

## The present perfect or the past simple?

This is the choice that matters most. The two forms often describe the same event. The question is not *when did it happen?* but *from where do I look at it?*

::: theorem Present perfect or past simple {#rule-pp-past}
1. Use the **past simple** when the situation belongs to a **finished time** — named (*yesterday*, *last week*, *in 2019*, *two days ago*, *when I was a child*), asked about (*When…?*, *What time…?*), or clear from the context (a story, a person who has died, a period of life that is over).
2. Use the **present perfect** when no finished time is named or meant, and the situation is connected to **now** (experience, result, continuing situation, unfinished period).
3. Do not use the present perfect with an expression of finished time: ✗ *I have seen him yesterday.* ✓ *I saw him yesterday.*
:::

::: proof
The two forms differ in their reference time ([[tense-and-aspect/time-tense-aspect#def-ser]]). A finished time expression such as *yesterday* names the reference time, and puts it in the past: that is the past simple. The present perfect fixes the reference time at now, so it cannot also have a past reference time; that is why *have seen … yesterday* is contradictory. Questions with *When…?* ask for a past time, so they also take the past simple: *When did you arrive?*, not ✗ *When have you arrived?*
:::

| present perfect (from now) | past simple (a finished time) |
|---|---|
| *I've been to Japan twice.* | *I went to Japan in 2019 and 2023.* |
| *She's lost her phone.* (it is still lost) | *She lost her phone on the bus.* (a story) |
| *Have you seen my glasses?* (I need them now) | *Did you see the match last night?* |
| *He's worked here since 2020.* (he still does) | *He worked here from 2015 to 2020.* (he doesn't now) |
| *I've written two emails today.* (today is not over) | *I wrote two emails yesterday.* |

A typical conversation starts with the present perfect, to introduce the topic from now, and then moves to the past simple for details, because the details belong to a definite past time:

**Fatima:** *Have you ever been to Harbin?*

**Zhou Yang:** *Yes, I've been there once.*

**Fatima:** *When did you go?*

**Zhou Yang:** *In January. It was minus thirty, and I saw the ice festival.*

::: example Completing a conversation {#ex-conversation}
Complete with the present perfect or the past simple, and explain.

**A:** *(1 you / ever / work) in a restaurant?*
**B:** *Yes, I (2 have) a job in a noodle bar when I was a student.*
**A:** *(3 you / like) it?*
**B:** *Not really. But I (4 learn) a lot. And I (5 not cook) noodles since then!*
::: solution
(1) *Have you ever worked* — experience in B's life up to now; no time is given.

(2) *had* — *when I was a student* is a finished time.

(3) *Did you like* — the question is about that finished time, the job in the past.

(4) *learned* (or BrE *learnt*) — still about that time. (*I've learned a lot* is also possible if B means that the knowledge is useful now.)

(5) *haven't cooked* — *since then* reaches from that time up to now.
:::
:::

::: warning The present perfect with a finished time
✗ *I have finished the report yesterday.* ✓ *I finished the report yesterday.*

✗ *We have met in 2021.* ✓ *We met in 2021.*

✗ *When have you come to Beijing?* ✓ *When did you come to Beijing?*

✗ *Shakespeare has written many plays.* ✓ *Shakespeare wrote many plays.* (he is dead: his life is a finished time)

If you name or ask about a finished time, use the past simple. Keep the present perfect for the past seen from now.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *I have bought this coat last winter.*
- [ ] *Did you ever see a panda?* (said in London, as natural British English)
- [x] *I've never seen a panda.*
- [ ] *When have you started learning English?*
::: solution
*I've never seen a panda* is experience up to now. *Last winter* needs the past simple (*I bought*), and *When…?* asks for a finished time (*When did you start…?*). *Did you ever see…?* is heard in American English for experience, but the usual British form is *Have you ever seen…?*
:::

### *Been* and *gone*

*Go* has two past participles in the present perfect, with different meanings:

- *Ravi **has gone** to Shanghai.* — he went and is there now (or on his way).
- *Ravi **has been** to Shanghai.* — he went and came back; it is an experience.

So *I've been to Paris three times* is correct, but ✗ *I've gone to Paris three times* is not: you cannot be in Paris three times now.

## *Just*, *already*, *yet*, *ever* and *never*

::: theorem Adverbs with the present perfect {#rule-adverbs}
- ***just*** ‘a very short time ago’ — before the participle: *I've **just** seen Mei.*
- ***already*** ‘sooner than expected, or before now’ — before the participle, or at the end for emphasis: *She's **already** left.* *She's left **already**!*
- ***yet*** ‘up to now’ — in negatives and questions, at the end: *I haven't finished **yet**.* *Have you finished **yet**?*
- ***ever*** ‘at any time up to now’ — in questions, before the participle: *Have you **ever** been to Tibet?*
- ***never*** ‘at no time up to now’ — before the participle: *I've **never** been to Tibet.*
- ***still*** in negatives goes **before** *haven't*: *I **still** haven't heard from him.*
:::

::: remark British and American usage
With *just*, *already* and *yet*, British English usually uses the present perfect. American English often uses the past simple in the same situations, especially in conversation:

| British English (usual) | American English (common) |
|---|---|
| *I've just had lunch.* | *I just had lunch.* |
| *Have you eaten yet?* | *Did you eat yet?* |
| *I've already seen that film.* | *I already saw that movie.* |

Both are correct in their varieties, and American speakers also use the present perfect here. Swan (2016) describes the difference, and Biber et al. (1999) found that the present perfect is used more often in British than in American English, in conversation and in news. In British exams and formal writing, use the present perfect with these words.
:::

## *For*, *since* and *How long…?*

For a situation continuing up to now (use 3), English uses the present perfect, not the present simple.

::: theorem *For* and *since* {#rule-for-since}
- ***for*** + a **period of time**: *for three days*, *for ten years*, *for a long time*, *for ages*.
- ***since*** + a **point in time** when the period began: *since Monday*, *since 2015*, *since 9 o'clock*, *since I was a child*.

Questions use ***How long…?*** + the present perfect: *How long have you lived here?* After *since* + a clause, the verb in that clause is usually past simple: *I've lived here **since** I **moved** to Xiamen.*
:::

- *I've lived in Xiamen **for** six years.* = *I've lived in Xiamen **since** 2020.*
- *She hasn't called **for** weeks.*
- *It's been raining **since** lunchtime.* (the progressive form: [[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect-progressive]])
- *How long have you known each other? — **Since** university.*

::: warning The present simple or progressive for a situation up to now
✗ *I live here since 2020.* ✓ *I've lived here since 2020.*

✗ *I am knowing her for ten years.* ✓ *I've known her for ten years.*

✗ *How long are you in China?* (meaning: since when) ✓ *How long have you been in China?*

✗ *I've lived here since three years.* ✓ *I've lived here for three years.*

With *for* and *since* up to now, English needs the perfect. *How long are you in China?* asks about the future: ‘How long will you stay?’
:::

::: example *For*, *since* and the present perfect {#ex-for-since}
Rewrite the information as one sentence with the present perfect and *for* or *since*. It is now 2026. (a) *Nadia started working at the hospital in 2019. She still works there.* (b) *I last saw Tom in March.* (c) *My brother got his car five years ago.* (d) *We arrived at 8 a.m. and we're still waiting.*
::: solution
(a) *Nadia has worked at the hospital since 2019.* or *… for seven years.*

(b) *I haven't seen Tom since March.* (negative: the period without seeing him reaches up to now)

(c) *My brother has had his car for five years.* (Not ✗ *has got his car for five years*: *get* is an achievement — a single moment — so it cannot last for five years. *Have* is the state that follows.)

(d) *We've been waiting since 8 a.m.* (*wait* is an activity, so the present perfect progressive is the natural form: see [[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect-progressive]].)
:::
:::

::: example A news report {#ex-news}
Read the short news item and explain the choice of tense in each sentence.

*A team of volunteers **has cleaned** the beach at Haitang Bay. More than two hundred people **took** part on Saturday, and they **collected** over a tonne of rubbish. "We **have never seen** so much plastic," said one volunteer. The organisers **have** already **planned** a second clean-up for next month.*
::: solution
1. *has cleaned* — the opening sentence gives the news from now: the beach is clean now (result).
2. *took*, *collected* — details with a finished time (*on Saturday*): past simple.
3. *have never seen* — experience up to now, in the volunteer's own words.
4. *said* — the reporting verb belongs to the finished event.
5. *have … planned* — a result now: the plan exists. *Already* goes before the participle.

This pattern — present perfect for the headline fact, past simple for the details — is typical of news in English. [[tense-and-aspect/tenses-in-texts]] looks at it more closely.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **了 covers both.** 我丢了钥匙 can be *I've lost my keys* or *I lost my keys*. 了 after the verb marks a completed, bounded event (Li and Thompson 1981), but it says nothing about where the speaker views it from. English must choose: is there a finished time (past simple) or a connection to now (present perfect)?
- **Sentence-final 了 and change.** Li and Thompson describe sentence-final 了 as marking a "currently relevant state", a change that matters now: 下雨了 *It's started raining* / *It's raining now*; 他走了 *He's left* / *He's gone*. This is close to use 2 of the present perfect. But English often uses other forms for the same meaning: 我饿了 is simply *I'm hungry*, and 我明白了 is *I see* or *Now I understand*.
- **过 and experience.** 我去过长城 is close to use 1: *I've been to the Great Wall.* But 过 can also go with a finished time, and then English needs the past simple: 我去年去过长城 → *I went to the Great Wall last year*, not ✗ *I've been to the Great Wall last year*.
- **已经 and 还没.** 已经 is used much more often than English *already*: 我已经吃了 is usually just *I've eaten* (or *I've already eaten* if it is sooner than expected). 还没（有）+ verb → *haven't … yet*: 我还没吃 *I haven't eaten yet*. Note that the negative 没（有） replaces 了: 我没吃, not ✗ 我没吃了.
- **Duration up to now.** 我在这儿住了三年了, with two 了, means the situation continues: *I've lived here for three years.* With only the first 了, 我在这儿住了三年 can describe a finished period: *I lived here for three years.* Learners who translate both as *I live here three years* lose the difference.
:::

::: history
The English perfect grew out of an ordinary sentence with *have* meaning ‘possess’. In Old English a sentence of the type *I have the letter written* meant something like ‘I have the letter, and it is in a written state’. Over many centuries *have* lost its meaning of possession in this construction, the participle moved next to it, and *I have written the letter* became a tense form that could be used with any verb, even verbs with no object (*I have slept*). For a long time verbs of motion and change formed the perfect with *be* instead: the King James Bible (1611) has *Father, the hour is come* (John 17:1). *Have* gradually replaced *be* in the following centuries, although the old form survives in fixed phrases such as *He is gone* and *Winter is come*. A similar *have* perfect developed in French, German and other European languages, but its uses differ: German and French often use their perfect where English needs the past simple, so speakers of these languages also say ✗ *I have seen him yesterday*.
:::

## Where this leads

[[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect-progressive]] adds the progressive to the present perfect (*I've been waiting for an hour*). The past perfect, the same structure with *had*, moves the reference point into the past ([[tense-and-aspect/narrative-tenses]]), and the future perfect moves it into the future ([[tense-and-aspect/future-forms]]). The comparison with 了 and 过 is completed in [[tense-and-aspect/tense-and-chinese]], and the use of the present perfect in news and academic writing (*Several studies have shown…*) in [[tense-and-aspect/tenses-in-texts]].

::: summary
- The present perfect is *have/has* + past participle (*been*, *gone*, *done*, *seen* …); it presents a past situation seen **from now** ([[#def-present-perfect]]).
- Its four uses: experience (*ever*, *never*), change with a present result (*just*, *already*, *yet*), a situation continuing up to now (*for*, *since*) and an unfinished period (*today*, *this year*) ([[#rule-pp-uses]]).
- Use the past simple with a finished time (named, asked about with *When…?*, or clear from context); never ✗ *I have seen him yesterday* ([[#rule-pp-past]]).
- *Has gone* = is there now; *has been* = went and came back.
- *Just*, *already*, *ever*, *never* go before the participle, *yet* at the end; American English often uses the past simple with them ([[#rule-adverbs]]).
- *For* + period, *since* + point; a situation up to now needs the perfect: *I've lived here since 2020*, not ✗ *I live here since 2020* ([[#rule-for-since]]).
- Chinese 了 can correspond to either the past simple or the present perfect; sentence-final 了 and 过 are close to some uses of the present perfect, but not to all.
:::
