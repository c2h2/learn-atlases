Here is the beginning of a short story:

> *It was a cold evening in December. The streets were wet, because it had been raining all afternoon, and people were hurrying home with their umbrellas. Mina was waiting for the last bus. She had missed the earlier one by two minutes. Suddenly a man in a grey coat stopped beside her and said her name. She looked up. She had not seen him for twenty years.*

The story uses four past forms, and each one has a job. *Stopped*, *said* and *looked* are the **events**: they move the story forward, one after another. *Were hurrying* and *was waiting* paint the **background**: things that were going on when the events happened. *Had missed* and *had not seen* go **further back**, to events before the story's main time. And *had been raining* describes an **activity that went on up to** that main time and left its traces — the wet streets.

In Chinese, a storyteller can order events with time words (那天晚上, 之前, 已经, 二十年来) and often leaves the verbs unmarked. In English, the tenses themselves tell the reader what is foreground, what is background and what happened earlier. This lesson shows how the four **narrative tenses** work together. You know the past simple from [[core-grammar/past-simple]]; here it is joined by the past progressive, the past perfect and the past perfect progressive.

## The four narrative tenses

| form | structure | job in a story | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| past simple | *worked*, *went* | the main events, in order | *She **looked** up.* |
| past progressive | *was/were* + *-ing* | background; an action interrupted | *People **were hurrying** home.* |
| past perfect | *had* + past participle | an earlier event, before the story's time | *She **had missed** the bus.* |
| past perfect progressive | *had been* + *-ing* | an activity up to a point in the past | *It **had been raining** all afternoon.* |

All four are past tense: the first verb is *worked*, *was*, *had*. They differ in aspect ([[tense-and-aspect/time-tense-aspect#def-aspect]]). The reference time R is the moment in the past that the story has reached; each form places its event in relation to R.

## The past progressive

::: definition Past progressive {#def-past-progressive}
The **past progressive** (or past continuous) is *was/were* + the *-ing* form: *I was working*, *they were working*. It presents a situation **in progress at a time in the past**: it had started before that time and had not yet ended.
:::

::: theorem Uses of the past progressive {#rule-past-progressive}
1. **An action in progress at a past time**: *At nine o'clock last night I **was watching** a film.* *What **were** you **doing** when the earthquake happened?*
2. **An action interrupted by a shorter event**, which is in the past simple: *I **was having** a shower when the phone **rang**.* *While we **were walking** home, it **started** to snow.*
3. **Two actions in progress at the same time**: *While Lin **was cooking**, her husband **was setting** the table.*
4. **The background or scene** of a story: *The sun **was shining** and the birds **were singing**.*
:::

::: proof
These are the uses of the progressive in general ([[tense-and-aspect/simple-and-progressive#def-progressive]]), with the reference time in the past. The progressive presents a situation from the inside, as a frame: the shorter event in the past simple happens inside the frame. Because the progressive says nothing about the end, *I was having a shower when the phone rang* does not tell us whether the shower continued; perhaps I got out to answer. Swan (2016) and Murphy (2019) give the same uses. State verbs ([[tense-and-aspect/simple-and-progressive#rule-states]]) normally stay simple in the past too: *I knew the answer*, not ✗ *I was knowing*.
:::

### *When* and *while*

- ***While*** usually introduces the **longer** action, in the past progressive: *While I **was waiting**, I **read** the news.*
- ***When*** usually introduces the **shorter** event, in the past simple: *I **was waiting** when the bus **arrived**.* But *when* can also introduce a progressive: *When I **was** living in Wuhan, I often ate hot dry noodles.*
- ***As*** can replace *while* for two things happening together, especially in writing: *As she **was leaving**, the phone rang.*

::: example *When* + simple or progressive? {#ex-when}
Explain the difference in meaning. (a) *When Ali arrived, we had dinner.* (b) *When Ali arrived, we were having dinner.* (c) *When Ali arrived, we had had dinner.*
::: solution
(a) Past simple + past simple: two events in order. Ali arrived first, **then** we had dinner (perhaps with him).

(b) Past progressive: dinner was **in progress** when he arrived. It had started before.

(c) Past perfect: dinner was **finished** before he arrived (*had had*: perfect *had* + the participle of *have*). Perhaps he was hungry.

The clause *When Ali arrived* sets the reference time in all three. The aspect of the main verb places dinner after, around or before it.
:::
:::

::: quiz
*I was reading in bed when I heard a strange noise.* Which is true?
- [x] I started reading before I heard the noise.
- [ ] I started reading after I heard the noise.
- [ ] I finished reading before I heard the noise.
- [ ] I heard many noises while I read.
::: solution
The past progressive *was reading* frames the event *heard*. The reading was already in progress, so it started before the noise. The sentence does not say whether I continued reading afterwards.
:::
:::

## The past perfect

::: definition Past perfect {#def-past-perfect}
The **past perfect** is *had* + past participle, the same for all subjects: *I had finished*, *she had gone*; negative *hadn't*; contracted *'d* (*I'd gone* — the same contraction as *would*). It presents an event **before a reference time in the past**: in the timeline of [[tense-and-aspect/time-tense-aspect#def-ser]], E is before R, and R is before S.
:::

::: theorem Uses of the past perfect {#rule-past-perfect}
1. **An event earlier than the story's time**, when the story goes back: *When I got to the station, the train **had left**.* *She was nervous. She **had never spoken** in public before.*
2. **The present perfect moved into the past**: experience, result or a situation up to a past point, often with *already*, *just*, *never … before*, *for*, *since*, *by the time*: *By the time we arrived, the concert **had** already **started**.* *I **had known** him for years when he moved to Canada.*
3. After ***It was the first time…*** and similar expressions: *It was the first time I **had seen** snow.*
4. In **reported speech** and **unreal past conditions**: *She said she **had lost** her ticket.* *If I **had known**, I would have helped.* (See [[complex-sentences/reported-speech]] and [[complex-sentences/unreal-past]].)
:::

::: proof
The past perfect does for a past reference time what the present perfect does for now. The present perfect looks back from now ([[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect#def-present-perfect]]); the past perfect looks back from a point in the past. This is why it goes with the same words (*already*, *never before*, *for*, *since*) and why reported speech turns *I have lost my ticket* into *She said she had lost her ticket*. Leech (2004) calls the past perfect "past in the past"; Huddleston and Pullum (2002) describe it as a past tense combined with the perfect.
:::

::: theorem When the past perfect is not needed {#rule-pp-optional}
The past perfect is needed only when the order of events would otherwise be unclear or wrong.

1. When events are told **in the order they happened**, use the past simple: *She **finished** her work, **switched off** the computer and **went** home.*
2. After ***after***, ***before*** and ***as soon as***, the order is already clear, so the past simple is common and the past perfect is optional: *After she **finished** / **had finished** her work, she went home.*
3. But with ***when***, the choice can change the meaning: *When I arrived, she **left*** (she left after I arrived) / *When I arrived, she **had left*** (she left before I arrived).
4. Do not use the past perfect only because something happened **long ago**. It needs a later past time to look back from: ✗ *I had visited Beijing in 2010.* ✓ *I visited Beijing in 2010.*
:::

::: warning The past perfect for "a long time ago"
✗ *When I was a child, I had lived in a small village.* ✓ *When I was a child, I lived in a small village.*

✗ *Yesterday I had gone to the cinema.* ✓ *Yesterday I went to the cinema.*

The past perfect is not a "more past" past. It is used when a story is at one past time and goes back to an earlier one. Some learners think of the past perfect (过去完成时) as the form for completed actions, because 完成 is also the word used to describe 了; but in English, completion in the past is normally expressed by the past simple.
:::

::: example Is the past perfect needed? {#ex-needed}
Decide whether the past perfect is needed, possible or wrong in the verbs in brackets. (a) *We got to the cinema late. The film (start).* (b) *She opened the door, (switch) on the light and (sit) down.* (c) *After he (finish) university, he moved to Chengdu.* (d) *I (be) born in 2003.* (e) *I recognised her immediately, although we (not meet) for ten years.*
::: solution
(a) **Needed**: *had started*. The story is at the moment we arrived; the start of the film was earlier. (*The film started* would mean it started when we arrived.)

(b) **Wrong**: *switched*, *sat*. The events are in order.

(c) **Possible**: *finished* or *had finished*. *After* already shows the order.

(d) **Wrong**: *was born*. There is no later past point to look back from.

(e) **Needed**: *hadn't met*. A situation up to a past point (the moment I recognised her), like the present perfect moved into the past.
:::
:::

## The past perfect progressive

::: definition Past perfect progressive {#def-past-perfect-progressive}
The **past perfect progressive** is *had been* + *-ing*: *I had been waiting*. It presents an **activity going on over a period up to a point in the past**, or just before it, often with visible results at that point: *I had been waiting for an hour when the bus finally came.* *Her eyes were red. She had been crying.*
:::

It is the present perfect progressive ([[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect-progressive#def-ppp]]) moved into the past, and the same choices apply. Use the simple past perfect for a result or a number (*She had written three letters*), and keep state verbs simple (*They had known each other since school*, not ✗ *had been knowing*).

- *The ground was wet. It **had been raining**.*
- *When I met Ren, he **had been working** at the hospital for six years.*
- *We were exhausted because we **had been walking** since dawn.*

::: quiz
*When the police arrived, the thieves ___.* The thieves were not there any more. Which form fits?
- [ ] *left*
- [ ] *were leaving*
- [x] *had left*
- [ ] *have left*
::: solution
The thieves left **before** the police arrived, so the past perfect *had left* is needed. *Left* would put their leaving after the police arrived; *were leaving* would mean they were in the middle of leaving, so they were still there; *have left* is present perfect and cannot look back from a past point.
:::
:::

## Putting the story together

::: theorem Tenses in a narrative {#rule-narrative}
In a story told in the past:

1. the **past simple** carries the main line of events (the **foreground**);
2. the **past progressive** describes what was going on around those events (the **background**);
3. the **past perfect** (simple or progressive) goes back to events before the main line, and is often used only for the first verb or two of a flashback, after which the story returns to the past simple once the earlier time is clear.
:::

::: proof
Studies of narrative have found the same division in many languages. Paul Hopper (1979) showed that the events which move a story forward (foreground) and the description around them (background) are often marked by different aspects: in English, simple for foreground and progressive for background. The third point is a matter of style: Swan (2016) and other usage guides note that a long flashback in the past perfect sounds heavy, so writers often start it with *had* + participle and then continue in the past simple: *She remembered her first day at school. Her mother **had taken** her to the gate. She **cried** all morning, and a kind teacher **gave** her a sweet.*
:::

::: example Completing a story {#ex-story}
Put each verb in the past simple, past progressive, past perfect or past perfect progressive, and explain.

*Last summer Daniel (1 travel) around Yunnan. One afternoon he (2 sit) in a café in Dali when he (3 realise) that his passport was missing. He (4 have) it that morning, because he (5 show) it at the hotel. He (6 search) his bag for ten minutes when the café owner (7 come) over, smiling. A waiter (8 find) the passport on the bus.*
::: solution
(1) *was travelling* (AmE *traveling*) — background over a period. (*travelled* is also possible, as a summary.)

(2) *was sitting* — in progress when the next event happened.

(3) *realised* (AmE *realized*) — a main event.

(4) *had had* — a state before the main time (he had the passport earlier that day).

(5) *had shown* — an event before the main time.

(6) *had been searching* — an activity up to the moment the owner came (*for ten minutes*).

(7) *came* — a main event.

(8) *had found* — an event earlier than the owner's arrival, reported as a flashback.
:::
:::

::: example Correcting a paragraph {#ex-correct}
A student wrote this paragraph. Find and correct the tense mistakes.

*Last year I had gone to Qingdao with my parents. On the first day we were visiting the beach. While we walked along the sand, my father was losing his hat in the wind. We laughed a lot. Before the trip I never saw the sea, so I was very excited.*
::: solution
1. *had gone* → *went*. No earlier time to look back from; *last year* is the main time.
2. *were visiting* → *visited*. A main event, not background.
3. *walked* → *were walking*. The longer action, in progress (*while*), frames the event.
4. *was losing* → *lost*. A short event inside the frame.
5. *never saw* → *had never seen*. Experience up to a past point (*before the trip*).

Corrected: *Last year I went to Qingdao with my parents. On the first day we visited the beach. While we were walking along the sand, my father lost his hat in the wind. We laughed a lot. Before the trip I had never seen the sea, so I was very excited.*
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence uses the past perfect correctly?
- [ ] *My grandfather had been born in 1950.*
- [x] *By the time I got home, everyone had gone to bed.*
- [ ] *We had gone to the park and played football yesterday.*
- [ ] *I had lived in Dalian when I was a child.*
::: solution
*By the time I got home* sets a past reference time, and going to bed happened before it. The other sentences have no later past point to look back from; they need the past simple: *was born*, *went … and played*, *lived*.

::: note English and Chinese
- **Background with 在 and 着, foreground with 了.** Chinese stories often mark background with 正在/在 or 着 (我正在洗澡的时候，电话响了; 他哼着歌走进来) and completed main events with 了. This is close to the English division of past progressive and past simple, but Chinese can leave either unmarked, and English cannot: every verb needs a past form.
- **已经 and 之前 instead of the past perfect.** 我到的时候，他已经走了 → *When I arrived, he had already left.* Chinese shows the earlier event with 已经, 早就 or 之前; English uses *had* + participle. If 已经 is missing in Chinese (我到的时候，他走了), the meaning depends on context; in English, *he left* and *he had left* give two different orders.
- **"完成" does not mean the perfect.** Chinese grammar books call the English perfect 完成时 and also describe 了 as marking 完成 (completion). This makes it tempting to translate every 了 in a story as *had* + participle. But a completed event in the past is normally the past simple; the past perfect needs a later past point.
- **Duration up to a past point.** 他在医院工作了六年了，后来去了加拿大 can be *He had been working / had worked at the hospital for six years when he moved to Canada*: the double 了 up to a past point corresponds to the past perfect.
:::

::: history
How do people organise stories? In 1967 the sociolinguists William Labov and Joshua Waletzky published a study of personal stories told aloud by ordinary speakers in the United States. They found a regular structure: an **orientation** that sets the scene (who, where, when, what was going on), a **complicating action** made of events in order, an **evaluation** that shows why the story matters, a **resolution** and a **coda** that returns to the present. In English the orientation is typically where the past progressive and past perfect appear, and the complicating action is told in the past simple. In 1979 the linguist Paul Hopper compared narratives in several languages and argued that marking **foreground** and **background** is one of the main jobs of aspect in discourse.
:::

## Where this leads

*Used to* and *would* add two more ways of telling about the past, for habits and repeated events ([[tense-and-aspect/past-habits]]). The past perfect returns in reported speech ([[complex-sentences/reported-speech]]) and in unreal conditions about the past ([[complex-sentences/unreal-past]]). Telling stories aloud is practised in [[listening-and-conversation/storytelling]], and writing narratives in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/describing-narrating]]. [[tense-and-aspect/tenses-in-texts]] looks at how fiction and news use these tenses, including the present tense for telling stories.

::: summary
- Stories use four past forms: the **past simple** for the main events, the **past progressive** for background and interrupted actions, the **past perfect** for earlier events, and the **past perfect progressive** for activities up to a past point.
- The past progressive (*was/were* + *-ing*) frames a shorter event in the past simple: *I was having a shower when the phone rang* ([[#def-past-progressive]], [[#rule-past-progressive]]).
- *While* usually goes with the longer, progressive action; *when* with the shorter event.
- The past perfect (*had* + participle) looks back from a past reference time: E before R before S ([[#def-past-perfect]], [[#rule-past-perfect]]).
- It is not needed when events are told in order, and it is optional after *after* and *before*; with *when* it changes the meaning. It is never just "long ago" ([[#rule-pp-optional]]).
- The past perfect progressive (*had been* + *-ing*) is an activity up to a past point, often with visible results: *She had been crying* ([[#def-past-perfect-progressive]]).
- Chinese marks background with 在/着 and earlier events with 已经/之前, and can leave verbs unmarked; English must choose a past form for every verb.
:::
