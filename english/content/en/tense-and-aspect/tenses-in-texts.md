One morning a small fire broke out in the kitchen of a school canteen in a city called Riverton. Nobody was hurt. Here is the same event in four texts, all written for this lesson:

> *A story:* *The smell of smoke reached the classroom just as Ms Adeyemi was writing on the board. Someone in the corridor shouted. By the time the children had lined up outside, two fire engines were already turning into the school gate.*

> *A news report:* *Fire hits school kitchen. A fire has damaged the kitchen of Riverton Primary School. The fire started at about 10 a.m. on Tuesday, and firefighters put it out within twenty minutes. No one was injured.*

> *An email:* *Dear parents, I'm writing to let you know that there was a small fire in the school kitchen this morning. All the children are safe. The canteen will be closed until Friday, so please send your child with a packed lunch.*

> *An academic report:* *Kitchen fires are the most common type of fire in schools. In this study we examined 120 reports of school fires. The results show that most fires started during cooking, and they suggest that staff training reduces the damage.*

The event is the same, but the tenses are very different. The story moves through the past with all four narrative tenses. The news report starts with the present perfect and continues in the past simple. The email uses the present for the situation now and the future for the plans. The academic report uses the present for general facts and for what the results show, and the past for what the researchers did.

Each lesson of this course has looked at one part of the system. This last lesson looks at whole texts: how a writer chooses a basic tense, when it is right to change it, and what the usual patterns are in stories, news, emails and academic writing.

## The time frame of a text

::: definition Time frame {#def-time-frame}
The **time frame** of a text (or of a part of it) is the basic time from which it is told: a **past frame** (a story, a report of past events) or a **present frame** (a description, a general explanation, a conversation about now). Within a frame, the other tenses are chosen in relation to it: in a past frame, earlier events take the past perfect; in a present frame, past events seen from now take the present perfect.
:::

| frame | basic tense | earlier | in progress | later |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| past | past simple | past perfect | past progressive | *would*, *was going to* |
| present | present simple | present perfect, past simple | present progressive | *will*, *going to*, present continuous |

The idea of a frame explains most tense choices in a text. Once the frame is set, it should stay the same until there is a reason to change it.

::: theorem Keeping tenses consistent {#rule-consistency}
1. **Keep the frame.** Once a text or paragraph has a past or present frame, keep its basic tense.
2. **Change the tense only for a reason**: a change of time (*Now I live in Wuhan*), a general truth that is still true (*Tigers are good swimmers*), a comment from the writer's present (*I still remember that day*), or direct speech (*"I'm lost," she said*).
3. **Signal the change** with a time expression or a new paragraph where possible (*Today…*, *Since then…*, *Years later…*).
:::

::: proof
A reader uses the tenses to follow the order of events. If the tense changes without a reason, the reader assumes that the time has changed, and becomes confused when it has not. Changes that are motivated, however, are normal and expected. Usage guides such as Swan (2016) note that a general truth can stay in the present inside a past narrative (*Our teacher explained that light travels faster than sound*), and a comment from the time of writing naturally uses the present. What matters is that each shift reflects a real difference in time or viewpoint.
:::

::: example Motivated and unmotivated shifts {#ex-shifts}
Read the paragraph. Decide whether each tense change is motivated (M) or a mistake (✗), and correct the mistakes.

*Last spring my grandfather (1) **took** me to see the cherry blossoms in Wuhan. The trees (2) **are** in full flower, and thousands of people (3) **walk** under them. My grandfather (4) **told** me that he (5) **had first come** there in 1975. Cherry trees (6) **flower** for only about two weeks a year, so we (7) **were** lucky. I still (8) **have** the photo we took that day.*
::: solution
(1) *took* — sets the past frame.

(2) *are* ✗ → *were*. The trees were in flower at that past time; nothing signals a change.

(3) *walk* ✗ → *were walking* (background) or *walked*.

(4) *told* — past frame, M (correct).

(5) *had first come* — M: an event before the past frame.

(6) *flower* — M: a general truth that is still true.

(7) *were* — past frame.

(8) *have* — M: a comment from the writer's present, signalled by *still*.
:::
:::

## Stories

Stories are usually told in a **past frame**, with the four narrative tenses of [[tense-and-aspect/narrative-tenses]]: the past simple for the main events, the past progressive for the background, and the past perfect for earlier events. Three other choices are worth knowing.

- **The present tense in spoken stories and jokes.** In informal storytelling, speakers often switch into the present at the exciting moment: *So I'm waiting at the bus stop, and this man comes up to me and says…* This is often called the **historic present** (or narrative present). It makes the story feel immediate. It is common in conversation, jokes and some modern fiction, but in formal writing a story should keep the past frame.
- **Present-tense fiction.** Some novels and short stories are written entirely in the present frame (*She opens the door. The room is dark.*). The writer then uses the present perfect for earlier events and the present progressive for background — the same system, moved to a present frame.
- **Summaries of plots** of books and films are normally written in the **present simple**: *In the novel, a young doctor **moves** to a small town and **discovers** a secret.* The story exists now, as a text, so the summary describes it in the present.

::: quiz
Which sentence best summarises the plot of a film?
- [ ] *A young woman had travelled to Lhasa and had found her lost brother.*
- [x] *A young woman travels to Lhasa to find her lost brother.*
- [ ] *A young woman is travelling to Lhasa and is finding her lost brother.*
- [ ] *A young woman has travelled to Lhasa and has found her lost brother.*
::: solution
Plot summaries are written in the present simple, because the story exists now as a film or book. The past perfect would need a later past point; the progressive would describe the events as happening around now; the present perfect would describe results now, not the plot.
:::
:::

## News

::: theorem Tenses in news reports {#rule-news}
1. **Headlines** use special forms: the **present simple** for recent past events (*Fire hits school kitchen*), the ***to*-infinitive** for future events (*Minister to visit Kenya*), and a **past participle** for passives, without *be* (*Two injured in crash* = *Two people were injured*).
2. The **first sentence** (the lead) often uses the **present perfect** to give the news from now: *A fire **has damaged** the kitchen of Riverton Primary School.*
3. The **details** follow in the **past simple**, because they belong to a definite past time: *The fire **started** at about 10 a.m. on Tuesday.*
4. **Reported speech** uses *said* + the past tenses of the original words, or keeps the present if it is still true: *The head teacher **said** that the children **were** / **are** safe.*
:::

::: proof
A headline must be short and must feel current. The present simple is shorter than the present perfect and presents the event as news now; *to* + infinitive is shorter than *is going to*. Biber et al. (1999) found that the perfect aspect is used more in news and fiction than in conversation or academic prose, largely because news so often presents past events as relevant now. The movement from present perfect to past simple is the same pattern as in conversation ([[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect#rule-pp-past]]): the topic is introduced from now, then the details are placed at their finished time. [[complex-sentences/reported-speech]] explains the tense changes after *said*.
:::

::: example Headlines into sentences {#ex-headlines}
Rewrite each headline as a full first sentence of a news report, and explain the tense. (a) *Heavy rain closes airport* (b) *Hospital to open new children's ward* (c) *Five rescued from mountain* (d) *City wins clean air award*
::: solution
(a) *Heavy rain **has closed** the city's airport.* — the present perfect gives a recent event with a present result (the airport is closed). In a later sentence: *The airport **closed** at 6 a.m.*

(b) *The hospital **is going to open** / **will open** / **is to open** a new children's ward.* — *to* + infinitive in a headline refers to the future.

(c) *Five people **have been rescued** from the mountain.* — a passive; *rescued* in the headline is a past participle, not a past simple.

(d) *The city **has won** an award for clean air.* — the headline's present simple refers to a recent past event.
:::
:::

## Emails and messages

Emails mix the present, the past and the future, because they are about a situation now and its plans. The tenses also help to make an email polite.

| purpose | usual tense | example |
|---|---|---|
| saying why you are writing | present progressive | *I'm writing to ask about the course.* |
| attachments and actions just done | present perfect | *I've attached the report.* *I've booked the room.* |
| referring to an earlier contact | past simple | *Thank you for your email. As we discussed on Monday, …* |
| plans and promises | *will*, *going to*, present continuous | *I'll send the details tomorrow.* *We're meeting at 3.* |
| softening a request | past progressive or past simple | *I was wondering if you could help.* *I wanted to ask…* |
| closing | *-ing* phrase | *Looking forward to hearing from you.* |

::: remark The polite past
*I was wondering if…* and *I wanted to ask…* use a past tense for a present request. The past puts a little distance between the speaker and the request, and the progressive makes it sound less fixed, so the request is easier to refuse. Compare *I want to ask you a favour* (direct) with *I was wondering whether you could do me a favour* (polite). This use of the past for politeness is part of [[modality/modality-politeness]]; for a learner, it is one of the most useful patterns in email English.
:::

::: example Completing an email {#ex-email}
Complete the email with the correct form of the verbs in brackets.

*Dear Dr Rahman,*

*Thank you for your reply. As you (1 suggest) last week, I (2 make) some changes to my proposal, and I (3 attach) the new version. I (4 wonder) if you (5 have) time to look at it before our meeting. I (6 fly) to Kunming on Thursday for a conference, so I (7 not be) able to check my email until Monday. I (8 send) you the final version as soon as I (9 get) back.*

*Best wishes,*
*Liu Fang*
::: solution
(1) *suggested* — a finished past contact (*last week*).
(2) *have made* — a result now (the proposal is changed).
(3) *have attached* (or *am attaching*, *attach*) — an action just done in this email.
(4) *was wondering* (or *am wondering*) — the polite past.
(5) *had* / *would have* / *might have* — after *I was wondering if*, the past *had* matches the politeness; *have* is also acceptable.
(6) *am flying* — an arrangement (the ticket is booked).
(7) *won't be* — a prediction.
(8) *will send* (*'ll send*) — a promise.
(9) *get* — a time clause after *as soon as*: present tense ([[tense-and-aspect/future-forms#rule-time-clauses]]).
:::
:::

## Academic writing

::: theorem Tenses in academic writing {#rule-academic}
1. The **present simple** for general facts and claims, and for what a text or figure says: *Kitchen fires **are** the most common…* *Smith (2019) **argues** that…* *Table 2 **shows**…*
2. The **past simple** for what was done or found in a particular study, especially the writer's own: *We **interviewed** forty students.* *Smith (2019) **found** that…* (one study, finished).
3. The **present perfect** to summarise a body of research up to now: *Several studies **have shown** that…* *Little research **has examined**…*
4. The **future** (*will*) mainly to announce what the text will do: *This essay **will argue** that…* *Section 3 **will discuss**…* Many writers use the present for this too: *This essay **argues**…*
:::

::: proof
The choice follows the timeline. A general claim is true now, so it takes the present. A study that someone carried out is a finished past event, so it takes the past simple. A summary of research "so far" looks at many past studies from now, like the experiential present perfect. And a published text exists now, so what it *says* or *argues* can be reported in the present, even if it was written years ago. Writing guides such as Swales and Feak's *Academic Writing for Graduate Students* describe these tendencies, and add that they vary between disciplines: in some fields, reporting verbs are usually in the present (*Lee argues*), in others in the past (*Lee found*). [[academic-writing/academic-style]] and [[academic-writing/literature-review]] develop this.
:::

::: example Tenses in an abstract {#ex-abstract}
Choose the better tense in this short abstract (a summary of a research paper), and explain.

*Many students (1 find / found) English tenses difficult. Previous research (2 focused / has focused) on written errors. In this study, we (3 recorded / have recorded) 30 conversations between Chinese and British students. The results (4 show / showed) that the present perfect was rarely used in speech. We (5 suggest / suggested) that teachers should give more attention to spoken forms.*
::: solution
(1) *find* — a general fact, true now.

(2) *has focused* — a summary of research up to now.

(3) *recorded* — what the writers did in this study: a finished past action.

(4) *show* — the results are reported as what they say now (*showed* is also used in some fields, especially for a single finding).

(5) *suggest* — the writers' present claim. (*was rarely used* stays past: it describes what happened in the recorded conversations.)
:::
:::

::: warning Changing frame in the middle of a story
✗ *Last week we went to a restaurant. The waiter brings us the menu, but we can't read it, so we asked for help.*

✓ *Last week we went to a restaurant. The waiter **brought** us the menu, but we **couldn't** read it, so we asked for help.*

In formal writing, keep a story in the past frame. The historic present (*brings*, *can't*) is fine in a spoken anecdote, but if you use it, use it consistently and on purpose, not for one or two verbs.
:::

::: example One event, two texts {#ex-rewrite}
Rewrite the story from the beginning of this lesson as the first three sentences of a short news report. Then explain how the tenses changed.

*The smell of smoke reached the classroom just as Ms Adeyemi was writing on the board. Someone in the corridor shouted. By the time the children had lined up outside, two fire engines were already turning into the school gate.*
::: solution
A possible news version:

*Children have been evacuated from a primary school in Riverton after a fire in the kitchen. Teachers led the pupils outside at about 10 a.m. on Tuesday, when they smelled smoke. Two fire engines arrived within minutes, and no one was hurt.*

- The news lead uses the **present perfect** (passive: *have been evacuated*) to give the result from now.
- The details are in the **past simple** with a definite time (*on Tuesday*, *within minutes*).
- The story's background (*was writing*, *were turning*) and earlier events (*had lined up*) disappear: news reports usually tell events in order and as complete wholes, while stories build a scene.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Time set once, or in every verb.** A Chinese narrative often sets the time in the first sentence (那年夏天……) and then leaves the verbs unmarked. Learners who write the same way in English start in the past and then slip into the present after a few sentences. Check every finite verb in a past-frame paragraph, not just the first.
- **Headlines.** Chinese headlines also leave out time marking (暴雨致机场关闭), so the English present-tense headline (*Heavy rain closes airport*) may feel natural. The difference comes in the article: Chinese often continues without tense, English moves to the present perfect and then to the past simple.
- **将 and *to* + infinitive.** Chinese news uses 将 for planned events (医院将开设新的儿童病房); English headlines use *to* + infinitive (*Hospital to open children's ward*), and the article uses *will* or *is to*.
- **Reporting research.** Chinese academic writing usually does not mark the difference between 史密斯认为 (a claim that holds now) and 史密斯发现 (a finding in one study) with tense. In English, the choice of *argues* (present) or *found* (past) carries that difference, so it is worth deciding which you mean.
- **Politeness through tense.** Chinese makes requests polite with words such as 请, 能不能 and 麻烦您; it does not use a past form for politeness. The English *I was wondering if…* can therefore feel strange at first, but it is one of the most common polite patterns in English emails.
:::

::: history
How often is each tense used in different kinds of text? Douglas Biber and his colleagues answered this question with corpus counts in the *Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English* (1999). They found that past tense verbs dominate in fiction, while present tense verbs dominate in conversation and academic prose; that the progressive is most common in conversation; and that the perfect is used most in fiction and news. Earlier, in 1979, the linguist Nessa Wolfson had studied the "historic present" in everyday spoken stories in American English, and argued that what matters is not the present tense itself but the **switch** between present and past, which helps the speaker divide the story into parts. Both studies show the main point of this lesson: tenses are not only a matter of correct sentences but also a tool for building texts.
:::

Correct the tense errors in this paragraph. *Last year I start a part-time job in a café. The work was hard, because I have to stand all day. But the other staff are friendly, and I learned a lot. I had worked there for six months.*

*Last year I **started** a part-time job in a café. The work was hard, because I **had** to stand all day. But the other staff **were** friendly, and I learned a lot. I **worked** there for six months.*

*Had worked* is wrong because there is no later past point; *worked … for six months* is a finished period in the past frame. (If the writer still works there: *I've worked there for six months now* — a change to the present frame, signalled by *now*.)

*University to launch AI degree*

- Headline: *to launch* (future) for 将开设.
- Lead: *has announced* (news from now) + *will launch* (future from now).

Choose an event you took part in recently (a trip, a celebration, a problem at work or school). Write about it three times, 40–60 words each: (a) as a story to a friend, in a past frame; (b) as a short news report; (c) as an email to a teacher or manager explaining what happened and what will happen next. Then write one sentence explaining how the tenses differ.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model, about a power cut:

(a) *Last night I was finishing my essay when all the lights went out. I hadn't saved the file for an hour! I sat in the dark, waiting, and after forty minutes the power came back. Luckily, the computer had saved a copy automatically.*

(b) *Power has returned to the Riverside district after a forty-minute cut. The power went off at about 9 p.m. on Monday, when a cable was damaged during roadworks. No injuries were reported.*

(c) *Dear Ms Grant, I'm writing to let you know that there was a power cut in my area last night, so I wasn't able to finish my essay. I've saved most of it, and I'll send it to you by Wednesday morning. I'm sorry for the delay.*

*The story uses the narrative tenses in a past frame; the news report starts with the present perfect and gives details in the past simple; the email uses the present, the past for the problem and the future for the promise.*
