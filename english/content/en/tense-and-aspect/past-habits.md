Lucy is talking to her grandmother, who grew up in a village in Guangxi.

**Grandmother:** *When I was a girl, we **used to** live in a wooden house by the river. There **used to** be no electricity in the village. Every morning my mother **would** wake us at five, and we **would** walk to school along the riverbank. In summer we **would** swim in the river on the way home.*

**Lucy:** *Didn't you **use to** be afraid of the water?*

**Grandmother:** *No — I **was used to** it. We all were.*

The grandmother describes her childhood with four different forms: *used to live*, *used to be*, *would wake* and *was used to it*. The first three talk about habits and situations in the past that are no longer true. The fourth looks similar but means something different: ‘accustomed to’. In Chinese, much of this could be said with 以前, 小时候, 常常 or 总是 and an unmarked verb; English has special forms for it.

You know from [[core-grammar/past-simple#rule-past-uses]] that the past simple can describe past habits (*I walked to school every day*). This lesson adds *used to* and *would*, explains when each is possible, and separates them from *be used to* and *get used to*.

## *Used to*

::: definition *Used to* {#def-used-to}
***Used to*** + base form describes **habits and states in the past that are no longer true**: *I used to play the piano* (but I don't now). *There used to be a cinema here* (but there isn't now). It has only a past form; there is no present ✗ *I use to*.
:::

| | form | example |
|---|---|---|
| statement | *used to* + base form | *She **used to** live in Harbin.* |
| negative | *didn't use to* + base form | *I **didn't use to** like coffee.* |
| question | *Did … use to* + base form? | ***Did** you **use to** play football?* |
| with *never* | *never used to* | *He **never used to** eat vegetables.* |

*Used to* is pronounced /ˈjuːstə/ (before a consonant) or /ˈjuːstu/ (before a vowel), with /s/, not /z/. The *d* is not heard, so learners often write ✗ *use to* in statements. The verb *use* ‘make use of’ is different: *I used* /juːzd/ *a knife.*

::: remark *Didn't use to* or *didn't used to*?
After *did*, the base form *use* is the logical spelling (*didn't use to*, *did you use to…?*), and dictionaries and Swan (2016) give it as the standard form. But because the pronunciation is the same, *didn't used to* is very common in informal writing, and some dictionaries now list it as a variant. In exams and formal writing, use *didn't use to*. A formal British alternative is *used not to* (*I used not to like coffee*), which sounds old-fashioned to many speakers. In questions, many people avoid the problem with *Did you … when you were younger?* or *Have you always…?*
:::

::: theorem Meaning of *used to* {#rule-used-to}
1. *Used to* describes **repeated actions** (*I used to cycle to school*) and **states** (*I used to have long hair*, *We used to live in Xi'an*) in the past.
2. It implies a **contrast with the present**: the habit or state has stopped. *I used to smoke* suggests ‘I don't now’.
3. It is **not used** for a single event, for a **specific number of times**, or for an **exact length of time**: ✗ *I used to go to Beijing three times.* ✓ *I went to Beijing three times.* ✗ *I used to live there for ten years.* ✓ *I lived there for ten years.*
:::

::: proof
*Used to* presents a whole period of the past as a "state of affairs" that once existed and is now over; it describes what life was like, not what happened. A number of times or an exact duration measures a period from outside and treats it as finished, which is the job of the past simple. Swan (2016) and Carter and McCarthy (2006) give the same restrictions. The contrast with the present in point 2 is an implication rather than part of the meaning: it can be cancelled (*I used to love jazz, and I still do*), but without such a comment listeners understand that things have changed.
:::

- *Kenji **used to** work in a bank. Now he's a teacher.*
- *There **used to** be fields where the airport is now.*
- *I **didn't use to** enjoy reading, but now I love it.*
- *What **did** people **use to** do before smartphones?*

::: example *Used to* or the past simple? {#ex-used-to-past}
Decide whether *used to* is possible, and correct the sentences where it is not. (a) *I used to visit my grandparents every summer.* (b) *I used to visit my grandparents last summer.* (c) *She used to be very shy.* (d) *We used to go to Hainan four times.* (e) *He used to study in Germany for two years.*
::: solution
(a) Correct: a repeated action in the past (*every summer*).

(b) Wrong: *last summer* is one occasion. ✓ *I visited my grandparents last summer.*

(c) Correct: a past state (now she is not shy).

(d) Wrong: a specific number of times. ✓ *We went to Hainan four times.* (or *We've been to Hainan four times*, if the period reaches up to now)

(e) Wrong: an exact length of time. ✓ *He studied in Germany for two years.* (*He used to study in Germany* without the duration is fine.)
:::
:::

## *Would* for past habits

::: theorem *Would* for past habits {#rule-would}
***Would*** + base form can describe **repeated actions** in the past, especially in stories and memories: *Every evening my father **would** read to us.* *On Sundays we'**d** go to the market.*

But *would*:

1. is **not used for states**: ✗ *We would live in a small flat.* ✓ *We used to live in a small flat.* ✗ *I would have a dog.* ✓ *I used to have a dog.*
2. usually needs a **past time frame** already set, often by *used to*, *when I was a child*, *every summer* and so on; it does not normally begin a description.
3. is often contracted to *'d*, the same as *had* (*We'd go* = *would*; *We'd gone* = *had*).
:::

::: proof
*Would* is the past form of *will*, and *will* can describe typical behaviour in the present: *She'll sit there for hours, reading* (= this is what she typically does). *Would* moves this typical behaviour into the past. Typical behaviour must be an action that the person does again and again, so states (*live*, *have*, *be*, *know*) are excluded. And because *would* has many other uses — conditional (*I would help if I could*), future in the past (*She knew she would miss them*), polite requests — the listener needs a past habitual frame to understand it as a habit. That is why storytellers typically start with *used to* or a time phrase and then continue with *would*, as the grandmother does at the beginning of this lesson. Leech (2004) and Swan (2016) describe this pattern; *would* in this sense is common in nostalgic or literary narration.
:::

::: example *Used to* or *would*? {#ex-used-to-would}
Complete the memory with *used to* or *would*. Where both are possible, say so.

*When I was ten, we (1) ___ live next to a bakery. I (2) ___ love the smell of fresh bread. Every morning the baker (3) ___ give me a warm roll on my way to school, and I (4) ___ eat it before I reached the corner. There (5) ___ be a big black cat in the shop, and it (6) ___ sleep in the window all day.*
::: solution
(1) *used to* — *live* is a state here; *would* is impossible.

(2) *used to* — *love* is a state.

(3) *would* or *used to* — a repeated action, and the past frame is already set.

(4) *would* or *used to* — a repeated action.

(5) *used to* — *there be* is a state.

(6) *would* or *used to* — sleeping in the window was a repeated, typical action. (*Used to* is slightly safer here, because sleeping is close to a state.)

The past simple is also possible for all of them (*we lived*, *I loved*, *the baker gave* …); *used to* and *would* make the contrast with the present and the nostalgic tone clearer.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is **not** correct?
- [ ] *We used to have a big garden.*
- [x] *We would have a big garden.*
- [ ] *On Sundays we would have lunch in the garden.*
- [ ] *We had a big garden when I was young.*
::: solution
*Have a big garden* is a state (possession), so *would* cannot describe it. *Have lunch* is an action, repeated every Sunday, so *would have lunch* is correct. *Used to have* and the past simple *had* can describe the state.
:::
:::

::: warning *Use to* for present habits
✗ *I use to get up at six.* ✓ *I usually get up at six.*

✗ *She uses to drink tea.* ✓ *She usually drinks tea.* / *She often drinks tea.*

*Used to* has no present form. For present habits use the present simple, often with *usually*, *normally* or *often* ([[core-grammar/present-simple]]).
:::

## *Be used to* and *get used to*

::: definition *Be used to* and *get used to* {#def-be-used-to}
***Be used to*** + noun or ***-ing*** form means ‘be **accustomed to**’: something is familiar and no longer strange or difficult. ***Get used to*** + noun or *-ing* form means ‘**become** accustomed to’. Here *to* is a preposition, so it is followed by a noun or an *-ing* form, not by the base form.
:::

- *I'**m used to** getting up early — I've done it for years.*
- *She **isn't used to** the cold yet. She only arrived last week.*
- *It took me a long time to **get used to** driving on the left.*
- *You'll soon **get used to** the noise.*
- *When I lived in Tokyo, I **was used to** taking crowded trains.*

Unlike *used to*, these phrases can be used in **any tense**: *I'm used to*, *I was used to*, *I'll get used to*, *I've got used to* (AmE also *I've gotten used to*), *I'm getting used to*.

::: theorem *Used to do* or *be used to doing*? {#rule-used-to-contrast}
- ***used to*** + **base form** = a past habit or state, now over: *I **used to live** alone.*
- ***be used to*** + ***-ing*** (or noun) = be accustomed: *I'**m used to living** alone.* (it is normal for me)
- ***get used to*** + ***-ing*** (or noun) = become accustomed: *I'**m getting used to living** alone.* (it is becoming normal)
:::

::: proof
The two structures look alike, but they have different grammar. In *used to* + base form, *used to* works like an auxiliary: its *to* belongs to it, and the base form that follows is the main verb. In *be used to*, *used* is an adjective meaning ‘accustomed’, and *to* is a preposition, like the *to* in *look forward to* or *object to*. A preposition takes a noun or an *-ing* form: *I'm used to the heat*, *I'm used to working at night*. A third structure, *be used to do*, is the passive of the verb *use* ‘make use of’: *This knife **is used to cut** bread* (= people use it for cutting bread).
:::

::: warning *Be used to* + base form
✗ *I'm used to get up early.* ✓ *I'm used to getting up early.*

✗ *I can't get used to live in a big city.* ✓ *I can't get used to living in a big city.*

✗ *I used to living in the country.* ✓ *I used to live in the country.* (a past habit) or *I'm used to living in the country.* (accustomed)

After *be used to* and *get used to*, the *to* is a preposition: use an *-ing* form or a noun.
:::

::: example Three meanings {#ex-three-meanings}
Explain the difference between each pair or group. (a) *I used to drive on the left.* / *I'm used to driving on the left.* (b) *She used to work at night.* / *She got used to working at night.* (c) *This room is used to store old furniture.* / *This room used to be a bedroom.*
::: solution
(a) *I used to drive on the left* — in the past I drove on the left (perhaps I lived in the UK or Hong Kong), but I don't now. *I'm used to driving on the left* — driving on the left is normal and easy for me now.

(b) *She used to work at night* — a past habit, now over. *She got used to working at night* — at first it was hard, but over time it became normal for her.

(c) *is used to store* — the passive of *use*: people use the room for storing furniture. *used to be a bedroom* — a past state: it was a bedroom, but it isn't now.
:::
:::

::: example Rewriting a memory {#ex-rewrite}
Rewrite this paragraph to make the past habits and states clearer, using *used to* and *would* where they fit. Keep the past simple for the single event.

*When I was a student, I lived in a tiny room. I didn't have a kitchen. Every evening I bought noodles from a stall near the station, and the owner always asked about my day. One night he gave me a free bowl because it was my birthday.*
::: solution
*When I was a student, I **used to live** in a tiny room. I **didn't use to have** a kitchen* (or *I didn't have a kitchen*). *Every evening I **would buy** noodles from a stall near the station, and the owner **would** always **ask** about my day. One night he **gave** me a free bowl because it was my birthday.*

- *used to live*, *didn't use to have* — past states; *would* is impossible. (*Didn't have* is just as good and perhaps more natural.)
- *would buy*, *would always ask* — repeated actions, after the past frame is set.
- *gave* — a single event: past simple only. *One night* marks the change from habit to event, a common pattern in stories.
:::

::: quiz
*After two years in London, Wang Fang ___ the rain.* Which form fits?
- [ ] *used to*
- [x] *was used to*
- [ ] *would*
- [ ] *used to be*
::: solution
The meaning is ‘accustomed to’: after two years, the rain was familiar. *Was used to* + noun (*the rain*). *Used to the rain* has no verb, *would the rain* is not English, and *used to be the rain* means nothing here. (*Had got used to* / *had gotten used to the rain* would also be correct.)
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **以前, 过去 and 小时候 + verb.** Chinese often expresses past habits with a time word and an unmarked verb: 我以前住在农村 → *I used to live in the countryside* (or *I lived in the countryside*). English needs a past form at least; *used to* adds the contrast with now that 以前 suggests.
- **常常 and 总是 in the past.** 小时候我常常去河边玩 → *When I was a child, I would often play by the river* or *I often played by the river*. Chinese uses the same 常常 for present and past habits; English must choose between *I often play* and *I often played* / *I used to play*.
- **曾经 is not *used to*.** 曾经 marks something that happened at some time in the past, often only once: 我曾经去过西藏 → *I've been to Tibet* / *I once went to Tibet*. It does not express a habit, so ✗ *I used to go to Tibet once* is wrong.
- **习惯 and *be used to*.** 我习惯了早起 → *I'm used to getting up early.* Chinese 习惯 is followed directly by a verb (习惯早起), which leads to ✗ *I'm used to get up early*. Remember that the English *to* is a preposition here, followed by *-ing*. 我还不习惯 → *I'm not used to it yet*; 慢慢就习惯了 → *You'll get used to it.*
- **Spelling from sound.** Because the *d* of *used to* is not heard before /t/, ✗ *I use to* is a common written error for speakers of every language, not only Chinese.
:::

::: history
The verb *use* came into English from French in the Middle Ages, and one of its early meanings was ‘be in the habit of’. For several centuries it could be used in this sense in the present as well as the past, so that older texts contain sentences of the type *as they use to do* (‘as they usually do’). The present-tense use disappeared, and only *used to* survived, with the special pronunciation /juːst/ that keeps it apart from *used* /juːzd/ ‘made use of’. Because it is now a past form with no present, *used to* behaves partly like a main verb (*did you use to…?*) and partly like an auxiliary (*used you to…?*, an old British question form now rarely heard). Grammars disagree about how to classify it: Quirk et al. (1985) call it a "marginal modal", and the uncertainty is reflected in the competing spellings *didn't use to* and *didn't used to*.
:::

## Where this leads

*Would* has many other uses, for unreal situations, politeness and the future in the past; they are brought together in [[modality/will-would-shall]] and [[complex-sentences/conditionals]]. The *-ing* form after prepositions such as the *to* of *be used to* is explained in [[verb-patterns/ing-or-infinitive]]. [[tense-and-aspect/tenses-in-texts]] shows how *used to* and *would* set the tone of memoirs and personal essays, and [[tense-and-aspect/tense-and-chinese]] compares them with 以前, 常常 and 曾经.

::: summary
- *Used to* + base form describes past habits and states that are no longer true: *I used to live in Xi'an* ([[#def-used-to]]); negative *didn't use to*, question *Did you use to…?*; no present form.
- *Used to* is not used for single events, a number of times or an exact length of time; use the past simple ([[#rule-used-to]]).
- *Would* + base form describes repeated past actions, usually after the past frame is set; it is not used for states: ✗ *We would live in a flat* ([[#rule-would]]).
- *Be used to* + *-ing* or noun = ‘accustomed’; *get used to* = ‘become accustomed’; they can be used in any tense ([[#def-be-used-to]]).
- *To* in *be used to* is a preposition: *I'm used to getting up early*, not ✗ *get up* ([[#rule-used-to-contrast]]).
- Chinese 以前, 常常 and 习惯 express these meanings with time words and unmarked verbs; 曾经 marks a past experience, not a habit.
:::
