Priya is planning a trip with her friend Hiroshi. Read their messages.

**Priya:** *My flight lands at 7.30 on Friday. Will you be waiting at the airport, or shall I take a taxi?*

**Hiroshi:** *I'll be there! I'm leaving work early. By the time you land, I'll have been waiting for an hour, probably — I always arrive too early.*

**Priya:** *Ha! I'll text you as soon as I get my bag.*

In five short messages there are seven different ways of talking about the future: *lands*, *will you be waiting*, *shall I take*, *I'll be*, *I'm leaving*, *I'll have been waiting* and *I'll text … as soon as I get*. Chinese could use 会, 要 or nothing at all in most of these places. English has no future tense on the verb ([[tense-and-aspect/time-tense-aspect#rule-two-tenses]]), but it has many forms for the future, and each one shows the future event in a slightly different way.

[[core-grammar/talking-about-the-future]] introduced the four basic forms: *will*, *be going to*, the present continuous and the present simple. This lesson reviews them briefly and then adds the rest of the system: the present tense after *when* and *if*, the future progressive and future perfect, *be about to* and *be to*, and the "future in the past" (*I was going to call you*).

## The four basic forms: a review

| form | the speaker sees the future as… | example |
|---|---|---|
| ***will*** + base form | a prediction, a decision made now, an offer or promise | *It'**ll** be cold tomorrow.* *I'**ll** help you.* |
| ***be going to*** + base form | coming from the present: a plan already made, or present evidence | *I'**m going to** study law.* *Look, it'**s going to** rain.* |
| **present continuous** | an arrangement already fixed | *I'**m meeting** Ana at six.* |
| **present simple** | a timetable or schedule | *The train **leaves** at 7.40.* |

The details, with the British use of *shall* in offers (*Shall I carry that?*), are in [[core-grammar/talking-about-the-future]]. Three points matter for this lesson.

1. The choice often depends on **how** the speaker sees the event, not on how certain it is. *I'm going to see Ali tomorrow* (my plan), *I'm seeing Ali tomorrow* (we have arranged it) and *I'll see Ali tomorrow* (a prediction, or a decision made now) can all be true of the same meeting.
2. With predictions, *will* and *going to* are often both possible. *Going to* is preferred when there is evidence now (*Look at the sky — it's going to rain*). In corpus counts *will* is much more common overall, while *going to* is used mainly in conversation (Biber et al. 1999).
3. *Will* is a modal verb. Other modals also talk about the future with less certainty: *It **may** rain*, *It **might** rain*, *It **could** rain*. These are treated in [[modality/ability-possibility]].

## The present tense after *when*, *if* and *as soon as*

::: theorem The present tense in time and condition clauses {#rule-time-clauses}
In clauses of **time** and **condition** that refer to the future, English uses a **present tense**, not *will*:

- after ***when***, ***as soon as***, ***before***, ***after***, ***until*** (*till*), ***once***, ***by the time***, ***while***: *I'll call you **when** I **arrive**.* *Wait here **until** I **come** back.*
- after ***if***, ***unless***, ***as long as***, ***in case***: ***If** it **rains**, we'll stay at home.* *Take an umbrella **in case** it **rains**.*

To show that one future event will be complete before another, use the **present perfect**: *I'll lend you the book **when** I'**ve finished** it.*
:::

::: proof
The main clause (*I'll call you*) already places the whole sentence in the future. The time clause only identifies **when**, and English expresses this with the present tense, just as it does for timetables. Quirk et al. (1985) and Swan (2016) give the same rule. Two exceptions show the logic. When *if* means ‘if you are willing’, *will* is possible: *If you'll wait a moment, I'll get the manager* (a polite request). And when the clause is not a time clause but the object of a verb such as *know* or *ask*, *will* is normal: *I don't know when she'll arrive* — here *when she'll arrive* answers the question *what don't I know?*, not *when?*.
:::

::: warning *Will* after *when* and *if*
✗ *When I will finish university, I want to travel.* ✓ *When I finish university, I want to travel.*

✗ *If you will need help, call me.* ✓ *If you need help, call me.*

✗ *I'll wait until you will be ready.* ✓ *I'll wait until you're ready.*

But: ✓ *I don't know if she'll come.* (*if* = *whether*: the clause is the object of *know*, not a condition.)
:::

::: example Time clauses {#ex-time-clauses}
Complete with the right form and explain. (a) *We'll start the meeting as soon as everyone (arrive).* (b) *I (phone) you when I get to the hotel.* (c) *You can't go out until you (finish) your homework.* (d) *Do you know when the results (come) out?* (e) *If the weather (be) good tomorrow, we'll climb the mountain.*
::: solution
(a) *arrives* — a time clause after *as soon as*: present simple.

(b) *'ll phone* — the main clause, about the future: *will*.

(c) *have finished* (or *finish*) — a time clause after *until*. The present perfect stresses that the homework must be complete first.

(d) *will come* — *when the results will come out* is the object of *know* (an indirect question), not a time clause, so *will* is correct.

(e) *is* — a condition after *if*: present simple.
:::
:::

## The future progressive

::: definition Future progressive {#def-future-progressive}
The **future progressive** (or future continuous) is ***will be*** + *-ing*: *I'll be working*. It presents an activity **in progress at a time in the future**, or an event that will happen **in the normal course of things**.
:::

::: theorem Uses of the future progressive {#rule-future-progressive}
1. **In progress at a future time**: *This time tomorrow I'**ll be lying** on a beach.* *Don't call at eight — we'**ll be having** dinner.*
2. **As a matter of course** — something that will happen anyway, without a special decision: *I'll be seeing Kenji at work tomorrow, so I can give him the message.* *The plane will be landing shortly.*
3. **Polite questions about plans**, which do not sound like requests: ***Will** you **be using** the car this evening?* (I'd like to borrow it, but I'm only asking about your plans.)
:::

::: proof
Use 1 is the progressive frame ([[tense-and-aspect/simple-and-progressive#def-progressive]]) with the reference time in the future. Uses 2 and 3 come from the same idea: by presenting the future event as already "in progress" in the course of events, the speaker removes the idea of a decision or a wish. Compare ***Will** you **come** to the meeting?*, which can sound like a request or invitation (*will* = ‘be willing’), with ***Will** you **be coming** to the meeting?*, which only asks what the plan is. Leech (2004) and Swan (2016) both describe this "neutral" use.
:::

::: example *Will* or *will be* + *-ing*? {#ex-future-progressive}
Explain the difference. (a) *Will you help me with my homework?* / *Will you be helping Dad with the shopping tomorrow?* (b) *I'll drive to Shanghai tomorrow.* / *I'll be driving to Shanghai tomorrow, so I can take your parcel.* (c) *At 10 a.m. the students will take the exam.* / *At 10 a.m. the students will be taking the exam.*
::: solution
(a) *Will you help me…?* is a request: will you agree to? *Will you be helping…?* asks only about the plan; it puts no pressure on the listener.

(b) *I'll drive* can be a decision made now (*OK, I'll drive*). *I'll be driving* presents the trip as already part of the plans, so the offer to take the parcel costs the speaker nothing extra.

(c) *will take* — the exam starts at 10 a.m. *will be taking* — at 10 a.m. the exam will be in progress (it started earlier).
:::
:::

## The future perfect

::: definition Future perfect {#def-future-perfect}
The **future perfect** is ***will have*** + past participle: *I'll have finished*. It presents an event as **complete before a reference time in the future**. In the timeline of [[tense-and-aspect/time-tense-aspect#def-ser]], R is after S, and E is before R. The reference time is usually given with ***by*** (*by Friday*, *by the time you get here*, *by 2030*).
:::

The **future perfect progressive**, *will have been* + *-ing*, presents an activity **going on up to a future reference time**, usually to say how long it will have lasted: *By June I'**ll have been working** here for ten years.*

::: theorem Uses of the future perfect {#rule-future-perfect}
1. **Complete before a future time**: *By the time you get home, I'**ll have cooked** dinner.* *The builders **will have finished** the roof by Friday.*
2. **A number or amount up to a future time**: *By the end of the trip we'**ll have visited** six countries.*
3. **Duration up to a future time** (future perfect progressive): *In May I'**ll have been living** in Chengdu for five years.*
4. **A guess about the present or past** (a use of *will* as a modal): *Don't call Mei now — she'**ll have gone** to bed.* (= I'm sure she has gone to bed.)
:::

::: proof
The future perfect is the perfect seen from a future reference time, just as the present perfect looks back from now and the past perfect from a past time ([[tense-and-aspect/narrative-tenses#def-past-perfect]]). The event may happen at any time before R, even before now: *By Friday I'll have finished* is true if I finish on Thursday or if I have already finished. Use 4 shows that *will* is a modal verb: like *must*, it can express a confident guess, and *will have* + participle makes the guess about something already complete. The future perfect is not a common form in speech or writing, but it is the clearest way to say "complete before a future point".
:::

::: example Planning ahead {#ex-future-perfect}
It is September 2026. Sara has made a plan. Write sentences with the future perfect (simple or progressive) and *by*.

- *September 2026: starts a three-year degree.*
- *June 2027: finishes her first year.*
- *July 2028: takes her driving test.*
- *June 2029: graduates.*

(a) *by July 2027* — her first year (b) *by the end of 2028* — her driving test (c) *by June 2029* — how long at university (d) *by 2030* — her degree
::: solution
(a) *By July 2027 she'll have finished her first year.*

(b) *By the end of 2028 she'll have taken her driving test.* (or *passed*, if all goes well)

(c) *By June 2029 she'll have been studying at university for nearly three years.* (future perfect progressive: duration up to a future point)

(d) *By 2030 she'll have graduated.* (or *she'll have got / gotten her degree*)

In each case the event is complete before the reference time given by *by*.
:::
:::

## *Be about to*, *be due to* and *be to*

::: theorem Other ways of talking about the future {#rule-other-future}
- ***be about to*** + base form: something will happen **very soon**: *Hurry up — the film **is about to** start.* *I **was** just **about to** call you.* (Not used with a time expression: ✗ *The film is about to start at eight.*)
- ***be due to*** + base form, or ***be due***: something is **expected or scheduled**: *The train **is due to** arrive at 9.15.* *The baby **is due** in March.*
- ***be to*** + base form: an **official arrangement**, especially in news and formal writing: *The Prime Minister **is to** visit Japan next month.* *You **are** not **to** leave the building without permission* (an order).
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *Wait here until I will come back.*
- [ ] *The concert is about to start at nine tomorrow.*
- [x] *By the time you read this, I'll have left.*
- [ ] *This time next week I'll lie on a beach.*
::: solution
*By the time you read this* sets a future reference time, and leaving will be complete before it: future perfect. *Until* needs the present (*until I come back*); *be about to* cannot take a time expression (*The concert starts at nine tomorrow*); and an action in progress at a future time needs the future progressive (*I'll be lying on a beach*).
:::
:::

## The future in the past

Sometimes a story in the past talks about what was then the future. English uses the past forms of the future structures.

::: theorem The future in the past {#rule-future-in-past}
- ***was/were going to*** + base form: a plan or intention in the past, often one that **did not happen**: *I **was going to** call you, but I forgot.* *We **were going to** go camping, but it rained.*
- ***would*** + base form: what was future from a past point: *She knew she **would** miss her family.* *Ten years later, he **would** become famous.* (The second sentence is typical of biographies and stories.)
- ***was/were about to***: very near in the past: *I **was about to** leave when the phone rang.*
- the **past progressive** for past arrangements: *I couldn't come because I **was meeting** a client that afternoon.*
:::

::: example Plans that changed {#ex-future-in-past}
Use *was/were going to* or *was/were about to* to explain each situation. (a) Leila planned to study medicine. She studied music instead. (b) Tom had his hand on the door handle. Then his boss called his name. (c) We planned to have a picnic. It rained. (d) You meant to phone your grandmother. You forgot.
::: solution
(a) *Leila was going to study medicine, but she studied music instead.*

(b) *Tom was about to leave when his boss called his name.*

(c) *We were going to have a picnic, but it rained.*

(d) *I was going to phone my grandmother, but I forgot.*

In each sentence the plan was in the past, and the speaker tells us it did not happen. *Was going to* very often means this, so *but* often follows it.
:::
:::

::: quiz
*I was going to tell you about the party.* What does this usually mean?
- [ ] I told you about the party.
- [x] I intended to tell you, but I didn't (or haven't yet).
- [ ] I will certainly tell you about the party.
- [ ] I told you many times about the party.
::: solution
*Was going to* reports a past intention. Without further information, listeners usually understand that the plan did not happen, which is why it is often followed by *but…* or used as an apology.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **会 is not always *will*.** 会 often expresses a prediction (明天会下雨 → *It'll rain tomorrow* or *It's going to rain*), which is close to *will*. But for plans and arrangements English prefers *going to* or the present continuous: 我明天要去医院 → *I'm going to the hospital tomorrow* (an arrangement), not necessarily ✗ *I will go*. And 会 also means ability (我会游泳 → *I can swim*).
- **No future marking in Chinese time clauses either — but no *will* in English ones.** 等我到了，我给你打电话 → *I'll call you when I get there.* Chinese often uses 了 here (到了), because the arrival will be complete before the call; English uses the present simple or present perfect (*when I've arrived*), never *will*.
- **了 with future time.** 明天下了课我去找你 shows clearly that 了 is not past: it marks completion before another event, which English expresses with *after* or *when* + present (*I'll come and find you tomorrow after class*). In the same way, 到时候我已经毕业了 → *By then I'll have graduated*: 已经……了 at a future point is the future perfect.
- **快要/就要……了 and *be about to*.** 火车就要开了 → *The train is about to leave.* 快要 is often translated *nearly* or *soon*, and *be about to* is used for something only moments away.
- **将 and *be to*.** In formal Chinese, especially in news, 将 marks scheduled future events (总理将访问日本); English news uses *be to* or *will*: *The Prime Minister is to visit Japan.*
:::

::: history
English grammars have argued about the future for more than three centuries. In 1653 John Wallis, writing a grammar of English in Latin, stated that *shall* with *I* and *we* simply predicts, while *will* with *I* and *we* expresses determination; with other subjects the meanings were reversed. Later prescriptive grammars repeated this rule, but studies of real usage found that speakers never followed it consistently, and today *will* is used with all persons, while *shall* survives mainly in British offers and suggestions (*Shall I…?*, *Shall we…?*) and in legal language. *Be going to* has a different history: it began as an ordinary verb of movement (‘be on the way to do something’), and studies of grammaticalization, such as Hopper and Traugott's *Grammaticalization* (2nd edition, 2003), trace its future meaning back to the fifteenth century. Today it is one of the most common future forms in conversation.
:::

## Where this leads

The use of *will*, *would* and *shall* as modal verbs — for willingness, habits, refusals and guesses — is treated in [[modality/will-would-shall]]. Conditional sentences (*If it rains, we'll…*; *If it rained, we'd…*) are the subject of [[complex-sentences/conditionals]], and the future in the past returns in reported speech ([[complex-sentences/reported-speech]]: *She said she would come*). [[tense-and-aspect/tenses-in-texts]] shows how future forms are used in emails and plans.

::: summary
- English has no future tense; it uses *will*, *be going to*, the present continuous and the present simple, each showing the future in a different way (prediction or decision, plan or evidence, arrangement, timetable).
- After *when*, *if*, *as soon as*, *until*, *before* and similar words, use a present tense for the future, and the present perfect for completion: *I'll call you when I've arrived* ([[#rule-time-clauses]]).
- The future progressive (*will be* + *-ing*) shows an activity in progress at a future time, an event in the normal course of things, or a polite question about plans ([[#def-future-progressive]], [[#rule-future-progressive]]).
- The future perfect (*will have* + participle) shows an event complete before a future time, usually with *by*; *will have been* + *-ing* shows duration up to it ([[#def-future-perfect]], [[#rule-future-perfect]]).
- *Be about to* = very soon; *be due to* = scheduled; *be to* = official arrangement ([[#rule-other-future]]).
- *Was going to*, *would* and *was about to* express the future in the past; *was going to* often means the plan did not happen ([[#rule-future-in-past]]).
- Chinese 会, 要 and 将 do not map one-to-one onto *will*; 了 in future time clauses corresponds to the English present or present perfect.
:::
