Zhang Min sends two messages to her manager on the same afternoon:

- *I've finished the report.*
- *I finished the report yesterday.*

Both messages are about the same past event. Yet the verbs are different: *'ve finished* in the first, *finished* in the second. Now look at a third sentence, from the same day: *The train to Hangzhou leaves at 6.40 tomorrow.* The verb *leaves* is a present form, but the train leaves in the future.

So the form of an English verb does not simply tell you "past", "present" or "future". It tells you something more precise: where the speaker stands, and how the speaker looks at the event. This lesson gives you three tools for understanding every English verb form: the difference between **time** and **tense**, the idea of **aspect**, and a simple **timeline** with three points on it. Later lessons use these tools again and again.

You already know the forms of the present simple, the present continuous and the past simple from [[core-grammar/present-simple]], [[core-grammar/present-continuous]] and [[core-grammar/past-simple]]. Here we look at the system that holds them together.

## Time and tense

::: definition Time and tense {#def-time-tense}
**Time** is part of the world: every event happens in the past, the present or the future. **Tense** is part of grammar: it is a form of the verb that shows the time of a situation in relation to the moment of speaking. Time exists in every language; tense exists only in languages that mark it on the verb.
:::

English marks tense with endings and changes of the verb. *Work* and *works* are present forms; *worked* is a past form. *Go* and *goes* are present; *went* is past. *Be* has three present forms (*am*, *is*, *are*) and two past forms (*was*, *were*).

::: theorem English has two tenses {#rule-two-tenses}
English verbs have **two tenses**: the **present tense** (*work*, *works*, *is*, *has*) and the **past tense** (*worked*, *was*, *had*). There is no future ending on the verb. English talks about the future with other means: the modal verb *will*, *be going to*, the present continuous and the present simple.
:::

::: proof
A tense is a change in the form of the verb itself. English verbs change for the present (*works*) and for the past (*worked*), but they have no form that changes for the future. *Will* is a separate word, and it behaves like the modal verbs *can*, *must* and *may*: it has no *-s* (*she will*, not ✗ *she wills*), it is followed by the base form, and it makes questions and negatives by itself (*Will you…?*, *won't*). For this reason most modern reference grammars — Quirk et al. (1985), Biber et al. (1999), Huddleston and Pullum (2002) — say that English has two tenses and several ways of referring to the future. Many school grammars and textbooks still call *will* + base form the "future simple" or "future tense". That name is convenient, and you may meet it in exams; this course uses the labels for the forms (*will*, *be going to*) and keeps the word *tense* for present and past.
:::

Tense and time often match. *She works in a bank* is present tense and present time. *She worked in a bank* is past tense and past time. But they do not always match:

- *The train **leaves** at 6.40 tomorrow.* — present tense, future time.
- *If it **rains** tomorrow, we'll stay at home.* — present tense, future time.
- *I **was wondering** if you could help me.* — past tense, present time (a polite request, made now).
- *If I **had** more time, I'd learn the piano.* — past tense, present time (an unreal situation).
- *Man **finds** gold ring in fish.* — present tense, past time (a newspaper headline).

These examples show why grammars separate the two words. Time is what happens in the world. Tense is a tool of grammar, and speakers use it for more than one job. The use of the past tense for politeness and for unreal situations is explained in [[modality/modality-politeness]] and [[complex-sentences/conditionals]].

::: example Tense and time in a message {#ex-message}
Read Tom's message. For each verb in bold, give the tense and the time it refers to.

*Hi Priya, I **was** in town yesterday and I **saw** your sister. She **says** hi! My exam **starts** at nine tomorrow, so I **can't** meet on Friday morning. I **was hoping** we could meet in the afternoon instead.*
::: solution
1. *was* — past tense, past time (*yesterday*).
2. *saw* — past tense, past time.
3. *says* — present tense, but the sister spoke in the past. With verbs such as *say* and *tell*, English often uses the present for a message that is still valid now.
4. *starts* — present tense, future time (a timetable: the exam has a fixed start).
5. *can't* — present tense of the modal *can*, future time (Friday).
6. *was hoping* — past tense, present time. The past makes the suggestion softer and more polite; Tom is hoping now.

So in a short, ordinary message, three of the six verbs have a tense that does not match their time. The tense is a choice of grammar, not a clock.
:::
:::

::: quiz
In which sentence does the tense **not** match the time?
- [ ] *I lived in Chengdu for five years.*
- [ ] *My brother plays the guitar.*
- [x] *Our flight leaves at nine tomorrow morning.*
- [ ] *We were tired after the walk.*
::: solution
*Leaves* is present tense, but *tomorrow morning* is future time. English uses the present simple for timetables and schedules. In the other sentences, past tense matches past time (*lived*, *were*) and present tense matches present time (*plays*, a habit).

## Aspect

Tense answers the question *when?* — before now or not before now. But a speaker can also show **how** an event is seen: as one whole thing, as something going on, or as something finished before a later point. This second kind of information is called aspect.

::: definition Aspect {#def-aspect}
**Aspect** is the grammatical form that shows how a situation is viewed in time: as a complete whole, as being in progress, or as completed before a later point. In Comrie's (1976) words, aspects are different ways of viewing the internal time structure of a situation. English has two aspects:

- the **progressive aspect**: *be* + *-ing* form (*is working*, *was working*);
- the **perfect aspect**: *have* + past participle (*has worked*, *had worked*).

A verb phrase with neither aspect is called **simple**: *works*, *worked*.
:::

The progressive shows a situation from the inside, as in progress and not yet complete: *I was cooking when you called.* The perfect connects a situation to a later point in time: *I have cooked dinner* (and now it is ready). The two aspects can also be combined: *I have been cooking all afternoon.*

Tense and aspect are independent of each other. Each of the two tenses can combine with each aspect. This gives eight forms, and with *will* another four:

| | simple | progressive | perfect | perfect progressive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **present** | *she works* | *she is working* | *she has worked* | *she has been working* |
| **past** | *she worked* | *she was working* | *she had worked* | *she had been working* |
| ***will*** | *she will work* | *she will be working* | *she will have worked* | *she will have been working* |

School grammars often give each of these forms a name — the present perfect, the past perfect progressive, and so on — and count "twelve tenses" or, with the future in the past, "sixteen". The names are useful and this course uses them. But the system is simpler than the count suggests: there are only **two tenses** and **two aspects**, and every form is built from them.

::: remark Is the perfect an aspect?
Grammars agree that the progressive is an aspect. They do not agree about the perfect. Quirk et al. (1985) and Biber et al. (1999) call it an aspect, as this course does. Huddleston and Pullum (2002) treat it as a second kind of past tense, because it places an event before a point in time. Both descriptions explain the same facts. For a learner, the important thing is what the perfect means, which is the subject of [[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect]].
:::

### How the verb phrase is built

::: theorem Order of the parts of the verb phrase {#rule-verb-phrase}
In a verb phrase, the auxiliaries always come in this order:

1. a **modal** (*will*, *can*, *must* …) + base form;
2. **perfect** *have* + past participle;
3. **progressive** *be* + *-ing* form;
4. the **main verb**.

The **first** verb in the phrase carries the tense (or is a modal). Each auxiliary decides the form of the verb after it.
:::

Look at *She has been working*:

- *has* — the first verb, so it carries the tense: present (*has*, not *had*). It is perfect *have*, so the next verb is a past participle: *been*.
- *been* — progressive *be*, so the next verb is an *-ing* form: *working*.
- *working* — the main verb.

The order never changes: *She has been working*, never ✗ *She is having worked*. Learners who know this order can build or take apart any verb phrase, however long. The passive adds one more *be* + past participle after the progressive (*The house is being painted*); passives are treated in [[verb-patterns/the-passive]].

::: example Naming tense and aspect {#ex-name}
Name the tense and the aspect of the verb phrases in bold. (a) *Yuki **had already left**.* (b) *We **are waiting** for a taxi.* (c) *They **have been living** here since May.* (d) *I **will be working** late tonight.* (e) *He **walked** home.*
::: solution
Find the first verb (it shows the tense or is a modal), then look for perfect *have* + participle and progressive *be* + *-ing*.

(a) *had* is past; *had* + *left* is perfect. → **past perfect**.

(b) *are* is present; *are* + *waiting* is progressive. → **present progressive** (present continuous).

(c) *have* is present; *have* + *been* is perfect; *been* + *living* is progressive. → **present perfect progressive**.

(d) *will* is a modal, so there is no tense; *be* + *working* is progressive. → ***will* + progressive** (often called the future progressive or future continuous).

(e) *walked* is past, with no auxiliary. → **past simple**.
:::
:::

::: warning *Have* and *be* in the wrong order, or with the wrong form
✗ *She is having worked here for ten years.* ✓ *She has been working here for ten years.* (or *She has worked here for ten years.*)

✗ *I have been work all day.* ✓ *I have been working all day.*

✗ *They had went home.* ✓ *They had gone home.*

Perfect *have* comes before progressive *be*. After *have* comes a past participle (*gone*, not the past simple *went*); after progressive *be* comes an *-ing* form.
:::

## A timeline with three points

How can we see what each form means? In 1947 the philosopher Hans Reichenbach proposed a simple model. Draw a line for time, from the past on the left to the future on the right. Then place three points on it.

::: definition Speech time, event time and reference time {#def-ser}
- **Speech time (S)** is the moment when the sentence is said or written: "now" for the speaker.
- **Event time (E)** is the time when the situation happens.
- **Reference time (R)** is the time **from which** the speaker views the event. Often it is named by a time expression (*yesterday*, *by then*, *when you called*), or it is clear from the context.
:::

With these three points, the main forms can be described like this:

| form | example | the points | in words |
|---|---|---|---|
| present simple | *I'm hungry.* | E = R = S | the situation and the viewpoint are now |
| past simple | *I lost my keys yesterday.* | E = R, before S | the event is seen from its own time in the past |
| present perfect | *I've lost my keys.* | E before R; R = S | a past event seen from now |
| past perfect | *I had lost my keys.* | E before R; R before S | an event seen from a later point in the past |
| *will* | *I'll see you tomorrow.* | E = R, after S | the event is seen from its own future time |
| future perfect | *I'll have finished by six.* | E before R; R after S | an event seen from a later point in the future |

The most important line is the difference between the past simple and the present perfect. In both, the event is in the past. In the past simple, the speaker views the event **from its own past time**: *yesterday*. In the present perfect, the speaker views the same event **from now**. That is why *I've lost my keys* says something about now (I don't have them) and *I lost my keys yesterday* does not (perhaps I found them again).

:::

::: intuition Where are you standing?
Think of R as the place where you stand to look at the event. In the past simple you go back and stand **in** the past: *Last year I visited Xi'an.* In the present perfect you stay **here, now**, and look back: *I've visited Xi'an* (so I know the city now). In the past perfect you stand at a point in the past and look **further back**: *When I moved to Beijing, I had already visited Xi'an.*
:::

::: example Placing S, E and R {#ex-ser}
For each sentence, say where E and R are in relation to S. (a) *Kenji has broken his leg.* (b) *Kenji broke his leg last winter.* (c) *By the time the ambulance came, Kenji had stopped crying.* (d) *By next Friday the doctors will have taken off the plaster.*
::: solution
(a) Present perfect. E (the break) is before S; R = S. The speaker views the event from now: the leg is still broken, or the news is recent.

(b) Past simple. E = R = *last winter*, before S. The sentence says nothing about the leg now.

(c) Past perfect. R = *the time the ambulance came*, before S. E (he stopped crying) is before R.

(d) Future perfect (*will* + perfect). R = *next Friday*, after S. E (taking off the plaster) is before R; it may happen at any time between now and Friday.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence views a past event **from now**?
- [ ] *I met Aisha in 2015.*
- [x] *I've met Aisha several times.*
- [ ] *I had met Aisha before the party.*
- [ ] *I was meeting Aisha when it started to rain.*
::: solution
The present perfect *I've met* puts R at S: the speaker looks back from now at meetings in the past. *Met in 2015* views the event from 2015 (past simple), *had met before the party* from the party (past perfect), and *was meeting* views the event from inside, as in progress at a past time.
:::
:::

## The same event, seen in different ways

Aspect lets a speaker choose a point of view on the same event. Here is one event — Amina reading a novel — described in four ways.

- *Amina **read** the novel last week.* — the event as a whole, in a finished past time.
- *Amina **was reading** the novel when I came in.* — the event in progress at a past moment; we don't know if she finished.
- *Amina **has read** the novel.* — the event complete, seen from now: she knows the story now.
- *Amina **has been reading** the novel all afternoon.* — an activity seen from now, going on up to now; perhaps she has not finished.

None of these sentences is "more correct". Each one tells the listener something different. Choosing a form is choosing what to tell.

::: example One event, four forms {#ex-four-forms}
Mateo is painting his flat. Write a sentence for each situation, and name the form you use. (a) A friend phones while he is painting. He explains why he can't talk. (b) He tells you on Monday what he did on Saturday. (c) The painting is done, and he shows you the flat. (d) He has paint on his hands and clothes, and you ask why.
::: solution
(a) *Sorry, I can't talk now — I'm painting the living room.* (present progressive: in progress now)

(b) *I painted the living room on Saturday.* (past simple: a finished time, *on Saturday*)

(c) *Look — I've painted the living room!* (present perfect: a completed event with a result now)

(d) *I've been painting the living room.* (present perfect progressive: an activity up to now, with visible signs)

The event is the same in all four answers. The forms change because the speaker's viewpoint changes.
:::
:::

::: warning *Will* after *when*, *if* and *as soon as*
✗ *I'll call you when I will arrive.* ✓ *I'll call you when I arrive.*

✗ *If it will rain, we'll stay at home.* ✓ *If it rains, we'll stay at home.*

Because *will* is often taught as "the future tense", learners put it everywhere the time is future. But in clauses of time and condition (after *when*, *if*, *as soon as*, *before*, *after*, *until*), English normally uses the **present tense** for the future. The time is future; the tense is present. More in [[tense-and-aspect/future-forms]].
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Chinese has no tense on the verb.** 我昨天去北京, 我现在去北京 and 我明天去北京 all use the same 去; the time words do the work. In English the verb must show the tense: *went*, *am going*, *am going* or *will go*. For this reason linguists describe Mandarin as a language without grammatical tense (Li and Thompson 1981): time is shown by time words and context.
- **Chinese has aspect.** The particles 了, 过 and 着 and the word 在 mark aspect, not tense (Li and Thompson 1981; 朱德熙《语法讲义》1982). 了 after a verb shows a completed, bounded event; 过 shows that something has been experienced; 在 and 着 show that an action or state is continuing. So Chinese and English share the idea of aspect, but they divide it differently.
- **了 is not a past tense marker.** It can appear with future time (明天下了课我去找你 ‘I'll come and find you tomorrow after class’), and many past sentences have no 了 (他以前很瘦 *He used to be thin*). Treating 了 as "the past" is the source of many mistakes. See [[tense-and-aspect/tense-and-chinese]].
- **English marks every finite verb.** Chinese can leave tense and aspect to the context; English cannot. Every sentence needs a decision: present or past, and simple, progressive or perfect.
:::

::: history
The first grammars of English, from the sixteenth century onwards, were modelled on Latin grammar. Latin has six tenses marked on the verb (present, imperfect, future, perfect, pluperfect and future perfect), and many early grammarians gave English six tenses too, treating *will* and *have* as if they were endings. Linguists of the twentieth century looked more closely at English itself. Otto Jespersen, in the fourth part of his *Modern English Grammar* (1931), separated the idea of time from the verb forms that express it. In 1947 the philosopher Hans Reichenbach, in *Elements of Symbolic Logic*, described the tenses with three points — speech, event and reference — the model used in this lesson. The word *aspect* itself comes from the grammar of Slavic languages such as Russian, where most verbs come in pairs that differ in aspect, and Bernard Comrie's book *Aspect* (1976) made the term standard in the comparison of languages.
:::

::: summary
- **Time** is part of the world; **tense** is a form of the verb. They often match, but not always: *The train leaves tomorrow* is present tense with future time ([[#def-time-tense]]).
- English has **two tenses**, present and past. There is no future ending: *will* is a modal verb ([[#rule-two-tenses]]).

:::
