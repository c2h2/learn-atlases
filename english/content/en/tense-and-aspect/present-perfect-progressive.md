Sofia comes into the flat. Her face is red, her hair is wet, and she is breathing hard.

**Her flatmate:** *What have you been doing?*

**Sofia:** *I've been running. I've run ten kilometres!*

Sofia uses two forms of the present perfect in two sentences. *I've been running* describes an **activity** that went on over a period up to now; its signs — the red face, the wet hair — are visible now. *I've run ten kilometres* describes a **result**: a finished distance, which she can count. Both look at the past from now, as all present perfect forms do. The difference is aspect again: progressive or simple.

This lesson explains the form of the **present perfect progressive**, its uses, and the choice between *I've read* and *I've been reading*. It builds on [[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect]] (the perfect: seen from now) and [[tense-and-aspect/simple-and-progressive]] (the progressive: seen from inside, in progress).

## Form

::: definition Present perfect progressive {#def-ppp}
The **present perfect progressive** (also called the present perfect continuous) is formed with *have/has* + ***been*** + the ***-ing*** form: *I have been waiting*, *she has been working*. It combines the present tense, the perfect aspect and the progressive aspect. It presents an **activity in progress over a period that reaches up to now**: the activity may still be going on, or it may have stopped a very short time ago.
:::

| | statement | negative | question |
|---|---|---|---|
| *I*, *you*, *we*, *they* | *I've been waiting.* | *I haven't been waiting long.* | *Have you been waiting long?* |
| *he*, *she*, *it* | *It's been raining.* | *It hasn't been raining.* | *Has it been raining?* |

The form is built by the order of [[tense-and-aspect/time-tense-aspect#rule-verb-phrase]]: perfect *have* comes first and is followed by a past participle (*been*); progressive *be* follows and is followed by an *-ing* form. In speech the auxiliaries are weak: *I've been waiting* sounds like /aɪv bɪn ˈweɪtɪŋ/ (GB), with *been* often /bɪn/ rather than /biːn/. In American English *been* is usually /bɪn/ in all positions.

## Meaning and use

::: theorem Uses of the present perfect progressive {#rule-ppp-uses}
Use the present perfect progressive for:

1. **An activity that started in the past and is still going on**, usually with *for*, *since*, *How long…?*, *all day*, *all morning*: *I'**ve been waiting** for forty minutes.* *How long **have** you **been learning** Chinese?* *It'**s been raining** all day.*
2. **An activity that has just stopped and has visible results now**, often without a time expression: *Your eyes are red. **Have** you **been crying**?* *Sorry about the mess — I'**ve been painting**.* *The ground is wet. It'**s been raining**.*
3. **A repeated or temporary activity up to now**, often with *lately*, *recently*, *these days*: *I'**ve been sleeping** badly lately.* *She'**s been going** to yoga classes since January.* *We'**ve been eating** out a lot recently.*
:::

::: proof
The perfect places the reference time at now and the event before it or up to it; the progressive presents the event from the inside, as an activity with duration that need not be complete ([[tense-and-aspect/simple-and-progressive#def-progressive]]). Together they give "an activity going on over a period up to now". In use 1 the activity reaches now and continues. In use 2 it reaches almost to now, and its traces are still there; the listener is interested in the activity, not in a result. In use 3 the activity is a series of repeated events over a period up to now, and the progressive adds the idea that this is temporary. Quirk et al. (1985) and Swan (2016) describe the same three uses.
:::

- *The phone **has been ringing** for ages — aren't you going to answer it?*
- *I'**ve been thinking** about what you said.*
- *We'**ve been trying** to call you all afternoon!*
- *Ahmed **has been working** at the airport since he left school.*

::: example Reading the evidence {#ex-evidence}
For each situation, say what the people have been doing, using the present perfect progressive. (a) Wen's hands are covered in flour. (b) Two children come in with muddy shoes and a football. (c) Kofi's eyes are tired, and there are papers all over his desk. (d) The road is wet and there are puddles everywhere.
::: solution
(a) *She's been baking* (or *She's been making dumplings*).

(b) *They've been playing football.*

(c) *He's been working / studying / reading all day.*

(d) *It's been raining.*

In each case the activity has just stopped (or is still going on), and we can see its effects now. This is use 2. The present perfect simple would sound wrong here: ✗ *She's baked* says nothing about flour on her hands; it would announce a result (*She's baked a cake* — here it is).
:::
:::

::: quiz
*Sorry I'm late. Have you been waiting long?* Why does the speaker use the present perfect progressive?
- [ ] Because the waiting is finished and in the past.
- [x] Because the waiting is an activity that has gone on over a period up to now.
- [ ] Because *wait* is a state verb.
- [ ] Because there is a finished time expression.
::: solution
The listener has been waiting from some time in the past up to now: an activity over a period up to now (use 1). *Wait* is an activity, not a state, so the progressive is natural. With a finished time (*Did you wait long yesterday?*) the past simple would be used.
:::
:::

## *I've read* or *I've been reading*?

Both forms connect the past to now. The question is what the speaker wants to talk about.

::: theorem Present perfect simple or progressive {#rule-simple-or-progressive}
1. Use the **simple** form for a **completed result**, especially when you say **how much** or **how many**: *I've read three chapters.* *She's written two reports this week.* *They've painted the kitchen* (it is finished).
2. Use the **progressive** form for the **activity** itself, especially when you say **how long**: *I've been reading all morning.* *She's been writing reports all week.* *They've been painting the kitchen* (perhaps they haven't finished).
3. Use the **simple** form with **state verbs**, even with *for*, *since* and *How long…?*: *I've known her for years*, not ✗ *I've been knowing her*. *How long have you had this bike?*, not ✗ *have you been having*.
4. With ***live***, ***work***, ***study***, ***teach***, ***stay*** and ***wait***, both forms are possible with *for* and *since*, with little difference: *I've lived / I've been living here for five years.* The progressive can suggest a more temporary situation.
:::

::: proof
The rule follows from the meaning of the aspects. A number (*three chapters*, *two reports*) counts complete units, so it needs a form that presents the event as complete: the simple form. A length of time (*all morning*) measures the activity from inside, so the progressive fits. A state has no "in progress" phase ([[tense-and-aspect/simple-and-progressive#rule-states]]), so it stays simple even when it lasts up to now. For verbs such as *live* and *work*, the situation can be seen either as a state (where I live) or as an activity (what I am doing), so both forms are correct; Swan (2016) and Murphy (2019) give the same rule.
:::

| simple: result, how much / how many | progressive: activity, how long |
|---|---|
| *I've read your book. It's great!* (I finished it) | *I've been reading your book.* (not finished) |
| *She's phoned ten customers.* | *She's been phoning customers all morning.* |
| *I've cleaned the car.* (it is clean now) | *I've been cleaning the car.* (that's why I'm dirty) |
| *How many cups of coffee have you drunk?* | *How long have you been drinking coffee?* |
| *I've known Liu Yang since 2010.* (state) | — (no progressive with *know*) |

::: example Simple or progressive? {#ex-simple-progressive}
Choose the better form and explain. (a) *I (have written / have been writing) emails since nine o'clock. I've (written / been writing) twenty-five.* (b) *Someone (has eaten / has been eating) my chocolate — there's none left!* (c) *Someone (has eaten / has been eating) my chocolate — there's only half of it left.* (d) *How long (have you had / have you been having) that cough?* (e) *My sister (has been learning / has learned) to drive for six months, but she still can't park.*
::: solution
(a) *have been writing* — the activity, with *since*; *written* — a number, so a result: *I've written twenty-five.*

(b) *has eaten* — complete: it is all gone (a result).

(c) *has been eating* — the activity was going on and is not complete; the half that is left is the evidence. (*Has eaten some of* would also be possible.)

(d) *have you had* — *have a cough* is a state here; the simple form is normal.

(e) *has been learning* — an activity over six months, not complete (she can't park yet). *Has learned to drive* would mean she can drive now.
:::
:::

::: warning The present continuous or simple with *for* and *since*
✗ *I'm waiting for you for an hour!* ✓ *I've been waiting for you for an hour!*

✗ *She studies English since 2019.* ✓ *She's been studying English since 2019.* (or *She's studied …*)

✗ *How long are you living in Beijing?* ✓ *How long have you been living in Beijing?*

A situation that started in the past and continues now needs a perfect form. The present forms say nothing about the time before now.
:::

::: warning The progressive with a number or a state
✗ *I've been drinking four cups of tea.* ✓ *I've drunk four cups of tea.*

✗ *I've been knowing him since primary school.* ✓ *I've known him since primary school.*

✗ *We've been having this flat for two years.* ✓ *We've had this flat for two years.*

A number of completed units needs the simple form. State verbs (*know*, *have* = own, *believe*, *like*) stay simple.
:::

### Negatives and duration

In negative sentences about a period up to now, the simple form is more common: *I **haven't seen** her for ages.* *We **haven't had** a holiday since 2022.* The negative progressive is used for an activity that has stopped being a habit: *I **haven't been sleeping** well* (= recently my sleep has been bad).

::: example From the past simple to the present perfect {#ex-transform}
It is now 4 p.m. Rewrite each pair of sentences as one sentence, using the present perfect simple or progressive and *for* or *since*. (a) *Lina started cooking at 1 p.m. She is still cooking.* (b) *Lina has cooked four dishes. She started at 1 p.m.* (ask: how many?) (c) *Jamal met Ana in 2018. They are still friends.* (d) *It started snowing on Monday. It hasn't stopped.*
::: solution
(a) *Lina has been cooking since 1 p.m.* (or *for three hours*) — activity, how long.

(b) *Lina has cooked four dishes since 1 p.m.* — a number of finished units: simple.

(c) *Jamal and Ana have been friends since 2018.* (or *Jamal has known Ana since 2018.*) — *be friends* and *know* are states: simple.

(d) *It has been snowing since Monday.* — activity up to now, with *since*.
:::
:::

::: example A conversation at the doctor's {#ex-doctor}
Read the conversation and explain each present perfect form.

**Doctor:** *What seems to be the problem?*

**Mr Okafor:** *I've been getting headaches. I've had three this week.*

**Doctor:** *How long have you been feeling like this?*

**Mr Okafor:** *About a month. I've been working very long hours since I started my new job.*

**Doctor:** *Have you taken anything for the pain?*

**Mr Okafor:** *I've tried some painkillers, but they haven't helped much.*
::: solution
1. *I've been getting headaches* — a repeated activity up to now (use 3).
2. *I've had three* — a number of complete events, so simple.
3. *have you been feeling* — *feel* is a physical feeling, so the progressive is possible, and the doctor asks about the activity's length (*How long*). *How long have you felt like this?* is also correct.
4. *I've been working … since I started* — an activity up to now, with *since* + a past simple clause.
5. *Have you taken anything* — a completed action (any time up to now) whose result matters.
6. *I've tried*, *haven't helped* — results up to now: the painkillers did not make him better.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *I've been knowing Mei for ten years.*
- [ ] *I've been buying three new shirts today.*
- [x] *I've been trying to call you all day.*
- [ ] *I'm living here since last year.*
::: solution
*Try to call* is an activity, repeated over a period up to now (*all day*): progressive. *Know* is a state (*I've known Mei for ten years*); *three new shirts* is a number of completed purchases (*I've bought three new shirts*); a situation up to now needs a perfect form (*I've been living / I've lived here since last year*).

::: note English and Chinese
- **一直 and 了……了.** Chinese usually shows an activity continuing up to now with 一直 or with a duration and two 了: 我一直在等你 or 我等了你半个小时了. English needs the present perfect progressive: *I've been waiting for you (for half an hour).* A direct translation with the present continuous (✗ *I'm waiting for you half an hour*) loses the period up to now.
- **一直在 is the closest match.** Chinese can combine 一直 and 在 (他一直在找工作), and this is very close to *He's been looking for a job*. But Chinese has no single form that matches *have been* + *-ing*; the meaning comes from several words together.
- **刚才 and evidence.** 你刚才在跑步吗？ asks about an activity a moment ago. English can use the past progressive (*Were you running just now?*) or, when the evidence is visible now, the present perfect progressive: *Have you been running?* The second is more natural when you are looking at a red face.
- **Results and amounts.** 我看了三章 is *I've read three chapters* (or *I read three chapters*, for a finished time), not ✗ *I've been reading three chapters*. Chinese shows the amount with a number and 了; English shows completion with the simple form.
- **State verbs again.** 我认识他十年了 is *I've known him for ten years*. Because 认识 takes no 在, learners rarely say ✗ *I'm knowing him*; but they often add the progressive after *have been* by analogy with other verbs. Check the verb first.
:::

::: history
The combination of the perfect and the progressive developed after each of them existed on its own. Otto Jespersen, in the fourth part of his *Modern English Grammar* (1931), called the progressive forms the **expanded tenses**, and he described them as forms that place a shorter event inside a longer "time-frame". The perfect progressive, in his description, is the expanded form of the perfect: an activity seen as a frame that reaches up to now. Corpus studies show how specialised the form is. Biber et al. (1999) found that verb phrases combining the perfect and the progressive are rare in every register compared with simple forms; they are most at home in conversation and fiction, where people explain what they have been doing. For a learner this means that the form is not frequent, but when it is needed — *I've been waiting for an hour!* — no other form will do.
:::

## Where this leads

The past perfect progressive (*I had been waiting for an hour when the bus came*) is the same structure seen from a point in the past, and is part of [[tense-and-aspect/narrative-tenses]]. The future perfect progressive (*By June I'll have been working here for ten years*) appears in [[tense-and-aspect/future-forms]]. [[tense-and-aspect/tense-and-chinese]] puts the Chinese means — 一直, 在, the double 了 — side by side with the English forms.

::: summary
- The present perfect progressive is *have/has been* + *-ing*: *I've been waiting* ([[#def-ppp]]).
- It presents an activity over a period up to now: still going on (*for*, *since*, *How long…?*), just stopped with visible results (*Have you been crying?*), or repeated recently (*I've been sleeping badly*) ([[#rule-ppp-uses]]).
- Use the simple form for a completed result and for **how much / how many** (*I've read three chapters*), the progressive for the activity and **how long** (*I've been reading all morning*) ([[#rule-simple-or-progressive]]).
- State verbs stay simple: *I've known her for years*, not ✗ *I've been knowing*.
- *Live*, *work*, *study*, *teach*, *stay* and *wait* allow both forms with *for* and *since*.
- A situation up to now needs a perfect form: not ✗ *I'm waiting for an hour*; Chinese 一直, 在 and the double 了 express this meaning with several words together.
:::
