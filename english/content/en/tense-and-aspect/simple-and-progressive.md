Read these two short stories.

- *Yesterday Aliyu crossed the road and bought a newspaper.*
- *Yesterday Aliyu was crossing the road when a car hit him.*

In the first story Aliyu got to the other side. In the second, we do not know: he was in the middle of the road when something happened. The only grammatical difference is aspect — *crossed* is simple, *was crossing* is progressive — and it changes what we know about the event.

Now compare *The bus stopped* and *The bus was stopping*. In the second sentence the bus has not stopped yet: it is slowing down. And *Someone knocked at the door* is one knock, while *Someone was knocking at the door* is many knocks. The progressive does not add the same meaning to every verb. What it adds depends on the kind of situation the verb describes.

In [[core-grammar/present-continuous]] you learned to use *am/is/are* + *-ing* for actions in progress now and for temporary situations, and you met the first state verbs. This lesson explains what the progressive means in every tense, how its meaning changes with different kinds of verbs, and why some verbs resist it.

## The progressive in every tense

The progressive is the same in all tenses: a form of *be* + the *-ing* form of the main verb. The form of *be* carries the tense or follows a modal or perfect *have* ([[tense-and-aspect/time-tense-aspect#rule-verb-phrase]]).

| | progressive | example |
|---|---|---|
| present | *am/is/are* + *-ing* | *Wang Lei **is cooking** dinner.* |
| past | *was/were* + *-ing* | *Wang Lei **was cooking** when the lights went out.* |
| *will* | *will be* + *-ing* | *At eight, Wang Lei **will be cooking**.* |
| present perfect | *has/have been* + *-ing* | *Wang Lei **has been cooking** all afternoon.* |
| past perfect | *had been* + *-ing* | *Wang Lei **had been cooking** for an hour when we arrived.* |

The perfect progressive forms are treated in [[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect-progressive]] and [[tense-and-aspect/narrative-tenses]], and the *will* form in [[tense-and-aspect/future-forms]]. Here we look at the meaning that all of them share.

::: definition Progressive aspect {#def-progressive}
The **progressive** presents a situation **in progress at a reference time**: it has started and has not ended, and we see it from the inside. Three ideas usually come with it:

1. **duration** — the situation lasts for some time;
2. **incompleteness** — the situation is not (yet) complete at the reference time;
3. **limited duration** — the situation is temporary, not permanent.

The **simple** form presents a situation **as a whole**, from the outside, with its beginning and end included or with no limits at all.
:::

::: proof
The three ideas follow from "seen from the inside". If you are inside a situation, it must last for some time (duration). From the inside you cannot see its end, so you do not know whether it is complete (incompleteness). And a situation that has a middle also has edges: it began and will end (limited duration). Leech (2004) and Quirk et al. (1985) both describe the progressive in these terms. The simple form makes no claim about the inside: it can present a short complete event (*He opened the door*), a permanent state (*Rivers flow to the sea*) or a habit (*She swims every day*).
:::

Because the reference time can be in the past, the present or the future, the progressive often works as a **frame** around another event:

- *When I got home, my sister **was watching** TV.* — the TV watching started before I got home and continued after.
- *Don't phone at seven — we'**ll be having** dinner.* — dinner will be in progress at seven.
- *Look, it'**s snowing**!* — in progress now.

The simple form, by contrast, presents events **one after another**, each as a whole: *When I got home, my sister **turned off** the TV* — first I got home, then she turned it off. Compare the two sentences carefully: the only difference is aspect, and it changes the order of events.

::: quiz
*When the teacher came in, the students were talking.* What does this sentence tell us?
- [ ] The students started talking after the teacher came in.
- [x] The students were already talking before the teacher came in.
- [ ] The students stopped talking when the teacher came in.
- [ ] The students talked for a short time only.
::: solution
The past progressive *were talking* frames the moment when the teacher came in: the talking was in progress at that time, so it had started before. Compare *When the teacher came in, the students stopped talking*, where the simple *stopped* follows the teacher's arrival. The sentence does not say whether they stopped afterwards.
:::
:::

## Four kinds of situation

Why does the progressive mean "not yet finished" with *cross the road*, "slowing down" with *stop*, and "many times" with *knock*? Because these verbs describe different kinds of situation. In 1957 the philosopher Zeno Vendler divided verbs into four classes, and later linguists such as Carlota Smith (1991) added a fifth.

::: definition Situation types {#def-situation-types}
- A **state** continues without change and needs no energy: *know*, *own*, *believe*, *be tall*, *like*.
- An **activity** goes on in time and has no natural end point: *run*, *read*, *rain*, *talk*, *play*.
- An **accomplishment** goes on in time and has a natural end point: *cross the road*, *build a house*, *write a letter*, *eat a sandwich*.
- An **achievement** happens at one moment, and changes one state into another: *arrive*, *reach the top*, *find*, *die*, *stop*, *win*.
- A **single-moment action** happens at one moment and changes nothing: *knock*, *cough*, *blink*, *jump*, *kick*.
:::

The type belongs to the whole verb phrase, not only to the verb. *Run* is an activity, but *run a kilometre* is an accomplishment: it has an end point. *Write* is an activity, but *write a letter* is an accomplishment. *Eat* is an activity, but *eat a sandwich* is an accomplishment.

::: theorem What the progressive adds to each type {#rule-progressive-types}
1. **Activities**: the activity is in progress. *It **was raining**.* *They'**re playing** cards.*
2. **Accomplishments**: the activity is in progress, and the end point **has not been reached** — it may never be reached. *She **was writing** a letter* does not tell us that she finished it; *She **wrote** a letter* does.
3. **Achievements**: the **lead-up** to the moment is in progress; the change itself has not happened yet. *The train **is arriving*** (it is not here yet). *The old dog **was dying*** (it was still alive).
4. **Single-moment actions**: the action is **repeated**. *Someone **was knocking*** (many knocks). *The baby **is coughing*** (again and again).
5. **States**: the progressive is **usually not used**: ✗ *I'm knowing her.* ✓ *I know her.* (See [[#rule-states]] for the exceptions.)
:::

::: proof
A situation must have a "middle" before we can be inside it. Activities and accomplishments have a middle, so the progressive simply puts us there; with an accomplishment, being in the middle also means that the end is still ahead. Achievements and single-moment actions have no middle — they happen at one moment — so the progressive has to find a middle somewhere else: in the time just before the moment (achievements), or in a series of repeated moments (single-moment actions). States have a middle, but they do not change or develop, so there is nothing "in progress" to show; using the progressive with a state is therefore strange unless the speaker wants to stress change or temporariness. Swan (2016) and Carter and McCarthy (2006) describe the same pattern in their entries on the progressive.
:::

::: example Classifying situations {#ex-classify}
Say what type each verb phrase is, and what the progressive would add. (a) *swim* (b) *swim across the lake* (c) *reach the station* (d) *belong to someone* (e) *sneeze* (f) *paint a portrait*
::: solution
(a) Activity: no end point. *She was swimming* — in progress.

(b) Accomplishment: the other side of the lake is the end point. *She was swimming across the lake* — in progress; she may not have reached the other side.

(c) Achievement: one moment, a change of place. *We were reaching the station* is rare; English prefers *We were getting near the station* or *We were about to reach it*. Where the progressive is used with an achievement, it shows the lead-up.

(d) State. No progressive: ✗ *This bike is belonging to Ana.* ✓ *This bike belongs to Ana.*

(e) Single-moment action. *He was sneezing* — many sneezes, one after another.

(f) Accomplishment: the finished portrait is the end point. *He was painting a portrait* — in progress, and not (yet) finished.
:::
:::

::: example Did it happen? {#ex-did-it-happen}
For each pair, say whether we know that the event was completed. (a) *Marta read the book.* / *Marta was reading the book.* (b) *The plane landed.* / *The plane was landing.* (c) *Hamid built a boat.* / *Hamid was building a boat when he got ill.*
::: solution
(a) *read* — yes, the whole book. *was reading* — no: she was in the middle of it; she may or may not have finished later.

(b) *landed* — yes. *was landing* — no: it was coming down; *landing* is an achievement, so the progressive shows the lead-up. (The plane probably landed a minute later, but the sentence does not say so.)

(c) *built* — yes, a finished boat exists. *was building … when he got ill* — no; the illness may have stopped him.

This is why a past progressive sentence often needs a second clause to complete the story: *Marta was reading the book when …*
:::
:::

## State verbs and the progressive

::: theorem State verbs {#rule-states}
Verbs and verb phrases that describe **states** are normally used in the **simple** form, even when the state is true at the moment of speaking. The main groups are:

1. **knowing and thinking**: *know*, *believe*, *understand*, *realise*, *suppose*, *mean*, *remember*, *recognise*;
2. **liking and wanting**: *like*, *love*, *hate*, *prefer*, *want*, *need*, *wish*;
3. **having and relations**: *have* (‘own’), *own*, *belong*, *contain*, *include*, *consist of*, *depend on*, *matter*, *deserve*;
4. **being and seeming**: *be*, *seem*, *appear* (‘seem’), *resemble*, *cost*, *weigh* (‘have a weight’), *fit*;
5. **perception without effort**: *see*, *hear*, *smell*, *taste*, *feel* (when they mean ‘receive through the senses’).
:::

- *I **understand** what you mean.* — not ✗ *I'm understanding.*
- *This box **contains** old letters.* — not ✗ *is containing.*
- *That jacket **suits** you.* — not ✗ *is suiting.*
- *Can you hear that? I **hear** music.* — not ✗ *I'm hearing music.*

Many of these verbs have a second meaning that is an action. Then the progressive is normal.

| state (simple) | action (progressive possible) |
|---|---|
| *I **have** two brothers.* (possess) | *I'**m having** a shower.* (take) |
| *I **think** it's a good idea.* (opinion) | *I'**m thinking** about my future.* (consider) |
| *I **see** what you mean.* (understand) | *I'**m seeing** the dentist at four.* (meet) |
| *The soup **tastes** salty.* (has a taste) | *The cook **is tasting** the soup.* (tries it) |
| *The flowers **smell** lovely.* (have a smell) | *Why **are** you **smelling** the milk?* (sniff it) |
| *This bag **weighs** five kilos.* (has a weight) | *She'**s weighing** the bag.* (measures it) |
| *He **is** rude.* (his character) | *He'**s being** rude.* (his behaviour now) |
| *The bag **looks** heavy.* (seems) | *She'**s looking** at the bag.* (directs her eyes) |

::: remark Where the line is not sharp
The rule for state verbs describes a strong tendency, not an absolute law. Three groups behave in between:

- **Physical feelings** can be simple or progressive with almost no difference: *I feel tired* / *I'm feeling tired*; *My back hurts* / *My back is hurting*.
- **Change and temporariness** allow the progressive even with states: *I'm understanding it better now* (a change in progress); *You're looking well* (at the moment); *I'm living with my cousin until I find a flat* (temporary).
- **Politeness and softness**: *I'm hoping you can help* and *We're wondering whether…* make a request less direct than *I hope* and *We wonder*.

In informal speech some state verbs appear in the progressive for emphasis, as in *I'm loving this weather!* or *I'm wanting a coffee*. Leech et al. (2009) found that the progressive as a whole has grown more frequent in recent decades. These uses are spreading, but in formal writing and in exams keep state verbs in the simple form.
:::

::: example State or action? {#ex-state-action}
Choose the simple or the progressive form and explain. (a) *Be quiet — I (think).* (b) *I (think) you're right.* (c) *We (have) a party on Saturday — can you come?* (d) *This hotel (have) a swimming pool.* (e) *Why (you / be) so difficult today? You're usually so easy-going.* (f) *The milk (smell) strange. I think it's gone off.*
::: solution
(a) *I'm thinking* — *think* = ‘consider’, an activity in progress.

(b) *I think* — *think* = ‘have an opinion’, a state.

(c) *We're having* — *have a party* is an event, here a future arrangement in the present progressive.

(d) *has* — *have* = ‘possess, include’, a state.

(e) *are you being* — *be* + adjective describes behaviour at the moment, a temporary, active situation. The contrast with *usually* shows that this is not the person's character.

(f) *smells* — *smell* = ‘have a smell’, a state.
:::
:::

::: warning Progressive forms of perception and possession verbs
✗ *I'm seeing a bird on the roof.* ✓ *I can see a bird on the roof.* (or *I see a bird…*)

✗ *Are you hearing me?* ✓ *Can you hear me?*

✗ *This phone is costing 3,000 yuan.* ✓ *This phone costs 3,000 yuan.*

✗ *I'm not believing you.* ✓ *I don't believe you.*

With *see* and *hear*, English very often adds *can*: *I can see…*, *Can you hear…?* This is the usual way to say that a perception is going on now. If you mean an action that you do on purpose, use *look at*, *watch* or *listen to*, which are activities: *I'm looking at a bird.* *Are you listening to me?*
:::

## The simple form: whole events, series and facts

The simple form is not just "the form without *-ing*". It has its own uses, where the progressive would be wrong or would change the meaning.

- **A series of events, each complete**: *She **opened** the letter, **read** it and **smiled**.* (Not ✗ *was opening … was reading …*, which would make the events overlap.)
- **Habits and repeated events**: *He **cycles** to work.* *We **went** to the beach every summer.*
- **Facts and permanent situations**: *Water **boils** at 100 °C.* *My parents **live** in Kunming.*
- **Commentaries and demonstrations**, where each action is complete as it is described: *Silva **passes** to Okafor, Okafor **shoots** — and it's a goal!* *First I **add** the flour, then I **stir** it slowly.*

::: example Choosing the form in a story {#ex-story}
Complete the story with the past simple or the past progressive. Explain each choice.

*It (1 rain) and the wind (2 blow) hard. Ling (3 wait) at the bus stop when she (4 notice) a small dog under the bench. It (5 shake) with cold. She (6 pick) it up, (7 wrap) it in her scarf and (8 take) it home.*
::: solution
(1) *was raining* and (2) *was blowing* — background activities in progress at the time of the story.

(3) *was waiting* — in progress when the next event happened; it frames *noticed*.

(4) *noticed* — an event, complete, which happens inside the frame.

(5) *was shaking* — shaking is a series of single-moment movements; the progressive shows it going on (and repeated) at that time.

(6) *picked*, (7) *wrapped*, (8) *took* — a series of complete events in order: the simple form.

The progressive forms set the scene; the simple forms move the story forward. [[tense-and-aspect/narrative-tenses]] develops this division.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence describes **one** knock?
- [ ] *Someone was knocking at the door.*
- [x] *Someone knocked at the door.*
- [ ] *Someone has been knocking at the door.*
- [ ] *Someone is knocking at the door.*
::: solution
*Knock* is a single-moment action. In the simple form it can describe a single knock (or a short series seen as one event). In the progressive forms it must be repeated, because a single knock has no middle that could be "in progress".
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is **not** standard in formal written English?
- [ ] *I'm having a coffee with Sara.*
- [ ] *The children are being very quiet today.*
- [x] *This book is belonging to the library.*
- [ ] *I'm thinking of changing jobs.*
::: solution
*Belong* is a state verb with no action meaning, so it stays simple: *This book belongs to the library.* The others use the action meanings of *have* (‘drink’), *be* (‘behave’) and *think* (‘consider’), so the progressive is correct.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **在 and 正在 are close to the progressive with activities.** 他在打球 = *He's playing ball.* But Chinese can leave the aspect unmarked when the context is clear (我到家的时候，我妹妹看电视), while English must use the progressive for the frame: *When I got home, my sister was watching TV.*
- **Chinese state verbs also refuse 在.** Mandarin does not say ✗ 我在知道 or ✗ 他在是老师, just as English does not say ✗ *I'm knowing*. Li and Thompson (1981) note that the durative 在 goes with activity verbs, not with stative ones. So the idea of state verbs is not foreign to Chinese speakers; the difficulty is that the two lists are not identical. English *see*, *hear* and *understand* are states, while 看 is an activity: 我在看书 = *I'm reading*, but ✗ *I'm seeing a book*.
- **在 can be a preposition.** In 我在北京工作 the word 在 means *in*, and the sentence describes a job, not an action in progress: *I work in Beijing*, not ✗ *I'm working in Beijing* (unless the job is temporary).
- **着 is often a state, not an action.** 他戴着眼镜 describes how he looks: *He wears glasses* (always) or *He's wearing glasses* (now). 门开着 is *The door is open*, a state with *be* + adjective, not ✗ *The door is opening*. 着 also marks a background action: 他笑着说 → *he said with a smile* or *he said, smiling*.
- **Achievements in progress.** Where English uses the progressive for the lead-up (*The train is arriving*, *The battery is dying*), Chinese often uses 快(要)……了 or 要……了: 火车快到了, 电池快没电了.
:::

::: history
The idea that verbs fall into classes according to their time structure is old, but its modern form comes from an article by the philosopher Zeno Vendler, "Verbs and Times", published in *The Philosophical Review* in 1957. Vendler noticed that we can ask *What are you doing?* — *I'm running* about activities, but not ✗ *What are you doing? — I'm knowing the answer* about states, and he used tests of this kind to divide verbs into states, activities, accomplishments and achievements. Linguists later added the class of single-moment actions, called **semelfactives** (Latin *semel* ‘once’); Carlota Smith's *The Parameter of Aspect* (1991) used all five classes to compare English with other languages, including Mandarin Chinese.
:::

## Where this leads

The progressive returns in every following lesson: in the present perfect progressive ([[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect-progressive]]), in the past progressive and past perfect progressive of stories ([[tense-and-aspect/narrative-tenses]]), and in the future progressive (*I'll be working*, [[tense-and-aspect/future-forms]]). The comparison with 在, 着 and 了 is completed in [[tense-and-aspect/tense-and-chinese]]. The use of the progressive to sound polite (*I was wondering…*) is part of [[modality/modality-politeness]].

::: summary
- The progressive (*be* + *-ing*) presents a situation **in progress at a reference time**, from the inside: with duration, not complete, temporary. The simple form presents it as a whole ([[#def-progressive]]).
- The progressive often frames another event: *When I got home, she was watching TV.* Simple forms present events one after another.
- What the progressive adds depends on the situation type: activities in progress; accomplishments not yet complete (*was crossing the road*); achievements not yet reached (*is arriving*); single-moment actions repeated (*was knocking*) ([[#def-situation-types]], [[#rule-progressive-types]]).
- State verbs (*know*, *own*, *belong*, *see*, *cost* …) are normally simple; many have a second, action meaning that allows the progressive (*have a shower*, *think about*, *be rude*) ([[#rule-states]]).
- Physical feelings, changing situations and polite requests allow the progressive with some state verbs; informal speech uses it more and more, formal writing less.
- Chinese 在 resembles the progressive with activities, and Chinese states also refuse 在; but 着 often describes a state (*The door is open*), and 在 + place is a preposition, not the progressive.
:::
