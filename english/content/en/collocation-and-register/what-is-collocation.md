Liu Yang has just started a master's course in Leeds, and she sends her tutor a short email to explain why she missed a seminar:

> *Dear Dr Evans, I am sorry that I could not attend the seminar yesterday. There was a very strong rain in the morning and my bus was cancelled. I know I did a big mistake by not telling you before. I will do my best to catch up the reading. Best regards, Liu Yang*

Dr Evans understands every word, and there is not a single grammatical error in the message. Yet three phrases tell him at once that the writer is not a native speaker: *a very strong rain*, *did a big mistake* and *catch up the reading*. An expert speaker would write *heavy rain*, *made a big mistake* and *catch up on the reading*. Nothing in the meanings of *strong* and *heavy*, or of *do* and *make*, explains why. Rain falls hard; *strong* means ‘having great force’; *do* is the general verb of action. By logic, Liu Yang's choices are perfectly reasonable. By usage, they are not what English speakers say.

This chapter is about that gap between what is logical and what is usual. It explains what a **collocation** is, how collocations differ in strength — from combinations anyone could make to fixed idioms — what kinds of collocation there are, and why they matter so much for learners whose grammar is already good. The next chapter, [[collocation-and-register/using-a-corpus]], shows how to check a combination for yourself, and [[collocation-and-register/verb-noun-adjective-noun]] teaches the most important collocations one group at a time.

## What collocation is

::: definition Collocation {#def-collocation}
A **collocation** is a combination of words that occur together more often than chance would predict, and that speakers of the language recognise as the normal way of expressing a meaning: *heavy rain*, *make a mistake*, *pay attention*, *highly unlikely*, *a flock of sheep*. In a collocation, the word whose behaviour we are studying is the **node** and the words it typically occurs with are its **collocates**: with *rain* as node, *heavy*, *light*, *torrential* and *pouring* are adjective collocates, and *fall*, *stop* and *pour down* verb collocates.
:::

The definition has two halves, because the word is used in two related ways.

1. **The statistical sense.** Corpus linguists in the tradition of J. R. Firth and John Sinclair use *collocation* for any pair of words that co-occur significantly often within a short distance (a few words to the left and right), whether or not the pair seems special. In this sense *rain* collocates with *heavy*, but also with *wind* and *umbrella*.
2. **The phraseological sense.** Teachers, lexicographers and many researchers on learner language, Nadja Nesselhauf (2005) among them, reserve the term for combinations in which the choice of one word is **restricted** by the other: *heavy* in the sense ‘large in amount’ goes with *rain*, *traffic* and *smoker*, but not with *wind* or *sunshine*.

In this course we use the word mainly in the second sense, because restricted combinations are the ones learners get wrong, and the statistical sense mainly as a tool for finding them ([[collocation-and-register/using-a-corpus]]).

The idea is old. Firth, who made *collocation* a technical term of linguistics, wrote in 1957 that part of the meaning of a word is the company it keeps: one thing we know about *night*, in his example, is that it goes with *dark*. Michael Halliday (1966) gave the most quoted illustration. *Strong* and *powerful* mean nearly the same, but English speakers say *strong tea* and not ✗ *powerful tea*, and *a powerful car* rather than ? *a strong car*; with *argument*, both are fine. No rule of grammar, and no difference in the meaning of the adjectives, predicts this. It is simply a fact about how the words are used, which every speaker learns.

::: theorem Collocations are conventional {#rule-conventional}
Which words combine naturally is decided by **convention** — by what speakers of the language habitually say — and not by the meanings of the words alone. Two words with nearly the same meaning (*strong* and *powerful*, *big* and *great*, *make* and *do*) can have quite different collocates, and a combination that is logical and grammatical may still be unusual. A learner therefore cannot work out a collocation; it must be noticed, learned and, when in doubt, checked.
:::

::: proof
The evidence is the large number of near-synonyms whose collocates differ for no reason that their meaning explains: *a great deal* but not ✗ *a big deal of* or ✗ *a large deal of*; *a big surprise* and *a great surprise* but not usually ? *a large surprise*; *a large amount* and *a large number*, where *great* is also possible but *big* is informal. Other languages make different, equally arbitrary choices: Chinese says 大雨 (literally ‘big rain’) where English says *heavy rain*, and German says *starker Regen* (literally ‘strong rain’). Because the restriction belongs to the particular language, it cannot be derived from a universal logic of meaning — which is exactly why it has to be learned. The rule stops short at one point: collocations are not completely arbitrary. *Heavy* combines with *rain*, *traffic*, *smoker* and *drinker* because they share the idea of ‘a large amount of something’, so once one member of such a group is known, the others are easier to accept and remember.
:::

::: quiz
Which of these is the natural English combination?
- [ ] *a strong rain*
- [ ] *a big rain*
- [x] *heavy rain*
- [ ] *a thick rain*
::: solution
English says *heavy rain* (and *light rain*, *torrential rain* for very heavy rain). *Strong rain*, *big rain* and *thick rain* are understandable, and each can be explained by the logic of another language (German *starker Regen*, Chinese 大雨), but they are not the conventional English combination. *Heavy* is also the collocate of *snow*, *traffic* and *smoker*.
:::
:::

## Strength: from free combinations to idioms

Word combinations are not simply "collocations" or "not collocations". They lie on a scale, from combinations in which each word keeps its ordinary meaning and can be replaced freely, to idioms in which the words have lost their separate meanings altogether. The scale below follows the categories that the British lexicographer A. P. Cowie proposed and that later research on learners, including Howarth (1998) and Nesselhauf (2005), adopted.

::: definition Free combination, collocation and idiom {#def-cline}
- In a **free combination**, each word has its ordinary meaning and each can be replaced by any word of suitable meaning: *buy a car* (*a house*, *a bike*, *some bread*; *sell*, *rent*, *borrow a car*).
- In a **restricted collocation** (usually just called a collocation), one word is used in a special or narrowed sense that is available only with a limited set of partners: *commit a crime* (*an offence*, *murder*, *fraud* — but not ✗ *commit a kindness*); *heavy rain*; *shrug your shoulders*; *foot the bill* (‘pay’).
- In an **idiom**, the meaning of the whole cannot be worked out from the meanings of the parts, and the words can be changed little or not at all: *kick the bucket* (‘die’), *spill the beans* (‘tell a secret’), *a piece of cake* (‘very easy’). Idioms are the subject of [[collocation-and-register/idioms]].
:::

The middle category is the one that matters most for learners, and it is itself a range. Some collocations are very restricted: the verb *shrug* has essentially one object, *shoulders*, and *foot* means ‘pay’ only in *foot the bill*. Others are much more open: *make* in its light, general sense goes with dozens of nouns (*a decision*, *a mistake*, *an effort*, *progress*, *a suggestion*), but it still excludes many that seem similar (✗ *make homework*, ✗ *make a favour*).

| combination | the words | can you change them? | category |
|---|---|---|---|
| *open the window* | both literal | freely: *close the window*, *open the door* | free combination |
| *pay attention* | *pay* in a special sense ‘give’ | the noun only within a small set: *pay a visit*, *pay a compliment*, *pay tribute* | collocation |
| *blond hair* | *blond* used only of hair (and people) | not: ✗ *a blond car*, ✗ *blond wine* | collocation (very restricted) |
| *break the ice* | the whole has a new meaning | hardly: ✗ *break the snow*, ✗ *smash the ice* | idiom (but its image can still be understood) |
| *kick the bucket* | neither *kick* nor *bucket* means anything here | not at all in the idiomatic sense | pure idiom |

::: example Placing combinations on the scale {#ex-cline}
Decide whether each combination is a free combination, a collocation or an idiom, and give your reason.

1. *She drank a glass of water.*
2. *He committed a serious crime.*
3. *The company went bankrupt and the directors had to face the music.*
4. *We reached an agreement after six hours.*
5. *He bought a new laptop.*
6. *I'm afraid you're barking up the wrong tree.*
::: solution
1. **Free combination.** *Drink* and *water* keep their ordinary meanings, and each can be replaced freely (*drank a cup of tea*, *poured a glass of water*). (*A glass of* is itself a common unit of measure, but nothing about it is restricted.)
2. **Collocation.** *Commit* in the sense ‘do something wrong’ combines only with nouns for wrong or harmful acts: *a crime*, *an offence*, *murder*, *fraud*, *suicide*. We do not say ✗ *commit a good deed*. The noun keeps its meaning; the verb is restricted.
3. **Idiom.** *Face the music* means ‘accept criticism or punishment for something you have done’. Nothing in *face* or *music* gives that meaning, and the words cannot be changed (✗ *face the song*).
4. **Collocation.** *Reach* in the sense ‘arrive at’ is used with a limited set of abstract nouns: *an agreement*, *a decision*, *a conclusion*, *a compromise*, *a verdict*. The meaning is still easy to understand, but the choice of verb is conventional — a learner might well have written ✗ *got an agreement* or ✗ *achieved an agreement*, which are less natural.
5. **Free combination.** *Buy* + any purchasable object.
6. **Idiom.** *Bark up the wrong tree* ‘be wrong about the cause of something, look in the wrong place’. The image (a dog barking at the wrong tree) helps to remember it, but the meaning is not built from the parts.

Notice that the test is always the same: how freely can each word be replaced, and does each word keep its ordinary meaning?
:::
:::

::: theorem Collocations are restricted in one direction {#rule-direction}
In most collocations the restriction runs mainly one way. One word — usually the noun — keeps its ordinary meaning and could have many partners, while the other word is chosen for it: we choose *heavy* because the noun is *rain*, and *commit* because the noun is *crime*. The noun is called the **base** of the collocation, and the restricted word the **collocator**. To find a collocation, start from the base.
:::

::: proof
The words *base* and *collocator* come from the European tradition of lexicography (Hausmann, among others), and the same asymmetry shows up in corpus counts: *shrug* almost always has *shoulders* near it, but most occurrences of *shoulders* have nothing to do with shrugging. The practical consequence is for dictionaries and for learners. A writer knows what they want to talk about — rain, a crime, a decision — and needs the verb or adjective that goes with it, so collocation dictionaries are arranged by the base: the entry for *decision* lists the verbs *make*, *take* (mainly BrE), *reach*, *come to* and the adjectives *difficult*, *final*, *important*, *snap*. The rule is a strong tendency, not a law: in some pairs both words are restricted (*curry favour*, where *curry* has no other modern use), and in others neither is (*strong argument*).
:::

::: warning Learning the collocator without the base
A list such as "*commit* = 犯; *heavy* = 大; *make* = 做" invites errors, because each collocator is right only with certain bases: ✗ *commit a mistake* (normal: *make a mistake*; *commit an error* exists but is formal), ✗ *heavy wind* (normal: *strong wind*), ✗ *make sport* (normal: *do sport*, BrE; *play sports*, AmE). Learn and record the **pair**, and if possible the group of bases that share the collocator: *heavy rain*, *heavy snow*, *heavy traffic*, *a heavy smoker*.
:::

## Kinds of collocation

Collocations are often classified by the word classes they combine. Benson, Benson and Ilson's *BBI Combinatory Dictionary of English* (1986) made the main distinction between **lexical collocations**, which combine two content words, and **grammatical collocations**, which combine a content word with a preposition or a grammatical pattern.

| type | examples | notes |
|---|---|---|
| verb + noun | *make a decision*, *pay attention*, *keep a promise*, *raise a question*, *meet a deadline* | the most frequent source of learner errors; [[collocation-and-register/verb-noun-adjective-noun]] |
| adjective + noun | *heavy rain*, *a strong accent*, *a narrow escape*, *a close friend*, *a rough idea* | |
| noun + verb | *dogs bark*, *the alarm went off*, *prices rise*, *rumours spread* | which verb a noun "does" |
| noun + *of* + noun | *a bar of chocolate*, *a slice of bread*, *a flock of sheep*, *a piece of advice* | including the words that count uncountable nouns |
| adverb + adjective | *highly unlikely*, *bitterly cold*, *deeply disappointed*, *fully aware* | the intensifiers are strongly restricted |
| verb + adverb | *apologise profusely*, *whisper softly*, *vary considerably* | |
| grammatical | *depend on*, *interested in*, *by accident*, *on purpose*, *afraid of* | preposition patterns: [[verb-patterns/prepositional-verbs]] |

The adverb + adjective group deserves a special mention because it is so easily overlooked. English has many words meaning ‘very’, and they are not interchangeable: *highly unlikely* and *highly successful*, but not ✗ *highly cold*; *bitterly cold* and *bitterly disappointed*, but not ✗ *bitterly happy*; *fully aware* but rarely ? *fully happy*. *Very* goes with almost any gradable adjective, which is why learners overuse it; the specific intensifiers sound more natural and more precise.

::: example Intensifiers {#ex-intensifiers}
Choose the most natural intensifier for each adjective from *highly*, *bitterly*, *deeply*, *fully*, *perfectly*, and explain the choice.

1. *I'm ___ aware of the risks.*
2. *It was a ___ cold night in January.*
3. *It's ___ unlikely that the results will change.*
4. *She was ___ moved by the letter.*
5. *You're ___ right.*
::: solution
1. *fully aware* — *fully* goes with words of knowledge and completeness (*fully aware*, *fully understand*, *fully booked*).
2. *bitterly cold* — *bitterly* combines with *cold* and with negative feelings (*bitterly disappointed*, *bitterly opposed*).
3. *highly unlikely* — *highly* goes with words of probability and evaluation (*highly likely*, *highly unlikely*, *highly successful*, *highly recommended*).
4. *deeply moved* — *deeply* goes with feelings and beliefs (*deeply moved*, *deeply concerned*, *deeply religious*).
5. *perfectly right* — *perfectly* goes with *right*, *clear*, *normal*, *possible*, *reasonable*; *absolutely right* is also very common.

*Very* would be grammatical in 2–4 (*very cold*, *very unlikely*, *very moved*), but less vivid; it does not fit 1 well (*very aware* is possible but less usual than *fully aware* or *well aware*), nor 5 (? *very right*), because *right* is not usually graded.
:::
:::

### Colligation and semantic prosody

Two related ideas complete the picture.

::: definition Colligation {#def-colligation}
**Colligation** is the grammatical company a word keeps: the patterns and word classes it typically occurs with, rather than particular words. *Look forward to* is followed by a noun or an *-ing* form (*I look forward to hearing from you*); the noun *advice* occurs with *a piece of* or with *some*, never with *a*; *afford* occurs mostly after *can*, *could* or *be able to*, and very often in the negative (*We can't afford it*).
:::

::: definition Semantic prosody {#def-prosody}
**Semantic prosody** is the positive or negative colouring that a word takes on from the words it regularly occurs with. The verb *cause* is neutral in its dictionary meaning, but its typical objects are unwelcome (*cause problems*, *cause damage*, *cause concern*, *cause delays*), so *cause* itself carries a negative expectation. The phrasal verb *set in* is used of unpleasant things that begin and continue (*the rain set in*, *decay set in*, *boredom set in*).
:::

Sinclair (1991) drew attention to *set in*, and Bill Louw (1993) named the phenomenon semantic prosody; Michael Stubbs (1995) showed the negative company of *cause*. The idea matters to learners because a word with a negative prosody sounds odd in a positive context: ? *The new teacher caused a big improvement in our results* is grammatical, but most speakers would write *brought about*, *led to* or *produced*. Prosody is part of what a word suggests, which is taken further in [[collocation-and-register/connotation]].

::: example Choosing a neutral verb {#ex-prosody}
In each sentence, decide whether *cause* fits. If it does not, suggest a better verb.

1. *Heavy rain caused flooding in several villages.*
2. *The new timetable has caused a lot of confusion.*
3. *Regular exercise causes better sleep.*
4. *The discovery caused great excitement among scientists.*
::: solution
1. Fits: *flooding* is unwelcome, the typical object of *cause*.
2. Fits: *confusion* is unwelcome.
3. Odd. *Better sleep* is a good result; *leads to*, *results in*, *promotes* or *helps you sleep better* sound natural.
4. Possible: *cause excitement* and *cause a stir* are used for strong reactions, especially surprise. The prosody of *cause* is a strong tendency, not a ban, and reactions that are intense rather than clearly good (*excitement*, *a sensation*) are the usual exceptions. *Created* or *generated great excitement* is also natural.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence uses *set in* naturally?
- [ ] *After the holiday, happiness set in.*
- [x] *By the third week of the course, tiredness had set in.*
- [ ] *Good weather set in, so we went to the beach.*
- [ ] *Success set in after years of hard work.*
::: solution
*Set in* is used of unpleasant states or processes that start and look likely to continue: *tiredness*, *boredom*, *winter*, *decay*, *panic*. *Good weather set in* is not impossible in older or literary English, but the usual company of *set in* is negative, so with *happiness* and *success* it sounds wrong.
:::
:::

## Why collocation matters for learners

Speakers rarely notice collocations in their own language, because they never have to think about them. Learners notice them only when someone corrects them. Yet the evidence from learner corpora — large collections of learners' writing, analysed by computer — shows that collocation is one of the main differences between advanced learner English and expert English.

::: theorem Advanced learners' errors are often errors of combination {#rule-learner-errors}
Learners whose grammar is good still produce many unconventional combinations, especially verb–noun collocations with frequent, general verbs (*make*, *do*, *take*, *have*, *give*, *get*), and many of these combinations can be traced to the learner's first language. Learners also tend to **overuse** a few safe combinations (*very*, *big*, *a lot of*) and **underuse** the more specific collocations an expert writer would choose.
:::

::: proof
Sylviane Granger and her colleagues at Louvain built the International Corpus of Learner English (first version 2002) from essays by advanced learners with many different first languages, and compared it with comparable essays by native speakers. One of the clearest findings of this work, summarised by Granger (1998) in a study of French-speaking learners, was that learners use fewer of the conventional collocations that native writers rely on, and lean heavily on a small number of all-purpose ones such as *very* + adjective. Nesselhauf (2005) examined the verb–noun combinations in essays by advanced German-speaking learners and found that a considerable proportion deviated from native usage; the commonest problem was the choice of the verb, and the influence of German was visible in many of the errors. For Chinese learners, the Chinese Learner English Corpus (CLEC, compiled by Gui Shichun and Yang Huizhong, published in 2003), about one million words of writing by Chinese students, has been used for similar studies of collocation errors. The detailed figures differ from study to study and depend on how an "error" is defined, so they are not given here; the direction of the finding is consistent across them.
:::

There is also a more positive reason to care. Collocations are not only a source of errors; they are what make reading and listening fast. A reader who knows that *pay* is likely to be followed by *attention*, *a visit* or *the bill* processes the phrase almost before reading it. The idea of chunks introduced in [[core-vocabulary/phrases-and-chunks]] — ready-made sequences that speakers store and retrieve as wholes — applies directly here: a collocation is a small chunk.

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese has collocation too — 词语搭配 — and many Chinese learners first meet the idea in the rule that 提高 goes with 水平 and 增加 with 数量. The difficulty is that the restrictions of the two languages do not match. One Chinese collocator often corresponds to several English ones, depending on the base:

- 大: 大雨 *heavy rain*, 大风 *a strong wind*, 大错 *a big* (*serious*) *mistake*, 大声 *loudly* / *in a loud voice*, 大火 *a big fire* (cooking: *a high heat*).
- 浓: 浓茶 *strong tea*, 浓雾 *thick* or *dense fog*, 浓烟 *thick smoke*, 浓妆 *heavy make-up*, 浓厚的兴趣 *a keen* (*strong*) *interest*.
- 犯: 犯错误 *make a mistake*, 犯罪 *commit a crime*, 犯规 *commit a foul* (sport), 犯病 *have a relapse*.
- 高: 高价 *a high price*, 高楼 *a tall building* (or *a high-rise*), 高烧 *a high temperature* (BrE) / *a high fever* (AmE).

The reverse also happens: English *heavy* covers what Chinese expresses with 大 (rain), 重 (a heavy box), 繁忙 (heavy traffic) and 很多 (heavy losses). So the safe question is never "what is the English for 大?" but "which adjective does English use with *rain*?". The chapter [[collocation-and-register/false-friends-chinglish]] looks at the most frequent Chinese-based combinations one by one.
:::

::: example Correcting Liu Yang's email {#ex-email}
Correct the collocations in Liu Yang's email at the start of the chapter, and say for each what kind of collocation was wrong.
::: solution
> *Dear Dr Evans, I am sorry that I could not attend the seminar yesterday. There was very **heavy** rain in the morning and my bus was cancelled. I know I **made** a big mistake by not telling you **beforehand**. I will do my best to catch up **on** the reading. Best regards, Liu Yang*

- *a very strong rain* → *very heavy rain*: an **adjective + noun** collocation (the base is *rain*; its intensity adjectives are *heavy*, *light*, *torrential*). Note also that *rain* is normally uncountable here: *heavy rain*, not *a heavy rain* (though *a heavy rain* occurs, especially in American English, for a single shower).
- *did a big mistake* → *made a big mistake*: a **verb + noun** collocation (*make a mistake*, *make an error*).
- *catch up the reading* → *catch up on the reading*: a **grammatical** collocation (*catch up on* + something you have fallen behind with; BrE also *catch up with*).
- *telling you before* → *telling you beforehand* (or *in advance*): *before* as an adverb needs a reference point (*the day before*); *beforehand* or *in advance* is the usual way to say ‘before the event’. This one is not a collocation error but a word-choice error, and it shows that not every unnatural phrase is a collocation.

The corrected email still sounds like Liu Yang, but nothing in it now distracts the reader from what she is saying.
:::
:::

## Learning collocations

Because collocations cannot be worked out, they have to be met, noticed and remembered. Research on vocabulary learning (summarised in Nation 2013) suggests that words met in context many times are learned gradually, and that deliberate attention speeds the process; the same holds for word combinations. Three habits help.

::: theorem How to learn a collocation {#rule-learn}
1. **Notice the base and its partners.** When you read or listen, look for the verb, adjective or preposition that comes with a noun you know: not just *decision*, but *make a decision*, *reach a decision*, *a difficult decision*, *the decision to* + verb.
2. **Record the whole combination**, with an example and, if relevant, its register or variety: *take a decision* (mainly BrE, formal); *make a decision* (both).
3. **Group collocations by base**, not by verb: one page or card for *decision*, one for *problem*, one for *rain*. A grid of bases and collocators also works for a set of near-synonyms (*strong*, *powerful*; *big*, *large*, *great*).
4. **Check before you rely on a combination** you have constructed yourself — in a learner's dictionary, a collocation dictionary or a corpus ([[collocation-and-register/using-a-corpus]]).
:::

::: example A collocation grid {#ex-grid}
Make a grid for *big*, *large* and *great* with the nouns *amount*, *deal*, *mistake*, *number*, *success*, *surprise*, *size*. Mark each combination as normal (✓), possible but less usual (?) or not used (✗), and check your grid in a learner's dictionary or a corpus.
::: solution
A grid based on the entries for these adjectives in learner's dictionaries; your own corpus checks may show slightly different preferences:

| | *big* | *large* | *great* |
|---|---|---|---|
| *amount* | ? (informal) | ✓ | ✓ (*a great amount of*, more formal) |
| *deal* (‘a lot’) | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ (*a great deal of*) |
| *mistake* | ✓ | ✗ | ? (*a great mistake*, rather formal or old-fashioned) |
| *number* | ? (informal) | ✓ | ✓ |
| *success* | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ |
| *surprise* | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ |
| *size* | ✓ (*a big size*, informal, of clothes) | ✓ | ✗ |

The grid shows the pattern the rule predicts: *large* goes mainly with quantities and sizes; *great* with abstract nouns and in fixed phrases; *big* is the general, rather informal word, especially in speech. *A big deal* exists, but it is a different, informal phrase meaning ‘something important’ (*It's no big deal*). Notice how many cells need a comment: a grid like this is worth making for any group of near-synonyms you use often.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which of these is the best way to record the collocation in *She paid a lot of attention to detail*?
- [ ] *pay* = 付
- [ ] *attention* = 注意
- [x] *pay attention to* + noun (‘give attention to’); also *pay a visit*, *pay a compliment*
- [ ] *a lot of* = 很多
::: solution
The useful unit is the whole combination with its grammar: *pay attention to* + noun or *-ing*. Recording only *pay* = 付 would suggest that *attention* is being bought; recording only the noun misses the verb, which is the part learners get wrong (✗ *give attention to* is possible but less usual; ✗ *put attention on* is a translation of 把注意力放在). Adding other nouns that share *pay* in this sense (*a visit*, *a compliment*, *tribute*) helps to remember the special sense.
:::
:::

::: history
The study of collocation in language teaching began before it became a topic in linguistics. Harold Palmer, working at the Institute for Research in English Teaching in Tokyo, compiled his *Second Interim Report on English Collocations* (1933), and A. S. Hornby carried the attention to word combinations into his learner's dictionary. J. R. Firth gave *collocation* its technical sense in linguistics in papers collected in 1957, and Michael Halliday's "Lexis as a linguistic level" (1966) proposed studying lexis through collocation, with the *strong*/*powerful* example. John Sinclair and his colleagues began to study collocation with computers in the 1960s, and his *Corpus, Concordance, Collocation* (1991) and the COBUILD dictionary (1987) put corpus evidence at the centre of the description of words. In the 1980s and 1990s specialised dictionaries appeared (*BBI*, 1986), and learner corpora from the 1990s onward showed how central collocation is to advanced learning.
:::

[[collocation-and-register/using-a-corpus]] shows how to check a combination in freely available corpora and dictionaries, and what association scores such as MI and the t-score measure. [[collocation-and-register/verb-noun-adjective-noun]] teaches the high-frequency verbs *make*, *do*, *take*, *have* and *give* and common adjective–noun collocations. Idioms, the fixed end of the scale, are the subject of [[collocation-and-register/idioms]], and the colouring that words take from their company is developed in [[collocation-and-register/connotation]]. For the grammatical side — which preposition follows a verb or adjective — see [[verb-patterns/prepositional-verbs]].

::: summary
- A **collocation** is a conventional combination of words that co-occur more often than chance: *heavy rain*, *make a mistake*, *pay attention*, *highly unlikely*.
- Collocations are **conventional**, not logical: near-synonyms have different partners (*strong tea*, *a powerful engine*), and each language makes its own choices (大雨 → *heavy rain*).
- Combinations range from **free combinations** (*buy a car*) through **restricted collocations** (*commit a crime*, *shrug your shoulders*) to **idioms** (*kick the bucket*).
- The restriction usually runs from the **base** (often the noun) to the **collocator**: start from the noun and look for its verb or adjective.
- Kinds include verb + noun, adjective + noun, noun + verb, noun + *of* + noun, adverb + adjective and grammatical collocations; **colligation** is a word's grammatical company and **semantic prosody** the colouring it takes from its collocates (*cause problems*).
- Learner corpora show that advanced learners' errors are often errors of combination, especially with general verbs, and often reflect the first language.
- Learn collocations by noticing, recording whole combinations grouped by base, and checking combinations you construct yourself.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Find the collocates {level=1}
Underline (or list) the collocations in these sentences, and say which word is the base.

1. *I need to make a phone call.*
2. *She has a strong accent.*
3. *The alarm went off at six.*
4. *It's highly unlikely to rain.*
5. *He kept his promise.*
::: solution
1. *make a phone call* — base *phone call*, collocator *make*.
2. *a strong accent* — base *accent*, collocator *strong*.
3. *the alarm went off* — base *alarm*, collocator *go off* (noun + verb).
4. *highly unlikely* — base *unlikely*, collocator *highly* (adverb + adjective).
5. *keep a promise* — base *promise*, collocator *keep* (also *break a promise*, *make a promise*).
:::
:::

::: exercise Strong or powerful? {level=1}
Complete each phrase with *strong* or *powerful*. If both are possible, say so.

1. *a ___ cup of coffee*
2. *a ___ computer*
3. *a ___ argument*
4. *a ___ accent*
5. *a ___ engine*
6. *a ___ smell of gas*
::: solution
1. *strong*. 2. *powerful*. 3. both (*a strong argument* is more frequent; *a powerful argument* stresses its effect on the listener). 4. *strong*. 5. *powerful*. 6. both (*strong* is more frequent).
:::
:::

::: exercise Which category? {level=1}
Label each combination F (free combination), C (collocation) or I (idiom).

1. *sell a house*
2. *foot the bill*
3. *let the cat out of the bag*
4. *a narrow escape*
5. *eat an apple*
6. *meet a deadline*
::: solution
1. F. 2. C (*foot* means ‘pay’ only with *the bill*; some dictionaries call it an idiom, because the verb is so restricted). 3. I (‘reveal a secret’). 4. C (*narrow* ‘only just successful’ goes with *escape*, *victory*, *defeat*, *majority*). 5. F. 6. C (*meet* ‘succeed in doing something by the required time’ goes with *a deadline*, *a target*, *a need*, *a demand*).

Answers on the boundary (2) are reasonable either way, if the reason is given: the categories are points on a scale.
:::
:::

::: exercise Correct the adjective {level=2}
Each sentence contains one adjective + noun combination that is not natural. Correct it.

1. *There was a strong snow last night.*
2. *I have a big headache.*
3. *She is a heavy coffee lover.*
4. *We had a high discussion about the plan.*
5. *He has a thick American accent.*
::: solution
1. *heavy snow* (also *a heavy snowfall*).
2. *a bad* (*terrible*, *splitting*) *headache*. *A big headache* is used in informal English, but mainly figuratively (‘a big problem’: *The new rules are a big headache for small shops*).
3. *a big coffee lover* or *a heavy coffee drinker*. *Heavy* goes with the person who consumes (*a heavy smoker*, *a heavy drinker*), not with *lover*.
4. *a long* (*serious*, *heated*, *lively*) *discussion*. *High* does not collocate with *discussion* (perhaps from 高层讨论, which is *a high-level discussion*, a different meaning).
5. *a strong* (or *a broad*, mainly BrE) *American accent*. *A thick accent* is used, especially in AmE, so this one is borderline; *strong* is the safest choice.
:::
:::

::: exercise Same Chinese word, different English {level=2}
Translate the bold phrases. The same Chinese word appears in each group, but the English collocator changes.

1. **浓茶** — **浓雾** — **浓妆**
2. **犯错误** — **犯罪**
3. **大风** — **大雨** — **大声说话**
::: solution
1. *strong tea* — *thick* (or *dense*) *fog* — *heavy make-up*.
2. *make a mistake* — *commit a crime*.
3. *a strong wind* (also *high winds*, in weather reports) — *heavy rain* — *speak loudly* (or *in a loud voice*).

For each, the English collocator is chosen by the noun (the base), not by the Chinese word.

::: exercise Prosody {level=2}
Complete each sentence with a suitable verb: *cause*, *bring about*, *set in*, *lead to*. Use each once.

1. *Changes in the law could ___ better working conditions.*
2. *Night was falling, and the cold began to ___.*
3. *Smoking can ___ serious illness.*
4. *Careful planning will ___ fewer delays.*
::: solution
1. *bring about* (a positive change; *lead to* also possible).
2. *set in* (an unpleasant state beginning).
3. *cause* (an unwelcome result).
4. *lead to* (neutral; *bring about* is also possible; *cause fewer delays* is grammatical but sounds odd because of the negative prosody of *cause*).
:::
:::

::: exercise Edit a paragraph {level=3}
Wang Lei wrote this paragraph about his gap year. The grammar is correct, but it contains seven unnatural combinations. Find and correct them.

> *Last year I made a lot of travelling in Europe. In Spain I learned a strong lesson: I lost my wallet and had to borrow money from strangers. It was a heavy experience, but people showed me big kindness. In Italy I did a lot of photos and tasted many delicious foods. By the end of the year I had grown up my confidence.*
::: hint
Look at each verb + noun and adjective + noun pair. Ask: which verb or adjective does English use with this noun?
:::
::: solution
> *Last year I **did a lot of travelling** (or **travelled a lot**) in Europe. In Spain I **learned a hard** (or **valuable**) **lesson**: I lost my wallet and had to borrow money from strangers. It was a **difficult** (or **tough**) **experience**, but people showed me **great kindness**. In Italy I **took a lot of photos** and **tried a lot of delicious food**. By the end of the year I had **built up** (or **gained**) **confidence**.*

1. *made a lot of travelling* → *did a lot of travelling* (*do* + *-ing* nouns of activity).
2. *a strong lesson* → *a hard* / *valuable* / *painful lesson*.
3. *a heavy experience* → *a difficult* / *tough* / *upsetting experience* (perhaps from 沉重; *heavy* is used of *heart*, *loss*, *responsibility*, but not of experiences).
4. *big kindness* → *great kindness* (*great* with abstract nouns of feeling and quality).
5. *did a lot of photos* → *took a lot of photos* (BrE and AmE; BrE also *took a lot of pictures*).
6. *tasted many delicious foods* → *tried a lot of delicious food* (*food* is usually uncountable; *taste* is possible, but *try* is the usual verb for eating something for the first time).
7. *grown up my confidence* → *built up* / *gained confidence*: *grow up* is intransitive and is used of people.
:::
:::

::: exercise Your own collocation page {level=3}
Choose a noun that you use often in your studies or work (for example *research*, *problem*, *data*, *customer*, *project*). Collect at least twelve collocations for it from a learner's dictionary, a collocation dictionary or a corpus: verbs before it, adjectives before it, verbs after it, and prepositions. Record each with a short example sentence of your own, and mark any that are formal, informal, British or American.
::: solution
Answers will differ. A model for *problem*:

- verbs + *problem*: *cause a problem*, *solve a problem*, *deal with a problem*, *tackle a problem* (more formal, active), *face a problem*, *raise a problem* (mention it), *overcome a problem*.
- adjectives + *problem*: *a serious problem*, *a major problem*, *a minor problem*, *a growing problem*, *an ongoing problem*, *a common problem*.
- *problem* + verb: *the problem arises* (formal), *the problem lies in* … (formal), *the problem gets worse*.
- prepositions: *a problem with* something (*a problem with the printer*), *the problem of* + noun or *-ing* (*the problem of finding housing*).

Examples: *Rising rents are a growing problem in big cities. — The problem lies in the design, not the materials.*

Check: every combination should come from a source, not from translation; *solve a problem* and *deal with a problem* differ (*deal with* does not say that the problem disappears).
:::
:::
