A lecture on Thursday has been cancelled because the lecturer is ill. Here is the same news in three texts.

> **A.** *hey, no lecture thurs btw — dr k's off sick. see u in the lab?*

> **B.** *Please note that the lecture scheduled for Thursday 14 March has been cancelled owing to staff illness. A replacement session will be arranged in due course, and students will be notified by email.*

> **C.** *OK, everyone, before you go — just to let you know, there's no lecture on Thursday. Dr Kumar isn't well, I'm afraid. We'll sort out another time and I'll email you about it, all right?*

A is a message between classmates; B is a notice from the department office; C is a teaching assistant speaking at the end of a seminar. The information is almost identical, but almost every choice of words is different: *no lecture* / *the lecture … has been cancelled*; *off sick* / *staff illness* / *isn't well*; *see u* / *will be notified* / *I'll email you*. None of the three is "better English" than the others. Each fits its situation, and each would be odd in another: imagine the department office posting text A, or a classmate texting B.

This variation according to situation is called **register**. Earlier chapters met it in particular cases: phrasal verbs against one-word verbs ([[collocation-and-register/phrasal-verbs-register]]), idioms in conversation but not in essays ([[collocation-and-register/idioms]]). This chapter gives the general picture: what register is, how to describe the situation behind a text, how formal, neutral and informal vocabulary differ, how speech and writing differ, and how to keep the register of your own texts consistent.

## What register is

::: definition Register {#def-register}
A **register** is a variety of a language associated with a particular situation of use — a kind of activity, a relationship between the people involved, and a medium — together with the vocabulary and grammar typical of that situation. Conversation, academic prose, news reporting, legal contracts, recipes and text messages are registers. Register is variation according to **use**; it differs from **dialect**, which is variation according to the **user** (where a speaker is from, which social group they belong to).
:::

The distinction between use and user goes back to Halliday, McIntosh and Strevens (1964). Everyone speaks a dialect (standard English is one), and everyone moves between registers many times a day. A Glaswegian lawyer and a Texan lawyer speak different dialects, but when they write contracts they use the same register.

::: definition Field, tenor and mode {#def-field-tenor-mode}
Michael Halliday described the situation behind a register in three dimensions:

- **Field** — what is going on: the activity and the topic (a lecture on chemistry, a conversation about football, a contract for renting a flat).
- **Tenor** — who is taking part, and the relationship between them: their relative status or power, how well they know each other, and their feelings (friends, a student and a professor, a company and its customers).
- **Mode** — the part language plays in the situation: spoken or written, spontaneous or planned, face to face or at a distance, and whether language is the whole activity (an essay) or accompanies another one (instructions while cooking).
:::

Each dimension shows itself in particular choices of language. Field shows most clearly in vocabulary: *lecture*, *seminar*, *lab* in the texts above; *enzyme*, *catalyst* in a chemistry lecture. Tenor shows in formality, forms of address and politeness: *hey* and *dr k* against *students will be notified*. Mode shows in features of speech or writing: *OK, everyone*, *I'm afraid*, *all right?* in the spoken text C; the passive and the long noun phrase *the lecture scheduled for Thursday 14 March* in the written text B.

::: example Describing three texts {#ex-describe}
Describe the field, tenor and mode of texts A, B and C at the start of the chapter, and give two language features of each that show it.
::: solution
| | A (message) | B (notice) | C (spoken announcement) |
|---|---|---|---|
| field | university life: a cancelled lecture | the same, as an administrative matter | the same |
| tenor | classmates, equal, close | institution to students, impersonal | teaching assistant to students, friendly but with some authority |
| mode | written, quick, informal (chat) | written, planned, public | spoken, spontaneous, face to face |
| features | abbreviations (*thurs*, *btw*, *u*), no capitals, *off sick* | passives (*has been cancelled*, *will be notified*), formal phrases (*owing to*, *in due course*) | discourse markers (*OK*, *just to let you know*, *all right?*), *I'm afraid*, phrasal verb *sort out*, contractions |

The field is the same in all three; the differences come from tenor and mode. That is typical: most of the variation a learner has to manage in daily life is variation of tenor and mode around a familiar field.
:::
:::

## Formal, neutral and informal vocabulary

Most English words are **neutral**: they can be used in almost any register (*house*, *water*, *walk*, *important*, *quickly*). Some are marked as **formal** or **informal**, and learner's dictionaries label them.

::: theorem Formal and informal vocabulary {#rule-formality}
1. **Formal** vocabulary tends to be longer and of Latin or French origin, and includes many nouns made from verbs (*purchase*, *obtain*, *require*, *commence*, *assistance*, *sufficient*, *numerous*, *prior to*, *regarding*).
2. **Informal** vocabulary includes phrasal verbs (*find out*, *put off*), short native words, clippings (*uni*, *info*, *fridge*, *ad*), vague words (*stuff*, *things*, *sort of*, *and so on*), intensifiers (*really*, *so*, *totally*), and slang.
3. Most meanings can be expressed at more than one level, so a writer chooses a **level** and keeps to it.
:::

| informal | neutral | formal |
|---|---|---|
| *kids* | *children* | *offspring* (biology, law) |
| *buy*, *get* | *buy* | *purchase* |
| *get* | *get*, *receive* | *obtain*, *receive* |
| *need* | *need* | *require* |
| *help* | *help* | *assist* |
| *start* | *start*, *begin* | *commence* |
| *loads of*, *lots of* | *a lot of*, *many* | *numerous*, *a large number of* |
| *enough* | *enough* | *sufficient* |
| *about*, *re* | *about* | *regarding*, *concerning* |
| *before* | *before* | *prior to* |
| *so* | *so* | *therefore*, *thus* |
| *anyway* | *anyway* | *nevertheless*, *in any case* |

::: proof
The pattern has a historical cause. After 1066, French was for some centuries the language of the court, the law and administration, and Latin the language of the church and scholarship, and English took thousands of words from both. The borrowed words kept their association with these institutions, while the native words stayed in everyday use ([[history-of-english/norman-french]]). The result is the layered vocabulary of the table: *start* / *begin* (both native) beside *commence* (French). Modern corpus work confirms the effect in use: Biber et al. (1999) found nouns, and especially nouns ending in *-tion*, *-ment* and *-ity*, far more frequent in academic prose than in conversation. Two cautions are needed. First, Latinate does not mean formal in every case: *very*, *people*, *use*, *question* and *problem* are French or Latin in origin and entirely neutral. Second, formal is not the same as better: a formal word in the wrong place sounds pompous (*I shall commence my dinner*).
:::

::: warning The thesaurus effect
Learners who want to sound sophisticated sometimes replace every neutral word with a formal synonym from a thesaurus or a translation app: ✗ *I endeavour to ameliorate my English by perusing numerous periodicals.* Every word exists, but the sentence sounds absurd, because the neutral version is what an educated writer would say: ✓ *I'm trying to improve my English by reading a lot of magazines.* Formal words belong where the situation is formal, and even there, the plain word is often best ([[style-and-rhetoric/clarity]]).
:::

::: quiz
Which word is the formal equivalent of *enough* in *We don't have enough data*?
- [ ] *plenty*
- [x] *sufficient*
- [ ] *loads of*
- [ ] *ample* (in every context)
::: solution
*Sufficient* is the usual formal equivalent: *We do not have sufficient data.* *Ample* means ‘more than enough’, so it changes the meaning; *plenty* and *loads of* also mean ‘more than enough’, and *loads of* is informal.
:::
:::

### Dictionary labels

Learner's dictionaries label words and senses that are not neutral. The labels differ a little between dictionaries, but these are the usual ones:

- **formal** — used in serious or official contexts (*commence*, *reside*);
- **informal** — used between friends, in conversation and informal writing (*kid*, *awesome*);
- **slang** — very informal, often used by particular groups, and often short-lived;
- **offensive**, **taboo**, **vulgar** — likely to upset people; learners should recognise these words but not use them;
- **literary** — used in literature, not in ordinary speech or writing (*steed*, *slumber*);
- **old-fashioned**, **dated** — no longer usual (*wireless* for *radio*);
- **technical**, **specialised**, or a subject label (*law*, *medical*) — used by specialists;
- **BrE**, **AmE**, **AustralE** and other variety labels — typical of one variety.

When a dictionary gives no label, the word or sense is treated as neutral. Labels are one of the most useful parts of an entry and one of the least read ([[core-vocabulary/using-a-dictionary]]).

## Spoken and written registers

Mode deserves its own section, because the differences between conversation and formal writing are very large — much larger than learners who have studied English mainly from books expect.

::: theorem Features of conversation and of academic writing {#rule-mode}
Compared with academic writing, **conversation** has many more personal pronouns (*I*, *you*), contractions, discourse markers (*well*, *you know*, *I mean*), vague expressions (*and stuff*, *or something*), questions, ellipsis (*Sounds good*, *Coming?*) and short clauses joined by *and*, *but* and *so*.

Compared with conversation, **academic writing** has many more nouns, nominalisations (*the investigation of*, *an increase in*), adjectives before nouns, prepositional phrases, passives, and long noun phrases, with ideas packed into fewer clauses.
:::

::: proof
Douglas Biber's *Variation across Speech and Writing* (1988) compared many registers on many linguistic features at once and found that features cluster: texts with many pronouns and contractions also tend to have many discourse markers and questions, and few nouns and prepositional phrases. Biber called the main contrast "involved" against "informational" production. The *Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English* (Biber et al. 1999) documented the same contrast for conversation and academic prose, feature by feature. The reasons are functional. Conversation is produced in real time, between people who share a situation, so it favours short units, reference to *I* and *you*, and markers that manage the interaction; academic writing is planned and edited, for distant readers, and its aim is to pack and organise information, which nouns and noun phrases do efficiently. The contrast is a cline, not two boxes: a lecture, a news broadcast and a personal email lie between the extremes.
:::

::: example From speech to writing {#ex-speech-writing}
A student explains her project result in a seminar:

> *So basically what we found was that, um, when people sleep less, they kind of make more mistakes the next day — like, quite a lot more — and that was true for everybody, didn't matter how old they were.*

Rewrite the result as one sentence for the results section of a report.
::: solution
> *Reduced sleep was associated with a substantial increase in errors the following day, regardless of participants' age.*

What changed:

- The clause *when people sleep less* became the noun phrase *reduced sleep*; *they make more mistakes* became *an increase in errors* (nominalisation).
- Discourse markers and fillers (*so*, *basically*, *um*, *like*, *kind of*) disappeared.
- The vague intensifier *quite a lot more* became *substantial*; in a real report it would be replaced by the figure.
- *Didn't matter how old they were* became *regardless of participants' age*.
- *What we found was that* disappeared: the report states the finding directly. *Was associated with* is a cautious verb that does not claim cause ([[modality/hedging-boosting]]).

The written sentence has seventeen words against thirty-eight, and one clause against six or seven. That is what "informational" packing means.
:::
:::

### New media

Email, text messages, chat and social media posts are written, but many of them are produced quickly, between people who know each other, and expect a reply. They therefore mix features of both modes: contractions, discourse markers and abbreviations (*btw*, *thx*) in a written medium. Their register depends, as always, on tenor and purpose: an email to a professor or an employer is a formal letter in electronic form; a chat with friends is a written conversation. Writing to people in different relationships is treated in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/emails-messages]] and [[pragmatics/writing-to-people]].

## Tenor: relationships in the words

Tenor shows in the vocabulary of formality, but also in forms of address, in the directness of requests, and in how much the writer hedges.

| choice | more distant or formal | closer or more informal |
|---|---|---|
| greeting | *Dear Professor Grant*, | *Hi Tom*, / *Hey* |
| request | *I would be grateful if you could …* / *Could you possibly …?* | *Can you …?* / *Could you …?* |
| thanks | *Thank you very much for your help.* | *Thanks a lot!* / *Cheers* (BrE) |
| closing | *Kind regards*, / *Best wishes*, / *Yours sincerely* (BrE letters) | *See you*, / *Talk soon* / *x* (close friends, BrE) |

Conventions for address differ between institutions, departments and even individual teachers: in some, staff are called by their first names from the start; in others, *Professor* or *Dr* + surname is expected, at least in a first email. The safe rule for a first contact is to use the title and surname the person uses themselves, and to follow their lead in replies. Politeness strategies in general are the subject of [[pragmatics/politeness-face]].

::: example An email that mixes registers {#ex-email}
Zhang Yu sends this email to a professor she has never met. Identify the register problems and rewrite it.

> *Hey Professor! I wanna ask whether I could possibly attend your course on urban planning, as I am exceedingly interested in this field. Plz let me know asap. Thx!!! Zhang Yu*
::: solution
Problems:

- The register is **mixed**: very informal items (*Hey*, *wanna*, *Plz*, *asap*, *Thx!!!*) sit beside formal or even over-formal ones (*whether I could possibly attend*, *exceedingly*). Mixing is the most noticeable fault, because each part makes the other look wrong.
- The tenor is wrong for a first email to a professor: no title and surname, an informal greeting, and a demand (*let me know asap*) where a request is expected.
- *Exceedingly* is the thesaurus effect; *very* or *particularly* is natural.

Rewrite:

> *Dear Professor Okonkwo,*
>
> *I am a second-year student in the Department of Geography, and I am writing to ask whether it would be possible for me to attend your course on urban planning next term. I am particularly interested in the module on housing policy.*
>
> *I would be grateful if you could let me know whether there are places available.*
>
> *Kind regards,*
> *Zhang Yu*

The new version is consistently neutral to formal: polite, but not stiff.
:::
:::

## Keeping register consistent

::: theorem Keep the register of a text consistent {#rule-consistent}
Once a text has a register, every word should fit it. A word from a different register stands out — a **register clash** — and the reader notices the word instead of the message. Check especially: informal words in formal texts (*a lot of*, *stuff*, *get*, *really*, contractions) and formal words in informal ones (*commence*, *thus*, *regarding*).
:::

::: proof
Readers build expectations from the first lines of a text and interpret everything after that against them. A single informal word in a report (*The results were pretty good*) suggests carelessness or a change of attitude; a single formal word in a chat (*I shall*) suggests irony or anger. Skilled writers do mix registers on purpose — a journalist may drop a colloquial phrase into a serious article for effect, and comedy often depends on register clashes — but the effect works only because readers notice the clash. For a learner, the first goal is control: being able to keep a text in one register, so that any later mixing is a choice.
:::

::: example Find the clash {#ex-clash}
Each sentence comes from a text written in a formal register. Find the word or phrase that clashes, and replace it.

1. *The committee has received a ton of complaints about the new parking rules.*
2. *Participants were asked to fill in a questionnaire, and loads of them didn't finish it.*
3. *The data indicate that the policy has had a really big impact on employment.*
4. *In conclusion, globalisation has both advantages and disadvantages, and stuff like that.*
::: solution
1. *a ton of* → *a large number of* (or *numerous*).
2. *loads of them didn't finish it* → *many did not complete it*. (*Fill in* is acceptable in most formal writing; *complete* is an alternative.)
3. *a really big impact* → *a significant* (*substantial*, *considerable*) *impact*. Use *significant* only for statistical significance in a scientific report.
4. *and stuff like that* → delete. Vague endings belong to conversation; in an essay, either list the advantages and disadvantages or say *which are discussed above*.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese distinguishes 书面语 and 口语, and Chinese learners use the distinction readily: 购买 against 买, 予以 against 给, 进行调查 against 查一查. The idea transfers well, but three differences matter.

First, the **sources** of formality differ. Chinese formal style draws on Classical Chinese (之, 其, 乃, 于) and on four-character expressions; English formal style draws on Latin and French vocabulary and on nominalisation. So the English formal equivalent of a 书面语 expression is not necessarily a long word: 鉴于 is *given* or *in view of*; 予以 is often simply a verb (予以批准 *approve*).

Second, Chinese **formal writing values ornament** — parallelism, 成语, balanced four-character phrases — more than English formal writing does. English academic and official writing values plainness and directness, so a literal rendering of a well-written Chinese paragraph can sound inflated in English.

Third, **tenor** is marked differently. Chinese has the respectful pronoun 您 and titles such as 王老师; English has no respectful *you*, and *Teacher Wang* is not an English form of address. In English, distance and respect are shown by titles and surnames (*Dr Wang*, *Professor Wang*, *Ms Wang*), by indirect requests (*Could you possibly …?*, *I was wondering if …*) and by hedges. In British schools, pupils traditionally call teachers *Sir* or *Miss*; at university, *Dr* or *Professor* + surname, or first names if invited.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence keeps a **consistent neutral-to-formal** register for a job application?
- [ ] *I'm super keen on this job and I reckon I'd be great at it.*
- [ ] *I wish to proffer my candidacy, as I am exceedingly enthusiastic.*
- [x] *I am very interested in this position and believe I have the skills it requires.*
- [ ] *I am very interested in this job, and I'd totally love to work with you guys.*
::: solution
The third sentence is consistently neutral to formal. The first is informal throughout (*super keen*, *reckon*); the second is over-formal (*proffer my candidacy*, *exceedingly*); the fourth starts in the right register and then slips into informal language (*totally*, *you guys*) — a register clash.
:::
:::

::: history
The idea that language varies with the situation is ancient — classical rhetoric distinguished high, middle and low styles — but its modern study began in the 1960s. Martin Joos's short book *The Five Clocks* described five styles of American English, from "frozen" to "intimate". Halliday, McIntosh and Strevens's *The Linguistic Sciences and Language Teaching* (1964) introduced the term *register* in its modern sense, with the dimensions that became field, tenor and mode. David Crystal and Derek Davy's *Investigating English Style* (1969) described the language of conversation, commentary, religion, news and law. Douglas Biber's *Variation across Speech and Writing* (1988) showed by computer analysis how linguistic features cluster across registers, and the *Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English* (1999) applied the comparison to the whole of English grammar.
:::

## Where this leads

Near-synonyms that differ in feeling as well as register (*slim*, *skinny*; *famous*, *notorious*) are the subject of the next chapter, [[collocation-and-register/connotation]]. The register of academic writing is developed in [[collocation-and-register/academic-vocabulary]] and [[academic-writing/academic-style]], and written style more generally in [[style-and-rhetoric/word-choice]]. Choices of politeness and directness are part of [[pragmatics/politeness-face]] and [[pragmatics/requests-refusals]]. Clippings such as *uni* and *info*, which mark informal register, are explained in [[word-formation/shortening]].

::: summary
- A **register** is a variety of language used in a particular situation, with its own typical vocabulary and grammar; it is variation by **use**, while dialect is variation by **user**.
- The situation can be described by **field** (activity and topic), **tenor** (participants and relationship) and **mode** (spoken or written, planned or spontaneous).
- Most words are **neutral**; **formal** words are often Latinate and nominal (*purchase*, *require*, *prior to*), **informal** ones include phrasal verbs, clippings, vague words and slang. Dictionaries label them.
- **Conversation** is full of pronouns, contractions, discourse markers and short clauses; **academic writing** of nouns, nominalisations, passives and long noun phrases (Biber 1988; Biber et al. 1999).
- **Tenor** shows in address forms, requests, thanks and closings; follow the other person's lead in forms of address.
- Keep the register of a text **consistent**; avoid the **thesaurus effect** and register clashes.
- 书面语 and 口语 help to understand register, but the sources of English formality (Latinate words, nominalisation) and its preference for plainness differ from Chinese.
:::

::: exercise Edit for consistency {level=3}
This paragraph is from a student's essay. Rewrite it in a consistent academic register, keeping the ideas.

> *These days loads of people reckon that online shopping is killing off high streets. I think that's kind of true, 'cause lots of shops have shut down. But it's not just online shopping — rents have gone through the roof too, and town centres are a pain to get to by car. So basically it's a bunch of things, not just one.*
::: hint
Look for: informal vocabulary (*loads of*, *reckon*, *a bunch of*), idioms, phrasal verbs with formal equivalents, contractions, vague language, and *I think*.
:::
::: solution
> *It is widely argued that online shopping is causing the decline of town centres* (BrE *high streets*)*. This view is supported to some extent by the large number of shop closures in recent years. However, online shopping is not the only factor: rents have risen sharply, and many town centres are difficult to reach by car. The decline therefore appears to result from a combination of factors rather than a single cause.*

Changes: *These days loads of people reckon* → *It is widely argued*; *killing off* → *causing the decline of*; *I think that's kind of true, 'cause* → *This view is supported to some extent by*; *shut down* → *closures*; *gone through the roof* → *risen sharply*; *a pain to get to* → *difficult to reach*; *So basically it's a bunch of things* → *therefore appears to result from a combination of factors*; contractions removed; *But* → *However*. (Beginning a sentence with *But* is not wrong in English, but *However* is more usual in academic prose.)
:::
:::
