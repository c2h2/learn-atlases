Wu Jing has just joined a design team in Manchester. At her first project meeting, the team leader says:

> *Right, let's get the ball rolling. I know the deadline's tight, but we're all in the same boat, so there's no point reinventing the wheel — we'll use last year's templates. Callum, you've got your hands full with the website, so Wu Jing, could you take the lead on the posters? Great. Let's call it a day there.*

Wu Jing knows every word, and she follows the gist. But *get the ball rolling*, *in the same boat*, *reinvent the wheel*, *have your hands full*, *take the lead* and *call it a day* do not mean what their words say: there is no ball, no boat and no wheel. Each is a fixed expression with a meaning of its own — ‘start’, ‘in the same difficult situation’, ‘waste time making something that already exists’, ‘be very busy’, ‘be in charge’, ‘stop working for now’. What is more, none of them is the kind of idiom she learned at school. Her textbook taught *It's raining cats and dogs*, which she has never heard anyone say.

This chapter is about such **fixed expressions**: idioms, binomials such as *sooner or later*, similes, proverbs and sentence frames. It explains how they differ from ordinary collocations ([[collocation-and-register/what-is-collocation]]), how fixed they really are, how their images can help you remember them, which ones are worth learning, and when using one is a good idea and when it is not.

## What an idiom is

::: definition Idiom {#def-idiom}
An **idiom** is a fixed expression of two or more words whose meaning cannot be worked out from the meanings of its parts: *spill the beans* ‘reveal a secret’, *break the ice* ‘make people feel relaxed at the start of a meeting’, *under the weather* ‘slightly ill’, *call it a day* ‘stop working’. An idiom is learned and used as a single unit of meaning, like a word.
:::

Idioms differ in how far their meaning can be guessed. Linguists describe this as **transparency**:

- **Transparent** idioms can be understood from their image once you know they are idioms: *reinvent the wheel*, *in the same boat*, *the tip of the iceberg*.
- **Semi-transparent** idioms have an image that makes sense once the meaning is explained: *break the ice* (the ice is the coldness between strangers), *spill the beans* (the beans are the secret).
- **Opaque** idioms have no image a modern speaker can see: *kick the bucket* ‘die’, *by and large* ‘in general’, *red herring* ‘something that distracts attention’. Their origins may be known to historians, but not to ordinary speakers.

::: definition Fixed expression {#def-fixed}
**Fixed expression** is the wider term for any conventional multi-word unit that speakers store and reproduce as a whole. Besides idioms, it includes:

- **binomials** and **trinomials**: pairs or triples joined by *and* or *or*, in a fixed order: *sooner or later*, *black and white*, *safe and sound*, *rules and regulations*, *ladies and gentlemen*, *ready, willing and able*;
- **similes**: *as light as a feather*, *as cold as ice*, *like a fish out of water*;
- **proverbs** and sayings: *Practice makes perfect*, *Don't count your chickens before they're hatched*;
- **sentence frames** and discourse formulas: *The thing is, …*, *It's not that …, it's just that …*, *The more …, the more …*, *Fair enough*, *Tell me about it* (‘I agree, I have had the same experience’).
:::

Rosamund Moon's corpus-based study *Fixed Expressions and Idioms in English* (1998) used the term **fixed expressions and idioms** for this whole family, and showed how different its members are in frequency, fixedness and use.

::: quiz
What does *under the weather* mean in *I'm feeling a bit under the weather today*?
- [ ] I'm worried about the weather.
- [x] I'm slightly ill.
- [ ] I've been out in the rain.
- [ ] I'm in a bad mood.
::: solution
*Under the weather* means ‘slightly unwell’. It is a semi-opaque idiom: the connection with weather is not obvious to most speakers today, so it must be learned as a unit. It is informal and very common in conversation, often with *a bit*.
:::
:::

## How frequent are idioms?

Learners often think that idioms are the heart of "real" English, and lists of colourful idioms are popular in textbooks and online. Corpus evidence gives a more sober picture.

::: theorem Most idioms are rare; some fixed expressions are very frequent {#rule-frequency}
Taken one by one, most figurative idioms are **rare** in large corpora, and many idioms found in textbooks (*raining cats and dogs*) are rarer still or sound dated. The fixed expressions that are frequent are mostly the less colourful ones: discourse formulas (*of course*, *in fact*, *at the end of the day*), binomials (*more or less*, *now and then*) and a limited set of everyday idioms (*make sure*, *take the lead*, *keep an eye on*). Learners should therefore **understand** many idioms but **actively use** a selected few, chosen for frequency and usefulness.
:::

::: proof
Moon (1998) counted several thousand fixed expressions and idioms in a corpus of about 18 million words and found that the great majority were infrequent, while a small number — mainly non-figurative expressions with discourse functions — were very frequent. Later corpus studies have supported the general finding. The reason is not that idioms are unimportant, but that each one is specialised: it expresses a particular evaluation of a particular situation (*the tip of the iceberg* is used only about problems that are bigger than they look), so no single idiom is needed often. Together, however, idioms are common, especially in conversation, journalism and fiction, which is why learners must be able to understand them. The textbook favourite *raining cats and dogs* illustrates the opposite problem: it is known to almost everyone but used by few, and a learner who uses it sounds as if speaking from a book.
:::

::: warning Learning idioms from lists
A list of idioms with Chinese translations teaches meaning but not use. It hides three facts a learner needs: how common the idiom is (*call it a day* is common; *a storm in a teacup* is BrE and much less common, AmE has *a tempest in a teapot*), its register (most idioms are informal), and its exact form (✗ *break the ices*, ✗ *spill the bean*, ✗ *in a same boat*). Learn idioms that you have met in real use, from reading, listening or a learner's dictionary, and copy the form exactly.
:::

## How fixed is fixed?

Idioms are called fixed, but they are not frozen. Many allow some grammatical change, and writers and speakers sometimes play with them on purpose.

::: theorem Idioms vary in fixedness {#rule-fixedness}
1. **Most idioms allow normal inflection**: tense, person and number (*She broke the ice*, *They've spilled* / BrE *spilt the beans*).
2. **Some allow other changes** — a modifier, the passive or a change of determiner — usually when parts of the idiom correspond to parts of the meaning: in *spill the beans*, *the beans* stands for ‘the secret’, so *Who spilled the beans about the merger?* and even, in journalism, *The beans were finally spilled* are possible.
3. **Others allow almost none**, because the parts have no separate meaning: *kick the bucket* has no passive in its idiomatic sense (✗ *The bucket was kicked*), and its object cannot be changed (✗ *kick the pail*).
4. **Binomials have a fixed order**: *sooner or later* (✗ *later or sooner*), *black and white*, *bread and butter*, *salt and pepper*.
5. **Deliberate variation** is a feature of journalism and humour (*the elephant in the classroom*, *a level playing field for small firms*); it works only when the reader recognises the original, so a learner should master the standard form before playing with it.
:::

::: proof
The linguists Geoffrey Nunberg, Ivan Sag and Thomas Wasow (1994) explained much of this pattern by distinguishing idioms whose parts can be mapped onto parts of the meaning (*spill* = reveal, *the beans* = the secret) from those whose parts cannot (*kick the bucket* as a whole = die). Only the first kind allow the parts to be modified or moved, because only then do the parts have something to refer to. Gibbs and colleagues reached similar conclusions in psychological experiments in the late 1980s. The order of binomials is partly explained by tendencies described by Cooper and Ross (1975): the shorter word usually comes first (*salt and pepper*), the word with the shorter vowel (*this and that*), and the item nearer the speaker or more important first (*here and there*, *now and then*, *men and women*). These are tendencies, not rules, and each binomial must still be learned with its order: *husband and wife* puts the longer word first.
:::

::: example Fixed or flexible? {#ex-fixed}
Which of these variations are acceptable? Explain.

1. *We broke the ice by playing a silly game.*
2. *Their first meeting was icy, but by lunch the ice had been broken.*
3. *My grandfather kicked the bucket last year.*
4. *The bucket was kicked by my grandfather last year.*
5. *She told me sooner or later.*
6. *She'll find out later or sooner.*
::: solution
1. Acceptable: normal past tense.
2. Acceptable: *the ice* stands for the coldness between people, so the passive is possible, and the writer has also extended the image (*icy*). This kind of play is common in written English.
3. Grammatical but **not appropriate**: *kick the bucket* is a very informal, jokey idiom, and using it about a family member's death sounds disrespectful. *My grandfather died* or *passed away* (a gentler euphemism) is usual.
4. Not acceptable in the idiomatic sense: *kick the bucket* has no passive. It could only be read literally.
5. Wrong meaning: *sooner or later* means ‘at some time in the future, certainly’, so it goes with a future: *She'll find out sooner or later.*
6. Not acceptable: the order of a binomial is fixed.
:::
:::

## Why idioms mean what they mean

Opaque idioms must simply be learned. But many idioms are less arbitrary than they seem, because they are built on images that English uses again and again.

::: definition Conceptual metaphor {#def-metaphor}
A **conceptual metaphor** is a pattern of thought in which one area of experience is understood in terms of another, and which shows itself in many expressions at once (Lakoff and Johnson 1980). In English, anger is often talked about as heat in a container (*boil over*, *let off steam*, *hot under the collar*, *blow your top*); ideas as food (*food for thought*, *a half-baked idea*, *swallow a story*, *digest information*); understanding as seeing (*I see what you mean*, *shed light on*, *a clear explanation*, *in the dark*).
:::

::: theorem Learn idioms through their images {#rule-images}
When an idiom has an image, learn the image with it: picture the ice being broken, the beans being spilled, the steam escaping. Where several idioms share an image, learn them as a group. Where the origin is known and memorable (*red herring*, *the bottom line*), it can help; where it is not, do not invent one.
:::

::: proof
Experiments by Frank Boers (2000) and later work by Boers and Lindstromberg found that learners who were shown the images or source domains behind idioms — sailing, sport, cooking, war — remembered the idioms better than learners who were given only the meanings. The explanation is the same as for word parts ([[word-formation/learning-with-word-formation]]): a new item connected to something already known is easier to store and retrieve. The warning is also the same. Popular origin stories for idioms are often false, and an invented story teaches false history; dictionaries of phrase origins say when an origin is unknown.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese learners bring a rich tradition of fixed expressions — 成语, 俗语, 歇后语 — and sometimes the images match English ones closely: 火上浇油 *add fuel to the fire* (or *flames*); 趁热打铁 *strike while the iron is hot*; 隔墙有耳 *walls have ears*; 一石二鸟 *kill two birds with one stone*. The English expression *lose face* is itself a translation from Chinese (丢脸), borrowed in the nineteenth century.

More often, the images differ, or there is no English idiom at all:

- 画蛇添足 — no common English idiom; *over-egg the pudding* (BrE, informal) is close, or simply *overdo it* / *spoil something by adding too much*.
- 对牛弹琴 — no close idiom in everyday use (*cast pearls before swine* is biblical and sounds insulting); usually *It was a waste of time explaining it to him*.
- 胸有成竹 — no idiom with bamboo; *have a clear plan*, *know exactly what you're doing*.
- 马马虎虎 — not an idiom with horses: *so-so*, *not bad*.

There is also a difference of **value**. In Chinese school writing, a well-chosen 成语 is a sign of good style, and teachers reward it. In English academic and formal writing, idioms are usually avoided: they sound informal or clichéd, and the plain word is preferred (*a waste of time*, not *like water off a duck's back*). So translating a 成语 into an English idiom in an essay may make the writing worse, not better. In conversation, journalism and informal writing, idioms are welcome — if they are used exactly.
:::

::: example Translating 成语 {#ex-chengyu}
For each sentence, decide whether an English idiom or a plain paraphrase is better, and write the English.

1. (Chat with a friend) 他这么说简直是火上浇油。
2. (Essay) 这个政策是一把双刃剑。
3. (Email to a colleague) 我们得趁热打铁，今天就把方案发给客户。
4. (Essay) 很多学生学英语时半途而废。
::: solution
1. Idiom works: *Saying that just added fuel to the fire.* Informal conversation, and the images match.
2. *A double-edged sword* exists in English and is used in journalism and some essays: *The policy is a double-edged sword.* But in an academic essay a plain statement is often stronger: *The policy has both benefits and serious drawbacks.* Either is acceptable; the plain version says more precisely what you mean.
3. Idiom possible: *We should strike while the iron is hot and send the proposal to the client today.* In a short work email, a plainer version is just as natural: *We should send the proposal today, while the client is still interested.*
4. Plain paraphrase: *Many students give up learning English halfway* or *… before they reach an advanced level*. There is no common English idiom with the same image (*fall by the wayside* is close but less exact), and an essay does not need one.
:::
:::

## When to use idioms

::: theorem Using idioms appropriately {#rule-use}
1. **Register.** Most idioms are informal or neutral; they belong in conversation, informal writing and journalism. In academic and formal writing, prefer the literal word unless the idiom has become a neutral term (*a vicious circle*, *a rule of thumb*, *the bottom line* in business).
2. **Exactness.** Use an idiom only in its standard form, with its articles, prepositions and number.
3. **Function.** Idioms usually **evaluate** — they comment on a situation more than they describe it — so they fit at points where a comment is natural (a summary, a reaction).
4. **Audience.** Avoid idioms that your listener may not know (in international settings, where English is a lingua franca, idioms often cause misunderstandings), and avoid dated or regional ones.
5. **Moderation.** One well-placed idiom sounds natural; several in a row sound like a parody.
:::

::: example One meeting, three registers {#ex-register}
The team leader's speech at the start of the chapter used six idioms. Rewrite it (a) for an email to an international partner team whose first language is not English, and (b) as one sentence in a formal project report.
::: solution
(a) *Let's start the project now. The deadline is tight for everyone, so we will use last year's templates rather than create new ones. Callum is very busy with the website, so Wu Jing will be in charge of the posters.*

Every idiom has been replaced by its plain meaning (*get the ball rolling* → *start*; *in the same boat* → *for everyone*; *reinvent the wheel* → *create new ones*; *have your hands full* → *be very busy*; *take the lead* → *be in charge*; *call it a day* → omitted). The message is shorter and safer for readers who learned English as a foreign language.

(b) *Owing to the tight deadline, the team reused the previous year's templates; responsibility for the posters was assigned to Wu Jing.*

Formal reports describe; they rarely need the evaluative colour that idioms add.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which context is **least** suitable for *at the end of the day* (‘when everything has been considered’)?
- [ ] A conversation about choosing a university
- [ ] A radio interview with a football manager
- [x] The conclusion of an academic essay
- [ ] An informal blog post
::: solution
*At the end of the day* is a very common spoken fixed expression, and in writing it is often criticised as a cliché. In an academic conclusion, *Ultimately* or *Overall* says the same thing in the expected register. In the other contexts it is natural.
:::
:::

### Binomials and similes

Binomials are among the most useful fixed expressions for learners, because many are frequent and neutral in register: *more or less*, *sooner or later*, *now and then*, *again and again*, *back and forth*, *pros and cons*, *trial and error*, *give and take*, *peace and quiet*, *odds and ends*, *by and large*. Legal and official English has its own set (*terms and conditions*, *null and void*, *goods and chattels*); some go back to the habit of medieval legal language of pairing two words of similar meaning, often one English and one French or Latin, as in *goods and chattels*. Learners meet them in contracts and on websites.

Similes with *as … as* are a different case: many are clichés (*as busy as a bee*, *as cool as a cucumber*), and some are dated. They are good to recognise, but in writing, a fresh comparison or a plain adjective is usually better ([[style-and-rhetoric/figures-of-speech]]).

::: example Complete the binomials {#ex-binomials}
Complete each binomial and give its meaning.

1. *pros and ___*
2. *trial and ___*
3. *___ and quiet*
4. *odds and ___*
5. *___ and large*
6. *back and ___*
::: solution
1. *pros and cons* — advantages and disadvantages.
2. *trial and error* — trying different methods until one works.
3. *peace and quiet* — a calm, quiet situation.
4. *odds and ends* — small, miscellaneous things (BrE also *odds and sods*, very informal).
5. *by and large* — in general, on the whole (opaque; it comes from sailing).
6. *back and forth* — moving in one direction and then the other, repeatedly.

In all six, the order is fixed.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which is the correct form?
- [ ] *We have to wait and to see.*
- [ ] *Sooner and later they'll agree.*
- [x] *By and large, the results were positive.*
- [ ] *There are many cons and pros.*
::: solution
*By and large* ‘on the whole’ is correct and is neutral enough for most writing. The others break the fixed form: *wait and see*, *sooner or later*, *pros and cons*.
:::
:::

::: history
Collections of proverbs and sayings are very old in English, but dictionaries of idioms for learners are a twentieth-century development. Ebenezer Cobham Brewer's *Dictionary of Phrase and Fable* (1870) collected the origins of phrases for general readers. Logan Pearsall Smith's *Words and Idioms* (1925) discussed English idioms as a feature of the language. For learners, the *Oxford Dictionary of Current Idiomatic English*, in two volumes by A. P. Cowie, R. Mackin and I. R. McCaig (1975 and 1983), described the grammar and variability of idioms. George Lakoff and Mark Johnson's *Metaphors We Live By* (1980) gave a new account of why many idioms mean what they mean, and Rosamund Moon's *Fixed Expressions and Idioms in English* (1998) used corpus evidence to show how frequent, how fixed and how varied they really are.
:::

## Where this leads

Idioms are part of the larger family of chunks introduced in [[core-vocabulary/phrases-and-chunks]]. Their use in conversation — for agreeing, sympathising and evaluating — connects with [[pragmatics/irony-understatement]] and [[pragmatics/small-talk-norms]]; their effect in writing, including the problem of clichés, with [[style-and-rhetoric/figures-of-speech]] and [[style-and-rhetoric/word-choice]]. Translating idioms between Chinese and English is treated in [[translation/idioms-culture]]. For register in general, see the next chapter, [[collocation-and-register/register]].

::: summary
- An **idiom** is a fixed expression whose meaning is not the sum of its parts: *spill the beans*, *call it a day*; idioms range from transparent to opaque.
- **Fixed expressions** also include binomials (*sooner or later*), similes, proverbs and sentence frames (*The thing is, …*).
- **Most idioms are individually rare** (Moon 1998); the frequent fixed expressions are mostly plain ones. Understand many idioms; use a selected few.
- Idioms **vary in fixedness**: most inflect normally; some allow modification or the passive when their parts carry meaning (*spill the beans*); others allow almost nothing (*kick the bucket*); **binomials have a fixed order**.
- Many idioms rest on **conceptual metaphors** (anger as heat, ideas as food); learning them by image helps memory (Boers 2000).
- Use idioms mainly in **informal** registers, in their **exact** form, sparingly, and with listeners who will know them; avoid them in academic writing.
- Some 成语 have close English equivalents (火上浇油 *add fuel to the fire*), many do not; a plain paraphrase is often better than a forced idiom.
:::

For each situation, decide whether you would use an idiom, and write what you would say or write.

1. A friend has just told you a long, complicated plan for a trip. You think it is too ambitious.

4. You are sympathising with a classmate who also had three exams in one day.

Possible answers:

1. An idiom fits: *Sounds great, but aren't you biting off more than you can chew?* (‘trying to do too much’). A plain version: *I think it might be too much for one week.*
2. No idiom: *Overall, the evidence suggests that the effects of social media on teenagers are mixed, and depend strongly on how it is used.* (Not *at the end of the day* or *a double-edged sword*.)
3. No idiom: *I'm sorry, but the delivery will be about three days late. It should now arrive on 14 May.* Idioms such as *hit a snag* may not be understood.
4. A conversational formula fits: *Tell me about it! I had three as well.* (*Tell me about it* ‘I know exactly what you mean’.) Or *We're in the same boat.*
