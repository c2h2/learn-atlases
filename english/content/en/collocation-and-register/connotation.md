Sun Qiang has been asked to write a reference for a former colleague who is applying for a new job. He admires her greatly, and he chooses his words carefully from a bilingual dictionary:

> *Ms Chen is a skinny, stubborn woman who is notorious in our office for her cheap lunches and her aggressive way of selling.*

He means: she is slim, she is determined and does not give up, she is well known for bringing simple, inexpensive food to work (which colleagues found charming), and she sells with energy and confidence. What he has written is that she is unattractively thin, unreasonably obstinate, famous for something bad, mean with money, and hostile. Every word has roughly the right meaning — the dictionary did not lie — but every word has the wrong **feeling**. The reference would probably cost Ms Chen the job.

Words carry more than their reference to the world. They also carry attitudes, associations and degrees of strength, and words that a dictionary or a thesaurus groups together can differ sharply in these. This chapter is about those differences: **connotation**, the positive or negative colouring of a word; and the other ways in which **near-synonyms** differ — strength, register, collocation, exact meaning and variety. It ends with the Chinese–English pairs whose connotations do not match.

## Denotation and connotation

::: definition Denotation and connotation {#def-connotation}
The **denotation** of a word is its basic meaning: what it refers to, the part of meaning a definition states. Its **connotation** is the additional meaning it carries — attitudes, feelings and associations shared by speakers of the language. *Slim*, *thin* and *skinny* have nearly the same denotation (‘having little fat on the body’) but different connotations: *slim* is approving, *thin* is neutral, *skinny* is usually disapproving.
:::

Many words fall into sets of three: one positive, one neutral, one negative.

| positive | neutral | negative |
|---|---|---|
| *slim*, *slender* | *thin* | *skinny*, *scrawny* |
| *determined*, *persistent* | *firm* | *stubborn*, *obstinate*, *pig-headed* (informal) |
| *confident* | *self-assured* | *arrogant*, *cocky* (informal) |
| *thrifty*, *economical* | *careful with money* | *mean* (BrE), *stingy*, *tight* (informal), *cheap* (AmE, of people) |
| *curious*, *inquisitive* | *interested* | *nosy* |
| *relaxed*, *easy-going* | *calm* | *lazy*, *idle* |
| *assertive* | *forceful* | *aggressive*, *pushy* |
| *youthful*, *childlike* | *young* | *childish*, *immature* |
| *well-known*, *famous*, *renowned* | *known* | *notorious*, *infamous* |
| *fragrance*, *scent*, *aroma* | *smell* | *stink*, *stench*, *odour* (in many contexts) |

The philosopher Bertrand Russell made a famous joke of this pattern in a BBC radio programme in 1948, "conjugating" an adjective like a verb: *I am firm; you are obstinate; he is a pig-headed fool.* The same quality looks different depending on whom we are describing — which is exactly why connotation matters in references, reviews, news and arguments.

::: theorem Connotation is part of a word's meaning {#rule-connotation}
Choosing a word chooses its connotation. A word with the right denotation and the wrong connotation is the wrong word, because the reader takes the attitude as part of the message. When describing people, products or events — and whenever you translate an evaluative word — check the connotation in a learner's dictionary (look for words such as *approving*, *disapproving*, *showing disapproval*) and in example sentences.
:::

::: proof
Geoffrey Leech's *Semantics* (1974) distinguished several kinds of meaning beyond the conceptual one, among them **connotative** meaning (what a word suggests), **affective** meaning (the speaker's feelings), **social** meaning (what the word shows about the situation — register) and **collocative** meaning (associations from the words it usually occurs with). The terms show that these "extra" meanings are systematic, not personal accidents: almost all speakers of English hear *skinny* as less flattering than *slim*. Learner's dictionaries mark the strongest cases with labels such as *approving* and *disapproving*, and their definitions often include the attitude: *notorious* ‘famous for something bad’. Some connotations are personal or cultural (*snake* is frightening to some people and not to others), but those that dictionaries record are shared, and readers will assume you meant them.
:::

## How near-synonyms differ

Connotation is only one of the ways in which words with similar meanings differ. Linguists agree that **absolute synonyms** — words interchangeable in every context with no difference at all — are very rare (Cruse 1986); what we normally call synonyms are **near-synonyms**, which overlap in some contexts and differ in others.

::: definition Near-synonyms {#def-near-synonyms}
**Near-synonyms** are words whose meanings are close enough that they can replace each other in some contexts, but that differ in at least one of these respects:

1. **connotation** (approving or disapproving): *slim* / *skinny*;
2. **strength** or intensity: *dislike* / *hate* / *loathe*;
3. **register**: *kids* / *children* / *offspring* ([[collocation-and-register/register]]);
4. **collocation**: *strong* / *powerful* ([[collocation-and-register/what-is-collocation]]);
5. **exact meaning** (denotational nuance): *look* / *watch* / *see*; *mistake* / *error*;
6. **variety**: *autumn* / *fall*; *mean* ‘unwilling to spend money’ (BrE) / ‘unkind’ (AmE and BrE);
7. **grammar**: *afraid* / *frightened* (*a frightened child*, but not ✗ *an afraid child*).
:::

### Strength

Many sets of near-synonyms form a **scale** of intensity, and choosing the wrong point on the scale changes the message: a hotel that is *fine* is not the same as one that is *excellent*, and a colleague who is *annoyed* is not *furious*.

| weaker | | stronger | strongest |
|---|---|---|---|
| *cool* | *cold* | *freezing* | *bitterly cold* |
| *like* | *love* | *adore* | — |
| *dislike* | *hate* | *loathe*, *detest* | — |
| *tired* | *exhausted* | *worn out* (informal) | — |
| *pleased* | *happy* | *delighted* | *thrilled*, *ecstatic* |
| *annoyed* | *angry* | *furious* | *livid* (informal) |
| *interesting* | *fascinating* | *riveting*, *gripping* (of stories) | — |

Strong adjectives (*freezing*, *exhausted*, *furious*, *delighted*, *fascinating*) are often called **extreme** or **non-gradable** adjectives: they already contain the idea of ‘very’, so they combine with *absolutely*, *really* or *completely* rather than *very*: *absolutely freezing*, ? *very freezing*; *completely exhausted*, ? *very exhausted*.

::: warning Quite in British and American English
The adverb *quite* changes strength across varieties. With an ordinary adjective, British English *quite good* usually means ‘fairly good, but not very’, while American English *quite good* usually means ‘very good’. With extreme adjectives, both varieties use *quite* to mean ‘completely’: *quite impossible*, *quite right*. A British teacher who writes *quite good* on an essay is giving moderate praise; an American reader may take it as high praise. Intonation and context matter too (Swan 2016).
:::

::: example Choosing by strength {#ex-strength}
Choose the word that fits each situation best, and explain.

1. A guest leaving a dinner party: *Thank you, the meal was (fine / delicious / acceptable).*
2. A customer complaint email: *I was (a bit disappointed / extremely disappointed / devastated) that my order arrived three weeks late.*
3. A weather forecast: *Tonight will be (cool / freezing / bitterly cold), with temperatures of about 12 °C.*
4. A film review: *The final scene is (interesting / riveting / OK).*
::: solution
1. *delicious* — *fine* and *acceptable* are faint praise; in this situation they sound like criticism.
2. *extremely disappointed* — strong enough to show a serious complaint; *a bit disappointed* undersells it; *devastated* is out of proportion for a late order and would sound sarcastic or childish.
3. *cool* — 12 °C is not freezing; the stronger words would be inaccurate.
4. *riveting* — a review uses strong words to recommend; *interesting* and *OK* are weak. (A reviewer who wanted to be cautious might choose *interesting* on purpose: in British English especially, *interesting* can be polite understatement for ‘strange’ or ‘not good’ — [[pragmatics/irony-understatement]].)
:::
:::

### Exact meaning

Some near-synonyms differ in what exactly they describe, and here a learner's dictionary, which explains differences in usage notes, is more useful than a thesaurus, which only lists words together.

- *mistake* is the general word; *error* is more formal and more often used of calculations, systems and procedures (*a spelling mistake*, *a computer error*, *human error*); *fault* is a responsibility or a defect (*It's my fault*, *an electrical fault*).
- *job* is a particular paid position (*I've got a new job*); *work* is uncountable, the activity (*I've got a lot of work*); *career* is the series of jobs over a lifetime.
- *look* (direct your eyes), *see* (perceive, often without trying), *watch* (look at something that moves or changes, for a period).
- *shade* is an area out of the sun (*sit in the shade*); *shadow* is the dark shape cast by something (*her shadow on the wall*).
- *economic* ‘relating to the economy’ (*economic growth*); *economical* ‘not using much money or fuel’ (*an economical car*).

::: example A thesaurus is not enough {#ex-thesaurus}
A thesaurus lists *error*, *mistake*, *fault*, *blunder* and *slip* together. Choose the best word for each sentence and explain.

1. *Sorry, that was a ___ of the tongue — I meant Tuesday.*
2. *The crash was caused by an ___ in the software.*
3. *Inviting both of them to the same party was a terrible ___.*
4. *It's not your ___ that the train was late.*
5. *I made a ___ in the second question.*
::: solution
1. *slip* — *a slip of the tongue* is a fixed phrase for a small spoken mistake.
2. *error* — formal and technical, typical with systems and software (*a software error*; *bug* is the informal technical word).
3. *blunder* — a stupid or careless mistake with bad consequences; stronger and more evaluative than *mistake*.
4. *fault* — responsibility (*It's not your fault*).
5. *mistake* — the general, neutral word (*error* is possible in a formal context).

The thesaurus gave the set; only the differences in meaning, strength and collocation decide the choice.
:::
:::

## Connotation in use

### Euphemism

::: definition Euphemism {#def-euphemism}
A **euphemism** is a mild or indirect word used instead of one that might seem harsh, rude or upsetting: *pass away* for *die*, *let go* for *dismiss*, *between jobs* for *unemployed*, *senior citizens* or *older people* for *old people*, *bathroom* (AmE) or *toilet* (BrE, itself once a euphemism) for the room. Its opposite, a deliberately harsh word, is sometimes called a **dysphemism**.
:::

Euphemisms are a matter of tenor and politeness: in a conversation with someone whose relative has died, *passed away* or *died* is appropriate and *kicked the bucket* is offensive ([[collocation-and-register/idioms#ex-fixed]]). They are also a matter of honesty: organisations sometimes use euphemisms to hide unpleasant facts (*downsizing*, *restructuring* for dismissing staff), and critical readers learn to notice them. The words used for groups of people change over time as speakers try to avoid negative connotations; the safest guide is the usage of the group itself and of current style guides.

### Loaded language

In news, politics and argument, the choice between near-synonyms can carry a whole point of view. *Protesters* or *rioters*? *Government* or *regime*? *Tax relief* or *tax cuts for the rich*? A reader who notices such choices can see the writer's stance even when it is not stated ([[reading-skills/inference]]). Reporting verbs work in the same way: *She said* is neutral; *She claimed* suggests doubt; *She admitted* suggests something unwelcome; *She pointed out* suggests the writer agrees ([[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]]).

::: example Reading for connotation {#ex-news}
Compare these two reports of the same event, written for this lesson. List the words that differ and say what attitude each version shows.

> **A.** *Hundreds of residents gathered outside the town hall on Saturday to protest against plans to close the local hospital. Organisers said the closure would leave thousands without emergency care.*

> **B.** *A crowd of activists besieged the town hall on Saturday, demanding that plans to restructure local health services be scrapped. Organisers claimed the changes would leave thousands without emergency care.*
::: solution
| A | B | effect |
|---|---|---|
| *Hundreds of residents* | *A crowd of activists* | *residents* are ordinary local people; *activists* suggests organised campaigners, perhaps from elsewhere; *crowd* is less respectful than a number |
| *gathered* | *besieged* | *gathered* is neutral; *besieged* (a military word) suggests aggression |
| *to protest against* | *demanding* | *demand* suggests force and impatience |
| *close the local hospital* | *restructure local health services* | B uses the euphemism of the plan's supporters; A names the concrete result |
| *said* | *claimed* | *claimed* suggests doubt about the statement |

A is broadly sympathetic to the protest, B broadly unsympathetic, though both report the same facts. Neither uses an openly evaluative word like *good* or *bad*: the attitude is carried entirely by connotation.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which reporting verb suggests that the writer **doubts** the statement?
- [ ] *She explained that the delay was unavoidable.*
- [ ] *She noted that the delay was unavoidable.*
- [x] *She claimed that the delay was unavoidable.*
- [ ] *She said that the delay was unavoidable.*
::: solution
*Claim* reports a statement while suggesting that it may not be true. *Say* is neutral; *note* and *explain* suggest that the writer accepts the statement.
:::
:::

## Connotations across languages

::: theorem Translated words may carry different connotations {#rule-translation}
A word and its dictionary translation often share a denotation but not a connotation. Before using a translated evaluative word, check the English word's connotation in a learner's dictionary and in examples; if the connotation differs, choose a different word or add a qualifier.
:::

::: proof
Connotations grow from the history and culture of a language community: from the contexts in which a word has been used, the texts in which it appeared and the values attached to what it names. Two languages that name the same thing therefore need not attach the same feeling to it. Bilingual dictionaries give the closest denotational equivalent and often cannot show the difference in connotation, which is why the pairs below are such frequent sources of misunderstanding. The same mechanism explains why connotations change over time within one language (*propaganda* was once neutral in English).
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Some frequent pairs whose connotations differ:

- **宣传 → *propaganda*.** 宣传 is neutral or positive in many contexts (宣传活动, 宣传部门 for publicity); English *propaganda* is strongly negative (‘misleading information spread to influence people’). Use *publicity*, *promotion*, *awareness campaign* or *information*, depending on context.
- **野心 → *ambition*.** 野心 is usually negative; English *ambition* and *ambitious* are usually positive or neutral (*She's ambitious* is often a compliment). For the negative sense, *ruthless ambition* or *ruthlessly ambitious*. Conversely, translating English *ambitious* as 有野心 can sound critical; 有雄心, 有抱负 or 上进 may fit better.
- **个人主义 → *individualism*.** In Chinese often associated with selfishness; in English often neutral or positive (self-reliance, independence). For the negative sense, *selfishness* or *self-centredness*.
- **骄傲 → *proud*.** Both languages use the word positively (为你骄傲 *proud of you*) and negatively (骄傲自满 *arrogant*, *conceited*). In English, *proud* alone is more often positive; use *arrogant* for the negative sense.
- **胖 → *fat*.** 胖 is used freely and even affectionately in Chinese; *fat* about a person is blunt or rude in English. *Overweight* (neutral, medical), *a bit heavy*, *plump* or, of babies, *chubby* are gentler.
- **老 → *old*.** 老王, 老朋友, 老师 use 老 with respect or affection. English *old* about a person can sound blunt; *older people* or *elderly* (some find this too suggestive of frailty) are more polite. *An old friend*, though, means ‘a friend of many years’, and is warm.
- **精明 → *shrewd*, not *cunning*.** *Shrewd* is approving (‘clever in practical matters’); *cunning* and *sly* suggest dishonesty.
:::

::: example Translating evaluative words {#ex-translate}
Translate the sentences so that the attitude matches the Chinese.

1. 她很有上进心，也很有野心。 (Said with admiration by her manager.)
2. 学校正在宣传垃圾分类。
3. 我为我的学生感到骄傲。
4. 这个宝宝胖乎乎的，真可爱。
::: solution
1. *She's very motivated and ambitious.* — Because the manager admires her, *ambitious* (positive in English) is right; a negative 野心 would need *ruthlessly ambitious* or *power-hungry*.
2. *The school is running a campaign to encourage* (or *raise awareness of*) *waste sorting* (BrE also *recycling*). — Not ✗ *propaganda*.
3. *I'm very proud of my students.* — Positive *proud* is a direct match.
4. *The baby is so chubby and cute.* — *Chubby* is affectionate of babies; ✗ *fat* would sound unkind.
:::
:::

::: theorem Choosing among near-synonyms {#rule-choosing}
To choose among near-synonyms:

1. Decide the **exact meaning** you need.
2. Decide the **attitude** (approving, neutral, disapproving) and **strength**.
3. Match the **register** and **variety** of your text.
4. Check the **collocations** and **grammar** of the word in a learner's dictionary or corpus ([[collocation-and-register/using-a-corpus]]).
5. Prefer a precise neutral word to an impressive one whose connotation you are not sure of.
:::

::: history
Writers have discussed the differences between near-synonyms for centuries. George Crabb's *English Synonymes Explained* (1816) explained the differences in meaning and use between hundreds of groups of words. Peter Mark Roget's *Thesaurus of English Words and Phrases* (1852) did the opposite: it grouped words by idea, without explaining how they differ — which is why a thesaurus is a good memory aid for a writer who already knows the words, and a dangerous tool for one who does not. C. K. Ogden and I. A. Richards's *The Meaning of Meaning* (1923) distinguished the referential and emotive uses of language, and S. I. Hayakawa's popular *Language in Thought and Action* discussed "snarl-words" and "purr-words". Geoffrey Leech's *Semantics* (1974) gave linguistics a systematic set of terms for the kinds of meaning beyond reference, and D. A. Cruse's *Lexical Semantics* (1986) analysed synonymy and its degrees.
:::

## Where this leads

Connotation and loaded language matter in reading between the lines ([[reading-skills/inference]]), in evaluating sources and claims ([[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]]) and in writing persuasively ([[style-and-rhetoric/word-choice]], [[style-and-rhetoric/persuasive-writing]]). Translation pairs whose connotations differ are collected with other traps in [[collocation-and-register/false-friends-chinglish]], and understatement, which depends on choosing a weaker word than the situation deserves, is discussed in [[pragmatics/irony-understatement]].

::: summary
- **Denotation** is a word's basic meaning; **connotation** is the attitude and associations it carries: *slim* (approving), *thin* (neutral), *skinny* (disapproving).
- Many words come in **positive–neutral–negative** sets (*determined* / *firm* / *stubborn*; *famous* / *known* / *notorious*); choosing a word chooses its attitude.
- **Absolute synonyms are rare**; near-synonyms differ in connotation, strength, register, collocation, exact meaning, variety and grammar.
- Words form **scales of strength** (*annoyed* — *angry* — *furious*); extreme adjectives take *absolutely* rather than *very*; *quite* differs between BrE and AmE.
- **Euphemisms** soften (*pass away*); **loaded words** and reporting verbs (*claim*, *admit*) show stance in news and argument.
- **Translations** often share denotation but not connotation: 宣传 ≠ *propaganda*, 野心 ≠ *ambition*, 胖 ≠ *fat* in tone.
- Use a learner's dictionary, not only a thesaurus, to choose among near-synonyms.
:::

::: solution

::: exercise Two reviews {level=3}
Write two short reviews (60–80 words each) of the same imaginary café, one positive and one negative, using the same facts: small, quiet, old furniture, simple menu, prices a little higher than average. Use connotation, not only *good* and *bad*, to show your attitude.
::: solution
Models:

Positive: *Tucked away on a quiet side street, this cosy little café is a real find. The vintage furniture gives it plenty of character, and the menu is simple but carefully chosen — a few dishes, all done well. Prices are slightly above average, but you're paying for quality and a peaceful hour away from the crowds.*

Negative: *Hidden on a deserted side street, this cramped café is easy to miss. The furniture is old and worn, and the menu is limited — barely a handful of dishes. Prices are on the high side for what you get, and the place is so silent that you'll find yourself whispering.*

Pairs: *cosy* / *cramped*; *quiet* / *deserted*, *silent*; *vintage*, *character* / *old and worn*; *simple but carefully chosen* / *limited*; *slightly above average* / *on the high side*; *tucked away* / *hidden*, *easy to miss*.

::: exercise Explain the difference {level=3}
A classmate asks: "What's the difference between *famous*, *well-known*, *renowned*, *notorious* and *infamous*?" Write an answer (100–150 words) that covers connotation, strength and register, with an example for each word.
::: solution
A model:

> *All five words mean ‘known by many people’, but they differ in attitude and register.* Well-known *is neutral and slightly less strong than* famous: *a well-known local restaurant.* Famous *is the general word, usually positive or neutral, and suggests that very many people know about someone:* a famous actor. Renowned *is formal and approving, often used for skill or quality:* a renowned surgeon, renowned for its wine. Notorious *means famous for something bad:* a notorious criminal, a road notorious for accidents. Infamous *is similar but stronger and more formal, often used of very evil acts or people:* an infamous dictator. *A useful test: if the reason for being known is bad, use* notorious *or* infamous; *if not, use* famous, well-known *or* renowned.
:::
