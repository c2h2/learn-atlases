Huang Lei works for a software company in Singapore. On Monday he writes two messages about the same problem. The first goes to a colleague on the team chat:

> *Hi Sam — just found out the server went down again last night. Can you look into it? We might have to put off the update till Friday.*

The second goes into the monthly report for the company's managers:

> *It was discovered that the server had failed again overnight. The technical team is investigating the cause, and the update may need to be postponed until Friday.*

Both messages are good English, and each would sound wrong in the other's place. A report that says *just found out the server went down* sounds careless; a team chat that says *it was discovered that the server had failed* sounds as if Huang Lei were angry or making fun of the report style. The main difference between them is a choice that runs through the whole of English vocabulary: between **phrasal verbs** such as *find out*, *go down*, *look into* and *put off*, and single verbs such as *discover*, *fail*, *investigate* and *postpone*.

The grammar of phrasal verbs — particles, word order, objects — is taught in [[verb-patterns/phrasal-verbs]]. This chapter is about choosing: when a phrasal verb fits and when its one-word equivalent does, why the two often differ in meaning as well as register, and how to move a text in either direction without making it stiff or careless. The wider idea of register follows in [[collocation-and-register/register]].

## Two kinds of verb

::: definition Phrasal verb and one-word equivalent {#def-equivalent}
A **phrasal verb** is a verb combined with an adverb particle (*up*, *out*, *off*, *down*, *over*, *back* …) to form a unit with its own meaning: *find out*, *put off*, *set up*, *turn down*. A **one-word equivalent** is a single verb with the same or a similar meaning in a particular sense: *discover*, *postpone*, *establish*, *reject*. Most one-word equivalents are of Latin or French origin; most phrasal verbs are built from short, old Germanic verbs (*go*, *come*, *put*, *take*, *get*, *set*).
:::

Here are some of the commonest pairs. In each, the phrasal verb is the usual choice in conversation, and the one-word verb the usual choice in formal writing.

| phrasal verb | one-word equivalent | example of the formal use |
|---|---|---|
| *find out* | *discover*, *establish*, *ascertain* (very formal) | *The inquiry established what had happened.* |
| *put off* | *postpone*, *defer* (formal) | *The meeting has been postponed.* |
| *look into* | *investigate*, *examine* | *The committee will investigate the complaint.* |
| *go up* / *go down* | *increase*, *rise* / *decrease*, *fall*, *decline* | *Prices rose by 4%.* |
| *set up* | *establish*, *found* | *The company was founded in 1998.* |
| *turn down* | *reject*, *refuse*, *decline* | *The council rejected the proposal.* |
| *come up with* | *devise*, *propose*, *develop* | *The team devised a new method.* |
| *put up with* | *tolerate*, *endure* | *Residents have endured years of noise.* |
| *leave out* | *omit*, *exclude* | *Two cases were excluded from the analysis.* |
| *go on* | *continue* | *The talks continued until midnight.* |

::: theorem Phrasal verbs and register {#rule-register}
Phrasal verbs are **most frequent in conversation and fiction** and **least frequent in academic prose**. In formal writing, prefer a one-word verb where a precise one exists; in conversation and informal writing, prefer the phrasal verb, which sounds natural and friendly. This is a tendency of register, not a rule of correctness: phrasal verbs are not slang, and many are normal in every register.
:::

::: proof
Biber et al. (1999), comparing conversation, fiction, news and academic prose in the corpus behind the *Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English*, found phrasal verbs most common in fiction and conversation and rarest in academic prose. Gardner and Davies (2007), working with the British National Corpus, showed that a small number of verbs (such as *go*, *come*, *take*, *get*, *set*) and particles (such as *up*, *out*, *down*) account for a large share of all phrasal verb use, which is one reason these verbs feel so everyday. Two explanations are usually given for the register difference. First, history: after the Norman Conquest and again in the Renaissance, English borrowed thousands of verbs from French and Latin, and these borrowings became the vocabulary of law, government, scholarship and the church, while the native verbs and their particles remained the vocabulary of everyday life ([[history-of-english/norman-french]]). Second, precision: a one-word Latinate verb often has a single, narrow meaning (*postpone*), while a phrasal verb may have several (*put off* can also mean ‘discourage’), and formal writing values the narrow one.
:::

::: warning "Never use phrasal verbs in essays"
Some learners are taught that phrasal verbs are informal and must be avoided in academic writing. That is too strong. Many phrasal verbs are standard in academic and professional writing because they have no exact one-word equivalent or because the equivalent sounds unnatural: *carry out* (an experiment, a survey), *point out* (a problem), *set out* (an argument, the aims), *set up* (a study), *find out* (in a description of research questions), *take into account*, *bring about* (change). Replacing them all produces writing that is stiff and sometimes wrong: ✗ *We executed a survey* (✓ *We carried out* / *conducted a survey*).
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is best for the results section of a research report?
- [ ] *We found out that scores went up a lot after the course.*
- [x] *Scores increased significantly after the course.*
- [ ] *Scores went way up after the course.*
- [ ] *We figured out that scores rose loads after the course.*
::: solution
The second sentence uses a precise one-word verb (*increased*) and an academic adverb (*significantly*, which in a results section should be used only of statistical significance). The others are natural in conversation; *way up*, *figured out* and *loads* are informal.
:::
:::

## One phrasal verb, several equivalents

The table above can mislead in one important way. It pairs each phrasal verb with one formal verb, but many phrasal verbs have several meanings, and each meaning has a different equivalent — or none.

::: theorem Choose the equivalent by sense {#rule-sense}
Before replacing a phrasal verb with a one-word verb, decide **which sense** it has in your sentence. A phrasal verb with several senses has a different equivalent for each, and the equivalent of one sense is usually wrong for the others.
:::

The verb *get* is the extreme case. It has so many senses that a formal equivalent always depends on the sentence: *get a letter* → *receive*; *get a visa* → *obtain*; *get worse* → *become* / *grow worse*; *get to the station* → *arrive at* / *reach*; *get the point* → *understand*. Students are often told to "avoid *get* in essays"; the better advice is "decide what *get* means in your sentence, and use that verb".

::: example Choosing by sense {#ex-sense}
Replace each phrasal verb with a one-word verb suitable for a formal report. Be careful: the same phrasal verb appears in different senses.

1. *The new building will take up most of the car park.*
2. *Several staff have taken up the offer of early retirement.*
3. *Two members brought up the question of costs.*
4. *Young people make up a third of the population.*
5. *The witness admitted that he had made up the story.*
6. *Rising costs have put off many potential buyers.*
::: solution
1. *occupy* — *The new building will occupy most of the car park.*
2. *accept* — *Several staff have accepted the offer of early retirement.*
3. *raise* — *Two members raised the question of costs.* (*Mention* is possible but weaker: raising a question asks for it to be discussed.)
4. *constitute* or *account for* — *Young people constitute* (*account for*) *a third of the population.*
5. *invent* or *fabricate* (formal, legal) — *… that he had invented the story.*
6. *deter* or *discourage* — *Rising costs have deterred many potential buyers.*

In every case, using the equivalent of a different sense would be wrong: ✗ *Two members occupied the question*; ✗ *Young people invent a third of the population*.
:::
:::

## Not always a synonym

Even within one sense, a phrasal verb and its one-word equivalent are rarely perfect synonyms. They differ in four ways that matter for choosing.

1. **Meaning.** *Find out* usually means getting information by asking, reading or watching (*I'll find out when the shop opens*); *discover* can also mean being the first to find something (*Fleming discovered penicillin*), which *find out* cannot. *Give up* (smoking, a plan) is neutral; *abandon* (a plan) suggests leaving it completely, often under pressure; *quit* is informal in British English but neutral in American English (*quit your job*).
2. **Collocation.** The two verbs may take different objects. We *carry out* or *conduct* research but not ✗ *perform research*; we *put up with* noise but *tolerate* a drug or a level of risk, where *put up with* would sound odd (? *The patient put up with the drug well*).
3. **Grammar.** Some one-word verbs need a different pattern: *look forward to* + noun or *-ing*, but *anticipate* + noun or *-ing* with a different meaning (‘expect’ rather than ‘be pleased about’). *Turn down an invitation* and *decline an invitation* are parallel, but *refuse* is more often followed by *to* + verb (*She refused to answer*).
4. **Strength and tone.** Some formal equivalents are much more formal than the phrasal verb: *find out* → *discover* is a small step, but *find out* → *ascertain* is a big one, suitable for legal or official writing and odd elsewhere.

There is therefore usually a **range** rather than a pair:

| informal / conversational | neutral | formal |
|---|---|---|
| *find out* | *discover*, *learn* | *ascertain*, *establish* |
| *put up with* | *stand*, *bear* | *tolerate*, *endure* |
| *get* (a visa) | *get*, *obtain* | *obtain*, *procure* (very formal) |
| *go up* | *rise*, *increase* | *escalate* (often negative) |
| *cut down on* | *reduce* | *curtail* |

::: example A report that is too casual {#ex-formalise}
Rewrite this paragraph from a student's project report in a more formal register, keeping any phrasal verbs that are normal in academic writing.

> *We set up a survey to find out how students get to campus. Lots of students turned down the chance to take part, so we had to put off the analysis. In the end we got 180 answers. We found that bus use has gone up, and we think the new bus lane has brought this about.*
::: solution
> *We **set up** (or **designed**) a survey to **establish** how students **travel** to campus. A large number of students **declined** to **take part**, so the analysis had to be **postponed**. **Eventually**, 180 responses **were obtained** (or **we received 180 responses**). The results show that bus use has **increased**, and it seems likely that the new bus lane has **brought about** this change.*

Notes:

- *set up* (a survey) and *take part* are normal in academic writing and can stay; *design* is more precise if that is what was done.
- *find out* → *establish* (or *investigate*); *get to* → *travel to*; *turned down the chance* → *declined*; *put off* → *postponed*; *gone up* → *increased*.
- *brought about* is a standard academic phrasal verb and can stay; *we think* → *it seems likely that* is a matter of hedging ([[modality/hedging-boosting]]).
- *Lots of* → *A large number of*; *In the end* → *Eventually*; *answers* → *responses*. These are not phrasal verbs, but they belong to the same register shift ([[collocation-and-register/register]]).
:::
:::

## Too formal is also wrong

The opposite mistake is just as common among learners who have studied English mainly from written texts and exam lists. Single Latinate verbs in conversation make a speaker sound like a document.

::: example A conversation that is too formal {#ex-informalise}
Zhao Ming says these sentences to his flatmate. Rewrite them as a native speaker would say them in the same situation.

1. *I shall investigate the problem with the washing machine.*
2. *Could you extinguish the light when you depart?*
3. *I have ascertained that the landlord will visit tomorrow.*
4. *We should postpone the party, because many people have declined.*
5. *I cannot tolerate this noise any longer.*
::: solution
1. *I'll have a look at* (or *look into*) *the washing machine.* — *have a look at* is the most natural for a practical problem.
2. *Could you turn off* (or *switch off*) *the light when you leave?*
3. *I've found out that the landlord's coming round tomorrow.* (*come round*, BrE; *come over*, AmE)
4. *We should put off the party — loads of people can't come.* (*Loads of* is informal; *a lot of* is neutral.)
5. *I can't put up with this noise any more.* (*I can't stand this noise* is also very natural.)

Notice the other features of conversational register that come with the phrasal verbs: contractions (*I'll*, *can't*), *any more* rather than *any longer*, and simpler subordination.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese has its own form–register contrast: 书面语 words such as 购买, 推迟, 调查 beside spoken 买, 往后推, 查一查. So the idea that one meaning can be expressed at different levels of formality is familiar to Chinese learners. The difference lies in where the formal words come from. In English, formal verbs are often longer, Latinate single words, while everyday verbs are short words with particles; Chinese 书面语 more often uses different characters or compounds.

Chinese also has a structure that resembles the phrasal verb: the verb + result or direction complement (动补结构), as in 找到, 想出, 放下, 拿出来, 站起来. The resemblance helps with the literal uses (*stand up* 站起来, *take out* 拿出来), but not with the idiomatic ones (*put off* ≠ 放下, *make up* ≠ 做起来). Research on Chinese learners (for example Liao and Fukuya 2004) has found that learners tend to **avoid** phrasal verbs, especially the figurative ones, and use one-word verbs instead. Textbooks and exam word lists add to this, because they favour the single-word verbs of written texts. The result is speech that sounds correct but bookish. In conversation, choose the phrasal verb; in a report, the single verb — and learn both members of each pair.
:::

## British and American differences

Most phrasal verbs are shared, but some are typical of one variety, and a few differ in meaning.

| British English | American English | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *fill in* (a form) | *fill out* (also BrE) | complete a form |
| *ring up*, *ring back* | *call up*, *call back* | telephone |
| *sort out* (a problem) | *work out*, *figure out*, *sort out* | solve, arrange |
| *get on with* (someone) | *get along with* (also BrE) | have a friendly relationship |
| *wash up* (‘wash the dishes’) | *wash up* (‘wash your hands and face’) | same form, different meaning |
| *queue up* | *line up* | wait in a line |

The *wash up* row is a reminder that informal phrasal verbs carry the most risk: a learner using one in the other variety may be understood to mean something else. A few informal phrasal verbs are even polite in one variety and rude in the other, which a good learner's dictionary marks. When in doubt, use the neutral form.

## Nouns from phrasal verbs

Many phrasal verbs have related nouns, and these nouns behave differently: they are often **neutral or formal**, and several are frequent in news and academic writing.

- *break down* → *a breakdown* (*a breakdown of the figures* ‘a detailed list’; *a breakdown in talks*)
- *break out* → *an outbreak* (*an outbreak of disease*)
- *come out* → *an outcome* (‘a result’, formal)
- *set back* → *a setback*
- *feed back* → *feedback*
- *put in* / *put out* → *input*, *output*
- *turn out* → *turnout* (the number of people who vote or attend)
- *draw back* → *a drawback* (‘a disadvantage’)
- *follow up* → *a follow-up* (*a follow-up study*)

Note the change of order in some nouns (*break out* → *outbreak*; *come out* → *outcome*) and the stress on the first part (*a **break**down*, *an **out**break*; [[word-formation/compounds]]). A formal text can often use one of these nouns where the verb would sound informal: *Feedback from participants was positive* rather than *The participants fed back that …*.

::: example Keep or replace? {#ex-keep}
Decide whether each phrasal verb is acceptable in an academic essay. If not, replace it.

1. *This essay sets out the main arguments for a sugar tax.*
2. *Many studies have pointed out the limits of this approach.*
3. *Scientists are still trying to figure out why the effect occurs.*
4. *The researchers carried out interviews with forty nurses.*
5. *The government's plan went down badly with voters.*
6. *Several factors account for the difference.*
::: solution
1. Acceptable: *set out* ‘present in an organised way’ is standard in academic writing.
2. Acceptable: *point out* is very common in academic prose (also *note*, *observe*).
3. Replace: *figure out* is informal (especially AmE) → *determine*, *establish* or *explain why*.
4. Acceptable: *carry out* is the usual verb with *interviews*, *experiments*, *research*; *conduct* is an alternative.
5. Replace: *go down badly with* is informal → *was poorly received by voters* / *was unpopular with voters*.
6. Acceptable: *account for* ‘explain’ is a standard academic prepositional verb ([[verb-patterns/prepositional-verbs]]).
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which phrasal verb would you most probably **replace** in a formal report?
- [ ] *carry out* (an experiment)
- [ ] *point out* (a weakness)
- [x] *put up with* (poor conditions)
- [ ] *set out* (the aims of a project)
::: solution
*Put up with* is conversational; in a report, *tolerate*, *endure* or *accept* is usual (*Staff have tolerated poor conditions for years*). The other three are normal in academic and professional writing.
:::
:::

::: history
English has combined verbs with particles since Old English, but such combinations became much more frequent in Middle English, as the old verb prefixes declined, and they have kept growing ever since. At the same time, the borrowing of French and Latin verbs after 1066 and during the Renaissance created the parallel formal vocabulary of *postpone*, *investigate* and *tolerate* ([[history-of-english/early-modern-english]]). Samuel Johnson, in the Preface to his *Dictionary* (1755), already singled out verbs whose meaning is changed by a following particle as a special difficulty for foreigners. The term *phrasal verb* is usually credited to Logan Pearsall Smith's *Words and Idioms* (1925). Dedicated dictionaries for learners followed, among them the first volume of the *Oxford Dictionary of Current Idiomatic English* by A. P. Cowie and R. Mackin (1975) and the *Longman Dictionary of Phrasal Verbs* (1983).
:::

## Where this leads

The general idea behind this chapter — that every text has a register, signalled by vocabulary and grammar — is developed in [[collocation-and-register/register]]. Academic verbs and their collocations (*conduct*, *establish*, *demonstrate*) are part of [[collocation-and-register/academic-vocabulary]], and the style of formal writing in general in [[academic-writing/academic-style]]. The meanings and grammar of the phrasal verbs themselves are in [[verb-patterns/phrasal-verbs]]; idiomatic expressions built on phrasal verbs (*come up against a brick wall*) appear in [[collocation-and-register/idioms]].

::: summary
- Many **phrasal verbs** have **one-word equivalents**, usually of Latin or French origin: *find out* / *discover*, *put off* / *postpone*, *look into* / *investigate*.
- Phrasal verbs are most frequent in **conversation and fiction**, least in **academic prose** (Biber et al. 1999); prefer the one-word verb in formal writing and the phrasal verb in speech.
- Phrasal verbs are not banned from formal writing: *carry out*, *point out*, *set out*, *set up*, *bring about*, *account for* are standard.
- Choose the equivalent **by sense**: *take up* = *occupy*, *begin* or *accept*; *make up* = *invent* or *constitute*; *get* = *receive*, *obtain*, *become*, *reach*.
- Pairs are rarely perfect synonyms: they differ in **meaning, collocation, grammar and strength**; think of a range (*find out* — *discover* — *ascertain*).
- Over-formal verbs in conversation sound stiff; Chinese learners in particular tend to avoid phrasal verbs.
- Some phrasal verbs differ between **British and American English**, and nouns from phrasal verbs (*outcome*, *outbreak*, *feedback*) are often neutral or formal.
:::
