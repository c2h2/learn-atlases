Here is part of a message that Chen Jie, an engineering student in Toronto, wrote to his cousin about his first week:

> *On Monday I made a test in maths and I think I made well. In the evening I did a party with my roommates and we made a lot of fun. On Tuesday I took a decision: I will make more sport. Yesterday I had a shower, made my homework and took a photo of the lake for you.*

Six of the nine verb combinations in the message are unnatural, and all six use *make* or *do*; a seventh, *took a decision*, is correct in British English but unusual in Canada. Chen Jie is not careless. These verbs are among the most frequent in English, and precisely because they are so frequent their meanings have become thin and general, so that which one is used with which noun has become a matter of convention. A message from a British student might also differ from an American one: *had a shower* is the British choice and *took a shower* the American, and both are right.

This chapter studies the two groups of collocation that learners need most: the **verb + noun** collocations of *make*, *do*, *take*, *have* and *give*, and the **adjective + noun** collocations that describe amount, intensity and quality (*heavy rain*, *a high price*, *a close friend*). The general idea of collocation is introduced in [[collocation-and-register/what-is-collocation]]; here we work through the high-frequency cases one by one. (Chen Jie's message is corrected in [[#ex-chen]].)

## Delexical verbs

::: definition Delexical verb {#def-delexical}
A **delexical verb** (also called a **light verb**) is a very common verb used with a noun that carries most of the meaning of the combination: *have a look*, *take a shower*, *make a suggestion*, *give a smile*, *do some reading*. The verb itself adds little more than the idea of performing an action. The main delexical verbs of English are *have*, *take*, *make*, *give* and *do*, with *get*, *go for* (*go for a walk*) and *pay* (*pay a visit*) in smaller sets.
:::

Many delexical combinations have a related single verb: *have a look* / *look*, *make a suggestion* / *suggest*, *take a break* / *break* (in one sense), *give a smile* / *smile*, *make a decision* / *decide*. Others have none: *do homework*, *take a photo*, *have breakfast*.

::: theorem Delexical phrase or single verb? {#rule-delexical}
When both a delexical phrase and a single verb exist, they are not simply interchangeable:

1. **The phrase lets you describe the action with adjectives**: *have a quick look*, *make a useful suggestion*, *take a long walk*, *give a nervous laugh*. With the single verb you would need adverbs (*look quickly*), which often sound less natural.
2. **The phrase often presents the action as a single, limited event**: *have a swim* (one occasion, often for pleasure) against *swim* (the activity in general: *Can you swim?*).
3. **Some phrases are more conversational** than the single verb (*have a go*, *give it a try*, *take a look*), and some more formal (*make a decision* is neutral; *reach a decision* and *take a decision* (BrE) are often used in formal and official contexts).
:::

::: proof
The COBUILD grammar (Sinclair and colleagues, 1990), based on corpus evidence, describes the delexical structure in these terms: the noun, not the verb, carries the content, and putting the action into a noun lets the speaker add modifiers and count it (*make two phone calls*). The second point explains pairs such as *I swim every morning* (habit) against *Let's have a swim before lunch* (one occasion), and the British *Shall we have a walk?* beside the more common *go for a walk*. The register difference is a tendency only; many delexical phrases are neutral and occur in every register. Grammars agree that the choice of verb in these combinations is not predictable from meaning and must be learned noun by noun, which is the subject of the rest of this section.
:::

::: example Single verb and delexical phrase {#ex-paraphrase}
Rewrite each sentence with a delexical phrase, adding the adjective in brackets. Then say what the new version adds.

1. *She looked at the report.* (quick)
2. *He suggested something.* (useful)
3. *They rested.* (short)
4. *She smiled at us.* (warm)
5. *We talked about the plan.* (long)
::: solution
1. *She had* (or *took*) *a quick look at the report.* — The adjective tells us how long and how carefully she looked; *looked quickly at* is possible but less natural.
2. *He made a useful suggestion.* — The phrase lets us evaluate the suggestion.
3. *They had* (or *took*) *a short rest.* — Presents the rest as one limited event.
4. *She gave us a warm smile.* — Note the change of structure: *give* + person + *a smile*. *She smiled warmly at us* is also natural.
5. *We had a long talk about the plan.* — *Have a talk* suggests a conversation, often a serious one; *give a talk* would mean a lecture.

The delexical phrase is the natural choice whenever you want to describe the action itself — its length, its quality, its manner.
:::
:::

## Make and do

*Make* and *do* both translate 做, and they are the pair learners confuse most. Grammars and dictionaries describe a tendency, which helps but does not decide every case.

::: theorem Make and do: the tendency {#rule-make-do}
- **Make** is used for **producing, creating or causing** something, including things said and plans: *make a cake*, *make a noise*, *make a mess*, *make a decision*, *make a plan*, *make a suggestion*, *make a comment*, *make a complaint*, *make a promise*, *make an excuse*, *make a phone call*, *make an appointment*, *make money*, *make a profit*, *make progress*, *make a difference*, *make friends*, *make an effort*.
- **Do** is used for **activities, work and tasks**, especially when the activity is general or not named exactly: *do homework*, *do the housework*, *do a job*, *do business*, *do research*, *do an exercise* (in a book) and *do exercise* (physical), *do your best*, and *do* + *the* / *some* + *-ing*: *do the shopping*, *do the cooking*, *do some reading*. *Do* is also used for harm and benefit: *do damage*, *do harm*, *do good*, *do someone a favour*.
- Many collocations do not follow the tendency and must simply be learned: *make a mistake*, *make the bed*, *make a speech*, *do your hair*, *do a degree* (BrE).
:::

::: proof
The tendency reflects the history and core meanings of the verbs: *make* has always meant ‘bring into existence’, and its delexical uses extend that idea to decisions, plans and words ("bringing a promise into existence"); *do* is the general verb of action, used where the activity matters more than any result. Swan (2016) gives essentially this account, together with lists of common combinations that have to be learned. Exceptions such as *make a mistake* are old, fixed and extremely frequent, which is why they resist analysis. The safest approach is to use the tendency to make a first guess, and then to learn each frequent combination as a unit ([[core-vocabulary/phrases-and-chunks]]).
:::

| British English | American English | note |
|---|---|---|
| *do the washing-up* | *do the dishes* | both understood everywhere |
| *do the washing* | *do the laundry* | *laundry* also BrE |
| *do sport* | *play sports* | ✗ *make sport* in both |
| *do a course*, *do a degree* (in history) | *take a course*, *study for* / *get a degree* | *do a course* also heard in AmE |
| *make an appointment* | *make an appointment* | the same; also *book an appointment* (BrE) |

::: warning Make and do with Chinese 做
Because 做 covers both verbs, the commonest errors are:

- ✗ *do a mistake* ✓ *make a mistake*
- ✗ *make homework* ✓ *do homework*
- ✗ *do a decision* ✓ *make a decision* (BrE also *take a decision*)
- ✗ *make research* ✓ *do* / *carry out* / *conduct research*
- ✗ *make a favour* ✓ *do someone a favour*
- ✗ *make exercise* ✓ *do exercise*, *take exercise* (BrE), *get exercise* (AmE)
- ✗ *make damage* ✓ *do damage* / *cause damage*
:::

::: quiz

- [ ] *The storm made a lot of damage to the roof.*
- [ ] *Could you make me a favour?*
- [x] *I've made a lot of progress this term.*
- [ ] *We made the shopping on Saturday.*
::: solution
*Make progress* is the collocation. The others need *do*: *did* (or *caused*) *a lot of damage*, *do me a favour*, *did the shopping*.
:::

## Take, have and give

### Take

*Take* combines with nouns for actions that a person chooses to perform, often with a sense of accepting or undertaking something:

- *take a photo* / *a picture*, *take notes*, *take a seat* (a polite invitation: *Please take a seat*), *take a deep breath*
- *take a break*, *take a rest*, *take a walk* (*go for a walk* is more common in BrE), *take a look* (more common in AmE; BrE prefers *have a look*)
- *take action*, *take steps*, *take measures* (formal), *take responsibility*, *take care (of)*, *take a risk* (also *run a risk*), *take a chance*, *take advantage of*
- *take place* (‘happen’, of planned events: *The meeting will take place on Monday*), *take part (in)*
- *take medicine* / *a pill* (✗ *eat medicine*), *take a bus* / *a taxi*, *take an exam* / *a test*

*Take* is also the verb of time and effort: *It takes time.* *It took me three hours to finish.*

### Have

*Have* is used for meals and drinks, social events, experiences and many everyday actions:

- *have breakfast* / *lunch* / *dinner*, *have a meal*, *have a drink*, *have a coffee* (‘drink a cup of coffee’)
- *have a party*, *have a meeting*, *have a chat*, *have a conversation*, *have an argument*, *have a talk* (a serious conversation)
- *have a look*, *have a go* / *a try* (BrE informal), *have a rest*, *have a break*
- *have a shower* / *a bath* (BrE; AmE prefers *take*), *have a holiday* (BrE; AmE *take a vacation*)
- *have a good time*, *have fun*, *have a dream*, *have an idea*, *have difficulty* (*in*) + *-ing* / *have trouble* + *-ing*, *have a baby* (‘give birth’)

### Give

*Give* is used for things one person offers or produces for others, including speech and some short, sudden actions:

- *give a talk* / *a speech* / *a presentation* / *a lecture*, *give an example*, *give a reason*, *give an answer*, *give advice*, *give permission*, *give evidence* (in court)
- *give someone a hand* (‘help’), *give someone a call* / *a ring* (BrE, informal), *give someone a lift* (BrE) / *a ride* (AmE)
- *give a smile*, *give a laugh*, *give a shout*, *give a sigh* (rather written; often *give a sigh of relief*), *give someone a hug*

::: example Chen Jie's message {#ex-chen}
Correct the collocations in Chen Jie's message at the start of the chapter, and explain each correction.
::: solution
> *On Monday I **took** (BrE also **sat**) a test in maths and I think I **did** well. In the evening I **had** a party with my roommates and we **had** a lot of fun. On Tuesday I **made** a decision: I will **do** more sport (AmE **play** more sports). Yesterday I had a shower, **did** my homework and took a photo of the lake for you.*

1. *made a test* → *took a test* (*make a test* means ‘write the questions for a test’, which a teacher does). British English also uses *sit a test*, especially for formal examinations.
2. *made well* → *did well*: *do well* / *badly* is the collocation for performance.
3. *did a party* → *had a party* (or *threw a party*, informal; *gave a party*, more formal).
4. *made a lot of fun* → *had a lot of fun*: *have fun*. (*Make fun of* someone is a different collocation: ‘laugh at them unkindly’.)
5. *took a decision* is correct in British English, but *made a decision* is the safer choice for a student in Canada, where usage follows American English.
6. *make more sport* → *do more sport* (BrE) / *play more sports* (AmE).
7. *made my homework* → *did my homework*.

*Had a shower* and *took a photo* were correct. *Had a shower* is the British choice; in Toronto, *took a shower* would be more usual, so Chen Jie could change it too for consistency.
:::
:::

## Adjective + noun collocations

Adjectives meaning ‘a lot’, ‘very strong’ or ‘very little’ are the most restricted of all, because English has many of them and each noun chooses its own. Learners who rely on *big* and *small* are understood, but their English sounds flat; learners who translate 大, 高, 重, 浓 or 深 directly often choose the wrong one.

::: theorem Intensifying adjectives are chosen by the noun {#rule-intensity}
To say that something is large in amount or intense, English uses different adjectives with different nouns:

| adjective | typical nouns |
|---|---|
| *heavy* | *rain*, *snow*, *traffic*, *losses*, *smoker*, *drinker*, *workload*, *fine* (a large penalty) |
| *high* | *price*, *cost*, *level*, *rate*, *standard*, *temperature*, *speed*, *risk*, *quality*, *unemployment* |
| *strong* | *wind*, *coffee*, *tea*, *accent*, *support*, *evidence*, *argument*, *opinion*, *smell*, *influence* |
| *deep* | *sleep*, *concern*, *regret*, *respect*, *understanding*, *voice* |
| *great* | *importance*, *success*, *difficulty*, *pleasure*, *interest*, *care*, *deal* (*a great deal of*) |
| *large* | *amount*, *number*, *quantity*, *scale*, *population*, *proportion*, *majority* |
| *serious* | *problem*, *illness*, *injury*, *mistake*, *crime*, *damage*, *concern* |
| *severe* | *weather*, *pain*, *damage*, *shortage*, *punishment*, *criticism* |

The opposites are chosen by the noun too: *light rain* and *light traffic*, but *low prices*, *weak tea*, *a slight accent*, *minor injuries*, *a small number*.
:::

::: proof
The groupings are not random: *heavy* is used for things imagined as weight or volume that falls on or burdens us; *high* for things measured on a scale (prices, levels, temperatures); *strong* for force and influence; *deep* for feelings and states imagined as depth. These images help to remember the groups, and learner's dictionaries often organise their collocation notes in the same way. But the images do not predict every case — *strong wind* but *heavy rain*, although both are weather and both have force — so a learner should treat the groups as memory aids, check new combinations in a dictionary or corpus ([[collocation-and-register/using-a-corpus]]), and expect variety differences: *a high fever* is usual in American English, *a high temperature* in British English.
:::

Other adjective + noun collocations describe kinds and qualities, and they too are partly fixed:

- people and relationships: *a close friend*, *an old friend* (‘known for a long time’), *a best friend*, *a bitter enemy*, *a hard worker*, *a heavy sleeper*, *a light sleeper*
- size and range: *a wide range* / *variety*, *the vast majority*, *a rough idea* / *estimate*, *a broad smile*, *a narrow escape*
- change: *a sharp rise* / *fall*, *a steep rise*, *a slight increase*, *a dramatic change*, *a gradual decline*
- food and drink: *strong* / *weak tea*, *black coffee*, *white coffee* (BrE, with milk), *fresh air*, *stale bread*, *rich food* (heavy, full of fat or sugar)

::: example Choose the adjective {#ex-adjectives}
Choose the most natural adjective from *heavy*, *high*, *strong*, *deep*, *serious*, *sharp* for each noun, and give the reason or the group it belongs to.

1. *There's a ___ risk of flooding this week.*
2. *The country suffered ___ losses in the war.*
3. *There is ___ evidence that the drug works.*
4. *The company reported a ___ fall in profits.*
5. *He fell into a ___ sleep.*
6. *She had a ___ injury and missed the season.*
::: solution
1. *high risk* — risk is measured on a scale (*high*, *low risk*).
2. *heavy losses* — amount imagined as weight (*heavy losses*, *heavy casualties*).
3. *strong evidence* — force of support (also *strong support*, *a strong argument*).
4. *sharp fall* — a sudden, large change (also *a sharp rise*, *a steep fall*).
5. *deep sleep* — states imagined as depth (*a deep sleep*; *fall into a deep sleep* is itself a common phrase).
6. *serious injury* — degree of something bad (*serious illness*, *serious damage*; *severe* is also possible).
:::
:::

::: warning High, tall and big
✗ *a high man* ✓ *a tall man*. *Tall* is used for people, trees and buildings (things that are narrow and upright); *high* for mountains, walls, ceilings, shelves and anything above the ground (*a high window*), and for numbers on a scale (*a high price*). With buildings both are used (*a tall building*, *a high building*). ✗ *a big price* ✓ *a high price*; ✗ *big traffic* ✓ *heavy traffic*; ✗ *a big wind* ✓ *a strong wind* (*high winds*, plural, in weather reports).
:::

::: note English and Chinese
The general verbs and adjectives of Chinese divide meaning differently from those of English, so a one-to-one table leads to errors. Some frequent correspondences:

| Chinese | English collocation | not |
|---|---|---|
| 做决定 · 做作业 · 做研究 | *make a decision* · *do homework* · *do research* | — |
| 拍照 | *take a photo* | ✗ *shoot a photo* (possible, but means ‘professionally’), ✗ *beat a photo* |
| 吃药 | *take medicine* | ✗ *eat medicine* |
| 洗澡 | *have a shower* / *a bath* (BrE), *take a shower* / *a bath* (AmE) | ✗ *wash a bath* |
| 参加考试 | *take an exam*, *sit an exam* (BrE) | ✗ *join an exam* |
| 采取措施 | *take measures* / *steps*, *take action* | ✗ *adopt actions* |
| 提建议 | *make a suggestion*, *give advice* | ✗ *raise advice* |
| 做报告 · 发言 | *give a presentation* / *a talk* · *make a speech* / *a statement* | ✗ *do a speech* |
| 做梦 | *have a dream* | ✗ *do a dream* |
| 学到知识 | *gain* / *acquire knowledge*, *learn a lot* | ✗ *learn knowledge* |

Two habits help. First, look up the **noun** in a learner's dictionary and read its examples for the verb, rather than looking up 做 or 提. Second, notice that Chinese often uses one verb with a whole family of nouns (提 with 建议, 问题, 意见, 要求), while English may divide the family: *make a suggestion*, *raise* / *ask a question*, *express* / *give an opinion*, *make a request*. The chapter [[collocation-and-register/false-friends-chinglish]] collects many more such cases.
:::

::: example Editing a paragraph {#ex-edit}
Li Na wrote this paragraph in a report about a volunteer project. Find and correct the six unnatural verb + noun or adjective + noun combinations.

> *Our group made a research on recycling in our district. We did a survey of 200 families and found a big amount of waste is not sorted. Many people have a high interest in the environment, but they lack of information. We raised some advice to the local council and they took actions quickly.*
::: solution
> *Our group **did** (or **carried out**) research on recycling in our district. We did a survey of 200 families and found **that a large amount** of waste is not sorted. Many people have a **strong** (or **keen**) **interest** in the environment, but they lack information. We **made some suggestions** (or **gave some advice**) to the local council and they **took action** quickly.*

1. *made a research* → *did* / *carried out research* (uncountable, so no *a*).
2. *a big amount* → *a large amount* (*large* with quantities; *big amount* is informal).
3. *a high interest* → *a strong* / *keen* / *great interest* (*high* is used with *interest rates*, not with personal interest).
4. *lack of information* → *lack information*: the verb *lack* takes a direct object; *lack of* is the noun (*a lack of information*). This is a grammatical collocation.
5. *raised some advice* → *made some suggestions* / *gave some advice* (*raise* goes with *a question*, *a concern*, *an issue*).
6. *took actions* → *took action* (uncountable in this collocation).

*Did a survey* is correct (also *carried out* / *conducted a survey*, more formal). Notice how several errors involve countability as well as the verb: collocations include their grammar.
:::
:::

## How to build your collocation knowledge

::: theorem Learning collocations of the commonest words {#rule-learning}
1. Learn high-frequency collocations **as units** — *make a mistake*, *take a break*, *have a look*, *heavy rain* — with their articles and countability.
2. Organise them **by noun**: for each important noun, record the verbs used with it, the adjectives used before it and the prepositions after it.
3. For the delexical verbs, keep **one page per verb** as well, grouped by meaning (*take* + transport, *take* + medicine, *take* + time …), so that you can see the patterns.
4. Mark **variety** (BrE / AmE) and **register** (informal / formal) where they matter, and choose one variety consistently in a text.
5. When you are not sure, **check** in a learner's dictionary, a collocation dictionary or a corpus rather than guessing from Chinese.
:::

::: history
The Danish grammarian Otto Jespersen called verbs such as *have*, *take* and *give* in *have a look* and *take a walk* "light verbs" in his *Modern English Grammar*, and the term is still used in linguistics. The COBUILD grammar (1990), edited by John Sinclair, used corpus evidence to describe the same structure under the name *delexical verbs*, and its emphasis on the noun as the carrier of meaning shaped how learner's dictionaries present these combinations. Specialised collocation dictionaries, from the *BBI Combinatory Dictionary of English* (1986) to the *Oxford Collocations Dictionary for Students of English* (first edition 2002), made it possible to look up a noun and find its verbs and adjectives. Learner-corpus research, such as Nesselhauf's (2005) study of verb–noun combinations, showed that these very verbs are where advanced learners most often go wrong.
:::

::: summary
- **Delexical verbs** (*make*, *do*, *take*, *have*, *give*) carry little meaning of their own; the noun carries the content: *have a look*, *make a suggestion*.
- A delexical phrase lets you **add adjectives** (*a quick look*) and often presents the action as **one event** (*have a swim*).
- **Make** tends to go with producing and causing (*a decision*, *a plan*, *a mistake*, *progress*), **do** with activities and tasks (*homework*, *research*, *the shopping*, *damage*), but many collocations must be learned one by one.
- **Take**, **have** and **give** each have their own sets (*take a photo*, *take medicine*, *have breakfast*, *have a party*, *give a talk*, *give advice*), and some nouns combine with two verbs with a difference of variety or meaning.
- British and American English differ in several delexical collocations: *have* / *take a shower*, *have* / *take a look*, *take a decision* (BrE), *do sport* / *play sports*.
- **Intensifying adjectives** are chosen by the noun: *heavy rain*, *high prices*, *strong wind*, *deep sleep*, *a large amount*, *a serious problem*.
- Look up the English **noun**, not the Chinese verb or adjective, and record collocations with their grammar, variety and register.
:::

> *After the game I **took a shower** and **did the dishes**. Then I **took a look** at the course website: I'm **taking a course** (or **a class**) in photography next semester, and I have to **take an exam** in May.*

Changes: *have* → *take a shower*; *do the washing-up* → *do the dishes*; *have a look* → *take a look* (*have a look* is also understood); *do a course* → *take a course* / *a class*; *sit an exam* → *take an exam*. Non-collocation changes an American reader would also expect: *match* → *game*, *term* → *semester* (depending on the school).
:::

::: exercise A collocation page for a noun {level=3}
Make a full collocation page for *mistake* or *decision*: verbs before it (at least five), adjectives before it (at least five), prepositions and patterns after it, and two common phrases. Mark any differences of variety or register, and check each entry in a dictionary or corpus.
::: hint
Think of the life of a decision or mistake: making it, the kind it is, what happens afterwards (*regret*, *correct*, *learn from* a mistake; *announce*, *reverse* a decision).
:::
::: solution
A model for *decision*:

- verbs: *make a decision*; *take a decision* (mainly BrE, often formal); *reach* / *come to a decision* (after discussion); *announce a decision*; *reverse* / *overturn a decision* (formal, of an authority); *postpone* / *put off a decision*; *regret a decision*.
- adjectives: *a difficult* / *tough decision*; *a big* / *major decision*; *an important decision*; *a final decision*; *a quick* / *snap decision* (*snap* ‘made suddenly’, informal); *the right* / *wrong decision*.
- patterns: *a decision to* + verb (*the decision to close the library*); *a decision on* / *about* something; *a decision that* + clause.
- phrases: *It's your decision*; *a decision-making process*.

Your page should only contain combinations you have checked; *do a decision* and *give a decision* should not appear (though a judge or committee can *give a decision* in legal or sports contexts, a specialised use).
:::
