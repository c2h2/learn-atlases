Here are two versions of the same paragraph from a first-year essay on screen time and sleep. The first is the student's draft; the second is the version she handed in after a session with a writing tutor.

> **Draft.** *Lots of people think that using phones at night is bad for sleep. Some researches show that the light from screens can change the body clock, and this makes a big difference to how well people sleep. But other studies don't agree, so we need to look at this more.*

> **Revised.** *It is widely assumed that evening phone use has a negative effect on sleep. Several studies suggest that light from screens may disrupt the body's circadian rhythm, with significant consequences for sleep quality. However, the evidence is not consistent, and further research is needed.*

The ideas are the same. The grammar of the draft is nearly correct (only *researches* is a problem). What changed is the vocabulary: *think* became *assume*, *bad for* became *a negative effect on*, *change* became *disrupt*, *a big difference* became *significant consequences*, *don't agree* became *the evidence is not consistent*, *we need to look at this more* became *further research is needed*. Most of the new words are not technical terms of medicine. They are words that appear in essays and articles in every subject — *assume*, *effect*, *suggest*, *significant*, *consequence*, *evidence*, *consistent*, *research* — together with their typical partners: *have an effect on*, *studies suggest*, *further research is needed*.

This chapter is about that vocabulary. It explains the difference between general, academic and technical words; describes the main groups of academic words and their families; shows that academic words have collocations as fixed as any others; and gives a method for learning them. It draws on the earlier chapters on collocation, register and connotation and leads directly to academic writing.

## Three layers of vocabulary

::: definition General, academic and technical vocabulary {#def-layers}
- **General (high-frequency) vocabulary** is the few thousand word families that make up most of any text, spoken or written: *people*, *use*, *make*, *important*, *because* ([[core-vocabulary/frequency-and-coverage]]).
- **Academic vocabulary** is the words that are frequent in academic texts across many subjects but much less frequent in general texts: *analyse*, *assume*, *concept*, *consistent*, *evidence*, *factor*, *indicate*, *significant*. They express the activities common to all scholarship: describing, comparing, arguing, reporting and evaluating.
- **Technical vocabulary** is the words specific to one field: *photosynthesis*, *tort*, *amortisation*, *phoneme*. Each subject has its own, and they are usually defined and taught within the subject.
:::

The best-known description of the middle layer is Averil Coxhead's **Academic Word List** (AWL, 2000). Coxhead built a corpus of about 3.5 million words of academic writing from 28 subject areas in four faculties (arts, commerce, law and science) and selected word families that were not among the most frequent general words, occurred in all four faculties and in many subject areas, and were frequent overall. The result was 570 word families, which covered about 10% of the words in her corpus. Later lists, such as the Academic Vocabulary List of Gardner and Davies (2014), built from a much larger American corpus, take a similar approach with different methods. Phrase lists have followed too: the Academic Formulas List of Simpson-Vlach and Ellis (2010) and the Academic Collocation List of Ackermann and Chen (2013) collect frequent multi-word units of academic English.

::: theorem Academic vocabulary is worth learning deliberately {#rule-worth}
For a learner who already knows the general high-frequency words and plans to study or work in English, the general academic vocabulary is the next most valuable layer: a few hundred word families give a large gain in coverage of academic texts, in every subject. Learn them **deliberately**, as **families**, and **with their collocations**.
:::

::: proof
The argument is one of coverage and range. Technical words are essential within one field but useless outside it; general words beyond the first few thousand are each rare. Academic words occupy a middle position: each family is moderately frequent, but because they are used across all subjects, together they account for a sizeable share of academic text (about one word in ten in Coxhead's corpus). Nation (2013) therefore recommends that learners heading for academic study give them special attention. Learning them as families exploits word formation ([[word-formation/learning-with-word-formation]]): *analyse*, *analysis*, *analytical*, *analyst* are one family. Learning them with collocations matters because academic words are no less restricted than general words: we *conduct* research, *draw* conclusions and *play* a role.
:::

A few cautions. A word list is a guide to **what** to learn, not a method of learning: a list of words with translations teaches neither their use nor their partners. Lists are made from particular corpora, so they reflect the subjects and dates of their texts. And a word on a list is not automatically "better" than a general word: in many sentences *use* is better than *utilise*.

### Words with special senses

A large group of words has both a general sense and a special academic or technical one. Learners who know the general sense may misread the special one:

- *significant* — general ‘important, large’; in statistics ‘unlikely to be due to chance’ (*a statistically significant difference*).
- *culture* — general ‘the customs and arts of a society’; in biology ‘bacteria or cells grown for study’.
- *solution* — general ‘the answer to a problem’; in chemistry ‘a liquid in which something is dissolved’.
- *volume* — ‘loudness’ or ‘a book in a series’; in science ‘the amount of space something fills’.
- *argument* — ‘a quarrel’; in academic writing ‘a reasoned case for a position’ (*the central argument of the essay*).
- *literature* — ‘novels, poems, plays’; in research ‘the published studies on a topic’ (*a review of the literature*).
- *critical* — ‘disapproving’ or ‘very important’; in academic writing also ‘careful and analytical’ (*critical reading*).

::: warning Significant
In a scientific or social-science report, *significant* in the results section is understood as **statistically** significant. Do not write *There was a significant increase* unless a statistical test showed it; otherwise use *a large*, *substantial*, *marked* or *considerable increase*. In essays outside the sciences, *significant* in the general sense ‘important’ is acceptable, but it is heavily overused.
:::

::: quiz
In *A review of the literature shows that few studies have examined older learners*, what does *literature* mean?
- [ ] novels and poems
- [x] the published research on the topic
- [ ] printed leaflets
- [ ] the history of a subject
::: solution
In research writing, *the literature* is the body of published studies on a topic; *a literature review* summarises and evaluates them ([[academic-writing/literature-review]]). The general sense ‘creative writing’ does not fit here.
:::
:::

## Academic words by function

It is more useful to learn academic words in groups by what they do than in alphabetical lists. These groups, with examples written for this lesson, cover much of what academic writing needs.

| function | verbs | nouns and adjectives |
|---|---|---|
| describing research | *investigate*, *examine*, *measure*, *assess*, *identify*, *conduct* | *method*, *approach*, *data*, *sample*, *variable*, *procedure* |
| reporting findings | *show*, *indicate*, *suggest*, *reveal*, *demonstrate*, *find* | *evidence*, *result*, *finding*, *trend*, *pattern* |
| explaining causes | *cause*, *affect*, *contribute to*, *result in*, *lead to*, *derive from* | *factor*, *effect*, *impact*, *consequence*, *influence* |
| arguing | *argue*, *claim*, *assume*, *maintain*, *propose*, *challenge* | *assumption*, *hypothesis*, *theory*, *perspective*, *issue* |
| comparing | *differ*, *contrast*, *resemble*, *vary*, *correspond* | *similar*, *distinct*, *consistent*, *comparable*, *relative* |
| evaluating | *support*, *justify*, *question*, *undermine* | *valid*, *reliable*, *relevant*, *limited*, *significant*, *crucial* |

### Reporting verbs and strength

Reporting verbs differ in how strongly they commit the writer, in the same way that near-synonyms differ in strength ([[collocation-and-register/connotation]]):

- **strong**: *show*, *demonstrate*, *establish*, *prove* (rare in empirical research, where results support but seldom prove a claim);
- **moderate**: *indicate*, *find*, *reveal*;
- **cautious**: *suggest*, *imply*, *point to*;
- **the writer distances themself**: *claim*, *assert*, *allege* (all suggesting doubt in many contexts).

Choosing the right strength is part of hedging, the subject of [[modality/hedging-boosting]] and [[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]].

::: example Choosing a reporting verb {#ex-reporting}
Choose the best verb for each sentence and explain.

1. *The survey of 40 students ___ that many prefer printed textbooks.* (proves / suggests)
2. *Three large trials have ___ that the drug lowers blood pressure.* (demonstrated / implied)
3. *The company ___ that its product is the safest on the market, but provides no data.* (shows / claims)
4. *These results ___ a link between sleep and memory, but further work is needed.* (point to / establish)
::: solution
1. *suggests* — a small sample supports a cautious conclusion; *proves* is far too strong.
2. *demonstrated* — three large trials justify a strong verb.
3. *claims* — the writer signals doubt because no evidence is given.
4. *point to* — the writer explicitly says that more work is needed, so the verb must be cautious.
:::
:::

## Word families

Academic words come in families, and academic writing constantly moves between members: a verb in one sentence (*We analysed the data*) becomes a noun in the next (*The analysis showed …*). Knowing the family is therefore part of knowing the word.

| verb | noun | adjective | adverb |
|---|---|---|---|
| *analyse* (AmE *analyze*) | *analysis* (pl. *analyses*), *analyst* | *analytical* | *analytically* |
| *assume* | *assumption* | — | — |
| *define* | *definition* | *definite*, *definitive* | *definitely* |
| *vary* | *variation*, *variable*, *variety* | *various*, *variable* | *variously* |
| *signify* | *significance* | *significant* | *significantly* |
| *consist* (*of*) | *consistency* | *consistent* | *consistently* |
| *interpret* | *interpretation* | *interpretive* / *interpretative* | — |
| *establish* | *establishment* | *established* | — |

Two practical points. **Stress often moves** within the family: ***AN**alyse*, *a**NA**lysis*, *ana**LY**tical*; *de**FINE***, *defi**NI**tion*; ***SIG**nify*, *sig**NI**ficance*, *sig**NI**ficant*. Check stress in a dictionary rather than guessing ([[word-formation/suffixes]]). And **family members can differ in meaning**: *consistent* means ‘agreeing with’ or ‘unchanging’, not ‘consisting of’; *definite* means ‘certain, clear’ while *definitive* means ‘final, authoritative’.

::: example Choosing the family member {#ex-family}
Choose the correct form.

1. *The (analyse / analysis / analytical) of the interviews took three months.*
2. *These results are (consist / consistency / consistent) with earlier studies.*
3. *Income (various / varies / variation) considerably between regions.*
4. *The difference was not statistically (significance / significant / significantly).*
5. *Our (assume / assumption / assuming) was that all participants understood the questions.*
::: solution
1. *analysis* — a noun is needed as subject.
2. *consistent* — adjective after *are*; *consistent with* is the collocation.
3. *varies* — the verb; *varies considerably* is a common collocation.
4. *significant* — an adjective after *was*; the adverb *statistically* modifies it.
5. *assumption* — a noun after *Our*.
:::
:::

## Academic collocations

Academic words are as restricted in their partners as the general words of [[collocation-and-register/verb-noun-adjective-noun]]. Many errors in otherwise good academic writing are collocation errors: ✗ *make a research*, ✗ *make a conclusion*, ✗ *give an influence*.

::: theorem Learn academic words with their partners {#rule-collocations}
For each important academic noun, learn the **verbs** used with it, the **adjectives** used before it, and the **preposition** that follows it. Many academic nouns take a fixed preposition: *an effect on*, *an impact on*, *an influence on*, *an increase in*, *a decrease in*, *a rise in*, *an approach to*, *a solution to*, *a contribution to*, *an emphasis on*, *a focus on*, *the reason for*, *the need for*, *evidence of* / *for*, *a lack of*, *access to*.
:::

::: proof
Corpus studies of academic writing, from Biber et al. (1999) onwards, show that much academic prose is built from recurrent combinations: noun + preposition frames (*the effect of X on Y*, *an increase in*), verb + noun collocations (*conduct a study*, *draw a conclusion*) and longer frames (*it should be noted that*, *as a result of*, *in terms of*). The phrase lists mentioned above (Simpson-Vlach and Ellis 2010; Ackermann and Chen 2013) were built because these combinations are frequent and useful. For learners the prepositions are a particular difficulty, because Chinese uses different structures (对……的影响, 在……方面的增长): the English preposition must be learned with the noun ([[verb-patterns/prepositional-verbs]]).
:::

Some of the most useful academic collocations and frames:

- **verb + noun**: *conduct research* / *a study* / *an experiment*; *carry out an analysis*; *collect* / *gather data*; *draw* / *reach a conclusion*; *raise a question* / *an issue*; *address an issue* / *a problem*; *pose a threat* / *a challenge*; *play a role* (*a key* / *crucial* / *central role*); *have an effect* / *an impact on*; *provide evidence*; *take into account*; *shed light on*; *give rise to*.
- **adjective + noun**: *a key factor*, *a major factor*, *a significant difference*, *a wide range*, *a growing body of research*, *previous studies*, *recent research*, *further research*, *empirical evidence*, *a central argument*, *a crucial role*.
- **adverb + adjective / verb**: *statistically significant*, *closely related*, *widely used*, *widely accepted*, *highly likely*, *strongly suggest*, *vary considerably*, *increase sharply*.
- **frames**: *the extent to which …*, *in terms of …*, *as a result of …*, *on the basis of …*, *it is widely assumed that …*, *there is little evidence that …*, *further research is needed to …*.

::: example Editing an abstract {#ex-abstract}
Correct the collocations and word forms in this abstract written by a student.

> *This study made a research about the influence of part-time work to students' grades. We made a survey with 200 students and analysis the results. The results show that working more than 15 hours a week gives a negative impact for grades. We make the conclusion that universities should pay more attentions on this problem.*
::: solution
> *This study **investigated the effect of** part-time work **on** students' grades. We **conducted a survey of** 200 students and **analysed** the results. The results **indicate** that working more than 15 hours a week **has a negative impact on** grades. We **conclude** (or **draw the conclusion**) that universities should **pay more attention to** this issue.*

| original | problem | correction |
|---|---|---|
| *made a research about* | verb, countability, preposition | *investigated*, or *conducted research on* |
| *influence of … to* | preposition | *effect* / *influence of X on Y* |
| *made a survey with* | verb and preposition | *conducted* / *carried out a survey of* |
| *analysis the results* | word class | *analysed* (AmE *analyzed*) |
| *gives a negative impact for* | verb and preposition | *has a negative impact on* |
| *make the conclusion* | verb | *conclude* / *draw the conclusion* |
| *pay more attentions on* | countability and preposition | *pay more attention to* |
| *show* | strength (a single survey) | *indicate* or *suggest* |

*Problem* → *issue* is optional: *issue* is neutral, while *problem* states that something is wrong, which the writer may intend.
:::
:::

::: warning Uncountable academic nouns
Several of the most frequent academic nouns are **uncountable** in general use, and Chinese learners, whose nouns are not marked for number, often make them plural: ✗ *researches*, ✗ *evidences*, ✗ *informations*, ✗ *knowledges*, ✗ *advices*, ✗ *equipments*. Use *research*, *evidence*, *information*, *knowledge*, *advice*, *equipment*, and count them with *a piece of*, *a study* or *an item of* where needed (*two studies* rather than ✗ *two researches*). *Data* is plural in origin (*datum*, *data*); many scientific journals still write *the data are*, while *the data is* is common elsewhere — follow your field's style. Note also the irregular plurals *analysis*/*analyses*, *hypothesis*/*hypotheses*, *phenomenon*/*phenomena*, *criterion*/*criteria*.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Several high-frequency Chinese academic words have no single English equivalent, so they lead to some of the most persistent errors:

- **影响** is a noun and a verb in Chinese. In English: verb *affect* (*Sleep affects memory*) or *influence*; noun *effect* (*the effect of sleep on memory*), *impact* (often of large or sudden effects), *influence* (gradual, often on people or ideas). ✗ *Sleep effects memory* confuses *affect* and *effect*.
- **研究** → *research* (uncountable, the activity), *a study* / *studies* (countable, individual pieces of research), *investigate* / *study* / *examine* (verbs).
- **方面** → *aspect* is much less frequent in English than 方面 in Chinese. ✗ *In the aspect of education* → *In education*, *As regards education*, *In terms of education*, or nothing at all.
- **提出** → *propose* (a theory, a plan), *put forward* (an idea), *raise* (a question), *suggest* (a solution), *make* (a suggestion).
- **证明** → *prove* is much stronger in English than 证明 is in many Chinese contexts; *show*, *demonstrate* or *confirm* usually fit better.
- **显著** → *significant* in statistics; outside statistics, *marked*, *notable*, *clear*.
- **随着……的发展** → ✗ *With the development of …* at the start of a sentence is grammatical but extremely overused in Chinese learners' essays; prefer a specific statement: *As technology has advanced, …*, *Since the 1990s, …*.

Chinese academic writing also uses more four-character formulas and broad opening statements than English academic writing usually does. The English convention is to begin close to the specific topic and to use plain academic words precisely, rather than to accumulate impressive ones ([[academic-writing/academic-style]]).
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *The new policy has effected many families.*
- [x] *The new policy has affected many families.*
- [ ] *The new policy has had an affect on many families.*
- [ ] *The new policy has given an effect to many families.*
::: solution
*Affect* is the verb (‘have an influence on’); *effect* is the noun (*have an effect on*). *Effect* as a verb exists but means ‘bring about’ (*effect a change*, formal). *Give an effect to* is not a collocation.
:::
:::

## How to learn academic vocabulary

::: theorem A method for academic vocabulary {#rule-method}
1. **Use a list as a checklist**, not as a textbook: go through a published academic word list and mark the families you already know well, know partly, or do not know.
2. **Learn each family** with its forms, stress and spelling variants (*analyse* / *analyze*).
3. **Collect collocations and frames** from reading in your own field: when you meet *evidence*, note *provide evidence*, *evidence suggests*, *little evidence that*.
4. **Organise a personal phrasebook by function**: introducing a topic, describing methods, reporting results, comparing, concluding.
5. **Check before using**: in a learner's dictionary (look for the *formal* label and the example sentences), a collocation dictionary, or the academic section of a corpus.
6. **Use the words in your own writing**, and look for them in feedback: a tutor's corrections of academic collocations are among the most useful feedback you can receive.
:::

Freely available resources (as of 2026) include the published academic word lists themselves, the academic sections of the corpora in [[collocation-and-register/using-a-corpus]], and university writing-centre websites. One widely used example is the *Academic Phrasebank* of the University of Manchester (compiled by John Morley), which collects phrases by function — introducing work, referring to sources, describing methods, reporting results — and can serve as a model for a personal phrasebook.

::: example From draft to academic {#ex-upgrade}
Rewrite this paragraph in an academic register, using at least six academic words or collocations from this chapter.

> *A lot of cities have bike-sharing schemes now. People think they cut traffic and pollution, but we don't really know if that's true, because there isn't much proof. Some studies found that people mostly use the bikes instead of walking, not instead of driving. So the schemes might not help as much as people say.*
::: solution
> *Bike-sharing schemes are now **widely used** in cities. **It is widely assumed** that they **reduce** traffic and pollution, but **there is little empirical evidence** to **support** this **assumption**. **Several studies** have **found** that users mainly replace walking, rather than driving, with cycling. The **environmental impact** of these schemes may therefore be **more limited** than is often **claimed**.*

Academic words and collocations used: *widely used*, *it is widely assumed that*, *reduce*, *there is little (empirical) evidence*, *support … assumption*, *several studies have found*, *environmental impact*, *limited*, *claimed*. Note also what changed beyond vocabulary: *we don't really know* became an impersonal statement about evidence; *might not help as much as people say* became a hedged conclusion with *may* and *therefore*; *proof* (too strong) became *evidence*. The revised paragraph is not longer, but it is more precise.
:::
:::

::: history
Lists of the vocabulary needed for academic study have a history of their own. After Michael West's *General Service List* (1953) defined a core of general words, researchers looked for the words university students needed beyond it: Campion and Elley (1971) in New Zealand, Praninskas (1972) with an *American University Word List*, and Xue and Nation (1984), whose *University Word List* combined earlier lists. Averil Coxhead's *Academic Word List* (2000), based on a purpose-built corpus and explicit criteria of range and frequency, became the most widely used. Since then, larger corpora have produced the *Academic Vocabulary List* (Gardner and Davies 2014), and attention has turned to multi-word units, with the *Academic Formulas List* (Simpson-Vlach and Ellis 2010) and the *Academic Collocation List* (Ackermann and Chen 2013).
:::

::: summary
- Vocabulary has three layers: **general** high-frequency words, **academic** words common across subjects, and **technical** words of one field.
- Coxhead's **Academic Word List** (2000) gave 570 families covering about 10% of her academic corpus; later lists cover larger corpora and multi-word units.
- Learn academic words **deliberately**, by **function** (describing research, reporting, explaining causes, arguing, comparing, evaluating), as **families**, and **with collocations**.
- Many words have **special academic senses**: *significant* (statistics), *literature* (published research), *argument* (a reasoned case).
- **Reporting verbs** differ in strength: *demonstrate* — *indicate* — *suggest* — *claim*.
- Academic nouns have fixed **prepositions** (*effect on*, *increase in*, *approach to*) and **verbs** (*conduct research*, *draw a conclusion*, *play a role*); several are **uncountable** (*research*, *evidence*, *information*).
- Chinese words such as 影响, 研究, 方面 and 证明 map onto several English words; choose by meaning and collocation, and prefer precise plain words to impressive ones.
:::

> *A number of studies have **suggested** that online learning **has a considerable influence on** students' motivation. As technology has advanced, **a growing number of** students have taken online courses. However, **there is some evidence** that students may feel isolated. **Further research** is therefore needed on this issue.*

Changes: *In the aspect of online learning* → deleted (the topic is clear); *many researches have proved* → *A number of studies have suggested* (countable *studies*; cautious verb); *gives a big influence to* → *has a considerable influence on*; *With the development of technology* → *As technology has advanced*; *more and more* → *a growing number of*; *But* → *However*; *some evidences show* → *there is some evidence*; *feel lonely* → *may feel isolated* (hedge; more precise); *So we need to do more researches about this problem* → *Further research is therefore needed on this issue*.
:::
