Gao Feng works in the international office of a company in Hangzhou. A partner from Manchester, Ms Davies, is coming to visit, and he writes to her:

> *Dear Ms Davies, Welcome to our company to visit! We will open a meeting at 3 p.m. to discuss the cooperation. Please call me when you arrive the airport, and I will drive you to the hotel. Our manager has a lot of opinions about the new design, so we can carry out a deep discussion. You must be very tired after the long flight. Add oil for the presentation!*

Ms Davies will understand almost all of it. She will smile at *open a meeting* and *add oil*, wonder whether the manager is unhappy with the design, and perhaps feel slightly patronised by *You must be very tired*. Nothing in the email is careless: each phrase is a faithful translation of an ordinary Chinese expression — 欢迎来我们公司参观, 开会, 到机场, 有很多意见, 进行深入讨论, 你一定很累了, 加油. The problem is that a faithful translation of the **words** is not a faithful translation of the **meaning**.

This chapter is about such translation traps. It explains what Chinglish (中式英语) is and why it arises, sorts the traps into types — one Chinese word with many English equivalents, transferred collocations, false friends, Chinese-style wordiness, and social formulas — and gives a method for finding the natural English expression. It brings together ideas from the whole course: collocation ([[collocation-and-register/what-is-collocation]]), checking in a corpus ([[collocation-and-register/using-a-corpus]]), register and connotation ([[collocation-and-register/connotation]]). (Gao Feng's email is rewritten in [[#ex-gao]].)

## What Chinglish is

::: definition Chinglish {#def-chinglish}
**Chinglish** (中式英语) is English produced under the influence of Chinese — its words, collocations, word order, or social conventions — in a way that is understandable but differs from what users of standard English would say. Typical examples are word-for-word translations such as ✗ *open the light* (开灯), ✗ *learn knowledge* (学习知识) and ✗ *Welcome to visit* (欢迎参观).
:::

Chinglish is the result of **transfer**: the normal process by which learners use what they know from their first language to fill gaps in a new one. Every group of learners does this. German speakers say ✗ *I become a coffee* (*bekommen* = ‘get’), Spanish speakers ✗ *I am constipated* for ‘I have a cold’ (*constipado*); English speakers learning Chinese produce their own "English-style Chinese". Transfer is not a sign of carelessness or poor intelligence; it is how language learning works. The aim of this chapter is not to laugh at it but to make it a matter of choice: a learner who knows where the traps are can choose the expression that will be understood as intended by an international reader.

::: definition China English {#def-china-english}
**China English** is a different idea: the standard English used to talk about Chinese things that English has no word for, such as *the Spring Festival*, *the gaokao*, *hukou* (household registration), *mahjong* or *dim sum*. These expressions are not errors; they are the normal way that English, like every language, names the realities of a particular place ([[world-englishes/english-in-china]]). Some have passed into general English: *lose face* (丢脸) and *kowtow* (叩头) are old borrowings, and *long time no see* is often said to come from Chinese Pidgin English, although its origin is not certain.
:::

::: quiz
Which of these is an example of China English rather than Chinglish?
- [ ] *Please open the air conditioner.*
- [x] *Many families travel home for the Spring Festival.*
- [ ] *I learned a lot of knowledge.*
- [ ] *Welcome to visit our school!*
::: solution
*The Spring Festival* is the standard English name for 春节, used by English-language media and dictionaries: it names a Chinese reality that English has no other word for. The other three are word-for-word translations of Chinese expressions for which ordinary English expressions exist: *turn on the air conditioning*, *I learned a lot*, *You are welcome to visit our school* (or *Welcome to our school!*).
:::
:::

## Trap 1: one Chinese word, many English words

The most frequent source of Chinglish is the assumption that a Chinese word has one English equivalent. Very common Chinese verbs — 开, 打, 做, 搞, 上 — have ranges of use so wide that English divides them among many different verbs, chosen by the noun.

::: widget graph
nodes: 开@-6,0; 打@6,0; open (a door or a shop)@-10,4; turn on (a light or the TV)@-10,2; drive (a car)@-10,0; have or hold (a meeting)@-10,-2; prescribe (medicine)@-10,-4; start or run (a company)@-6,-5; play (basketball)@10,4; make (a phone call)@10,2; take (a taxi)@10,0; type (a document)@10,-2; give (a discount)@10,-4; work part-time (打工)@6,-5

title: 开 and 打 in English
caption: Some common uses of 开 and 打 and the English verbs that express them: 开门, 开灯, 开车, 开会, 开药, 开公司; 打篮球, 打电话, 打车, 打字, 打折, 打工. The English verb is chosen by the noun, not by the Chinese verb, so ✗ *open the light*, ✗ *open a meeting*, ✗ *hit a phone call* and ✗ *hit basketball* are all traps. (*Open a meeting* exists, but means ‘formally start it’, as the chair does.) Drag the boxes to rearrange them.
:::

::: theorem Translate from the noun, not from the verb {#rule-from-noun}
When a Chinese verb + noun combination has to be put into English, take the **noun** as the starting point, find its English equivalent, and then find the verb that English uses with that noun (in a learner's dictionary, a collocation dictionary or a corpus). Do not translate the Chinese verb first.
:::

::: proof
This is the base-and-collocator principle of [[collocation-and-register/what-is-collocation#rule-direction]] applied to translation. In both languages the noun keeps its ordinary meaning and the general verb is the restricted, conventional choice; but the two languages make their conventional choices independently. Chinese 开 extends from ‘open’ to ‘start’ and ‘operate’ (开车, 开公司, 开会) and to ‘write out’ (开药, 开发票); English spreads the same meanings across *drive*, *run*, *have*, *prescribe*, *issue*. No general rule maps 开 onto English, so the only reliable path runs through the noun, whose English collocations can be looked up. The same holds for adjectives: translate 浓 from *tea*, *fog* or *interest*, not from 浓 ([[collocation-and-register/verb-noun-adjective-noun#rule-intensity]]).
:::

::: example 开 and 打 {#ex-kai-da}
Translate the bold phrases.

1. 天黑了，**开灯**吧。
2. 医生给我**开了点药**。
3. 我们明天上午**开会**。
4. 他大学毕业后**开了一家咖啡店**。
5. 下雨了，我们**打车**回去。
6. 这件外套**打八折**。
7. 他暑假在餐馆**打工**。
::: solution
1. *It's getting dark — let's **turn on** (or **switch on**) **the light**.*
2. *The doctor **prescribed** me some medicine* (or *gave me a prescription*).
3. *We're **having a meeting** tomorrow morning* (more formally, *A meeting will be held* …).
4. *After university he **opened a café*** (or ***started*** / ***set up a café***; *runs a café* for the ongoing business).
5. *It's raining — let's **take a taxi** back* (or ***get a taxi***).
6. *This coat is **20% off*** (or *has a 20% discount*). Note the arithmetic: 八折 means the customer pays 80%, so the discount is 20% — a classic trap in itself.
7. *He **works part-time** in a restaurant during the summer holidays* (AmE *summer vacation*; or *has a summer job* in a restaurant).
:::
:::

## Trap 2: transferred collocations

The second type keeps the right words but combines them in the Chinese way.

| Chinese | ✗ word for word | ✓ natural English |
|---|---|---|
| 学习知识 · 学到很多知识 | *learn knowledge*, *learn a lot of knowledge* | *gain* / *acquire knowledge*, *learn a lot* |
| 提高英语水平 | *improve my English level* | *improve my English* |
| 看书 | *see a book* | *read* (*a book*) |
| 讲话 | *say a speech* | *give* / *make a speech* |
| 很大的进步 | *get big progress* | *make great progress* |
| 增长知识 | *increase knowledge* | *broaden* / *expand your knowledge* |
| 上网 | *go up the internet* | *go online* |
| 交朋友 | *hand in friends* | *make friends* |
| 很高的要求 | *tall requirements* | *high standards*, *demanding requirements* |

Two of these are worth looking at closely, because they are so frequent in learners' writing.

**学习知识.** English *learn* normally takes as its object a skill or a subject (*learn French*, *learn to drive*, *learn about history*) or a piece of information (*learn the truth*). *Knowledge* is what you have once you have learned; the verbs that go with it are *gain*, *acquire*, *have*, *share*, *apply* and *broaden*. So ✗ *I learned a lot of knowledge at university* becomes *I learned a lot at university* or *I gained a lot of knowledge* (more formal). This is the kind of question a corpus answers in seconds ([[collocation-and-register/using-a-corpus#rule-check]]).

**提高水平.** English *level* is used for measurable quantities (*the level of pollution*, *water levels*) and for stages in a course (*a beginners' level*, *level 3*), but rarely for a person's ability in a skill. *Improve my English level* is understood, but the natural form is simply *improve my English*, or *raise the standard of …* in formal writing.

::: warning "Very" and "more and more"
Two habits from Chinese 很 and 越来越 make learner writing sound flat: using *very* with every adjective (✗ *very delicious*, ✗ *very excellent*, ✗ *very unique*), and *more and more* in almost every paragraph. Extreme adjectives do not take *very* (*absolutely delicious*, *excellent*; *unique* means ‘the only one’, so ✗ *very unique*; [[collocation-and-register/connotation]]). And *more and more* can often be replaced by a verb or adjective of change: *increasingly popular*, *a growing number of*, *Prices continue to rise*.
:::

## Trap 3: false friends

::: definition False friend {#def-false-friend}
A **false friend** is a word in one language that looks or seems equivalent to a word in another, but differs in meaning, range of use or connotation. Between European languages, false friends often look alike (*actual* in English ‘real’, *actuel* in French ‘current’); between Chinese and English, they are usually **translation equivalents** that a dictionary pairs but that do not match in all contexts.
:::

| Chinese | usual dictionary equivalent | the trap |
|---|---|---|
| 意见 | *opinion* | 有意见 usually means ‘have a complaint or objection’ (*I have some concerns about …*); 提意见 ‘give feedback, make suggestions’. *Opinion* is just a view. |
| 便宜 | *cheap* | *Cheap* can mean ‘low quality’ (*cheap furniture*) and, of people, ‘unwilling to spend money’ (AmE). For a good price: *inexpensive*, *affordable*, *good value*, *reasonably priced*. |
| 紧张 | *nervous* | 时间紧张 *a tight schedule*; 关系紧张 *tense relations*; 供应紧张 *a shortage*. *Nervous* is only about a person's feeling. |
| 老板 | *boss* | *Boss* is the person you work for (often a manager); for the owner of a shop or restaurant, *owner* or *manager*. |
| 宣传 | *propaganda* | *Propaganda* is strongly negative ([[collocation-and-register/connotation]]); *publicity*, *promotion*, *campaign*. |
| 知识分子 | *intellectual* | *Intellectual* means someone who works with ideas (writers, thinkers); for educated people generally, *educated people*, *professionals*, *graduates*. |
| 同情 | *sympathy* | Close in meaning, but *I sympathise with you* can also mean ‘I agree with your view’; and *Sympathy* (on a card) is used for a death. |
| 干部 | *cadre* | *Cadre* exists in English but is rare and specialised; *official*, *officer*, *manager* or *staff member*, depending on context. |

::: example 意见 and 便宜 {#ex-false-friends}
Translate the sentences so that the meaning is not misunderstood.

1. 经理对这个计划有意见。
2. 欢迎大家多提意见。
3. 这家酒店很便宜，服务也好。
4. 下周我的时间很紧张。
::: solution
1. *The manager has some concerns* (or *objections*) *about the plan.* — Not ✗ *has opinions about the plan*, which says only that he has views.
2. *Your feedback* (or *comments*, *suggestions*) *would be very welcome.* — A natural English formula in this situation; ✗ *Welcome to give more opinions* is a word-for-word translation.
3. *This hotel is very reasonably priced* (or *good value*, *affordable*), *and the service is good too.* — *Cheap* is possible in informal speech, but next to praise for the service it may suggest low quality.
4. *My schedule is very tight next week* (or *I'm very busy next week*). — Not ✗ *My time is very nervous*.
:::
:::

## Trap 4: Chinese-style wordiness

The fourth type is less visible, because every word is correct. It concerns the way ideas are packaged.

::: theorem Avoid empty verbs and category nouns {#rule-empty}
Chinese formal writing often uses general verbs such as 进行, 加以, 给予, 做出 with an action noun (进行调查, 加以分析), and category nouns after a noun (交通问题, 学习方面, 清洁工作). Translated word for word, these give **empty verbs** (*carry out an investigation*, *make an analysis*) and **redundant category nouns** (*the problem of traffic problems*, *in the aspect of study*). In English, prefer the **verb** where the action is the point, and drop category nouns that add nothing.
:::

::: proof
Lian Shuneng's *English–Chinese Comparative Studies* (连淑能《英汉对比研究》) describes a general contrast between the two languages: English often expresses actions with nouns, Chinese more often with verbs. That might suggest that English likes 进行 + noun structures. But the English nominal style works differently: it uses a noun **instead of** a verb, packing an action into a noun phrase (*the investigation found …*), not a dummy verb **plus** a noun (*we carried out an investigation and the investigation found …*). Where the action is the main point of the clause, English uses the plain verb (*We investigated the complaint*). Style guides for English writing, such as Williams and Bizup's *Style*, recommend exactly this: turn empty verb + noun combinations back into verbs. Some verb + noun combinations are of course normal (*carry out research*, *conduct a survey*, *make a decision*) — the test is whether a simple verb would say the same more directly.
:::

::: example Unpacking 进行 {#ex-jinxing}
Rewrite each sentence more directly.

1. *We carried out a discussion of the problem of traffic.* (我们对交通问题进行了讨论)
2. *The teacher gave praise to the students' performance.* (老师对学生的表现给予了表扬)
3. *We should make improvements to the aspect of service quality.* (我们应该在服务质量方面加以改进)
4. *The government has made great efforts in the work of environmental protection.* (政府在环保工作方面做出了很大努力)
::: solution
1. *We discussed the traffic problem* (or *the traffic*).
2. *The teacher praised the students' performance.*
3. *We should improve the quality of our service* (or *improve our service*).
4. *The government has worked hard to protect the environment* (or *has made great efforts to protect the environment*).

In each case the action noun becomes a verb (*discuss*, *praise*, *improve*, *protect*) and the category nouns (*the problem of*, *the aspect of*, *the work of*) disappear. Sentence 4 keeps *make great efforts* as an alternative, because that combination is natural in English.
:::
:::

## Trap 5: social formulas

The last type is the hardest to see, because it is not about words at all. Every language has fixed expressions for social situations — greeting, thanking, encouraging, saying goodbye — and these formulas are rarely translations of each other ([[core-vocabulary/phrases-and-chunks]]).

| Chinese formula | ✗ word for word | what English speakers usually say |
|---|---|---|
| 欢迎参观 / 欢迎光临 | *Welcome to visit* | *Welcome!* / *Welcome to …* / *You're welcome to visit any time.* |
| 加油！ | *Add oil!* | *Good luck!* / *You can do it!* / *Come on!* (sport) / *Go for it!* |
| 辛苦了 | *You are hard-working.* | *Thanks for all your hard work.* / *You must be tired — thanks so much.* |
| 慢走 | *Walk slowly.* | *Take care.* / *Get home safely.* / *See you.* |
| 注意安全 | *Pay attention to safety.* | *Be careful.* / *Take care.* / *Stay safe.* |
| 吃了吗？ (as a greeting) | *Have you eaten?* | *How are you?* / *How's it going?* / *Hi!* |
| 您先请 | *You first please.* | *After you.* |

The formulas differ not only in words but sometimes in the social act: in English, *You must be very tired* to a guest after a flight can sound like a comment on their appearance, whereas *How was your flight?* invites them to say how they feel. Such differences are the subject of [[pragmatics/small-talk-norms]] and [[pragmatics/directness-across-cultures]].

*Add oil*, interestingly, has begun to cross over: as a borrowing from Hong Kong English it now appears in some major English dictionaries, labelled as Hong Kong English. That makes it China English or Hong Kong English, not an error — but a reader in Manchester may still not know it, so for an international audience *Good luck!* is safer.

::: quiz
A British colleague has just finished a long, difficult project for your team. What would be most natural to say?
- [ ] *You are very hard!*
- [ ] *You have worked hard, you are a hard worker.*
- [x] *Thanks so much for all your hard work on this.*
- [ ] *Add oil!*
::: solution
*Thanks (so much) for all your hard work* is the English formula for the situation of 辛苦了: it thanks the person rather than describing them. *You are very hard* means ‘you are unkind’ or ‘difficult’. *Add oil* is for encouragement before something, not thanks after it.
:::
:::

::: example Rewriting Gao Feng's email {#ex-gao}
Rewrite Gao Feng's email from the start of the chapter, and list the traps.
::: solution
> *Dear Ms Davies,*
>
> *We are very much looking forward to welcoming you to our company. We have arranged a meeting at 3 p.m. to discuss the partnership. Please call me when you arrive at the airport, and I will drive you to your hotel. Our manager has a few concerns about the new design, so it would be good to discuss it in detail. I hope you have a good flight, and good luck with the presentation!*
>
> *Best regards,*
> *Gao Feng*

| original | type of trap | correction |
|---|---|---|
| *Welcome to our company to visit!* | social formula (欢迎来参观) | *We are looking forward to welcoming you* |
| *open a meeting* | one-to-many (开会) | *have* / *arrange a meeting* |
| *the cooperation* | collocation and register (合作) | *the partnership* or *our work together*; *cooperation* is fine in general (*thank you for your cooperation*) but not for a business relationship |
| *arrive the airport* | grammar (到) | *arrive at the airport* |
| *has a lot of opinions about* | false friend (有意见) | *has a few concerns about* |
| *carry out a deep discussion* | wordiness and collocation (进行深入讨论) | *discuss it in detail* |
| *You must be very tired after the long flight.* | social formula (一路辛苦了) | *I hope you have a good flight* (before) or *How was your flight?* (on arrival) |
| *Add oil for the presentation!* | social formula (加油) | *Good luck with the presentation!* |

Only one correction (*arrive at*) is grammatical; all the others concern collocation, meaning, register or social convention.
:::
:::

## Finding the natural expression

::: theorem A method for translation traps {#rule-method}
1. **Translate the meaning, not the words.** Ask: what is happening, who is talking to whom, and what does the speaker want to achieve?
2. **Start from the English base word** (usually the noun) and find its collocations in a learner's dictionary, a collocation dictionary or a corpus.
3. **Check connotation and register** of evaluative words ([[collocation-and-register/connotation]], [[collocation-and-register/register]]).
4. **For social situations, ask what English speakers say in that situation**, not how to translate the Chinese formula.
5. **Prefer the plain verb** to an empty verb + noun, and drop category nouns that add nothing.
6. **Check suspicious combinations** in a corpus of English written by expert users; do not rely on web hits or automatically collected bilingual examples.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Machine translation and AI tools now handle many of these traps well — they usually produce *turn on the light*, not *open the light* — but not all, and they can introduce errors of their own, especially with context-dependent words such as 意见, 紧张 and 加油, and with tone. They are useful for a first draft or to check an idea, but the method above still applies: a learner should be able to see whether the result is natural. The study of translation between the two languages, including the systematic differences in sentence structure (形合 and 意合, nominal and verbal styles), is the subject of the *Translation* course: [[translation/english-chinese-compared]] and [[translation/chinese-into-english]].
:::

::: history
Contact between English and Chinese produced a trade pidgin, Chinese Pidgin English, in Canton (Guangzhou) from the eighteenth century; a few words from that period, such as *chop-chop*, survive in English. In 1980 the translator and lexicographer Ge Chuangui (葛传槼) proposed the term *China English* for the legitimate English expressions needed to talk about China, distinguishing it from errors of transfer, and the distinction has been discussed by Chinese scholars ever since. Joan Pinkham's *The Translator's Guide to Chinglish* (《中式英语之鉴》, 2000), based on years of editing English translations in Beijing, catalogued the commonest types of Chinglish in formal writing, especially wordiness. Before the 2008 Olympic Games, Beijing and other cities began systematic programmes to standardise the English on public signs, and national guidelines for English in public services followed.
:::

## Where this leads

The systematic differences between English and Chinese that lie behind many of these traps are explained in [[translation/english-chinese-compared]] and [[translation/translating-words]]. China English and the status of English in China are discussed in [[world-englishes/english-in-china]]. The social formulas of Trap 5 belong to pragmatics: [[pragmatics/small-talk-norms]] and [[pragmatics/directness-across-cultures]]. The wordiness of Trap 4 is part of good style in any writing: [[style-and-rhetoric/concision]].

::: summary
- **Chinglish** (中式英语) is English shaped by Chinese words, collocations or conventions; it results from normal **transfer** and should be described neutrally. **China English** names Chinese realities (*the Spring Festival*, *hukou*) and is not an error.
- **One Chinese word, many English words**: 开 → *open*, *turn on*, *drive*, *have a meeting*, *prescribe*; 打 → *play*, *make a call*, *take a taxi*, *type*. Translate **from the noun**.
- **Transferred collocations**: ✗ *learn knowledge* → *gain knowledge* / *learn a lot*; ✗ *improve my English level* → *improve my English*.
- **False friends**: 意见 ≠ *opinion* (often ‘concern’), 便宜 ≠ always *cheap*, 紧张 ≠ always *nervous*, 宣传 ≠ *propaganda*.
- **Wordiness**: replace empty verb + noun (进行讨论 → *discuss*) and drop category nouns (问题, 方面, 工作).
- **Social formulas**: say what English speakers say in the situation (加油 → *Good luck!*; 辛苦了 → *Thanks for all your hard work*).
- Use the **method**: meaning first, base word, collocation check, connotation and register, situation, corpus.
:::

::: exercise Edit a notice {level=3}
A school wrote this notice for international parents. Rewrite it in natural English, keeping all the information, and list the changes.

> *Warm welcome to the parents to visit our school on Open Day! Please pay attention to safety in the car park. We will open a meeting in the hall at 10:00 to make an introduction of our teaching work. If you have any opinions, welcome to raise them. Thank you for your cooperation!*
::: hint
Check each social formula, each 开/进行-type verb, and the false friend 意见.
:::
::: solution
> *Welcome to our Open Day! Please take care in the car park. At 10:00 there will be a meeting in the hall, where we will introduce our teaching. If you have any questions or comments, please let us know. Thank you!*

Changes: *Warm welcome to the parents to visit* → *Welcome to our Open Day!* (social formula); *pay attention to safety* → *take care* (formula); *open a meeting* → *there will be a meeting* (开会); *make an introduction of our teaching work* → *introduce our teaching* (empty verb + category noun); *opinions* → *questions or comments* (false friend 意见); *welcome to raise them* → *please let us know* (formula); *Thank you for your cooperation* is correct English but sounds like an instruction in this context (it suggests rules to obey), so *Thank you!* is friendlier; keep *Thank you for your cooperation* if the notice is about rules.
:::
:::
