Chen Jing is spending a term at a university in Cape Town. On her first morning she asks a lab technician when the equipment will be ready. *I'll do it just now*, he says, and she waits by the bench. Twenty minutes later she asks again. *Now-now*, he says with a smile, and this time it is ready in two minutes. A week later a visiting lecturer from Dublin tells her *I'm after reading your report*, and she wonders for a moment whether he has read it yet. At a conference dinner a Canadian student tells her about a [hʌʊs] on the lake, and an Australian colleague asks whether she'll be at the barbie on Sunday arvo.

Nothing in these sentences is a mistake. *Just now* in South African English means ‘in a while, later’, and *now-now* means ‘very soon’; *I'm after reading it* in Irish English means ‘I have just read it’; the Canadian vowel in *house* and the Australian words *barbie* ‘barbecue’ and *arvo* ‘afternoon’ are ordinary features of standard varieties spoken by millions. Each of these countries is in Kachru's Inner Circle ([[world-englishes/models#def-circles]]): English is the first language of most of the population, or of a large part of it. Yet each has its own English, shaped by who settled there, when, and which other languages they met.

This chapter describes seven of them: the Englishes of Ireland, Scotland and Wales, which grew up beside Celtic languages, and those of Canada, Australia, New Zealand and South Africa, which grew up in settler colonies. It uses Wells's lexical sets ([[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet#def-lexical-set]]) to describe vowels, and it builds on the comparison of British and American English in [[world-englishes/british-american]].

## How new varieties form

::: definition Dialect, accent and variety {#def-variety}
A **dialect** is a form of a language with its own grammar and vocabulary, and usually its own pronunciation, shared by a regional or social group; an **accent** is a way of pronouncing ([[listening-and-conversation/accents#def-accent]]). **Variety** is the neutral term linguists use for any distinguishable form of a language — a national standard, a regional dialect, a social dialect — without implying anything about its status.
:::

When English-speaking settlers from many parts of Britain and Ireland arrived in a new country, they brought many dialects with them. Their children did not speak any one of these; they spoke a new mixture. The British linguist Peter Trudgill, in *New-Dialect Formation: The Inevitability of Colonial Englishes* (2004), described how this happens, using recordings of the first New Zealanders born to settlers, made in the 1940s and studied by the Origins of New Zealand English project (Gordon et al. 2004).

::: theorem Stages of new-dialect formation {#rule-new-dialect}
According to Trudgill (2004), when speakers of different dialects settle together, a new dialect forms over about three generations:

1. **Rudimentary levelling** — adult settlers drop some of the most local features of their own speech.
2. **Variability** — the first generation born in the colony speaks with a great deal of individual variation, mixing features of their parents' and neighbours' dialects.
3. **Focusing** — the next generation settles on a single shared variety. The **majority** form usually wins: features used by most of the original settlers survive, and minority features disappear.

Some minority features survive with new functions (**reallocation**), and the new variety may develop forms that none of the original dialects had.
:::

::: proof
The theory explains why Australian, New Zealand and South African English sound alike in many respects. All three were settled largely in the nineteenth century, from the same mixture of southern English, other English, Scottish and Irish speakers, with southern English speakers the largest group. Majority features of the mixture — non-rhoticity, the long /ɑː/ in BATH words — became features of the new varieties. American English, by contrast, was settled earlier, in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, before non-rhoticity had spread in southern England, and from a different mixture, which helps to explain why it is rhotic. Trudgill's claim that the outcome is largely determined by the proportions of the mixture has been debated: other researchers give more weight to social factors such as prestige and identity, as Schneider's Dynamic Model does ([[world-englishes/models#def-dynamic]]). But the stages themselves are well documented.
:::

## Ireland

English came to Ireland with the Anglo-Norman invasion of the twelfth century, but it became the language of most of the population much later: through the plantations of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and above all in the nineteenth century, when the Great Famine (1845–1852), emigration and English-medium schooling led most Irish speakers to shift to English within a few generations. Irish (Gaelic) is today the first official language of the Republic under the Constitution of 1937, with English as the second; English is the first language of most people. **Irish English** (also called Hiberno-English) shows the influence of Irish, especially in grammar, because a whole population learned English as a second language and passed it on to their children.

::: theorem Some features of Irish English {#rule-irish}
1. **The *after* perfect**: *be after* + *-ing* for a recent completed action: *I'm after finishing the report* ‘I have just finished the report’. It corresponds to an Irish construction with *tar éis* ‘after’.
2. **Habitual *do be***: *He does be tired in the evenings* ‘he is usually tired in the evenings’.
3. **Plural *you***: *ye* or *youse* for the plural (*Are ye coming?*).
4. **Embedded inversion**: *I wonder is he coming* (Standard English *I wonder whether he is coming*).
5. **Clefting for emphasis**: *It's tired you are* ‘you are tired’, with the focused element brought forward after *it's*.
6. **Pronunciation**: rhotic; FACE and GOAT often single vowels [eː], [oː]; /θ/ and /ð/ often dental stops [t̪], [d̪] (*think*, *this*); /l/ clear in all positions.
:::

::: example Reading Irish English {#ex-irish}
Explain the meaning of each sentence in Standard English, and name the feature. (a) *She's after telling me the news.* (b) *Ye'll be hungry after the journey.* (c) *They do be working late on Fridays.* (d) *I asked him was he ready.*
::: solution
1. (a) ‘She has just told me the news’ — the *after* perfect ([[#rule-irish]], point 1). The meaning is of a **recent** completed event, not a future one; a learner who reads *after* as ‘later’ will misunderstand.
2. (b) ‘You (plural) will be hungry after the journey’ — plural *ye* (point 3).
3. (c) ‘They usually work late on Fridays’ — habitual *do be* (point 2).
4. (d) ‘I asked him whether he was ready’ — embedded inversion (point 4): the question word order of *Was he ready?* is kept inside the reported question.

All four are standard in Irish English speech, and (a), (c) and (d) also appear in the writing of Irish authors who represent dialogue.
:::
:::

## Scotland

Scotland has two closely related forms of speech derived from Old English: **Scots**, which developed from the Northumbrian dialect of Old English and was the language of the Scottish court and of literature until the seventeenth century, and **Scottish Standard English**, Standard English spoken with a Scottish accent. Many speakers move along a continuum between them according to setting. Scots is recognised by the UK as a regional or minority language under the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages, and in Scotland's 2011 census about one and a half million people reported that they could speak it. Gaelic, a Celtic language, is spoken mainly in the Highlands and Islands.

::: theorem Some features of Scottish English {#rule-scottish}
1. **Rhotic**: /r/ after vowels, often as a tap [ɾ] (*car* [kaɾ]).
2. **Fewer vowel contrasts**: no distinction between FOOT and GOOSE (*pull* and *pool* are homophones, [pʉl]), and often none between TRAP and PALM, or between LOT and THOUGHT (*cot* and *caught* sound the same).
3. **Single vowels in FACE and GOAT**: [e], [o].
4. **The Scottish Vowel Length Rule**: vowel length depends on the following sound — vowels are long before /r/, before voiced fricatives and at the end of a morpheme, and short elsewhere — so *brewed* has a long vowel and *brood* a short one.
5. **/x/** in *loch* and in Scots words and names.
6. **Words** such as *wee* ‘small’, *outwith* ‘outside’, *kirk* ‘church’, *bairn* ‘child’, *ken* ‘know’ (Scots).
7. **Grammar**: *I'll not* beside *I won't*; *need* + participle (*The car needs washed*).
:::

The Scottish Vowel Length Rule is a good example of how an accent can be systematic in a way the reference accents are not. In GB, the difference between *brewed* and *brood* is not a matter of vowel length; in Scottish English it is, and it is predictable from the grammar of the word (*brewed* is *brew* + *-ed*).

## Wales

English spread in Wales from the sixteenth century, and especially with industrialisation in the nineteenth; Welsh, a Celtic language, remains the first language of a substantial minority, particularly in the north and west. The Welsh Language Act 1993 gave Welsh equal standing with English in public business in Wales, and the Welsh Language (Wales) Measure 2011 gave it official status. **Welsh English** varies a great deal between the Welsh-speaking north and west and the industrial south-east. Features described for many speakers (Wells 1982, vol. 2) include a clear /l/ in all positions, FACE and GOAT often as single vowels, consonants lengthened between vowels (*money* [ˈmʌnːi]), the invariant tag *isn't it?* (*You're coming tomorrow, isn't it?*), and the fronting of a phrase for focus (*Coal they were carrying*), which corresponds to a pattern of Welsh. Most varieties are non-rhotic.

::: quiz
A speaker says *I'm after phoning the office.* Which variety is it most likely to be, and what does it mean?
- [ ] Scottish English; ‘I will phone the office later.’
- [x] Irish English; ‘I have just phoned the office.’
- [ ] South African English; ‘I am about to phone the office.’
- [ ] Canadian English; ‘I am looking for the office’s number.’
::: solution
The *be after* + *-ing* perfect is a well-known feature of Irish English ([[#rule-irish]]), expressing a recently completed action. It never means ‘later’.
:::
:::

## Canada

**Canadian English** grew from the English of Loyalists, supporters of the British Crown who left the United States during and after the American Revolution (1775–1783), joined later by large numbers of settlers from Britain and Ireland. As a result it is close to American English in pronunciation and much vocabulary, but has kept some British conventions. French is the other official language under the Official Languages Act of 1969, and Canadian English has borrowed from it (*poutine*, *tuque* or *toque* ‘knitted hat’).

::: theorem Some features of Canadian English {#rule-canadian}
1. **Rhotic**, like GA, and generally with GA-like vowels and tapped /t/.
2. **Canadian raising**: the diphthongs of PRICE and MOUTH begin with a higher, more central vowel before voiceless consonants. *Write* is [rʌɪt] but *ride* is [raɪd]; *about* and *house* have [ʌʊ], while *loud* and *houses* do not.
3. **The LOT–THOUGHT merger**: *cot* and *caught* sound the same for most speakers.
4. **Spelling**: a mixture — *colour*, *centre* and *cheque* as in Britain, but *-ize*, *tire* and *curb* as in America; style guides such as the *Canadian Oxford Dictionary* (1998) record the usual choices.
5. **Vocabulary**: *washroom* ‘public toilet’, *loonie* ‘one-dollar coin’, *toque*; *eh* as a tag inviting agreement (*Nice day, eh?*).
:::

::: example Canadian raising {#ex-raising}
Predict whether the vowel is raised ([ʌɪ], [ʌʊ]) or not ([aɪ], [aʊ]) in a Canadian speaker's pronunciation of: (a) *knife*, (b) *knives*, (c) *mouth* (noun), (d) *mouths*, (e) *rice*, (f) *rise*.
::: solution
Apply [[#rule-canadian]], point 2: raised before a **voiceless** consonant, not raised before a voiced one or at the end of a word.

1. (a) *knife* /f/ — voiceless: **raised** [nʌɪf].
2. (b) *knives* /vz/ — voiced: **not raised** [naɪvz].
3. (c) *mouth* /θ/ — voiceless: **raised** [mʌʊθ].
4. (d) *mouths* — usually /ðz/, voiced: **not raised** [maʊðz].
5. (e) *rice* /s/ — voiceless: **raised** [rʌɪs].
6. (f) *rise* /z/ — voiced: **not raised** [raɪz].

The rule is so regular that it produces vowel differences between singular and plural forms of the same word. Many speakers in parts of the northern United States have a similar pattern, at least for PRICE.
:::
:::

## Australia

English came to Australia with the First Fleet of convicts and officials in 1788. The first generations born there, from a mixture of London, south-eastern, Irish and other dialects, formed a recognisably Australian accent by the middle of the nineteenth century ([[#rule-new-dialect]]). In 1965 Alexander Mitchell and Arthur Delbridge, in *The Speech of Australian Adolescents*, described a range from **broad** through **general** to **cultivated** Australian English, the last closest to RP; since then the cultivated end has become less common and General Australian has become the national standard. The *Macquarie Dictionary* (1981) codified Australian vocabulary and spelling, which mostly follows British practice.

::: theorem Some features of Australian English {#rule-australian}
1. **Non-rhotic**, with BATH words mostly /ɑː/ (though many words, such as *dance* and *castle*, vary by region and speaker between /ɑː/ and /æ/).
2. **Diphthongs with shifted starting points**: FACE begins lower, close to [æɪ] (*day*); PRICE begins further back, close to [ɑe]; GOAT begins more open and central.
3. **Tapped /t/** between vowels, as in GA (*water* [ˈwoːɾə]).
4. **High rising terminal**: a rising tone at the end of statements, especially in narratives and explanations, used to check that the listener is following. It does not turn a statement into a question.
5. **Diminutives** in *-o* and *-ie*/*-y*: *arvo* ‘afternoon’, *servo* ‘petrol station’, *brekkie* ‘breakfast’, *barbie* ‘barbecue’; many are informal but used across all social groups.
6. **Borrowings from Aboriginal languages**, especially for plants, animals and places: *kangaroo*, *koala*, *boomerang*, *Canberra*.
:::

Australia also has **Aboriginal English**, a group of varieties spoken by many Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, with features of grammar, vocabulary and discourse — such as a different use of direct questions and of silence in conversation — that the linguist Diana Eades has described in *Aboriginal Ways of Using English* (2013). Misunderstandings between Aboriginal English and Standard Australian English speakers in courts and schools have been a focus of her work. Kriol, an English-lexifier creole of northern Australia, is described in [[world-englishes/pidgins-creoles]].

## New Zealand

Systematic British settlement of New Zealand began after the Treaty of Waitangi (1840) between the British Crown and Māori chiefs. **New Zealand English** is similar to Australian English but distinct from it, and recordings analysed by the Origins of New Zealand English project show it forming in the late nineteenth century. Māori became an official language under the Māori Language Act 1987, and the influence of Māori on New Zealand English vocabulary has grown strongly since then.

::: theorem Some features of New Zealand English {#rule-nz}
1. **A front vowel chain**: TRAP and DRESS are raised (so *bad* is close to [bed] and *pen* to [pin] in other accents' terms), and KIT is centralised to a vowel close to [ə] (*fish*, *six*).
2. **The NEAR–SQUARE merger**: for many younger speakers, *beer* and *bear*, *here* and *hair* sound the same, usually with the NEAR vowel.
3. **Non-rhotic** (except in parts of the south of the South Island, where a rhotic accent associated with Scottish settlement survives).
4. **High rising terminal** in statements, as in Australia.
5. **Māori words** in everyday use by all New Zealanders: *kia ora* ‘hello, thanks’, *whānau* ‘extended family’, *kai* ‘food’, *haka*, *Aotearoa* ‘New Zealand’.
:::

::: example Hearing New Zealand vowels {#ex-nz}
A New Zealand speaker tells Chen Jing: *Put the pen next to the bed.* She hears *pin* and *bid*. A moment later he says *There's a fish in the tank*, and she hears something close to *fush*. Explain each perception, and say how she can adapt.
::: solution
1. *pen*, *bed* — New Zealand DRESS is raised ([[#rule-nz]], point 1), so it sounds to a listener used to GB or GA like their KIT vowel: *pin*, *bid*.
2. *fish* — New Zealand KIT is centralised, close to [ə], so it sounds to her like her STRUT vowel: *fush*.

The two changes are linked: as DRESS moved up into the space of KIT, KIT moved to the centre, so the two words stay distinct for New Zealanders (*pen* ≠ *pin*). To adapt, Chen Jing can apply the correspondences consistently: this speaker's [i]-like vowel in *pen*, *bed*, *ten* is DRESS; his [ə]-like vowel in *fish*, *six*, *big* is KIT. As [[listening-and-conversation/accents#rule-adapt]] says, listeners usually learn such correspondences within minutes. The vowels are as regular as GB's; they are simply arranged differently.
:::
:::

## South Africa

The British took the Cape from the Dutch in 1795 and again in 1806, and English-speaking settlers arrived in numbers from 1820. English in South Africa has always been in contact with Afrikaans (which developed from Dutch) and with African languages such as isiZulu, isiXhosa and Sesotho. The Constitution of 1996 recognised eleven official languages, English among them; South African Sign Language was added as a twelfth in 2023. English is the home language of a minority (in the 2011 census, under a tenth of the population), but it is the main language of government, higher education, business and the national media, and a second language for millions.

Linguists distinguish several **South African Englishes**: White South African English (the first language of most English-speaking South Africans of British descent), Black South African English (a second-language variety, increasingly a first language among the urban middle class), Indian South African English (now the first language of most South Africans of Indian descent), and the English of the Cape Flats, among others. The *Dictionary of South African English on Historical Principles* (1996) codified the vocabulary they share.

::: theorem Some features of South African English {#rule-sae}
1. **Words from Afrikaans and African languages**: *braai* ‘barbecue’, *bakkie* ‘pickup truck’, *veld* ‘open grassland’, *indaba* ‘meeting, conference’ (from isiZulu), *lekker* ‘nice, good’.
2. **Distinctive time expressions**: *just now* ‘in a while, later’; *now-now* ‘very soon’; *now* ‘soon’.
3. **Local senses of English words**: *robot* ‘traffic light’; *location* (historically) ‘township’.
4. **The response *Is it?*** used invariably to show interest, like *Really?* (*I've just come back from Durban. — Is it?*).
5. **Pronunciation** (White South African English): non-rhotic; the "KIT split", in which KIT is [ɪ] next to velar consonants and /h/ (*kit*, *big*, *his*) and a more central vowel elsewhere (*bit*, *limit*); a fronted GOOSE vowel.
:::

::: example Interpreting time words {#ex-sae}
Return to the beginning of this chapter. The technician in Cape Town said *I'll do it just now*, and later *Now-now*. Explain the misunderstanding, and suggest how Chen Jing could have avoided it.
::: solution
In South African English *just now* means ‘in a while, later’ ([[#rule-sae]], point 2), while in British and American English it means ‘a moment ago’ or ‘at this moment’. Chen Jing interpreted it in the GB or GA sense and waited for immediate action. *Now-now* means ‘very soon’, which is why the second answer was followed by quick action.

She could have **asked specifically**: *Sorry — will that be in a few minutes, or later today?* This strategy works with every variety ([[listening-and-conversation/clarification]]). Nobody spoke incorrectly; the same words have different conventional meanings in different standard varieties, as *table* and *quite* do between Britain and America ([[world-englishes/british-american]]).
:::
:::

::: warning Learning a feature as a joke
✗ Imitating Australian *barbie* or Irish *I'm after…* to make a class laugh, or describing a variety by its most stereotyped feature.
✓ Learning features as facts about how millions of people speak, with their meaning and where they are used.

Many features of these varieties are known abroad mainly through jokes and advertisements, which exaggerate them and attach them to stereotypes of the speakers. A learner who uses an exaggerated feature with speakers of the variety is likely to cause offence, not to show knowledge. The aim of learning these features is to **understand** speakers, not to imitate them.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **A familiar pattern.** The development of new varieties in settler colonies has a parallel in Chinese: the Mandarin spoken in Singapore, Malaysia and Taiwan developed its own vocabulary and pronunciation after communities separated, and words such as 巴刹 ‘market’ (from Malay *pasar*) in Singapore and Malaysia show contact with local languages. These are varieties, not mistakes.
- **Time words.** Chen Jing's confusion over *just now* is common: Chinese *刚才* (‘just now’) matches the GB/GA meaning, so learners expect it everywhere. Whenever a time expression matters, check it.
- **Listening practice.** Learners who have heard mainly GB or GA often find Scottish, New Zealand and South African accents hard at first. The features in this chapter are regular, and knowing them in advance shortens the time it takes to adapt.
:::

::: history
Descriptions of these varieties came with their codification. In the nineteenth century, glossaries of "colonial" words — often written by visitors who found them amusing — were the main sources. Serious description began in the twentieth century: Mitchell and Delbridge's study of Australian speech (1965); John Wells's *Accents of English* (1982), whose second and third volumes covered the British Isles and the Englishes "beyond"; the *Macquarie Dictionary* (1981) in Australia; the *Dictionary of South African English on Historical Principles* (1996); the *Dictionary of New Zealand English* (1997); and the *Canadian Oxford Dictionary* (1998). The Origins of New Zealand English project, using recordings of early New Zealanders made in the 1940s, provided rare evidence of a new variety forming, analysed by Gordon et al. (2004) and Trudgill (2004).
:::

## Where this leads

The postcolonial Englishes in which English is mostly a second language — South Asian, Southeast Asian, African and Caribbean — are the subject of the next three chapters, beginning with [[world-englishes/south-asian-englishes]]. Strategies for understanding unfamiliar accents are in [[listening-and-conversation/accents]], and the attitudes that surround regional accents in [[world-englishes/accent-identity]]. How linguists study regional and social variation is the subject of [[linguistics/variation-change]].

::: summary
- English is the first language of most people in Ireland, Scotland, Wales, Canada, Australia and New Zealand, and of a minority in South Africa; each has its own standard variety ([[#def-variety]]).
- New varieties formed from mixtures of settlers' dialects over about three generations, usually keeping the majority forms (Trudgill 2004; [[#rule-new-dialect]]).
- Irish English has the *after* perfect, habitual *do be* and plural *ye* ([[#rule-irish]]); Scottish English is rhotic, has fewer vowel contrasts and the Scottish Vowel Length Rule, and lives beside Scots ([[#rule-scottish]]).
- Canadian English is close to American English, with Canadian raising and mixed spelling ([[#rule-canadian]]).
- Australian and New Zealand English are non-rhotic with shifted diphthongs and front vowels; New Zealand English has the NEAR–SQUARE merger and many Māori words ([[#rule-australian]], [[#rule-nz]]).
- South African English has words from Afrikaans and African languages and its own time expressions (*just now* ‘later’, *now-now* ‘very soon’) ([[#rule-sae]]).
- Learn features to understand speakers, not to imitate or mock them.
:::

Raised: *bike*, *shout*, *wife*, *ice* — each has PRICE or MOUTH before a voiceless consonant (/k/, /t/, /f/, /s/). Not raised: *tribe*, *loud*, *wives*, *eyes* — before voiced consonants (/b/, /d/, /vz/, /z/) ([[#rule-canadian]]).

::: exercise Explain the vowel {level=2}
A Scottish speaker pronounces *tied* with a long vowel and *tide* with a short one, although both have the PRICE vowel and end in /d/. Using [[#rule-scottish]], explain why.
::: solution
By the Scottish Vowel Length Rule, vowels are long at the end of a morpheme. *Tied* is *tie* + *-d*: the vowel ends the morpheme *tie*, so it is long. In *tide* the vowel is followed by /d/ within the same morpheme, so it is short. The difference is predictable from the structure of the word, not from the sounds alone.
:::
:::

::: exercise New dialects {level=2}
Using [[#rule-new-dialect]], explain in three or four sentences why Australian and New Zealand English are non-rhotic but American and Canadian English are rhotic.
::: solution
New varieties tend to keep the features of the majority of the original settlers. Australia and New Zealand were settled mainly in the nineteenth century, when non-rhotic speech had become usual in southern England, and southern English speakers formed the largest group of settlers; non-rhoticity survived in the new varieties. North America was settled earlier, from the seventeenth century, before non-rhoticity had spread, and from regions that were rhotic; Canadian English grew largely from the English of American Loyalists. So the difference reflects the date and the origin of settlement.
:::

::: exercise Describe a variety {level=3}
Choose one variety from this chapter and write a paragraph of 180–220 words describing it for a classmate: where it is spoken, how it developed, three features (at least one of grammar or vocabulary and one of pronunciation, each with an example), and one point about its status today. Use neutral, descriptive language throughout.
::: solution
Answers vary. A good paragraph is accurate, cites at least one source (Wells 1982, Trudgill 2004, Hickey 2007 for Irish English, Gordon et al. 2004 for New Zealand English), gives each feature with an example and a gloss (*I'm after finishing it* ‘I’ve just finished it’), and avoids evaluative words such as *strange*, *funny*, *lazy* or *wrong*. It should mention status: for example, that Irish is the first official language of Ireland while English is the first language of most people, or that Māori has been an official language of New Zealand since 1987.
