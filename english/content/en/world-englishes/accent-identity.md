In 1992 the American researcher Donald Rubin played a short recorded lecture to a group of undergraduate students. The lecturer was a native speaker of English from central Ohio, and every student heard exactly the same recording. While it played, half the students saw a photograph of a white American woman, said to be the lecturer; the other half saw a photograph of an Asian woman, dressed and posed in the same way. Students who saw the Asian photograph were more likely to report that the lecturer had a foreign accent, and they scored lower on a test of how much of the lecture they had understood. Nothing in the sound had changed. What had changed was what the listeners expected to hear.

Rubin's experiment ("Nonlanguage factors affecting undergraduates' judgments of nonnative English-speaking teaching assistants", *Research in Higher Education*, 1992) is a good place to begin the last chapter of this course, because it shows that judgements about accents are not only about sounds. They are also about people: about who listeners think a speaker is, which groups they associate with prestige or with failure, and what they believe "good English" sounds like. This chapter looks at the evidence on accent prejudice and prestige, at how a learner can choose a pronunciation model, and at the idea of the **native speaker** — perhaps the most powerful and the most debated idea in English teaching. The methods by which sociolinguists study such attitudes are described in [[linguistics/variation-change]]; the listening skills for unfamiliar accents are in [[listening-and-conversation/accents]].

## How listeners judge accents

::: definition Matched-guise technique {#def-matched-guise}
The **matched-guise technique**, introduced by Wallace Lambert and colleagues in Montreal (Lambert, Hodgson, Gardner and Fillenbaum 1960), measures attitudes to languages or accents by having the **same speaker** record the same passage in two or more "guises" — for example in English and in French, or in two accents. Listeners, who are not told that the voices belong to one person, rate each recording for qualities such as intelligence, kindness or trustworthiness. Because the voice, the speaker and the content are the same, differences in the ratings show attitudes to the language or accent itself.
:::

Lambert's first study found that both English-speaking and French-speaking Canadian listeners rated the English guises more favourably on many traits, including intelligence and good looks — the French-speaking listeners even rating their own language's guises lower. Since then, matched-guise and related studies have been carried out in many countries, and their results are remarkably consistent.

::: theorem What accent studies have found {#rule-attitudes}
1. **Standard accents are rated higher for status** — intelligence, education, competence — than regional, working-class or non-native accents. Howard Giles (1970) found this pattern for British accents, with RP at the top and some urban accents, such as that of Birmingham, near the bottom.
2. **Non-standard accents are often rated higher for solidarity** — friendliness, honesty, sense of humour — especially by listeners who share them. Peter Trudgill (1972) found that some men in Norwich reported using local forms less than they actually did, and associated them with toughness and local loyalty: a **covert prestige** that competes with the overt prestige of the standard.
3. **The patterns persist but change slowly.** In a large online survey for the BBC's *Voices* project, Nikolas Coupland and Hywel Bishop (2007) found that "standard English" accents and listeners' **own** accents were rated highly, while some urban accents were still rated low; younger respondents were less negative about non-standard accents than older ones.
4. **Expectation affects perception.** Listeners who expect an accent may hear one that is not there and understand less (Rubin 1992).
:::

::: proof
These findings reflect social knowledge, not the qualities of sounds. There is nothing in the vowels of Birmingham that makes them less intelligent than those of London; listeners have learned to associate some accents with powerful groups and others with less powerful ones, and they transfer those associations to individual speakers. This is why the same accent is evaluated differently in different countries: an accent that is prestigious in one place may be unknown or neutral in another. The persistence of covert prestige shows that standard accents do not simply win: speakers keep local accents because those accents express belonging. And Rubin's result shows that comprehension itself can be affected by stereotype — a listener who believes a speaker will be hard to understand may make less effort to understand them. Matched-guise studies have limits, since rating a recording is not the same as meeting a person, but their results agree with studies of real decisions ([[#rule-consequences]]).
:::

::: example Designing an attitude study {#ex-design}
A student wants to find out whether listeners in Shanghai rate General British, General American and Singapore English accents differently for competence. She plans to record three different speakers, one for each accent, reading three different news stories. Using [[#def-matched-guise]], identify two weaknesses of this design and suggest improvements.
::: solution
1. **Different speakers**: differences in ratings might come from the speakers' voices (pitch, speed, voice quality) rather than their accents. *Improvement*: use one speaker who can produce all three accents convincingly (a matched guise), or, if that is impossible, several speakers per accent so that individual voices average out (a "verbal guise" design).
2. **Different texts**: one news story may be more interesting, more serious or easier than another. *Improvement*: have every speaker read the **same** text.
3. (A further point.) Listeners should not know the purpose of the study, and the order of the recordings should vary between listeners, so that the first or last recording is not always the same accent.

With these changes, differences in ratings can more plausibly be attributed to the accents themselves — which is the purpose of the technique.
:::
:::

## When attitudes have consequences

Attitudes become important when they affect decisions about people's lives.

::: theorem Where accent prejudice has been documented {#rule-consequences}
1. **Housing.** Thomas Purnell, William Idsardi and John Baugh (1999) made telephone enquiries about advertised flats in the San Francisco area using African American, Chicano and Standard American English guises (Baugh is a linguist who can produce all three). Enquiries in the non-standard guises received fewer appointments to view, and listeners could identify the dialect from the single word *hello*.
2. **The courts.** John Rickford and Sharese King (*Language*, 2016) analysed the testimony of Rachel Jeantel, a key witness in a 2013 murder trial in Florida, who spoke African American English. They argued that her testimony was misunderstood and discounted partly because of her dialect, and that speakers of vernacular varieties are at a disadvantage in courts in many countries.
3. **Employment and education.** Studies of hiring and of students' evaluations of teachers have repeatedly found that the same content is judged differently when delivered with a non-standard or non-native accent; in English teaching, many job advertisements have asked for "native speakers" regardless of qualifications ([[#def-native-speakerism]]).
:::

::: definition Standard language ideology {#def-ideology}
**Standard language ideology**, in Rosina Lippi-Green's *English with an Accent* (1997; 2nd edition 2012), is the belief that there is one correct, homogeneous form of a language — usually identified with the speech of educated, powerful groups — and that other forms are inferior and their speakers deficient. Lippi-Green argued that this belief is used to justify treating speakers of other varieties unfavourably, and that **accent** is often a publicly acceptable proxy for discrimination on grounds of race, ethnicity, origin or class.
:::

None of this means that every judgement about clarity is prejudice. Some speech is genuinely hard to understand — for any listener, in any accent — and it is reasonable for a listener to say so and to ask a speaker to repeat. The distinction drawn by Munro and Derwing ([[listening-and-conversation/accents#def-intelligibility]]) is the key one: **intelligibility** can be measured, and a speaker can be helped to improve it; **accentedness** is a property of difference, and judging a speaker's competence or character by it is prejudice.

::: example Recognising the ideology {#ex-ideology}
Which of these statements express standard language ideology ([[#def-ideology]])? Explain. (a) *Formal academic writing in this journal follows the conventions of standard English.* (b) *People who say "I done it" just don't know their own language.* (c) *Scottish English is a lazy way of speaking English.* (d) *For this exam, write in either British or American standard spelling, consistently.*
::: solution
1. (a) **No.** It states a convention for a particular setting, without judging other varieties or their speakers.
2. (b) **Yes.** It treats a non-standard form, used systematically in many dialects, as evidence that its speakers are deficient.
3. (c) **Yes.** It describes a whole variety, with its own regular system ([[world-englishes/other-first-language-varieties#rule-scottish]]), as a careless version of the standard.
4. (d) **No.** It sets a requirement for a task and accepts two standards.

The difference is between **stating what a setting expects** — which is legitimate, and which learners need to know — and **treating one form as the only real language and its speakers' other forms as failures**, which is the ideology Lippi-Green describes.
:::
:::

::: warning Treating accent as a measure of ability
✗ *His English isn't very good — he has a strong accent.*
✗ *We need a native speaker for this job, so that the clients understand.*
✓ *I found some of his words hard to catch; I'll ask him to repeat the figures.*
✓ *We need someone whom the clients find easy to understand and who can adapt to them.*

A strong accent is compatible with excellent grammar, vocabulary and fluency, and with full intelligibility. Statements that equate the two misdescribe the speaker and, in hiring or assessment, may be unfair. If clarity matters for a job, it can be assessed directly, for every candidate.
:::

::: quiz
In a matched-guise study, listeners rate the same speaker as less intelligent when she speaks in a regional accent than when she speaks in a standard accent. What does this show?
- [ ] That the regional accent is harder to understand.
- [ ] That the speaker is less intelligent in her regional accent.
- [x] That listeners associate the regional accent with lower status.
- [ ] That the standard accent is more correct.
::: solution
Because the speaker, the voice and the content are the same, the difference can only come from the listeners' associations with the accents ([[#def-matched-guise]], [[#rule-attitudes]]). The study does not measure intelligibility, and it says nothing about correctness.
:::
:::

## Prestige accents change

The status of accents is not fixed. **Received Pronunciation** — the term was used by Alexander Ellis in 1869 and adopted by Daniel Jones in the 1920s — was the accent of the English upper and upper-middle classes and of the public schools. When the BBC began broadcasting in the 1920s it used RP for its announcers and set up an Advisory Committee on Spoken English in 1926 to advise on the pronunciation of difficult words. For decades RP was the accent of authority in Britain and the model of British English teaching abroad.

In the second half of the twentieth century this changed. Regional accents became common on radio and television; the number of people who speak traditional RP declined; and the accent itself changed, so that phoneticians now describe a modern successor, which Alan Cruttenden calls **General British** ([[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet#def-gb-ga]]), with a wider social base. The history shows that prestige belongs to speakers, not to sounds: an accent rises and falls with the status of those who use it. Lynda Mugglestone's *Talking Proper* (1995; 2nd edition 2003) traces how accent became a symbol of class in Britain in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

## Choosing a pronunciation model

Every learner needs a model: a set of sounds to aim at, with dictionaries and recordings to learn from. The question is which, and for what purpose.

::: theorem Two principles of pronunciation teaching {#rule-principles}
John Levis ("Changing contexts and shifting paradigms in pronunciation teaching", *TESOL Quarterly*, 2005) described two principles that have guided pronunciation teaching:

1. The **nativeness principle**: the aim is to sound as much as possible like a native speaker of a reference accent, such as GB or GA.
2. The **intelligibility principle**: the aim is to be easily understood by one's listeners, giving priority to the features that most affect intelligibility, whether or not one sounds native.

Levis argued that research supports the intelligibility principle as the more realistic and more useful aim for most learners, while recognising that some learners have good reasons to aim at nativeness.
:::

::: proof
Three kinds of evidence support the intelligibility principle. First, Munro and Derwing's research ([[listening-and-conversation/accents#rule-accent]]) shows that accent and intelligibility are separate: a strong accent can be fully intelligible, so removing an accent is not necessary for being understood. Second, research on age and pronunciation has found that learners who begin a language as adults very rarely become indistinguishable from native speakers when listeners judge carefully (Abrahamsson and Hyltenstam, *Language Learning*, 2009), so nativeness is an aim that almost no adult learner fully reaches. Third, the listeners most learners will meet are themselves non-native speakers ([[world-englishes/lingua-franca]]), for whom a native accent is not necessarily the easiest to understand. The nativeness principle is not thereby wrong: an actor, a learner who wants to pass as a local, or someone who simply enjoys imitating an accent has good reasons to pursue it, and a reference accent remains useful as a **model** — a guide for practice and for dictionary pronunciations — even when it is not the **target**.
:::

::: widget venn

labels: Intelligible; Nativelike
expr: Intelligible - Nativelike

caption: Two separate properties of speech (Munro and Derwing 1995; Levis 2005). The shaded region is speech that is easily understood but does not sound native — the speech of most successful second-language users of English, and the realistic aim of the intelligibility principle ([[#rule-principles]]). Try *Intelligible & Nativelike* for speech that is both, and *Nativelike - Intelligible* for native speech that some listeners find hard to follow, such as very fast, heavily reduced or strongly regional speech in an international meeting.

::: theorem Choosing a model {#rule-choosing}
When choosing a pronunciation model, consider:

1. **Purpose and listeners.** Who will you speak to most? Colleagues from many countries (intelligibility, Lingua Franca Core); clients in one country (that country's educated accent may help); examiners (most international exams assess intelligibility and control, not a particular accent — check the current descriptors).
2. **Available input.** Choose a model you can hear often, with good recordings and dictionaries: GB and GA have the most; some local standards (Indian, Singaporean) are well described too.
3. **Consistency.** Within a model, consistency matters more than which model: a speaker who is rhotic in some words and not in others is harder to follow than one who is consistently either.
4. **Priorities.** Whatever the model, work first on the features that most affect intelligibility — for Chinese speakers usually final consonants, clusters, vowel length, word stress and the nucleus ([[sounds-of-english/sounds-for-chinese-speakers]]).
5. **Identity.** Decide how far you want your English to show where you come from. Keeping features of your first language is a legitimate choice; so is aiming at a native-like accent.
:::

::: example Three learners, three choices {#ex-choosing}
Advise each learner on a model using [[#rule-choosing]]. (a) Li Na, an engineer in Shanghai, works mainly with suppliers in Germany, Vietnam and Brazil. (b) Zhao Lei will begin a degree in Toronto next year and hopes to stay in Canada. (c) Chen Yu, a teacher in Chengdu, wants to prepare her secondary-school students for both exams and future international use.
::: solution
1. **Li Na**: her listeners are non-native speakers of many backgrounds, so the **intelligibility principle** fits best. A GB or GA model is useful for practice and dictionaries, but her priorities should be the Lingua Franca Core features ([[world-englishes/lingua-franca#rule-lfc]]) and accommodation strategies, not native-like vowels or weak forms.
2. **Zhao Lei**: he will live among speakers of Canadian English, which is close to **GA**; choosing GA (or Canadian English itself) as a model will make listening easier and may help him feel at home. Whether he aims at nativeness is his choice; intelligibility features should still come first.
3. **Chen Yu**: she needs a well-described model for teaching and materials — **GB or GA**, consistently — while emphasising intelligibility priorities and exposing students to many accents, including non-native ones ([[listening-and-conversation/accents#rule-strategies]]). She can present the model as a model, not as the only correct English.

In each case the model is chosen for a purpose; none is "the right accent" in general.
:::
:::

## The native speaker

The idea of the native speaker has been central to language teaching and to linguistics: dictionaries, grammars and teaching materials have traditionally described the language of native speakers, and native speakers have often been treated as the final authority on what is correct. Since the 1980s the idea has been questioned from several directions.

- **What is a native speaker?** Thomas Paikeday's *The Native Speaker Is Dead!* (1985) and Alan Davies's *The Native Speaker: Myth and Reality* (2003) showed how hard the term is to define. Is it the language learned first? The one spoken best? The one used most? Many people — like the Singaporean and Nigerian engineers at the start of this course ([[world-englishes/models]]) — grew up with several languages and fit no simple definition.
- **Expertise, affiliation, inheritance.** Ben Rampton ("Displacing the 'native speaker'", *ELT Journal*, 1990) proposed replacing the single idea of the native speaker with three: **expertise** (how well someone knows a language), **affiliation** (how far they identify with it) and **inheritance** (whether they were born into it). A person may have great expertise in a language they did not inherit.
- **The L2 user.** Vivian Cook ("Going beyond the native speaker in language teaching", *TESOL Quarterly*, 1999) argued that second-language users should not be measured against monolingual native speakers at all. A person who knows two languages has a different kind of competence — **multicompetence** — and successful L2 users, not native speakers, are the appropriate models for learners.
- **Non-native teachers.** Péter Medgyes ("Native or non-native: who's worth more?", *ELT Journal*, 1992; *The Non-Native Teacher*, 1994) argued that native and non-native teachers have different strengths, and that non-native teachers can model successful learning, anticipate learners' difficulties and use the learners' first language to explain.

::: definition Native-speakerism {#def-native-speakerism}
**Native-speakerism**, in Adrian Holliday's term (*ELT Journal*, 2006), is the belief that native-speaker teachers represent a superior "Western culture" from which the ideals of the English language and of English-teaching methods come. Holliday and others argued that this belief leads to unfair treatment of non-native teachers — for example, in job advertisements that require "native speakers" whatever their qualifications — and to the undervaluing of teaching traditions outside English-speaking countries. Robert Phillipson (1992) had earlier called the idea that the ideal teacher is a native speaker the **native speaker fallacy**.
:::

These critiques have not settled the question. Many learners, and many employers, still prefer native-speaker teachers, and some learners report that native speakers offer something they value: a model they trust, exposure to natural speech, practice with the people they hope to talk to. Researchers who criticise native-speakerism reply that these qualities belong to good teachers of many backgrounds, and that qualifications, teaching skill and proficiency can be assessed directly. Professional associations for English teachers, such as TESOL International Association, have issued statements opposing hiring based on native-speaker status alone. The debate is partly about evidence (what makes teaching effective) and partly about values (fairness, identity, the ownership of English; [[world-englishes/models]]).

::: example Revising a job advertisement {#ex-advert}
A language school advertises: *Native English teacher wanted. Must be from the UK, USA, Canada, Australia or New Zealand. Native accent essential.* Using [[#def-native-speakerism]] and [[#rule-principles]], explain what is problematic about the advertisement, and write a version that states what the school actually needs.
::: solution
1. **It uses origin as a proxy for ability.** Being from one of five countries says nothing about teaching qualifications, experience or skill, and it excludes highly proficient teachers from Ireland, South Africa, India, Singapore, China and elsewhere.
2. **It confuses accent with intelligibility.** A "native accent" is neither necessary nor sufficient for being easily understood by students ([[#rule-principles]]).
3. **It reflects native-speakerism** ([[#def-native-speakerism]]), which professional associations have criticised.

A revised version: *English teacher wanted. Requirements: a recognised teaching qualification; at least two years' experience teaching teenagers; proficiency in English equivalent to CEFR C2; clear, easily understood pronunciation. Experience of teaching exam classes is an advantage.*

The revised advertisement states what the school needs and lets every candidate be assessed against it.
:::
:::

## Accent and identity

An accent is not only a way of pronouncing: it says where a speaker comes from and which groups they belong to. Speakers can use it, consciously or not, to express identity — converging towards a listener to show friendliness, or diverging to show difference ([[world-englishes/lingua-franca#rule-strategies]]). Second-language users face this choice too. Jennifer Jenkins, interviewing non-native teachers of English for *English as a Lingua Franca: Attitude and Identity* (2007), found many who were ambivalent: they valued a native-like accent as a sign of professional competence, and at the same time felt that their own accent was part of who they were and should not have to be hidden.

There is no right answer for everyone. A learner who wants to sound as close as possible to GB or GA, for professional or personal reasons, is making a legitimate choice, and pronunciation teaching can help. A learner who decides to keep features of a Chinese accent while working on intelligibility is making an equally legitimate choice. What the research in this course suggests is that the second choice is not a failure, and that the first need not be a betrayal of identity. Both are choices about how to be oneself in English.

::: note English and Chinese
- **A familiar debate.** Mandarin speakers know the same pattern from Chinese. 普通话 is the standard, and accents from some regions are sometimes judged as less educated or are the subject of jokes, while others carry local pride; speakers may switch between a regional accent at home and Putonghua at work. The attitudes in [[#rule-attitudes]] — status for the standard, solidarity for the local — apply to Chinese too.
- **"Chinese accent" anxiety.** Many Chinese learners worry about having a Chinese accent and spend effort on features, such as /θ/, that matter little for intelligibility. Use [[#rule-choosing]] to set priorities: features that affect understanding first, the rest by choice.
- **Names and identity.** Many Chinese speakers use an English name at work or school, and many prefer to use their Chinese name; both are personal choices. If you use your Chinese name, it helps to tell people how to say it, and you may ask colleagues to try ([[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet]]).
- **Teachers.** Chinese teachers of English are themselves successful L2 users — the models Cook (1999) recommends. Their knowledge of how Chinese speakers learn English is an expertise that native-speaker teachers usually lack.
:::

::: history
Alexander Ellis used the phrase *received pronunciation* in 1869, and Daniel Jones made "RP" the standard name in the 1920s, when the BBC adopted it for broadcasting. Wallace Lambert's matched-guise study (1960) began the experimental study of language attitudes; William Labov's study of New York City (1966) and Peter Trudgill's of Norwich (1972) showed how prestige shapes variation; Howard Giles (1970) measured the status of British accents. From the 1980s the native speaker was questioned by Paikeday (1985), Rampton (1990), Medgyes (1992) and Cook (1999); Lippi-Green's *English with an Accent* (1997) named standard language ideology; and Levis (2005) set the intelligibility principle beside the nativeness principle.
:::
