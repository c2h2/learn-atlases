In a meeting room in Shenzhen, four people are negotiating a delivery schedule: a production manager from Hunan, a buyer from São Paulo, an engineer from Stuttgart and a supplier from Bangkok. None of them is a native speaker of English, and English is the only language they share. The meeting takes an hour. Several times someone repeats a number, writes a date on the whiteboard or rephrases a question; once the Brazilian buyer says *How you say… the date before which?* and the German engineer offers *the deadline?*; nobody comments on anyone's grammar. They reach an agreement. In the afternoon a consultant from London joins the call, and things become harder: he says *Let's not jump the gun* and *We're in the same ballpark*, and two of the four quietly lose the thread.

The morning meeting is a typical example of **English as a lingua franca** (ELF): English used between people who do not share a first language. It is very probably the most common use of English in the world today, and the kind of English that most Chinese users of English will need most often — with colleagues in Korea, customers in Germany, suppliers in Vietnam, fellow students from forty countries. This chapter describes what research has found about it, the **Lingua Franca Core** of pronunciation, the strategies that make it work, and the debate about what it means for teaching. The listening chapter on accents introduced the core briefly ([[listening-and-conversation/accents#rule-intelligibility]]); here we look at it, and at the research behind it, in detail.

## What English as a lingua franca is

The term *lingua franca*, Italian for ‘Frankish language’, first named a contact language of the Mediterranean, based largely on Italian and other Romance languages, which sailors, merchants and others used from the Middle Ages to the nineteenth century. It came to mean any language used for communication between people who have no other language in common: Latin in medieval Europe, Arabic, Swahili, Malay, and today English.

::: definition English as a lingua franca {#def-elf}
**English as a lingua franca** (ELF) is, in Barbara Seidlhofer's definition (*Understanding English as a Lingua Franca*, 2011), "any use of English among speakers of different first languages for whom English is the communicative medium of choice, and often the only option". ELF interactions may include native speakers, but most involve none. ELF is defined by its **function** — communication across first languages — not by a set of forms.
:::

The definition separates ELF from two neighbouring ideas. English as a **foreign language** (EFL) is usually taught with the aim of approaching a native-speaker model and of communicating with native speakers; ELF research asks instead how people actually communicate with each other, whatever their models. And ELF is not a **variety** like Indian or Nigerian English, with a community and stable features of its own; ELF interactions are made by constantly changing groups of speakers, each bringing their own English. Early ELF research (around 2000) looked for common features of ELF; later research, reviewed by Jennifer Jenkins, Alessia Cogo and Martin Dewey in *Language Teaching* (2011), concentrated on the **processes** by which speakers make themselves understood.

::: theorem What ELF research has found {#rule-elf-findings}
Studies of recorded ELF interaction have found that:

1. **Communication usually succeeds**, and misunderstandings, when they occur, are typically noticed and repaired by the speakers themselves.
2. **Speakers cooperate**: they repeat, rephrase, check understanding, supply words for each other and let minor unclear points pass when the meaning is likely to become clear later.
3. **Some forms differ regularly from native-speaker norms without causing problems**, for example dropping the third-person *-s* or using *isn't it?* as an invariant tag.
4. **Native-speaker idiom can cause problems**: idioms, phrasal verbs, cultural references and wordplay that are opaque to non-native listeners are a frequent source of difficulty — sometimes greater than any non-native feature.
5. **Speakers accommodate** to each other, adjusting their speech towards what their listeners will understand.
:::

::: proof
These findings come from corpora of recorded interaction compiled for the purpose. The **Vienna-Oxford International Corpus of English** (VOICE), directed by Seidlhofer, contains about a million words of spoken ELF from meetings, conversations, interviews and seminars, with speakers of dozens of first languages. The **ELFA** corpus (English as a Lingua Franca in Academic Settings), directed by Anna Mauranen in Helsinki, contains a million words of spoken academic English, and the **Asian Corpus of English** (ACE), directed by Andy Kirkpatrick, records ELF among Asian speakers. Alan Firth's analysis of telephone calls between Danish traders and their foreign partners ("The discursive accomplishment of normality", *Journal of Pragmatics*, 1996) described the **let-it-pass** principle: listeners let unclear words or phrases pass, assuming they will become clear or will not matter, and so keep the conversation normal. Mauranen (2006) showed how ELF speakers in academic settings anticipated and prevented misunderstandings by rephrasing and checking. Seidlhofer (2011) named the difficulty caused by native-speaker idiom **unilateral idiomaticity**: an idiom works only if both sides know it. The research describes interaction among fairly proficient users — professionals, students, academics — and its findings should not be taken to mean that anything goes at any level of proficiency.
:::

::: theorem Some lexicogrammatical forms common in ELF {#rule-elf-forms}
Seidlhofer (2004) listed forms that occurred often in VOICE without causing communication problems, among them:

1. no *-s* on third-person present verbs (*she look*);
2. *who* and *which* used interchangeably (*a book who*, *a person which*);
3. articles omitted where native speakers use them, or added where they do not;
4. invariant tags (*isn't it?*, *no?*) after any clause;
5. redundant prepositions (*discuss about*, *mention about*);
6. heavy use of verbs of general meaning (*do*, *have*, *make*, *put*, *take*);
7. *that*-clauses instead of infinitives (*I want that we discuss*);
8. extra explicitness (*black colour* for *black*, *how long time*).
:::

The list was a research finding, not a recommendation, and Seidlhofer was careful to say so: it describes forms that occurred and did not cause trouble, and leaves open how teachers should respond. Several of the forms are familiar from Outer Circle varieties ([[world-englishes/south-asian-englishes#rule-indian-grammar]], [[world-englishes/african-caribbean-englishes#rule-nigerian]]) and from learners' English, which is one reason the list has been controversial ([[#rule-elf-debate]]).

::: example Where did the trouble come from? {#ex-trouble}
Return to the meetings at the start of this chapter. In the morning, the Brazilian buyer said *How you say… the date before which?* and the Thai supplier said *We need that you confirm the quantity.* In the afternoon, the London consultant said *Let's not jump the gun* and *We're in the same ballpark.* Which utterances departed from native-speaker norms, and which caused problems? Explain.
::: solution
1. *How you say… the date before which?* — no inversion in the question (*How do you say…?*) and a paraphrase for an unknown word. It departs from native-speaker grammar but caused **no problem**: the paraphrase was clear enough for a colleague to supply *deadline* — cooperation of the kind in [[#rule-elf-findings]], point 2.
2. *We need that you confirm the quantity* — a *that*-clause instead of an infinitive ([[#rule-elf-forms]], point 7). It departs from native norms (*We need you to confirm*) but its meaning is transparent: **no problem**.
3. *Let's not jump the gun* — a native-speaker idiom (‘let’s not act too soon’), grammatically standard but **opaque** to listeners who do not know it ([[#rule-elf-findings]], point 4).
4. *We're in the same ballpark* — an American idiom from baseball (‘our figures are roughly similar’), also **opaque** to many.

The utterances that broke the norms of native-speaker grammar communicated; the grammatically perfect idioms did not. This is the pattern Seidlhofer called unilateral idiomaticity. The lesson is not that grammar does not matter, but that in ELF the question to ask is *will my listener understand this?* rather than *would a native speaker say this?*
:::
:::

## The Lingua Franca Core

Jennifer Jenkins's *The Phonology of English as an International Language* (2000) asked which features of pronunciation matter most for intelligibility when neither speaker nor listener is a native speaker. She recorded and analysed interactions between learners of different first languages, noting where communication broke down and what caused it, and proposed a **Lingua Franca Core** (LFC): the features that should be given priority in teaching for international communication.

::: theorem The Lingua Franca Core {#rule-lfc}
In Jenkins's (2000) proposal, the **core** features are:

1. **Consonants**: all the consonants of English, except that /θ/ and /ð/ may be replaced (for example by [f v], [t d] or [s z]) and dark [ɫ] may be clear [l]; /r/ as the approximant [ɹ] of GA, pronounced wherever the spelling has *r*; /t/ between vowels as [t], as in GB, rather than the GA tap.
2. **Aspiration** of /p t k/ at the start of stressed syllables, so that *pin* is not heard as *bin*.
3. **Vowel length before consonants**: vowels shorter before voiceless consonants (*seat*) than before voiced ones (*seed*).
4. **Initial consonant clusters** kept intact (*proper*, *street*, not ✗ *poper*, *sutreet*); medial and final clusters simplified only in the ways native speakers simplify them.
5. **Vowel length contrasts** kept (*ship*–*sheep*, *full*–*fool*); vowel qualities may vary, if they are consistent, except for the NURSE vowel /ɜː/, which should be kept distinct.
6. **Nuclear stress**: the nucleus placed correctly, especially for contrast ([[connected-speech/sentence-stress]]).

The **non-core** features, which Jenkins found mattered little for ELF intelligibility and which she suggested could be left to the learner's choice, include exact vowel qualities, weak forms, connected-speech processes such as assimilation, the direction of pitch movements, word stress placement and stress-timed rhythm.
:::

::: proof
The core is based on Jenkins's analysis of where breakdowns occurred in her data: a missing initial consonant, an unaspirated /p/ heard as /b/, a short vowel for a long one or a nucleus in the wrong place made words unrecognisable, while a non-native vowel quality or the absence of a weak form rarely did. Her proposal has two unusual features. First, it does not take one native accent as the model: it combines GB /t/ with GA /r/, because each is the clearer choice for international listeners. Second, it treats the replacement of /θ ð/ as acceptable, because these sounds are rare in the world's languages, are hard to learn and caused no breakdowns in her data. The non-core features are the very ones that make an accent sound native — weak forms, assimilation, stress-timing — which is why the proposal implied that sounding native and being internationally intelligible are different goals ([[world-englishes/accent-identity]]).
:::

::: example Applying the core {#ex-lfc}
Two learners of English are preparing a presentation for an international audience. With little time, which of these should each practise first, according to the LFC? Explain.

(a) Kim, who says *ship* and *sheep* the same and uses [s] for /θ/ (*think* [sɪŋk]).
(b) Ana, who pronounces *product* as [poˈdʌk] and *the* with a full vowel [ðiː] before every noun.
::: solution
1. **Kim**: the merger of *ship* and *sheep* breaks a core feature, vowel length contrast ([[#rule-lfc]], point 5); [s] for /θ/ does not, because /θ ð/ are outside the core. Kim should practise the vowel length contrast first.
2. **Ana**: *product* as [poˈdʌk] simplifies the initial cluster /pr/ (point 4) and drops the final cluster /kt/ in a way native speakers do not, so the word may be unrecognisable; she has also moved the stress, which Jenkins placed outside the core but which later research found important. The full vowel in *the* is a non-core feature (weak forms). Ana should practise the clusters, and probably the word stress, first.

The answer shows the practical value of the core: it tells a learner with limited time where effort will most improve intelligibility. The full lists of priorities for Chinese speakers are in [[sounds-of-english/sounds-for-chinese-speakers]].
:::
:::

::: quiz
According to Jenkins's Lingua Franca Core, which of these is most likely to cause a misunderstanding between two non-native speakers?
- [ ] Saying *think* as [fɪŋk].
- [ ] Using a full vowel in *to* instead of the weak form [tə].
- [x] Saying *sheep* with a short vowel, so that it sounds like *ship*.
- [ ] Using a rising tone instead of a falling one at the end of a statement.
::: solution
Vowel length contrasts are in the core ([[#rule-lfc]], point 5). The replacement of /θ/, the absence of weak forms and the direction of pitch movement are non-core: they rarely caused breakdowns in Jenkins's data.

## Making ELF work: accommodation

Communication accommodation theory, developed by Howard Giles and colleagues from the 1970s (Giles, Coupland and Coupland 1991), describes how speakers adjust their speech to the people they are talking to. They may **converge** — become more similar to their listener, in speed, vocabulary, accent or style — to be understood or to show solidarity; or **diverge**, emphasising difference. In ELF, convergence towards what the listener will understand is the key skill, and research has found it to be one of the main things that successful ELF users do ([[pragmatics/directness-across-cultures]]).

::: theorem Strategies for lingua franca communication {#rule-strategies}
Successful ELF speakers, native and non-native:

1. **Speak clearly rather than fast**, with clear word boundaries and careful pronunciation of the core features.
2. **Prefer transparent words to idioms**: *postpone* or *put back* rather than *kick into the long grass*; *roughly similar* rather than *in the same ballpark*. Single-word verbs are often clearer than phrasal verbs (*continue* rather than *carry on*).
3. **Repeat and rephrase** key points, numbers and dates, and write them down when possible.
4. **Check understanding** explicitly but without condescension: *Does that work for you?*; *Shall I say that again?*
5. **Help others find words**, and accept help for your own.
6. **Let minor points pass** when the meaning will probably become clear, and ask when it matters (*Sorry — by the fifteenth or the fiftieth?*; [[listening-and-conversation/clarification]]).
7. **Avoid culture-bound references** — sports metaphors, television, local jokes — and irony that depends on intonation ([[pragmatics/irony-understatement]]).
8. **Adapt to the listener**, not to an abstract standard: what is clear to one group may not be to another.
:::

::: example Rewriting for international readers {#ex-rewrite}
A British manager writes to a project team in China, Mexico and Poland: *Just a heads-up — the client's moved the goalposts again, so we'll have to pull out all the stops to get the draft over the line by Friday. Shout if you're snowed under.* Rewrite it using [[#rule-strategies]].
::: solution
Identify each idiom and replace it with a transparent expression.

1. *Just a heads-up* — ‘a short warning’ → *Please note:*
2. *moved the goalposts* (football) — ‘changed the requirements’ → *the client has changed the requirements again*
3. *pull out all the stops* — ‘make every possible effort’ → *we will need to work very hard*
4. *get the draft over the line* (sport) — ‘finish it’ → *to finish the draft*
5. *Shout if…* — ‘tell me if…’ → *Please tell me if…*
6. *snowed under* — ‘have too much work’ → *you have too much work*

✓ *Please note: the client has changed the requirements again, so we will need to work very hard to finish the draft by Friday (14 March). Please tell me if you have too much work and need help.*

The rewrite is longer but every word is transparent, and the date is given in full ([[world-englishes/british-american]] on date formats). It is also more courteous for readers who would otherwise have to guess: unclear writing puts the burden of interpretation on the reader ([[pragmatics/writing-to-people]]).
:::
:::

::: warning "Native speakers communicate best"
✗ *If we hire a native speaker to lead the meeting, communication will be easier.*
✓ *Communication will be easier with people — native or non-native — who speak clearly and adapt to their listeners.*

In ELF settings, native speakers are not automatically the most effective communicators. Many have never had to adapt their English to non-native listeners and use idioms, fast connected speech and cultural references without noticing. ELF research and the experience of international organisations suggest that the skill that matters is accommodation, which native speakers often need to learn too.
:::

## The debate about ELF

ELF research has changed how many teachers think about English, and it has been sharply criticised. The debate is not about whether ELF interactions exist — they plainly do — but about what follows from them.

::: theorem Positions in the ELF debate {#rule-elf-debate}
1. **ELF as a legitimate use of English.** Jenkins, Seidlhofer and others argue that forms which communicate successfully among non-native speakers should not automatically be called errors; that learners preparing for international communication need accommodation skills and exposure to many Englishes more than a native-speaker accent; and that teaching and testing should reflect this. They stress that they do not propose a single "ELF variety" to replace British or American English.
2. **ELF forms as learner errors.** Critics such as Michael Swan ("ELF and EFL: are they really different?", *Journal of English as a Lingua Franca*, 2012) argue that most "ELF features" are ordinary features of learners' English, that proficient users avoid them, and that relabelling them does not help learners. I-Chun Kuo ("Addressing the issue of teaching English as a lingua franca", *ELT Journal*, 2006) argued that learners need a full model to aim at, that many want a native-speaker standard, and that ELF descriptions cannot provide one.
3. **Questions about the evidence.** Others accept the general findings but question particular claims. Rebecca Dauer (2005) and John Field (2005, in *TESOL Quarterly*) argued that word stress, which Jenkins put outside the core, matters a great deal for intelligibility: Field found that misplaced word stress, especially a shift to the right, seriously affected listeners' recognition of words, for native and non-native listeners alike.
:::

::: proof
The positions differ because they answer different questions. Researchers in the first group ask how communication works among non-native speakers and what that implies for the aims of teaching; critics in the second ask what a learner should be taught as a target, and doubt that a description of successful interaction can be a target. The third group asks a narrower empirical question — which features matter for intelligibility — on which evidence can decide. There is wide agreement that intelligibility rather than native-likeness is a reasonable aim of pronunciation teaching (a view that Munro and Derwing's research supports, [[listening-and-conversation/accents#rule-accent]]), that learners benefit from hearing many Englishes, and that native speakers in international settings need accommodation skills. There is less agreement on whether ELF forms such as *she look* should be accepted in teaching and examinations. Many teachers take a middle course: they teach a standard model, explain that some deviations matter far more than others, and include ELF awareness and accommodation in their courses.
:::

::: example Weighing a claim {#ex-claim}
A teacher says: *ELF research shows that the third-person -s doesn't matter, so we don't need to teach it.* Using [[#rule-elf-findings]] and [[#rule-elf-debate]], evaluate this claim.
::: solution
1. **What the research found**: Seidlhofer (2004) observed that dropping the third-person *-s* occurred often in VOICE **without causing communication problems** ([[#rule-elf-forms]], point 1). That is a finding about intelligibility in ELF interaction.
2. **What it did not show**: it did not show that the form is unnoticed or accepted in other settings — exams, formal writing, job interviews, publications — where readers and assessors expect Standard English. Seidlhofer herself presented the list as a description, not a teaching syllabus.
3. **The critics' view**: Swan (2012) and Kuo (2006) would argue that proficient users use the *-s*, that learners want and are judged by a full standard, and that the form is easy enough to teach.
4. **A balanced conclusion**: the finding justifies **lower priority** for the *-s* in speaking practice aimed at international communication, compared with features that do cause breakdowns; it does not justify removing it from teaching, because learners will also need Standard English for exams and formal writing.

The claim moves from "does not cause misunderstanding" to "does not need to be taught", and the second does not follow from the first.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Your English is mostly ELF.** For most Chinese users of English, the people they write to and speak with in English are not British or American: they are colleagues, clients and fellow students from other countries. The strategies in [[#rule-strategies]] are therefore at least as useful as a native-like accent.
- **A shared-L1 advantage.** Research has found that listeners often understand speakers who share their first language particularly well (Bent and Bradlow 2003; [[listening-and-conversation/accents#rule-adapt]]). Two Chinese speakers using English with each other may find each other easy to understand; that is no guarantee that a Brazilian or a Nigerian listener will. Practise with speakers of many backgrounds.
- **Priorities.** For Chinese speakers, the core features most often at risk are final consonants and clusters (Mandarin syllables end only in vowels, *-n*, *-ng* or *-r*), vowel length contrasts and the nucleus; /θ ð/ matter less. [[sounds-of-english/sounds-for-chinese-speakers]] gives the full list.
- **Idioms.** Chinese learners sometimes study English idioms intensively. In ELF settings, idioms are a risk rather than an asset unless you are sure your listeners know them; learn them to understand native speakers, and use them with care.
:::

::: history
The original Lingua Franca of the Mediterranean was used from the Middle Ages until the nineteenth century, and gave its name to the whole phenomenon. Research on English in this role began in the 1990s: Alan Firth's study of Danish business calls (1996) described the let-it-pass principle, and Jennifer Jenkins proposed the Lingua Franca Core (2000). In 2001 Barbara Seidlhofer, in "Closing a conceptual gap" (*International Journal of Applied Linguistics*), called for a corpus of ELF, which became VOICE; Anna Mauranen's ELFA corpus followed, and Andy Kirkpatrick's Asian Corpus of English extended the work to Asia. Jenkins's *English as a Lingua Franca: Attitude and Identity* (2007) and Seidlhofer's *Understanding English as a Lingua Franca* (2011) set out the field, and the *Journal of English as a Lingua Franca* was founded in 2012.
:::

## Where this leads

The question of which pronunciation model to aim at, and the place of the native speaker in teaching, is the subject of [[world-englishes/accent-identity]]. English as a lingua franca in China, and the debate about China English, are discussed in [[world-englishes/english-in-china]]. Cooperative strategies in conversation are practised in [[listening-and-conversation/clarification]], and writing for international readers in [[pragmatics/writing-to-people]].

::: summary
- **English as a lingua franca** is English used between speakers of different first languages for whom it is the medium of choice (Seidlhofer 2011); it is defined by function, not by forms ([[#def-elf]]).
- Corpus research (VOICE, ELFA, ACE) has found that ELF communication usually succeeds through cooperation, that some non-native forms cause no problems, and that native-speaker idioms often do ([[#rule-elf-findings]], [[#rule-elf-forms]]).
- Jenkins's **Lingua Franca Core** (2000) gives priority to consonants (except /θ ð/), aspiration, initial clusters, vowel length and nuclear stress; vowel qualities, weak forms and stress-timing are non-core ([[#rule-lfc]]).
- **Accommodation** — speaking clearly, preferring transparent words, repeating, checking, helping — is the key ELF skill, for native and non-native speakers alike ([[#rule-strategies]]).
- The debate: ELF researchers argue that successful forms should not automatically be errors; critics (Swan 2012; Kuo 2006) see them as learner errors; others (Field 2005) question details such as the place of word stress ([[#rule-elf-debate]]).
- A finding that a form does not cause misunderstanding does not by itself mean that it need not be taught.
:::

::: exercise Strategies in a transcript {level=2}
Identify the strategies from [[#rule-strategies]] in this exchange (written for this lesson).

**Park Ji-ho:** *So the shipment is on the twenty… twenty-five?*
**Meera:** *Twenty-fifth, yes. Two-five. I write it in the chat.*
**Park Ji-ho:** *OK. And the — how do you call — the paper for customs?*
**Meera:** *The customs declaration? I send it tomorrow. Is it OK for you?*
::: solution
Meera **repeats and rephrases** the number (*Twenty-fifth … Two-five*) and **writes it down** (point 3); she **helps Ji-ho find a word** (*customs declaration*, point 5); and she **checks understanding** (*Is it OK for you?*, point 4). Ji-ho uses a paraphrase (*the paper for customs*) when he lacks the word, which invites help. Neither comments on the other's grammar (*I write it*, *how do you call*), which does not affect understanding — an example of letting minor points pass (point 6).

::: exercise Evaluate the core {level=2}
In three or four sentences, explain why John Field (2005) disagreed with Jenkins about word stress, and what that disagreement suggests about the LFC.
::: solution
Jenkins placed word stress outside the core, partly because rules of word stress are complex and her data did not show it causing many breakdowns. Field's listening experiments found that moving the stress of a word, especially to a later syllable, seriously reduced listeners' ability to recognise it, for both native and non-native listeners. The disagreement suggests that the LFC is a proposal based on particular data, which later research can revise, and that word stress probably deserves priority in teaching for international intelligibility.
:::

::: exercise Write a balanced paragraph {level=3}
Write 180–220 words on the question *Should English exams accept ELF forms such as "she look" or "discuss about"?* Present the arguments of ELF researchers and of their critics, refer to at least two sources from this chapter, and end with a sentence stating what kind of evidence would help settle the question. Do not give your own opinion.
::: solution
A model answer: *ELF researchers such as Jenkins and Seidlhofer have shown that forms like "she look" and "discuss about" occur frequently in successful communication between non-native speakers, and Seidlhofer (2004) found that they did not cause misunderstandings in the VOICE corpus. On this basis, some argue that exams which aim to measure the ability to communicate internationally should not penalise forms that do not affect intelligibility, and should instead reward accommodation skills. Critics such as Swan (2012) and Kuo (2006) reply that these forms are typical of learners rather than of proficient users, that many learners want and need a full standard for academic and professional purposes, and that exams which accepted them would lower expectations without helping candidates. They also point out that readers in formal settings often judge such forms negatively, whatever their intelligibility. Evidence that would help settle the question includes studies of how such forms are perceived by the readers and listeners candidates will actually meet — employers, universities, colleagues — and of whether accepting them affects how well learners acquire the standard forms.*
