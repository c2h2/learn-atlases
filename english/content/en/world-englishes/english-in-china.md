On a single day in 2026, English appears in China in many forms. A sixteen-year-old in Chengdu practises reading passages for the gaokao. A news agency reports in English on reform of the *hukou* system and on the *Spring Festival* travel rush. A researcher in Hangzhou revises a paper for an international journal. A museum in Xi'an updates the English captions under its exhibits. In Hong Kong, a crowd at a race shouts *Add oil!* to the runners, and a teacher explains to visiting students that the phrase comes from Cantonese 加油. On the internet, someone shares a photograph of a translated sign and asks whether it is correct.

Each of these belongs to a different part of the story of English in China, which is the subject of this chapter: a history that begins with the pidgin of the Canton trade, the place of English in Chinese education, the idea of **China English** (中国英语) and its difference from **Chinglish** (中式英语), and **Hong Kong English**, which has a colonial history unlike that of the rest of China. The chapter describes debates about English in Chinese society neutrally, with the main positions, and treats Chinglish as what it is: the understandable result of transfer between two very different languages, not a joke.

## A short history

English-speaking traders reached the Pearl River delta in the seventeenth century, and in the eighteenth century the East India Company became the largest of the foreign traders at Canton (Guangzhou). From 1757 the Qing court restricted European trade to Canton under the **Canton System**, which lasted until the First Opium War (1839–1842). Communication between Chinese merchants and foreign traders took place largely in **Chinese Pidgin English** ([[world-englishes/pidgins-creoles#rule-cpe]]), which spread after 1842 with the treaty ports to Shanghai and other cities, where it was called 洋泾浜英语.

Formal teaching of English came through missionaries and, later, the Qing state. Robert Morrison, the first Protestant missionary in China, arrived in Canton in 1807 and compiled a *Dictionary of the Chinese Language* (1815–1823). Mission schools taught English; one of their pupils, Yung Wing (容闳), graduated from Yale in 1854, the first Chinese graduate of an American university, and later led the **Chinese Educational Mission** (1872–1881), which sent 120 boys to study in New England. After the Second Opium War, the government founded the **Tongwen Guan** (同文馆) in Beijing in 1862 to train interpreters, beginning with English. Translation brought Western ideas into Chinese: Yan Fu's (严复) version of T. H. Huxley's *Evolution and Ethics*, published as 《天演论》 in 1898, set out in its preface the three criteria of 信, 达, 雅 (‘faithfulness, comprehensibility, elegance’) that are still discussed in translation studies ([[translation/what-translation-is]]). In the Republican period English was taught widely in secondary schools and was the medium of several missionary universities.

After 1949 Russian became the main foreign language in the People's Republic. English returned from the late 1950s and early 1960s, as relations with the Soviet Union deteriorated, was severely disrupted during the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976), and expanded rapidly after the restoration of the national university entrance examination in 1977 and the policy of reform and opening from 1978. The **College English Test** (CET) was first held in 1987 (band 4) and 1989 (band 6). In 2001 the Ministry of Education directed that English should be taught from the third year of primary school, beginning in the cities. The Beijing Olympics of 2008 and the Shanghai Expo of 2010 brought large public campaigns to improve English signs and to encourage residents to learn English.

## English in Chinese education

As of 2026, English is a compulsory subject in most schools from the third year of primary school (earlier in many cities), and a foreign language — usually English — is one of the three main subjects of the gaokao, with Chinese and mathematics, carrying 150 points in most provinces. Universities require a foreign language for most degrees, and the CET remains widely taken. Reforms since 2014 have allowed candidates in some provinces to take the English examination more than once. Exam formats change, so details should always be checked against current official sources.

How much weight English should have has been debated repeatedly in China since the 2010s, in newspapers, online and in proposals by delegates to national and provincial assemblies. The main positions are these.

- **Reduce the weight of English.** Supporters argue that the time spent on English, and on English examinations, is out of proportion to the use most students will make of it; that it adds to the burden on students and families, including the cost of private tutoring; that the time could be given to Chinese, science or other subjects; and that machine translation reduces the need for every student to master a foreign language.
- **Keep or strengthen English.** Supporters argue that English is the main language of international science, technology, trade and diplomacy; that China's engagement with the world requires many people with a high level of English; that reducing English in public education would mainly harm students who cannot afford private lessons; and that learning a foreign language has educational value beyond its use.
- **Reform rather than reduce.** Others argue that the problem is not the amount of English but the way it is taught and tested: they propose more emphasis on use and less on examination technique, more choice of foreign languages, and different expectations for different students.

All three positions agree that English matters for China's international role; they differ over how many students need how much of it, and over how to balance efficiency, equity and the interests of individual students.

## China English

In 1980 the translator and lexicographer **Ge Chuangui** (葛传槼), writing on Chinese–English translation in the journal 《翻译通讯》, argued that translators need English expressions for things that exist only in China, and that such expressions are not errors. He called them **China English** (中国英语) and contrasted them with Chinglish. The term began a debate that has continued for more than forty years.

::: definition China English {#def-china-english}
**China English** (中国英语) is English used to express things specific to Chinese society, culture and history, with standard English as its basis and with features that reflect Chinese thought and language — through borrowing (*hukou*, *gaokao*, *guanxi*, *mahjong*), loan translation (*Spring Festival*, *paper tiger*, *the Four Modernizations*) and new senses of English words. The definition was developed by Wang Rongpei (1991) and Li Wenzhong (1993), among others. Xu Zhichang (*Chinese English: Features and Implications*, 2010) prefers the term **Chinese English** for a developing variety used by Chinese speakers, which includes features of grammar, discourse and pragmatics as well as vocabulary.
:::

::: theorem How China English names Chinese things {#rule-naming}
China English vocabulary is formed mainly in four ways:

1. **Transliteration** in Pinyin (or older romanisations): *hukou* ‘household registration’, *gaokao*, *guanxi* ‘personal connections’, *jiaozi*, *qipao*, *kung fu*, *tai chi*.
2. **Loan translation** (calque), translating the parts: *Spring Festival* (春节), *Lantern Festival* (元宵节), *paper tiger* (纸老虎), *lose face* (丢脸), *iron rice bowl* (铁饭碗).
3. **Hybrids**, Pinyin with an English word: *Peking opera*, *Mid-Autumn Festival* with *mooncakes*, *Xuanwu Lake*, *Zhongshan Road*.
4. **Explanation** on first use, for readers who may not know the term: *the gaokao, China's national university entrance examination*.
:::

::: proof
Every variety of English names the realities of its place: Australian English has *billabong*, Indian English *lakh*, South African English *braai* ([[world-englishes/other-first-language-varieties]]). China English does the same, and the four methods are those of all borrowing between languages. Transliteration keeps the Chinese word and works best for things with no close English equivalent; loan translation makes a term transparent; hybrids are typical of place names; and explanation makes either method accessible to readers outside China. Some China English terms have passed into general English and appear in English dictionaries — *kowtow* and *lose face* long ago, *guanxi* more recently — and many are used in English-language news and scholarship worldwide. This is why linguists such as Li Wenzhong distinguished China English sharply from Chinglish: China English is the **normal** way English expresses Chinese content, and Chinglish is a matter of form ([[#def-chinglish]]).
:::

### Names of places

Chinese place names in English follow conventions that show how English adapts to local naming, and they also involve English articles ([[nouns-and-articles/articles-with-names]]). Since 1979 the Chinese government has used Hanyu Pinyin for Chinese names in foreign-language publications, and Pinyin is now the international standard (ISO 7098): *Beijing* rather than the older *Peking*, *Guangzhou* rather than *Canton*, although older forms survive in fixed names (*Peking University*, *Peking duck*, *Tsinghua University*). A place name usually combines a Pinyin specific element with an English generic element (*Zhongshan Road*, *Xuanwu Lake*, *Mount Tai*), and English article rules then apply: *the* with rivers and with plural mountain ranges (*the Yangtze*, *the Yellow River*, *the Qinling Mountains*), but not with single mountains, lakes or most streets (*Mount Emei*, *West Lake*, *Nanjing Road*). Some names are written entirely in Pinyin (*Taishan*, *Huangshan*), which then needs no article; national standards for English on public signs, issued from 2017 as GB/T 30240, set out conventions for such names.

::: example China English or Chinglish? {#ex-china-english}
Classify each as China English (an acceptable way of expressing a Chinese reality in English) or as a form that departs from English norms through transfer (Chinglish). Explain.

(a) *During the Spring Festival, families gather for a reunion dinner.* (b) *My hukou is still in my hometown in Anhui.* (c) *Please open the light, the room is too dark.* (d) *Our company has good guanxi with the local government.* (e) *I very like this city.*
::: solution
1. (a) **China English** — *Spring Festival* is the established loan translation of 春节 and *reunion dinner* of 团圆饭; both are standard in English-language writing about China.
2. (b) **China English** — *hukou* is the usual transliteration for a Chinese institution with no English equivalent; for readers outside China, add an explanation on first use (*household registration, or hukou*).
3. (c) **Chinglish** — *open the light* transfers 开灯; standard English uses *turn on* or *switch on* (*Please turn on the light*). The meaning is clear, but the form departs from the norm. (The same form is standard in Philippine English: [[world-englishes/southeast-asian-englishes#rule-philippine]].)
4. (d) **China English** — *guanxi* is in English dictionaries and widely used in business writing about China.
5. (e) **Chinglish** — *very* placed before a verb transfers 很喜欢; English requires *I like this city very much* or *I really like this city*.

The distinction is not about whether a phrase comes from Chinese — all five do — but about whether it uses English normally to express a Chinese idea (a, b, d) or carries Chinese grammar or collocation into English in a way that departs from its norms (c, e).
:::
:::

### Beyond vocabulary

Xu Zhichang (2010), drawing on interviews and writing by educated Chinese speakers, described features of **Chinese English** beyond vocabulary. Some concern grammar, such as leaving out subjects and objects that are clear from context, or placing a topic first (*This movie, I have seen it*). Others concern discourse and pragmatics:

- **Yes and no in answers to negative questions.** Chinese answers agree or disagree with the proposition: to 你不去吗？ ‘aren’t you going?’, 是的 ‘yes’ means ‘right, I’m not going’. Many Chinese speakers carry this into English: *Aren't you coming? — Yes* (‘yes, that’s right, I’m not coming’), where GB and GA speakers would say *No*.
- **Information order.** In many genres Chinese places background and reasons before the main point (*因为…所以…*). Linda Young (*Crosstalk and Culture in Sino-American Communication*, 1994) and Andy Kirkpatrick found this "because … therefore" order in Chinese speakers' English, where American listeners often expected the main point first.
- **Address and politeness.** Forms such as *Teacher Wang* (王老师), responses to compliments that deny them, and polite formulas translated directly reflect Chinese pragmatic norms ([[pragmatics/apologies-thanks-compliments]], [[pragmatics/directness-across-cultures]]).

Whether such features make up a variety — and whether they should be accepted in teaching — is disputed. Studies of attitudes, such as He Deyuan and David Li's survey of Chinese university students and teachers ("Language attitudes and linguistic features in the ‘China English’ debate", *World Englishes*, 2009), found that most preferred a native-speaker model for teaching, while many were willing to include features of China English, especially in vocabulary. Supporters of China English, such as Hu Xiaoqiong (*English Today*, 2004), argued that it should stand alongside other world Englishes; sceptics argue that, without internal functions and a community that uses English among itself, China English remains a set of conventions for expressing Chinese content rather than a variety in Kachru's or Schneider's sense ([[world-englishes/models#def-dynamic]]). The debate continues; most participants accept that China English vocabulary is legitimate, and they disagree mainly about grammar, discourse and models for teaching.

::: example Yes and no {#ex-yes-no}
A visiting professor asks Wang Fang: *You haven't finished the report, have you?* Wang Fang, who has not finished it, answers *Yes.* The professor thinks the report is finished. Explain the misunderstanding and give Wang Fang a clearer answer.
::: solution
In Chinese, the answer agrees with the proposition in the question: to 你还没写完，对吧？ ‘you haven’t finished, right?’, 对 ‘right’ means ‘I haven’t finished’. Wang Fang's *Yes* means ‘yes, what you say is right’. In GB and GA, *yes* and *no* refer to the positive form of the answer: *Yes* means ‘I have finished’, *No* means ‘I haven’t’. The professor interpreted her *Yes* in the English way.

A clearer answer avoids the ambiguity entirely by adding the full clause: *No, I haven't finished it yet* — or, if Wang Fang is unsure which word the listener expects, *I haven't finished it yet.* Repeating the verb is the safe strategy in any international setting ([[world-englishes/lingua-franca#rule-strategies]]).
:::
:::

## Chinglish

::: definition Chinglish {#def-chinglish}
**Chinglish** (中式英语) is an informal name for English that shows transfer from Chinese in vocabulary, grammar, collocation or pragmatics in ways that depart from the norms of standard English and may make it harder for readers or listeners elsewhere to understand. Linguists distinguish it from China English ([[#def-china-english]]): China English uses English normally to express Chinese content; Chinglish carries Chinese structures into English forms.
:::

Chinglish is not a variety; it describes the output of individual learners and translators, and it is the natural result of learning or translating between two languages that differ greatly ([[translation/english-chinese-compared]]). Joan Pinkham's *The Translator's Guide to Chinglish* (2000), written for Chinese translators, catalogued its main kinds and showed how to revise them. The common types correspond to the main differences between the two languages.

::: theorem Main types of transfer in Chinglish {#rule-transfer}
1. **Word-for-word translation of collocations**: *open the light* (开灯), *learn knowledge* (学习知识), *eat medicine* (吃药), where English uses *turn on the light*, *gain knowledge* or simply *learn*, *take medicine*.
2. **Grammatical transfer**: *very* before a verb (*I very like*); paired connectives (*Although it rained, but we went*, from 虽然…但是…); topic-comment order without a resumptive structure (*This problem we must pay attention*); no article or plural ending where English requires one ([[nouns-and-articles/articles-with-names]]).
3. **Redundancy**: words that are needed in Chinese but repeat meaning in English: *the reason is because* (原因是因为), *return back* (返回), *advance forward*.
4. **Abstract or formulaic nouns** translated directly: *We should strengthen the construction of the work of propaganda* for 加强宣传工作建设 — where English prefers a verb and fewer nouns (*We should improve our publicity*; [[style-and-rhetoric/concision]]).
5. **Pragmatic transfer**: formulas translated literally, such as *You must be tired* (辛苦了) to a visitor, which an English listener may hear as a comment on their appearance; *Where are you going?* as a greeting (去哪儿？), which may be heard as an enquiry into private matters.
6. **Public signs** translated word by word, so that a short Chinese instruction becomes an English phrase with a different grammar or an unintended meaning.
:::

::: proof
Each type follows from a systematic difference between Chinese and English. Chinese collocations differ from English ones (开 covers ‘open’, ‘turn on’, ‘start’, ‘drive’), so a direct translation chooses the wrong English verb ([[collocation-and-register/false-friends-chinglish]]). Chinese adverbs of degree precede verbs, and paired connectives are normal in Chinese but redundant in English. Chinese official style uses chains of abstract nouns, which in English sound heavy. Pragmatic formulas carry meanings fixed by convention, which literal translation loses. Public signs are especially vulnerable because they are short, lack context and are often translated by people without training, sometimes with dictionaries or machine translation that choose the wrong sense of a word. None of this reflects poorly on the people involved: the same processes produce "Franglais" and "Denglish" in French and German speakers' English, and English speakers' Chinese shows the reverse transfer.
:::

::: example Revising transferred sentences {#ex-revise}
Revise each sentence into standard English and name the type of transfer: (a) *Although the price is high, but the quality is good.* (b) *The reason why I am late is because the bus broke down.* (c) *We will further strengthen the management of the construction of the project.* (d) *You must be tired! Let me help you with your bag.* (said to a visiting professor arriving at the airport)
::: solution
1. (a) Grammatical transfer, paired connectives (虽然…但是…): ✓ *Although the price is high, the quality is good* or ✓ *The price is high, but the quality is good.* English uses one connective, not both.
2. (b) Redundancy (原因是因为): ✓ *I am late because the bus broke down* or ✓ *The reason I am late is that the bus broke down.*
3. (c) Abstract nouns (加强项目建设的管理): ✓ *We will manage the project more closely.* One verb replaces three nouns.
4. (d) Pragmatic transfer (辛苦了): the formula expresses appreciation for the effort of travelling, but *You must be tired!* may sound like a comment on how the professor looks. ✓ *Welcome! How was your flight? Let me help you with your bag.*

In each case the meaning was clear to a Chinese reader and usually recoverable by others. Revision makes the English conventional, so that the reader's attention goes to the content.
:::
:::

::: warning Laughing at signs
✗ Sharing photographs of mistranslated signs to laugh at them, or describing Chinglish as funny or ridiculous.
✓ Analysing a mistranslation to understand the transfer behind it, and suggesting a conventional version.

Collections of mistranslated signs circulate widely online, and some have become well known in English-speaking countries. Their humour depends on reading a translation as if it were a native English sentence, while ignoring the Chinese text that explains it; it also attaches the errors of individual translators to a whole country. A sign that reads oddly in English is a translation problem with a cause that can be identified ([[#rule-transfer]]). Many Chinese cities have run campaigns to revise their English signs, and national standards now guide them.
:::

A few expressions that began as transfer have passed into English as accepted idioms. *Long time no see* is often said to come from Chinese Pidgin English or to translate 好久不见, although its origin is not certain. *Add oil*, from Cantonese 加油 ‘keep going’, is widely used in Hong Kong English and has been recorded by English dictionaries. What begins as transfer can become innovation once it is used and accepted by a community ([[world-englishes/models#rule-innovation]]).

## Hong Kong English

Hong Kong Island was ceded to Britain in 1842, Kowloon in 1860, and the New Territories were leased in 1898; the whole territory returned to Chinese sovereignty on 1 July 1997 as a Special Administrative Region. English was the sole official language for most of the colonial period; the Official Languages Ordinance of 1974 made Chinese an official language too, and the Basic Law provides that English may also be used as an official language. Most of the population speaks Cantonese as its first language. Since 1997 the government's language policy has aimed at **biliteracy and trilingualism** (两文三语): written Chinese and English, and spoken Cantonese, Putonghua and English. In 1998 most government-funded secondary schools were required to teach in Chinese, with a minority allowed to continue in English; the policy was relaxed in 2010.

Hong Kong English has been described by Kingsley Bolton (*Hong Kong English: Autonomy and Creativity*, 2002) and by Jane Setter, Cathy Wong and Brian Chan (*Hong Kong English*, 2010). Its status is debated: Schneider (2007) saw it as moving into nativisation, but others have argued that English is used too little among Hong Kong people themselves for a local variety to stabilise, and that the Dynamic Model fits Hong Kong only partly.

::: theorem Some features of Hong Kong English {#rule-hk}
1. **/θ/ as [f]** at the start of words (*three* [friː], *think* [fɪŋk]); **/ð/ as [d]** (*this* [dɪs]).
2. **Initial /n/ and /l/** not distinguished by some speakers, reflecting a change in progress in Hong Kong Cantonese (*night* and *light* may sound alike).
3. **Final stops** unreleased and final clusters simplified (*act* [æk̚]).
4. **Fewer vowel length contrasts** (*ship*–*sheep*), and syllable-based rhythm with fewer reduced vowels.
5. **Code-mixing** with Cantonese in informal speech, with English nouns, verbs and adjectives in Cantonese sentences (*我哋 later 再 discuss* ‘we’ll discuss it later’).
6. **Vocabulary** from Cantonese and local senses: *add oil* ‘keep going’, *yum cha* ‘have dim sum and tea’, *dai pai dong* ‘open-air food stall’, *wet market* ‘market selling fresh food’, *sitting-out area* ‘small public seating area’.
:::

::: example Hong Kong English pronunciation {#ex-hk}
Predict how some speakers of Hong Kong English may pronounce *I think the three lines are fine*, and say which features might cause difficulty for a listener from Canada.
::: solution
Using [[#rule-hk]]:

1. *think* — [fɪŋk]: /θ/ as [f] (point 1).
2. *the* — [də]: /ð/ as [d] (point 1).
3. *three* — [friː]: /θ/ as [f] (point 1).
4. *lines* — possibly [naɪns] for speakers who do not distinguish initial /n/ and /l/ (point 2).
5. *fine* — [faɪn], as in GB.

The /θ/ → [f] and /ð/ → [d] substitutions rarely cause difficulty; they are outside the Lingua Franca Core ([[world-englishes/lingua-franca#rule-lfc]]), and context makes *three* clear. The most likely difficulty is *lines* heard as *nines*, which in a sentence about numbers could matter; that contrast belongs to the core consonants. A speaker aiming at international intelligibility would give priority to /n/–/l/ rather than to /θ/.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Three terms.** 中国英语 (China English) is generally used for legitimate English expressing Chinese content; 中式英语 (Chinglish) for transfer that departs from English norms; and *Chinese English* is used by Xu (2010) for a developing variety of Chinese speakers' English. In everyday Chinese, 中式英语 is sometimes used loosely for both — which is precisely the confusion Ge Chuangui wanted to avoid.
- **Your own English.** Most Chinese learners produce some transferred forms, as every learner does. The useful question is not "Is this Chinglish?" but "Will my reader understand this easily, and does the setting require standard forms?" In an exam or a journal article, revise transferred forms; in a chat with a colleague who shares your language, they rarely matter.
- **Chinese content.** You do not need to avoid Chinese terms when writing in English about China. Use the established China English terms (*hukou*, *Spring Festival*, *gaokao*), explain them on first use for international readers, and follow English article rules with names.
- **Accent.** A Chinese accent of English is systematic ([[listening-and-conversation/accents]]); for international intelligibility, final consonants, clusters, vowel length and the nucleus matter more than /θ/ ([[world-englishes/lingua-franca#rule-lfc]]). The choice of model is discussed in [[world-englishes/accent-identity]].
:::

::: history
Chinese Pidgin English of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries was the first English widely used in China. Missionaries such as Robert Morrison (from 1807), the Tongwen Guan (1862), the Chinese Educational Mission (1872–1881) and the translations of Yan Fu (1898) brought English and Western learning into Chinese education. After 1978 English became the main foreign language of the People's Republic, with the CET from 1987 and primary-school English from 2001. Ge Chuangui named China English in 1980; Wang Rongpei (1991) and Li Wenzhong (1993) developed the concept; Joan Pinkham's *Translator's Guide to Chinglish* (2000) analysed transfer; Kingsley Bolton's *Chinese Englishes* (2003) wrote the history; and Xu Zhichang's *Chinese English* (2010) described its features.
:::

Revise each sentence and name the type of transfer from [[#rule-transfer]]: (a) *We should learn more knowledge.* (b) *Please return back the books by Friday.* (c) *Because the weather was bad, so the flight was delayed.* (d) *I very appreciate your help.*
::: solution
(a) Collocation (学习知识): *We should learn more* or *We should gain more knowledge.* (b) Redundancy (返回): *Please return the books by Friday.* (c) Paired connectives (因为…所以…): *Because the weather was bad, the flight was delayed* or *The weather was bad, so the flight was delayed.* (d) *very* before a verb (很感谢): *I really appreciate your help* or *I appreciate your help very much.*
:::

::: exercise Explain for a reader {level=2}
Rewrite this sentence for an international reader, keeping the China English terms but making them clear: *After the gaokao, many students who failed to get into a key university move their hukou to a bigger city.*
::: solution
A model answer: *After the gaokao, China's national university entrance examination, many students who did not gain a place at a leading ("key") university move their hukou — their household registration, which affects access to public services — to a larger city.* The terms are kept and explained on first use ([[#rule-naming]], point 4).
:::

::: exercise Hong Kong English {level=2}
A Hong Kong student writes in a chat: *Add oil for the exam! 我哋 later 再 discuss the project.* (a) What does *Add oil* mean, and where does it come from? (b) What is the second sentence an example of? (c) Would either be appropriate in a formal email to a professor in London?
::: solution
(a) ‘Keep going, good luck’ — from Cantonese 加油, literally ‘add oil (fuel)’. (b) Code-mixing of English and Cantonese (‘we’ll discuss the project later’), common in informal Hong Kong speech and writing ([[#rule-hk]], point 5). (c) No: *add oil* would be understood by many readers who know Hong Kong English, but in a formal email to someone outside Hong Kong, *Good luck with the exam* is safer, and code-mixing is not used in formal English writing.
:::

::: exercise Present the debate {level=3}
Write 200–250 words presenting the debate about the weight of English in the gaokao for a class discussion. Present at least two positions with their main arguments, mention at least one fact about English in Chinese education from this chapter, and end with two questions that would help the class think about the issue. Do not give your own view.
::: solution
A good answer accurately describes the current position (as of 2026, English or another foreign language is one of three main gaokao subjects, usually worth 150 points; English is taught from the third year of primary school), presents the argument for reducing its weight (time and cost, burden on students, priority of Chinese and other subjects, machine translation) and for keeping it (international science and trade, equity for students who cannot afford private tuition, educational value), and perhaps the reform position (change teaching and testing rather than weight). Possible questions: *How many students actually use English after school, and for what?* *Who would gain and who would lose if English were made optional?* The answer should be balanced and avoid one-sided language.
:::

::: exercise Analyse a sign {level=3}
Find (or imagine) a public sign or a menu item in China whose English version reads oddly. Write 150–200 words: give the Chinese and the English, explain the transfer that produced the English (with reference to [[#rule-transfer]]), and propose a conventional English version. Write respectfully, as a translator would.
::: hint
Look first at the structure of the Chinese: is a character ambiguous? Is a collocation being translated word by word? Is an instruction phrased as a noun?
:::
::: solution
Answers vary. A good answer identifies the source precisely — for example, a sign 小心地滑 ‘caution: slippery floor’, where 地 can be read either as the noun ‘floor’ or as the adverbial particle (小心地 ‘carefully’), so a translator who chose the second reading produced an instruction about moving carefully rather than a warning about the floor. It then proposes a conventional sign (*Caution: Wet Floor* or *Caution: Slippery Floor*), and notes the general point: short signs give translators no context, so ambiguous characters and collocations are easily mistranslated. The tone should be analytical, not mocking.
:::
