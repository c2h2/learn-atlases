Liu Yang has just started work at an engineering firm in Abuja. In his first week, three small things puzzle him. A colleague, Chinedu, gets up in the middle of a conversation and says *I'm coming*, then walks out of the room; he returns five minutes later. When Liu Yang drops his phone, two people say *Sorry!* although neither of them touched it. And a manager asks him to *off the projector* when the presentation ends. By the end of the month he says all three himself. Chinedu's *I'm coming* means ‘wait, I’ll be right back’; the *Sorry!* expresses sympathy, not apology; and *off* as a verb, ‘switch off’, is used across West Africa.

Africa and the Caribbean are home to some of the oldest Englishes outside Britain and to some of the most varied. In West and East Africa, English is mostly a second language, used for education, government and business beside hundreds of African languages, and it is often an official language of the state. In the Caribbean, English arrived with plantation slavery, and the everyday languages of most people are **creoles** that developed from English and West African languages ([[world-englishes/pidgins-creoles]]). In both regions English is tied to a painful history, and the question of what role it should have has been argued over by writers, politicians and linguists. This chapter describes the varieties and sets out that debate.

## English in Africa

British trade on the West African coast goes back to the sixteenth century, and the slave trade, which carried millions of Africans across the Atlantic, used forms of English along the coast long before colonial rule. Most of the continent was divided among European powers only in the late nineteenth century, and the British colonies became independent between the 1950s and the 1980s: Ghana in 1957, Nigeria in 1960, Sierra Leone in 1961, Tanzania (as Tanganyika) in 1961, Uganda in 1962, Kenya in 1963, Zambia and Malawi in 1964, Zimbabwe in 1980. Liberia has a different history: it was founded in 1822 as a settlement for freed African Americans and became independent in 1847, and its English has American roots.

::: theorem The position of English in anglophone Africa {#rule-africa}
In most African countries that were British colonies, English:

1. is an **official language** — often the only one, or one of two — used in government, the courts, the national media and higher education;
2. is a **second language** for most of its users, acquired at school, beside one or more African first languages;
3. serves as a **link language** between speakers of different African languages, especially in countries with many languages and no single majority language;
4. exists beside an **English-lexifier pidgin or creole** in West Africa (Nigerian Pidgin, Ghanaian Pidgin, Cameroon Pidgin English, Krio in Sierra Leone), and beside **Swahili** as a wider lingua franca in East Africa;
5. is used along a **range** from a local standard to more informal forms, by speakers who move along it according to setting.
:::

::: proof
The pattern follows from the colonial history and from the linguistic situation after independence. The new states had many languages: Nigeria alone has more than five hundred according to *Ethnologue*, with three large ones — Hausa, Yoruba and Igbo — each associated with a region. Choosing one of them as the national language would have favoured one group over the others, and English, the language of the colonial administration and of the educated elite, was available as a neutral option, even if its neutrality was itself debatable. In East Africa the situation differed because Swahili was already a lingua franca across much of the region; Tanzania promoted Swahili strongly after independence, and Kenya's Constitution of 2010 makes Swahili the national language and Swahili and English the official languages. The pidgins of West Africa grew from the trade languages of the coast and are now spoken by tens of millions, often more widely than English itself ([[world-englishes/pidgins-creoles]]).
:::

## Nigerian English

Nigeria, the most populous country in Africa, has the largest number of English users on the continent. Nigerian linguists have described **Nigerian English** since the 1960s: Ayo Banjo (1971) distinguished several varieties along a range from the speech of people with little schooling to a standard close to British English, and identified the second-highest of these, which is both internationally intelligible and recognisably Nigerian, as the appropriate model. David Jowitt's *Nigerian English Usage* (1991) described the vocabulary and grammar of educated Nigerians.

::: theorem Some features of Nigerian English {#rule-nigerian}
**Pronunciation**

1. **A smaller vowel system**: many speakers do not distinguish KIT from FLEECE, FOOT from GOOSE, or TRAP and STRUT from BATH and START, so that *ship* and *sheep*, *full* and *fool*, *cat* and *cut* may sound alike; FACE and GOAT are single vowels [e], [o].
2. **/θ/ and /ð/** as [t] and [d] (*think*, *those*).
3. **Non-rhotic**, with fuller vowels in unstressed syllables and a syllable-based rhythm.
4. **Pitch** that can behave more like the tones of Yoruba, Igbo or Hausa than like English intonation, with high pitch on stressed syllables.

**Grammar and vocabulary**

5. **Plural forms of nouns that are uncountable in GB and GA**: *furnitures*, *equipments*, *advices*, *luggages*.
6. **Invariant tags**: *You will come, isn't it?*; *not so?*
7. **Local words and senses**: *go-slow* ‘traffic jam’; *next tomorrow* ‘the day after tomorrow’; *senior brother* ‘older brother’; *flash* ‘call someone’s phone and hang up so that they call back’; *off* and *on* as verbs ‘switch off, switch on’; *I'm coming* ‘wait, I’ll be right back’.
8. **Pragmatic conventions**: *Sorry!* to express sympathy when someone has a small accident; *Well done!* as a greeting to someone at work; elaborate greetings and enquiries after family.
:::

::: proof
Many of these features reflect the languages Nigerians speak first. Yoruba, Igbo and Hausa have fewer vowel contrasts than English and no vowels exactly like the English central vowels, so English vowels are mapped onto a smaller set; they are tone languages, in which pitch distinguishes words, which affects how English stress is realised. The pluralisation of nouns such as *furniture* reflects a regularisation found in many second-language varieties around the world — eWAVE records it in varieties from Africa to Asia — and the fact that the count/mass distinction is arbitrary in English (*furniture* is uncountable but *chairs* is not). Features such as *go-slow* and *next tomorrow* are local innovations; the pragmatic conventions — sympathetic *Sorry!*, *Well done!* to a worker — transfer politeness routines from Nigerian languages and cultures into English. Banjo's and Jowitt's descriptions, and the use of many of these forms in Nigerian newspapers and broadcasting, show that the most widely shared of them are features of educated Nigerian English, not individual errors ([[world-englishes/models#rule-innovation]]).
:::

::: example Understanding Nigerian English {#ex-nigerian}
Explain the meaning of each sentence and name the feature. (a) *I'll see you next tomorrow.* (b) *Please flash me when you arrive.* (c) *The go-slow on the bridge was terrible.* (d) *All the equipments are in the store.* (e) Liu Yang trips on a step; a colleague says *Sorry!*
::: solution
1. (a) ‘I’ll see you the day after tomorrow’ — local vocabulary ([[#rule-nigerian]], point 7).
2. (b) ‘Call my phone briefly and hang up when you arrive’ (so that I know, at no cost to you) — a local sense of *flash* (point 7).
3. (c) ‘The traffic jam on the bridge was terrible’ — *go-slow*, a long-established Nigerian word (point 7).
4. (d) ‘All the equipment is in the store’ — plural of a noun that is uncountable in GB and GA (point 5); in Nigerian English *equipments* is very common, although edited writing often uses *equipment*.
5. (e) The colleague is expressing sympathy — ‘oh dear, I hope you’re all right’ — not apologising (point 8). The reply is *Thank you* or *I'm fine*, not *It's not your fault*.
:::
:::

::: example Hearing a smaller vowel system {#ex-vowels}
A Nigerian colleague says [ʃip] and [ful]. Without context, which words might she mean? Why is this rarely a problem in practice?
::: solution
With KIT and FLEECE merged ([[#rule-nigerian]], point 1), [ʃip] can be *ship* or *sheep*; with FOOT and GOOSE merged, [ful] can be *full* or *fool*. In practice context almost always decides: *The ship left the port*, *The bus was full*. A listener used to GB can adapt by relying on context and stress, as with any accent ([[listening-and-conversation/accents#rule-strategies]]). In lingua franca communication, however, vowel **length** contrasts of this kind are among the features Jenkins (2000) found most important for intelligibility ([[world-englishes/lingua-franca]]), so speakers who use English internationally often pay attention to them.
:::
:::

## Ghana and East Africa

**Ghana**, the first colony in sub-Saharan Africa to become independent, uses English as its official language beside many Ghanaian languages, of which Akan (including Twi and Fante) is the most widely spoken. **Ghanaian English** shares many West African features with Nigerian English — a smaller vowel system, syllable-based rhythm, invariant tags, pluralised mass nouns — and has its own vocabulary. A Ghanaian Pidgin, used especially by young men and in schools and universities, has become a marker of informal solidarity; the word *chale* ‘friend, mate’ is widely known.

In **Kenya**, **Uganda** and **Tanzania**, English shares the space with Swahili. Kenyan English is the language of the courts, Parliament (beside Swahili) and higher education, and has local vocabulary such as *matatu* ‘shared minibus taxi’ and the formal *alight* ‘get off a vehicle’, which is more common than in GB or GA. In Nairobi, young people use **Sheng**, a mixed code combining Swahili grammar with words from English, Swahili and other Kenyan languages, which has spread through music and broadcasting; it is not English, but it shows how English vocabulary is absorbed into other languages. Tanzania, under its first president Julius Nyerere, made Swahili the language of government and primary education, and English remains the language of secondary and higher education.

::: quiz
A Ghanaian colleague says: *Let us meet at 3 p.m., not so?* What does *not so?* do here?
- [ ] It means ‘not at 3 p.m.’
- [x] It is an invariant tag asking for agreement, like *OK?* or *shall we?*
- [ ] It shows that the speaker is unsure of the time.
- [ ] It is a mistake for *isn't it so?*
::: solution
*Not so?* is an invariant tag ([[#rule-nigerian]], point 6), used in West African Englishes after a clause of any form to ask for agreement, as *isn't it?* is in Indian and Welsh English and *no?* in many varieties. GB would use a tag that matches the clause (*shall we?*) or *OK?*
:::
:::

## Language and colonialism

English came to Africa and the Caribbean with conquest and slavery, and its role today raises a question that has no single accepted answer: is English a means of continued domination, or a resource that its users have made their own? Three positions have been especially influential.

::: definition Linguistic imperialism {#def-imperialism}
**Linguistic imperialism**, in Robert Phillipson's book of that name (1992), is the dominance of one language over others, maintained by the unequal allocation of resources and status to it — for example, when English is the language of the best schools, jobs and government positions and other languages are left without such resources. Phillipson argued that the global spread of English since 1945 has been actively promoted by Britain and the United States and by the English-teaching profession, and that it disadvantages speakers of other languages.
:::

The three positions are these.

- **Return to African languages.** The Kenyan novelist **Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o**, who had written his first novels in English, wrote his novel *Caitaani Mũtharaba-Inĩ* (1980; *Devil on the Cross*) in Gĩkũyũ while in detention, and in *Decolonising the Mind* (1986) announced that he would no longer write fiction in English. He argued that a language carries a culture, that colonial education had taught African children to see their own languages as inferior, and that African literature and education should be in African languages. Supporters of this view also point to research showing that children learn best when they are first taught in a language they understand.
- **An African English.** The Nigerian novelist **Chinua Achebe**, in a 1965 essay later reprinted as "The African Writer and the English Language" in *Morning Yet on Creation Day* (1975), accepted English as a language that had been imposed but was now available to Africans, and argued that it could carry African experience if it became "a new English, still in full communion with its ancestral home but altered to suit its new African surroundings". His own novels, beginning with *Things Fall Apart* (1958), put that view into practice.
- **Agency and appropriation.** Some linguists have questioned the account of English as imposed from outside. Janina Brutt-Griffler, in *World English: A Study of Its Development* (2002), argued that colonial governments often **limited** access to English, and that its spread was driven largely by Africans and Asians who demanded it for their own purposes; on this view, the users of English are its agents, not only its victims. Others have argued that Phillipson's account underestimates the choices of individuals and communities.

These positions are not simple opposites, and many writers combine elements of them. Most agree that a child's first language matters for early education and that access to English is unequal; they disagree about what follows for language policy, and about whether English in Africa today is best seen as a legacy of empire, a neutral tool, or an African language in its own right.

::: example Recognising the positions {#ex-positions}
Which of the three positions above does each statement express most closely? (a) *My grandparents paid school fees so that my parents could learn English; nobody forced it on them.* (b) *Our children should first learn to read in the language they speak at home.* (c) *I write in English, but my characters speak a Nigerian English that no Londoner would write.*
::: solution
1. (a) **Agency and appropriation**: the speaker stresses that English was sought, not imposed (Brutt-Griffler 2002).
2. (b) **Return to African languages**, at least for early education: the view associated with Ngũgĩ and supported by research on learning in a familiar language. It does not necessarily reject English later in school.
3. (c) **An African English**: the writer uses English but reshapes it, as Achebe proposed.

Notice that one person could hold all three views at once — about different domains (school, literature, history). This is why the debate is best presented as a set of positions with reasons rather than as two camps.
:::
:::

## The Caribbean

English came to the Caribbean with the colonies of the seventeenth century — Barbados was settled by the English in 1627, and Jamaica was taken from Spain in 1655 — and with the plantation economy, which brought hundreds of thousands of enslaved Africans to work on sugar estates. In this setting, as described in [[world-englishes/pidgins-creoles]], **creoles** developed: new languages with vocabulary mostly from English and grammar shaped by West African languages and by the process of language creation itself. Jamaica and Trinidad and Tobago became independent in 1962, and Barbados and Guyana in 1966.

Today most Caribbean countries that were British colonies have English as their official language, and a creole — Jamaican Creole (often called *Patwa* or *Patois*), Bajan in Barbados, Trinidadian Creole, Guyanese Creole — as the everyday language of most people. Between the creole and the standard lies a range of intermediate forms.

::: definition Creole continuum {#def-continuum}
A **creole continuum** is a range of varieties between a creole and the standard language from which most of its vocabulary comes, with no clear boundary between them. Linguists call the variety furthest from the standard the **basilect**, the standard end the **acrolect**, and the forms between them the **mesolect**. Most speakers command a stretch of the continuum and move along it according to setting, as David DeCamp (1971) described for Jamaica and Derek Bickerton (*Dynamics of a Creole System*, 1975) for Guyana.
:::

Standard Caribbean English, the acrolect, is close to British English in grammar and spelling but has its own vocabulary and pronunciation, recorded in Richard Allsopp's *Dictionary of Caribbean English Usage* (1996) and, for Jamaica, in Frederic Cassidy and Robert Le Page's *Dictionary of Jamaican English* (1967). Examples are *lime* ‘spend time socially with friends’ (Trinidad and elsewhere), *duppy* ‘ghost’ and *pickney* ‘child’ (both widely used in creole and informal English). Pronunciation varies by island: some Caribbean Englishes are rhotic and others are not, /θ ð/ are often [t d], and in Jamaica FACE and GOAT often have rising diphthongs [ie], [uo] or single vowels.

::: example Placing utterances on the continuum {#ex-continuum}
Place each Jamaican utterance on the continuum (basilect, mesolect or acrolect) and give your reason. (a) *Im did go a town yesside.* (b) *He went to town yesterday.* (c) *Him went to town yesterday.*
::: solution
1. (a) **Basilect**: *im* for ‘he’, the pre-verbal past marker *did* with an unmarked verb *go*, *a* for ‘to’, and *yesside* ‘yesterday’ — all creole features.
2. (b) **Acrolect**: Standard English throughout.
3. (c) **Mesolect**: the creole pronoun *him* as subject, but the English past form *went* and English *to town*.

The same speaker might produce all three in a single day — (a) with family, (c) with a neighbour, (b) in a job interview — moving along the continuum as Singaporeans move between Singlish and Standard Singapore English ([[world-englishes/southeast-asian-englishes#def-sse]]).
:::
:::

Caribbean writers have made the creoles a language of literature. The Jamaican poet **Louise Bennett** wrote and performed in Jamaican Creole from the 1940s, when it was widely dismissed as "bad English". The Barbadian poet **Kamau Brathwaite**, in *History of the Voice* (1984), called the creole-influenced English of Caribbean poetry **nation language**, to insist that it was a language with its own rhythms and not a corruption. **Derek Walcott**, from St Lucia, who received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1992, moved between Standard English and creole in his poetry and plays. Their work made a question asked across this course concrete: a variety that speakers have been taught to call broken can carry literature of the highest order.

::: warning Calling a creole "broken English"
✗ *Jamaican Patwa is broken English.*
✓ *Jamaican Creole is a language with its own grammar, which takes most of its words from English; speakers also use Standard Jamaican English.*

A creole is not a failed attempt at English. Its grammar has rules of its own — a progressive marker *a*, a past marker *did* or *ben*, a plural marker *dem* after the noun ([[world-englishes/pidgins-creoles]]) — and a speaker who uses them is speaking the creole correctly. Calling it broken English confuses the source of its vocabulary with the system of its grammar.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Familiar features.** Several West African features have parallels in Chinese learners' English: plurals such as *equipments* and *informations* are common among Chinese learners too, and the use of *off* and *on* as verbs is similar to Chinese 关 and 开 used alone (关灯, 开灯). In West Africa these forms are widespread among educated speakers; whether they are acceptable in a piece of writing depends on the readers and the standard expected.
- **Sympathetic *sorry*.** Chinese speakers may say 不好意思 in situations where English speakers would not apologise. The West African *Sorry!* of sympathy is another reminder that words for apology have different ranges in different varieties and cultures ([[pragmatics/apologies-thanks-compliments]]).
- **China and Africa.** Chinese companies, engineers and students now work in many African countries where English is an official language. Knowing the local variety — its vocabulary, its pragmatic conventions, its pronunciation — is part of working well there, in the same way that an African engineer in China benefits from knowing Chinese conventions.
:::

::: history
English in West Africa was first recorded in trading glossaries and missionary grammars. Systematic description of African Englishes began after independence: Ayo Banjo's classification of Nigerian English (1971), David Jowitt's *Nigerian English Usage* (1991) and Ayo Bamgbose's work on norms (1998) in Nigeria; and the volumes on African varieties in Kortmann and Schneider's *Handbook of Varieties of English* (2004) and in eWAVE (from 2011). For the Caribbean, Beryl Loftman Bailey's *Jamaican Creole Syntax* (1966), Cassidy and Le Page's *Dictionary of Jamaican English* (1967), DeCamp's and Bickerton's studies of the continuum (1971, 1975) and Allsopp's *Dictionary of Caribbean English Usage* (1996) laid the foundations. The literary debate about English in Africa was framed by Achebe's essay of 1965 and Ngũgĩ's *Decolonising the Mind* (1986).
:::

## Where this leads

The creoles of the Caribbean and the pidgins of West Africa, with their history and their grammar, are the subject of the next chapter, [[world-englishes/pidgins-creoles]]. English used between speakers of different first languages, as it is across Africa, is the subject of [[world-englishes/lingua-franca]], and attitudes to accents and varieties of the kind discussed here are examined in [[world-englishes/accent-identity]]. Postcolonial writing in English is placed in the history of literature in [[reading-literature/literary-periods]].

::: summary
- In anglophone Africa English is usually an official, second and link language beside many African languages, with English-lexifier pidgins in West Africa and Swahili in East Africa ([[#rule-africa]]).
- Nigerian English has a smaller vowel system, [t d] for /θ ð/, syllable-based rhythm, pluralised mass nouns, invariant tags and local vocabulary and pragmatics (*go-slow*, *next tomorrow*, *flash*, sympathetic *Sorry!*) ([[#rule-nigerian]]).
- Ghanaian and Kenyan English share many features with Nigerian English; Sheng in Nairobi shows English words absorbed into a Swahili-based code.
- The role of English after colonialism is debated: Phillipson's **linguistic imperialism** ([[#def-imperialism]]), Ngũgĩ's return to African languages, Achebe's "new English", and Brutt-Griffler's emphasis on learners' agency.
- In the Caribbean, English-lexifier creoles are the everyday languages of most people, with a **continuum** from basilect to acrolect ([[#def-continuum]]); Standard Caribbean English is codified in Allsopp (1996).
- Caribbean writers — Bennett, Brathwaite, Walcott — made the creoles a language of literature.
:::
