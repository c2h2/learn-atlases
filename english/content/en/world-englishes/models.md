Five engineers are on a video call to plan a bridge. One grew up in Leeds, one in Chennai, one in Singapore, one in Lagos and one in Wuhan. All five work in English, all five are fluent, and the meeting goes well. Afterwards someone asks a simple question: how many of them are *native speakers* of English? The engineer from Leeds, certainly. The one from Singapore spoke English at home with her parents and Mandarin with her grandmother, so perhaps she is too. The engineer from Chennai learned Tamil first but has been educated in English since he was four and uses it for every serious purpose. The one from Lagos spoke Yoruba, Nigerian Pidgin and English as a child and cannot say which came first. The engineer from Wuhan began English at school at eight. The question turns out to be much harder than it looked — and so does the question of whose English the meeting was held in.

This course is about the many forms that English takes around the world, and this first chapter is about the **models** that linguists have built to make sense of them. A model, here, is a simplified picture: it groups countries or speakers, names the groups and suggests how they relate. We look at two influential models — Braj Kachru's **three circles** (1985) and Edgar Schneider's **Dynamic Model** (2003, 2007) — at the main criticisms of each, and at the idea of a **standard** that lies behind all of them. The history of how English reached these places, up to the colonial period, is told in [[history-of-english/spread-of-english]]; this chapter starts where that story leaves off.

## Counting the speakers

Before modelling the English-speaking world we need to know roughly how large it is, and here the first lesson of the subject appears: every number depends on a definition.

::: definition First, second and foreign language {#def-l1-l2}
A speaker's **first language** (L1) is a language acquired in early childhood, usually at home; a person may have more than one. A **second language** (L2), in the sense used in world Englishes, is a language learned after the first but used regularly inside the speaker's own society — in education, government, law or the media — as English is in India or Nigeria. A **foreign language** is one learned mainly for contact with people outside the speaker's society, as English traditionally has been in China, Japan or Brazil. The line between second and foreign language is a matter of the language's role in the society, not of how well an individual speaks it.
:::

David Crystal, in *English as a Global Language* (2nd edition, 2003), gave ranges rather than single figures: roughly 320–380 million first-language speakers, 300–500 million second-language speakers, and 500 million to a billion foreign-language users, the last depending entirely on how much English one requires before counting a person. Crystal's totals suggested that non-native users outnumbered native speakers by about three to one. Later estimates, such as those of *Ethnologue* (26th edition, 2023), put the total number of users of English at roughly one and a half billion, with fewer than four hundred million of them first-language speakers. They agree on the main point: **most users of English are not native speakers of it.**

::: theorem Reading a speaker estimate {#rule-estimates}
When you meet a number of "English speakers", check four things before using it:

1. **Who made it, and when.** Estimates are dated; populations and school systems change quickly.
2. **What counts as a speaker.** First language only? Any regular use? Any study at school? The third definition can multiply the total several times.
3. **What source lies behind it.** National censuses ask different questions (some ask about the language "spoken at home", some about "languages known"), and many countries collect no figures at all.
4. **Whether it is a range or a point.** A single precise figure for the whole world (*1,452 million*) suggests more certainty than any source has. Report ranges and name the source.
:::

::: proof
The rule follows from how the numbers are produced. A global estimate is a sum of national figures, each based on a different census question, a different year and sometimes a guess. India's census, for example, records languages people report as their first, second and third, while many countries record only the language of the home. Foreign-language users are the hardest to count, because "having learned English at school" describes hundreds of millions of people whose English ranges from a few words to full professional fluency. This is why careful writers such as Crystal give ranges and explain how they were reached, and why a lesson that gives one number without a source should be read with care.
:::

::: quiz
A website says: "English has exactly 1.13 billion speakers." What is the most useful first question to ask?
- [ ] Is the number higher than the number of Mandarin speakers?
- [x] How does the source define a "speaker", and when was the figure made?
- [ ] Is the website British or American?
- [ ] Does the figure include children?
::: solution
Every estimate depends on the definition of a speaker (first language only, or second- and foreign-language users too) and on the date and sources of the data ([[#rule-estimates]]). Comparisons with other languages, or questions about children, only make sense once you know what is being counted. The word *exactly* is itself a warning sign: no source can count the world's users of English to three significant figures.
:::
:::

::: example Two estimates that disagree {#ex-estimates}
One source says that English has about 380 million speakers; another says about 1.5 billion. Can both be right?
::: solution
Apply [[#rule-estimates]], point 2. The first figure is close to the usual estimates of **first-language** speakers (Crystal 2003: about 320–380 million). The second counts **all users**, including second- and foreign-language users. Both can be right, because they answer different questions. When you quote either, say which question it answers, who made it and when: *about 380 million first-language speakers and roughly 1.5 billion users in all (Ethnologue, 26th edition, 2023)*.
:::
:::

## Kachru's three circles

In the 1960s and 1970s, the usual way of describing English around the world followed the three-way division of [[#def-l1-l2]]: English as a native language (ENL), as a second language (ESL) and as a foreign language (EFL). The Indian-born linguist Braj B. Kachru, working at the University of Illinois, reshaped this division in a chapter published in 1985 in *English in the World*, edited by Randolph Quirk and H. G. Widdowson. His picture of three concentric circles became the best-known diagram in the field.

::: definition Kachru's three circles {#def-circles}
In Kachru's model (1985), countries are grouped by the history of English in them and the functions it now has:

- The **Inner Circle**: countries where English is the first language of most of the population, the result of settlement from Britain — the United Kingdom, Ireland, the United States, Canada, Australia and New Zealand. Kachru called these varieties **norm-providing**: their standards are taken as models elsewhere.
- The **Outer Circle**: countries where English was brought by colonisation and now has an official or institutional role alongside other languages, used in education, law and government — India, Pakistan, Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, Singapore, the Philippines and many others. These varieties are **norm-developing**: they have their own forms, and their speakers are increasingly willing to treat these as standard.
- The **Expanding Circle**: countries where English has no official internal role but is widely learned and used for international purposes — China, Japan, Korea, Russia, Brazil, Germany and most of the world. These users are **norm-dependent**: they have traditionally looked to Inner Circle models.
:::

The diagram is usually drawn as three rings, the Inner Circle at the centre. It was meant to make a point, and the point was more important than the geometry. Kachru argued that Outer Circle Englishes are not failed attempts at British English but **varieties in their own right**, with their own history, their own literature and their own norms, just as American English had once been regarded as a corrupt form of British English and later came to be accepted as a standard of its own. He used the plural *Englishes* to make the same point in one word, and with Larry Smith he edited the journal *World Englishes*, which gave the field its name.

::: example Placing countries in the circles {#ex-placing}
Place each country in one of Kachru's circles and give your reason: (a) Ghana, (b) South Korea, (c) New Zealand, (d) Singapore, (e) the Netherlands.
::: solution
Use the two criteria of [[#def-circles]]: how English arrived, and its role in the country today.

1. **Ghana** — British colonial rule; English is still the official language after independence (1957): **Outer Circle**.
2. **South Korea** — never a colonial or official language; a school subject and business language: **Expanding Circle**.
3. **New Zealand** — settlement from Britain; the first language of most people: **Inner Circle**.
4. **Singapore** — English came with British colonial rule and is an official language and the medium of education: by history, **Outer Circle**. But for a large and growing share of Singaporeans English is also the main home language, so Singapore shows the limits of the model (see [[#rule-critiques]]).
5. **The Netherlands** — English has no official status, so by Kachru's criteria the country is in the **Expanding Circle**. Yet English is used in university teaching, business and daily media to a degree that resembles many Outer Circle countries. This too is a case the model handles badly.

The first three answers are straightforward; the last two show why the circles describe the history of a country more reliably than the way English is used there today.
:::
:::

## The critics

Kachru's model succeeded because it was simple, and most criticism concerns that simplicity. The objections are summarised in Jenkins's *Global Englishes* (3rd edition, 2015) and set out in detail by Paul Bruthiaux in "Squaring the circles" (*International Journal of Applied Linguistics*, 2003).

::: theorem The main criticisms of the three circles {#rule-critiques}
1. **It is based on countries, not speakers.** A country is placed in one circle, but within it speakers differ greatly: an Indian professor and an Indian street vendor use very different Englishes, and many people in the Outer Circle grow up speaking English at home.
2. **It is based on history, not use.** The Inner and Outer Circles are defined by how English arrived. Countries such as the Netherlands, Sweden or Denmark, where English is used intensely, sit in the Expanding Circle only because English never had official status there.
3. **The borders are unclear.** South Africa (where English is the first language of a minority and one of several official languages), Singapore (where English is now a home language for many), and countries where English's status has changed (Malaysia, Kenya) fit no single circle.
4. **It places native speakers at the centre.** Calling the Inner Circle "norm-providing" can be read as confirming the authority of native speakers, even though Kachru's own aim was the opposite.
5. **It hides variation inside the Inner Circle.** British and American English are themselves many varieties ([[world-englishes/british-american]], [[world-englishes/other-first-language-varieties]]), and the model treats each country as if it spoke one.
6. **It does not describe English used between non-native speakers**, which is now probably the commonest use of English in the world ([[world-englishes/lingua-franca]]).
:::

::: proof
Each criticism points to a fact the model was not designed to capture. Kachru wanted to show that the Englishes of former colonies deserved description and respect, and for that a country-by-country grouping was enough. As English spread, the facts it left out mattered more: Singapore's censuses show the share of residents who speak English most often at home rising steadily since the 1980s ([[world-englishes/southeast-asian-englishes]]), and research on English as a lingua franca describes interactions that belong to no circle. Alternatives were proposed — Tom McArthur's "circle of World English" (1987), with a "World Standard English" at its centre, and Marko Modiano's "centripetal circles" (1999), which centre proficient international users whatever their origin — but none replaced Kachru's model, which remains the usual starting point.
:::

::: warning "Outer Circle English is incorrect English"
✗ *Indian English and Nigerian English are just mistakes that became habits.*
✓ *Indian English and Nigerian English are established varieties with regular features; some of their forms differ from British or American standard usage.*

A form such as *prepone* ‘bring forward to an earlier time’ is not a mistake in Indian English, where educated speakers use it and newspapers print it; it is a word British and American readers may not know. Whether a form counts as an error depends on the variety and the setting ([[#rule-innovation]]).
:::

## Schneider's Dynamic Model

Kachru's circles describe where English is; they say little about how a new variety comes into being. Edgar W. Schneider, a German linguist, addressed that question in an article in *Language* (2003) and in his book *Postcolonial English: Varieties around the World* (2007). He argued that the Englishes of former colonies, from the United States to Singapore, have developed through a similar sequence, and that the motor of the sequence is **identity**: how the people involved see themselves and each other.

::: definition The Dynamic Model {#def-dynamic}
Schneider's **Dynamic Model** (2003, 2007) describes how a postcolonial English develops in five phases:

1. **Foundation** — English is brought to a new territory by colonisers or settlers. It is used by a small group; contact with the local population is limited; local place names are borrowed.
2. **Exonormative stabilisation** — the colony is established. The norm is external (usually British); English is learned by a local elite; words for local plants, animals and customs enter the settlers' English.
3. **Nativisation** — the most important phase, usually linked to independence movements. Both settlers and the local population begin to see themselves as belonging to the new place rather than to Britain; English spreads widely, and its sounds, grammar and vocabulary are restructured under the influence of local languages. Conservative speakers often complain that standards are falling.
4. **Endonormative stabilisation** — usually after independence, the local variety is accepted as a standard in its own right; dictionaries and grammars of it are written, and a literature in it flourishes.
5. **Differentiation** — the new nation's English, now secure, divides into regional, social and ethnic dialects of its own.

Throughout, Schneider follows two groups: the **settler strand** (the colonisers and their descendants) and the **indigenous strand** (the local population who acquire English), whose paths converge as both come to share a local identity.
:::

At each phase the model looks at four kinds of evidence: political history, the identities of the two strands, sociolinguistic conditions and linguistic effects. The third phase is the turning point, when speakers stop measuring their English against Britain. Its "complaint tradition" — letters to newspapers lamenting the decline of English — is, for Schneider, a sign that the new variety is emerging: people complain only about forms that are becoming common.

Schneider placed many countries along the sequence. In his account, American and Australian English had reached the fifth phase, with clear internal dialects; Singapore English had reached endonormative stabilisation; and the Englishes of India, Nigeria, Kenya and the Philippines were in nativisation, with some features of the next phase. These placements are judgements about complex societies, and other researchers have disagreed with several of them — about Hong Kong in particular ([[world-englishes/english-in-china]]).

::: example Recognising a phase {#ex-phase}
Read this description of an imaginary former colony and say which phase of the Dynamic Model it shows, giving two pieces of evidence.

*Twenty years after independence, the national broadcaster uses local newsreaders whose accent is clearly local, and nobody remarks on it. A dictionary of the national English has just appeared, listing several thousand local words. Novels by local writers, using local idioms, are set texts in schools. Politicians describe the national variety as "our English".*
::: solution
This is **phase 4, endonormative stabilisation**. The evidence:

1. **Codification**: a dictionary of the national English has been published — a typical sign of phase 4.
2. **Acceptance of the local norm**: local accents on national news pass without comment, and politicians call the variety "our English"; the norm is now internal ("endonormative") rather than British.
3. **Literary creativity**: novels in the local variety are set texts.

It is not phase 3, because there is no sign of the complaint tradition or of uncertainty about whether local forms are acceptable; and it is not yet phase 5, because nothing is said about new regional or social dialects within the country.
:::
:::

### What the Dynamic Model leaves out

The model was designed for **postcolonial** Englishes, so it says little about the Expanding Circle, where most learners live. It assumes that development moves forward, while in some countries the role of English has shrunk (Malaysia made Malay the main medium of education after independence). And it takes the nation as the unit, while migration and the internet have created users of English whose identities are not national.

Sarah Buschfeld and Alexander Kautzsch (2017, in *World Englishes*) extended it as the **Extra- and Intra-territorial Forces** model, which keeps the phases but adds forces acting on postcolonial and non-postcolonial countries alike — globalisation, language policy, foreign policy, demographic change — so that it can describe English in countries such as Namibia. Schneider himself (2014, also in *World Englishes*) concluded that the model fits China and other Expanding Circle countries only partly, and proposed **transnational attraction**: English now spreads less because a power imposes it than because people choose it for the opportunities it brings.

::: quiz
Which feature is the clearest sign that a variety is in Schneider's **nativisation** phase?
- [ ] English is used only by a small group of officials from the colonial power.
- [x] Local sounds, words and grammatical patterns spread widely, and some people write to newspapers complaining that English is being "spoiled".
- [ ] A dictionary of the national variety is accepted as the standard for schools.
- [ ] The national variety has divided into regional dialects within the country.
::: solution
Nativisation (phase 3) is marked by heavy linguistic restructuring and by the "complaint tradition", which shows that local forms are becoming common. The first option describes phase 1, the third phase 4 (codification) and the fourth phase 5 (differentiation).

## Standard English and standards

Both models talk about **norms** and **standards**, and these words need care, because they are easily confused with "correct".

::: definition Standard English {#def-standard}
**Standard English** is the variety of English used in most published writing, in education and in formal public speech, and described in dictionaries and reference grammars. It is a set of conventions of **grammar, vocabulary and spelling**, not an accent: it can be spoken with any accent (Trudgill 1999). There is no single world standard, but several national standards — British, American, Australian, and increasingly Indian, Singaporean, Nigerian and others — which differ from each other only slightly and are mutually intelligible in writing.
:::

The point that Standard English is not an accent matters for everything that follows. A Glasgow doctor, a Delhi lawyer and a Houston journalist may all write the same standard English and speak it with three very different accents. Pronunciation models such as General British and General American ([[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet#def-gb-ga]]) are separate choices, discussed in [[world-englishes/accent-identity]].

Standards are created by use and by **codification** — the writing of dictionaries, grammars and style guides that record the usage of educated writers. A form that is common among educated speakers of a variety but has not yet been codified is in an uncertain position: teachers may mark it wrong while newspapers print it. The Nigerian linguist Ayo Bamgbose ("Torn between the norms", *World Englishes*, 1998) proposed ways of deciding when such a form should count as an **innovation** in the variety rather than an **error**.

::: theorem Error or innovation? {#rule-innovation}
Following Bamgbose (1998), a local form has a strong claim to be an accepted feature of a variety, rather than an individual error, when:

1. it is used by **many** speakers (demographic factor), including educated speakers;
2. it is used **widely** across the country, not only in one region or group (geographical factor);
3. it is used by **authorities** — writers, journalists, teachers, broadcasters — in formal settings (authoritative factor);
4. it is **codified** in a dictionary, grammar or style guide of the variety (codification);
5. it is **accepted** by users as part of their English, not seen as a slip (acceptability).

Bamgbose considered codification the most important, and the most often missing. A form that satisfies these tests is standard in its variety, even if it is unknown in others.
:::

::: proof
The test separates two things that look alike. A learner who writes ✗ *He go to school* is producing a form that no community of educated speakers uses and that the writer would probably correct if asked: it is an error by any standard. Indian English *prepone*, Nigerian English *go-slow* ‘traffic jam’ or Singapore English *HDB flat* ‘public housing flat’ are used by large numbers of educated speakers, appear in national newspapers and in some dictionaries, and are accepted by their users: they are innovations, like American *gotten* or Australian *arvo* ‘afternoon’. The difficulty in many Outer Circle countries is that local dictionaries and grammars are few, so local innovations lack the authority of codification and teachers and examiners continue to judge by British or American reference books. This is the situation that Bamgbose called being "torn between the norms".
:::

::: example Applying the test {#ex-innovation}
A teacher in Nairobi marks the following sentence in a student's essay as wrong: *We discussed about the new timetable.* A colleague says that many educated Kenyans say this. Using [[#rule-innovation]], discuss whether the sentence should be marked wrong in (a) a Kenyan secondary-school exam, (b) a paper submitted to an international journal.
::: solution
Start from the facts and apply each criterion.

1. **Many and widely used?** The colleague says many educated Kenyans use *discuss about*; it is also reported in other varieties (Indian, Nigerian) and in English used as a lingua franca (Seidlhofer 2004 lists added prepositions such as *discuss about* among common lingua franca forms). It passes the demographic test.
2. **Used by authorities?** Possibly in speech; it is much less common in edited writing in Kenya, where the transitive *discuss the timetable* remains usual in newspapers.
3. **Codified?** The standard dictionaries used in Kenyan schools give *discuss* as transitive; no codified Kenyan norm accepts *discuss about*.
4. **Accepted?** Many users accept it in speech; many teachers do not in writing.

So: (a) in a Kenyan school exam, which follows the codified standard, the correction matches the expected norm, though the teacher can explain that the form is widespread, not a careless slip; (b) in an international journal *We discussed the new timetable* is the safer choice. The form is an uncodified feature of spoken usage — exactly the uncertain position the test identifies.
:::
:::

## Who owns English?

In 1990 the debate about standards became a public exchange in the magazine *English Today*. Randolph Quirk, the leading British grammarian, argued in "Language varieties and standard language" that learners abroad should be taught a single standard English (in practice British or American), because non-native varieties were not stable or codified enough to serve as models, and because learners needed the English that would be most widely useful. Kachru replied the next year in "Liberation linguistics and the Quirk concern" that this ignored the sociolinguistic reality of Outer Circle countries, where established varieties were used by millions of people and where an external standard was neither available to most learners nor needed for most of their purposes.

The two positions are still the main ones. For a common standard: international communication, examinations and publishing need shared conventions, and many learners want the English that opens doors. For local norms: a variety used by millions of educated people is a standard for them, and learners are badly served by a target they will rarely meet. The linguist H. G. Widdowson put the question memorably in an article titled "The ownership of English" (*TESOL Quarterly*, 1994): if English is an international language, no nation can claim to own it.

The same question has been asked by writers. Literature in English is no longer written only in Britain and America: the Nigerian novelist Chinua Achebe, the Indian novelists R. K. Narayan and Raja Rao, the Caribbean poet Derek Walcott and many others made English a medium of world literature ([[reading-literature/literary-periods]]). Raja Rao, in the foreword to his novel *Kanthapura* (1938), described the writer's task as conveying "in a language that is not one's own the spirit that is one's own". Achebe wrote in a 1965 essay that English could carry African experience, but that "it will have to be a new English". Others, such as the Kenyan novelist Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, drew the opposite conclusion and returned to writing in their first languages; that debate is described in [[world-englishes/african-caribbean-englishes]].

::: note English and Chinese
- **Where China fits.** In Kachru's model China is in the Expanding Circle: English has no official internal role, but it is studied by a very large number of people. In Schneider's original model China has no place, because English did not come there as a colonial language (Hong Kong, which was a British colony from 1842 to 1997, is the exception). Extensions such as Buschfeld and Kautzsch's (2017) and Schneider's own discussion of transnational attraction (2014) are attempts to describe countries like China.
- **Norm-dependent — for now?** Kachru's label "norm-dependent" describes the traditional position of Chinese learners, who take British or American English as the model. Whether a Chinese variety of English is developing its own norms — *China English* — has been debated in China since Ge Chuangui raised the term in 1980 ([[world-englishes/english-in-china]]).
- **A comparison.** Chinese too has a standard, 普通话, and many varieties; that a standard is a convention chosen for certain purposes, not the only "real" form of a language, is familiar from Chinese.
:::

::: history
The study of English as a world language grew out of practical questions after the Second World War, when newly independent countries had to decide what English to teach. In 1964 M. A. K. Halliday, Angus McIntosh and Peter Strevens wrote in *The Linguistic Sciences and Language Teaching* that English was no longer the possession of the British, or even of the British and Americans. Kachru's essays on Indian English, collected in *The Indianization of English* (1983), prepared the three circles (1985); Quirk and Kachru debated standards in *English Today* in 1990–1991; Schneider's Dynamic Model (2003, 2007) added a theory of how varieties develop.
:::

::: exercise Match the phase {level=1}
Match each description to a phase of the Dynamic Model (1–5).

(a) A dictionary of the local English is published and used in schools. (b) Settlers borrow the local names of rivers and mountains. (c) The national English develops distinct urban and rural dialects. (d) Letters to newspapers complain that young people's English is full of local words. (e) Children of the local elite are sent to schools run by the colonial power, where British English is the model.
::: solution
(a) 4 (endonormative stabilisation) (b) 1 (foundation) (c) 5 (differentiation) (d) 3 (nativisation) (e) 2 (exonormative stabilisation)

::: exercise Evaluate a claim {level=2}
A textbook says: "There are 400 million native speakers of English and 1.5 billion learners." Using [[#rule-estimates]], write three questions you would want answered before quoting this sentence, and suggest a more careful version of it.
::: solution
Possible questions: (1) Who made these estimates, and when? (2) What counts as a "learner" — anyone who studied English at school? Are second-language speakers in India or Nigeria, who are not really learners, included? (3) Are these ranges or point figures, and from which surveys?

A more careful version: *Estimates vary with the definition of a speaker. Crystal (2003) estimated roughly 320–380 million first-language speakers and several times as many second- and foreign-language users; later estimates, such as Ethnologue's, put the total at around one and a half billion.*
:::

::: exercise Which criticism? {level=2}
Each fact below supports one criticism in [[#rule-critiques]]. Say which.

(a) In Singapore, the proportion of residents who speak English most often at home has risen sharply over the last forty years. (b) Much of the English spoken in a European university is between students from Spain, Poland and Vietnam. (c) In Kachru's diagram the United States and Britain are each a single dot, although each has dozens of regional dialects. (d) Many Danish professionals use English every working day, but Denmark is placed in the Expanding Circle.
::: solution
(a) Criticism 1 (countries, not speakers) and 3 (unclear borders): a country classed as Outer Circle has a large and growing group for whom English is a home language. (b) Criticism 6: lingua franca use between non-native speakers belongs to no circle. (c) Criticism 5: variation inside the Inner Circle is hidden. (d) Criticism 2: the model is based on history, not use.
:::

::: exercise Innovation or error {level=2}
Using the five criteria of [[#rule-innovation]], decide whether each form is best treated as an established feature of a variety or as an individual error. Explain briefly.

(a) Indian English *prepone*, found in national newspapers and in some dictionaries. (b) A student writes *She don't likes coffee*, and corrects it to *doesn't like* when asked. (c) South African English *robot* ‘traffic light’, used by speakers of all backgrounds and listed in South African dictionaries.
::: solution
(a) An **established feature**: widely used by educated speakers across India, used by authorities (newspapers), codified in at least some dictionaries, accepted by users. (b) An **error**: no community of educated speakers uses this combination, and the writer recognises and corrects it. (c) An **established feature**: demographic, geographical and authoritative criteria are all met, and it is codified — it is standard South African English, though readers elsewhere may not know it.
:::

::: exercise Two positions {level=3}
Write a balanced paragraph (150–200 words) on the question *Should learners in Outer Circle countries be taught their local standard English or a British or American standard?* Present the strongest form of each position (you may draw on Quirk 1990 and Kachru 1991) and end with a sentence saying what further information would help decide the question in a particular country. Do not give your own opinion.
::: hint
Think about purposes (local jobs, international study), availability of codified local norms, teachers' own English, and learners' identities.
:::
::: solution
A model answer: *One position, associated with Randolph Quirk (1990), holds that learners should be taught a single international standard, in practice British or American English. Such standards are fully codified, are understood everywhere and give learners access to international study, publishing and employment; local varieties, on this view, are not yet stable or described enough to serve as teaching models. The opposing position, put forward by Braj Kachru (1991), holds that established local varieties are already standards for millions of educated users, that teachers themselves speak them, and that an external model is rarely met in learners' lives and treats their own usage as deficient. Supporters of this view argue that local norms can be codified and that international intelligibility does not require identity with British or American English. Which approach serves learners better in a particular country would depend on evidence about what learners will use English for, whether a codified local standard exists, and how intelligible the local standard is to the people they are likely to meet.* Answers should present both positions fairly and keep the writer's opinion out.
:::

::: exercise Apply both models {level=3}
Choose one country not discussed in detail in this chapter (for example Jamaica, Malaysia, Fiji, Cameroon or Hong Kong). Using a reference such as Schneider (2007) or Jenkins (2015), write 200–250 words that (a) place the country in Kachru's circles and explain any difficulty, (b) identify the phase of the Dynamic Model it seems to have reached, with two pieces of evidence, and (c) name one thing that neither model captures well about English there.
::: solution
Answers vary with the country. For Malaysia, a good answer would say that (a) by history it is Outer Circle, but after independence Malay became the main medium of education and the role of English declined, a change the circles cannot show; (b) Malaysian English shows nativisation (local words and sounds), although the change of school language interrupted its development ([[world-englishes/southeast-asian-englishes]]); (c) neither model captures the mixing of English with Malay and Chinese in informal speech. The best answers cite their source for each factual claim.
