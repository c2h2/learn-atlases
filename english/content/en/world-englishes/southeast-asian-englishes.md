At 9.30 on a Monday morning, Tan Wei Ling, a civil servant in Singapore, presents a report to her department: *The survey shows that most residents are satisfied with the new bus routes, although several have asked for later services on weekends.* At 12.30 she has lunch with two old school friends at a hawker centre. *Eh, you want to eat here or not? This one very nice one, you try lah.* When one friend says she cannot stay long, Wei Ling replies: *Like that lor, next time then.*

Wei Ling is not speaking good English in the morning and bad English at lunch. She is using two varieties that most educated Singaporeans command: **Standard Singapore English**, which differs from British English only in small details, and **Colloquial Singapore English**, usually called **Singlish**, which has its own grammar, its own vocabulary and a set of particles — *lah*, *lor*, *leh*, *meh* — that do important work in conversation. Choosing between them according to the setting is a skill, just as choosing between a formal and an informal register is in any language.

This chapter describes English in three countries of Southeast Asia with very different histories: Singapore, where English is now the most common home language; Malaysia, where English was replaced as the main language of education after independence; and the Philippines, where English came with American rule. It ends with English as the working language of ASEAN.

## English in Singapore

Singapore was founded as a British trading post by Stamford Raffles in 1819, became part of the Straits Settlements in 1826, and was a British colony until 1963, when it joined the Federation of Malaysia; it became an independent republic in 1965. Its population was and is multilingual: Chinese (speaking Hokkien, Teochew, Cantonese, Hakka and other varieties as well as Mandarin), Malay and Indian (speaking Tamil and other languages), with smaller communities.

After independence the government adopted a **bilingual policy**: every child would learn English, as a neutral language belonging to no single ethnic group and as the language of international trade and technology, together with an official "mother tongue" — Mandarin for Chinese Singaporeans, Malay for Malay Singaporeans, Tamil for most Indian Singaporeans. By 1987 English was the medium of instruction in all schools. Singapore has four official languages — English, Malay, Mandarin and Tamil — with Malay as the national language, but English is the main language of government, business and education.

The result has been a rapid language shift. In the 1980 census a small minority of Singaporean households reported English as the language spoken most often at home; in the 2020 census it was the most common home language, reported by nearly half of residents aged five and over, ahead of Mandarin. For a growing number of Singaporeans English is a first language, and Singapore is often described as moving out of the Outer Circle altogether ([[world-englishes/models#rule-critiques]]). In Schneider's Dynamic Model it is the clearest Asian example of **endonormative stabilisation** ([[world-englishes/models#def-dynamic]]).

::: definition Standard Singapore English and Singlish {#def-sse}
**Standard Singapore English** (SSE) is the standard variety used in education, government, the media and formal writing in Singapore; in grammar and vocabulary it is very close to British English, and it is spoken with a Singaporean accent. **Colloquial Singapore English** (CSE), known as **Singlish**, is the informal variety used among friends and family, with its own grammar, vocabulary from Malay, Hokkien, Cantonese, Tamil and other languages, and discourse particles. Most educated Singaporeans speak both and move between them according to the situation.
:::

How the two relate has been described in three main ways. John Platt (1975) described a **continuum** from an **acrolect** (closest to standard English) through a **mesolect** to a **basilect** (furthest from it), with each speaker's range depending on their education — a model borrowed from the study of creoles ([[world-englishes/pidgins-creoles]]). Anthea Fraser Gupta (1994) argued instead that Singapore has **diglossia**: two distinct varieties with different functions, a "high" one for formal purposes and a "low" one for informal ones, both available to the same speakers. Lubna Alsagoff (2007) proposed a **cultural orientation** model, in which speakers choose features according to whether they want to sound international and formal or local and friendly. The models agree on one point: the choice is systematic and social, not random.

## Singlish grammar

::: theorem Some grammatical features of Singlish {#rule-singlish}
1. **Copula absence** with adjectives and progressives: *This one very nice*; *He sleeping*.
2. ***Already*** after the verb as a completive marker: *I eat already* ‘I have eaten’.
3. ***Got*** for existence and possession: *Got many people there* ‘there are many people there’; *I got two brothers*.
4. ***Can*** as a full answer and in questions: *Can or not?* ‘is it possible?’ — *Can.*
5. **Subjects and objects left out** when clear from context: *Already finish* ‘(I’ve) already finished (it)’.
6. **Nominal *one*** at the end of a clause, marking a characteristic: *He very stubborn one* ‘he’s a very stubborn person’.
7. **Discourse particles** at the end of utterances, from Chinese and Malay sources: *lah*, *lor*, *leh*, *meh*, *hor*, *what* (see [[#rule-particles]]).
8. **Topic first**: *This book, I read already* ‘I’ve read this book’.
:::

::: proof
These features are not random departures from standard English; most correspond to grammatical patterns of the languages that Singaporeans spoke before English, especially Hokkien, Cantonese and Malay. Chinese has no copula with adjectives (他很忙 ‘he very busy’); it uses 有 ‘have, there is’ for both possession and existence, which matches *got*; it marks completion with a post-verbal particle (了), which matches *already*; it allows subjects and objects to be left out when they are clear; it places a topic before the comment ([[translation/english-chinese-compared]]); and its sentence-final particle 的 can mark a characteristic, as *one* does. Malay also lacks a copula with adjectives and has *sudah* ‘already’ as a completive marker. When a community acquires English from many sources and uses it among themselves, such patterns can become stable features of the new variety. eWAVE (Kortmann, Lunkenheimer and Ehret, eds.) records most of these features as pervasive in Colloquial Singapore English. Their regularity is the reason linguists treat Singlish as a variety with a grammar of its own.
:::

::: example Analysing Singlish sentences {#ex-singlish}
Explain each sentence in Standard English and name the features it uses: (a) *Got parking there or not?* (b) *Your mother cook already?* (c) *This movie very long one.* (d) *Tomorrow can?*
::: solution
1. (a) ‘Is there parking there?’ — existential *got* ([[#rule-singlish]], point 3), and the question tag *or not?*, which makes a yes/no question (compare Mandarin 有没有, ‘have-not-have’).
2. (b) ‘Has your mother cooked yet?’ — *already* as completive marker (point 2), copula and auxiliary absent, no inversion; the rising intonation marks the question.
3. (c) ‘This movie is very long’ (with the sense ‘that’s what it’s like’) — copula absence (point 1) and nominal *one* (point 6).
4. (d) ‘Is tomorrow possible for you?’ or ‘Can we do it tomorrow?’ — *can* in a question, with the subject and verb left out (points 4 and 5).

Each sentence is short because the context supplies what is left out. In a meeting the same speaker would say *Is there parking there?* and *Would tomorrow suit you?*
:::
:::

## Particles

The particles of Singlish are its most noticeable feature and the hardest to translate, because they express the speaker's attitude rather than content. Their meanings have been studied by Gupta (1992) and Lionel Wee (2004), among others; the glosses below are approximate, and the meaning depends on intonation and context.

::: theorem Some Singlish particles {#rule-particles}
1. ***lah*** — makes an utterance more persuasive, friendly or emphatic: *Come lah!* ‘oh, do come!’; *No lah* ‘no, really not’.
2. ***lor*** — signals resignation or that something is obvious: *Like that lor* ‘that’s just how it is’.
3. ***leh*** — softens a request or complaint, or shows tentativeness: *Help me leh* ‘could you help me?’.
4. ***meh*** — expresses surprise or scepticism in a question: *He coming meh?* ‘is he really coming?’.
5. ***hor*** — seeks the listener's agreement: *You said you'd come, hor* ‘you did say so, didn’t you’.
6. ***what*** — contradicts, with the sense ‘as you should know’: *I told you already what!* ‘but I did tell you!’.
:::

::: example Choosing a particle {#ex-particles}
Which particle from [[#rule-particles]] best fits each situation, and why? (a) Wei Ling wants to persuade a shy friend to join the photo. *Join us ___!* (b) A friend complains that the shop is closed; Wei Ling shrugs: *Closed ___, we come back tomorrow.* (c) Wei Ling doubts that a colleague has really finished: *You finish already ___?* (d) A friend says Wei Ling never told her about the party; Wei Ling replies: *I told you last week ___!*
::: solution
1. (a) ***lah*** — persuasive and friendly.
2. (b) ***lor*** — resignation: nothing can be done.
3. (c) ***meh*** — scepticism in a question.
4. (d) ***what*** — contradiction: ‘but I did tell you, as you should remember’.

Choosing a particle is a pragmatic decision, like choosing between *Could you…?* and *Would you mind…?* in British English ([[pragmatics/requests-refusals]]). Using one in the wrong place can sound rude or odd to Singaporeans, which is why outsiders who imitate them often get them wrong.
:::
:::

## Singapore English pronunciation

::: theorem Features of Singapore English pronunciation {#rule-singapore-sounds}
Following Deterding (*Singapore English*, 2007), many educated Singaporeans have:

1. **Syllable-based rhythm**: fewer reduced vowels, so that successive syllables are more even in length than in GB or GA ([[connected-speech/rhythm]]).
2. **Fewer vowel contrasts**: often no length contrast between KIT and FLEECE or between FOOT and GOOSE, and DRESS and TRAP merged before some consonants.
3. **Single vowels in FACE and GOAT**: [eː], [oː].
4. **Final stops** often unreleased or replaced by a glottal stop, and final clusters simplified (*last* [lɑːs]).
5. **/θ/** often [t] at the start of words and [f] at the end (*think* [tɪŋk], *with* [wɪf]); **/ð/** often [d].
6. **Stress and intonation** that often give prominence to the final syllable of a phrase, and less contrast between new and given information.
:::

::: proof
The first feature has been measured. Ee Ling Low, Esther Grabe and Francis Nolan ("Quantitative characterizations of speech rhythm", *Language and Speech*, 2000) compared the durations of successive vowels in British English and Singapore English using a **pairwise variability index**: the average difference in duration between each vowel and the next. British English had a much higher index, because full vowels alternate with short reduced ones; Singapore English had a lower index, because successive vowels are more similar in length. The measurement confirmed what listeners had long described impressionistically and gave a way of comparing rhythms objectively. The other features reflect the vowel and consonant systems of the languages Singaporeans spoke before English, together with the history of the variety; like the features of every accent, they are consistent within a speaker.
:::

::: example Listening to a Singaporean speaker {#ex-sg-sounds}
A Singaporean engineer says: *I think the last test was fine, but we must check it with the team.* Using [[#rule-singapore-sounds]], predict four ways in which her pronunciation may differ from GB, and say which could affect a listener's understanding.
::: solution
1. *think* — [tɪŋk], with [t] for /θ/ at the start of the word (point 5). Context makes the word clear.
2. *last test* — final clusters simplified and stops unreleased: [lɑːs tes] (point 4). *Last* and *test* are still recognisable, but a listener expecting [lɑːst test] may need a moment.
3. *was*, *but*, *with*, *the* — fewer reduced vowels: function words such as *was* and *but* keep fuller vowels, so the rhythm is more even (point 1). This often makes individual words **easier** to hear.
4. *team* — possibly no length contrast between KIT and FLEECE (point 2): *team* may be close to [tim]. Here the context leaves no doubt, but in a minimal pair (*leave*/*live*) the listener may need context.

The feature most likely to slow down a GB or GA listener is the even rhythm, because such listeners use the contrast between strong and weak syllables to find words ([[connected-speech/rhythm]]). Listeners adapt to it quickly ([[listening-and-conversation/accents#rule-adapt]]).
:::
:::

::: quiz
A Singaporean colleague says in a meeting: *The deadline is Friday, so we must finish the report this week.* Later, at lunch, she says: *Deadline Friday lor, must finish this week.* Which statement is accurate?
- [ ] The first sentence is Singlish and the second is Standard Singapore English.
- [ ] The second sentence shows that she does not know English grammar.
- [x] She has moved from Standard Singapore English to Colloquial Singapore English, as the setting changed.
- [ ] *lor* is a mistake for *or*.
::: solution
The first sentence is standard; the second has copula absence, an omitted subject and the particle *lor* (‘nothing can be done about it’). Choosing the variety by setting is normal for educated Singaporeans ([[#def-sse]]).
:::
:::

### A public debate

Singlish has been the subject of public argument in Singapore. In 2000 the government launched the **Speak Good English Movement**, which encouraged Singaporeans to use standard English and expressed concern that Singlish could make it harder for children to learn the standard and for Singaporeans to be understood abroad. Others — writers, artists, some linguists — argued that Singlish is a marker of Singaporean identity, that most speakers can switch to the standard when needed, and that a variety used by almost everyone deserves respect rather than discouragement. The *Oxford English Dictionary* has added a number of Singapore English words, such as *shiok* ‘delicious, excellent’ and *kiasu* ‘anxious not to lose out’, which supporters cited as recognition. The movement's later campaigns placed more emphasis on the ability to use standard English when it is needed than on discouraging Singlish, and the two positions have come closer, though they have not merged. As with similar debates elsewhere, the disagreement is less about the facts — which are not much disputed — than about how a society should value them.

## Malaysia

The Malay states came under British control in stages during the nineteenth century, and British Malaya became independent in 1957 (Malaysia was formed in 1963 with Sabah, Sarawak and, until 1965, Singapore). English had been the language of the colonial administration and of a network of elite English-medium schools. After independence the government made Malay (Bahasa Malaysia) the national language and, from 1970, gradually converted English-medium schools to Malay medium; by the early 1980s the conversion had reached the universities. English remained a compulsory subject.

The place of English in Malaysian education has changed more than once since then. From 2003 mathematics and science were taught in English in many schools, a policy known by its Malay acronym PPSMI; in 2009 the government decided to return to Malay and other school languages for these subjects, a decision implemented from 2012, and later programmes allowed some schools to teach them in English again. These changes show what Schneider's model, which assumes forward movement, finds hard to describe: the role of English can shrink as well as grow ([[world-englishes/models]]).

**Malaysian English** shares many features with Singapore English, including particles (*lah* is common in informal Malaysian speech), copula absence and syllable-based rhythm, with vocabulary from Malay, Chinese and Tamil. The informal variety is sometimes called **Manglish**. Malaysia's population of Chinese, Malay and Indian origin means that English, Malay and Chinese words often mix in one conversation.

## The Philippines

The Philippines was a Spanish colony from the sixteenth century until 1898, when it was ceded to the United States after the Spanish–American War; the Philippine–American War followed. The Americans introduced English-medium public education quickly: in 1901 several hundred American teachers arrived on the transport ship *Thomas*, and teachers who came at this time are still known in the Philippines as the **Thomasites**. English became the language of government, law and education, and remained so after independence in 1946.

The Constitution of 1987 made **Filipino**, based on Tagalog, the national language, and Filipino and English the official languages "for purposes of communication and instruction". English is used in the higher courts, much of government, higher education, the national newspapers and business, including a very large call-centre and business-process industry that serves English-speaking clients abroad. In everyday speech many Filipinos mix English and Filipino within a sentence, a style called **Taglish**: *Mag-meeting tayo later, okay lang ba?* ‘Let’s have a meeting later — is that okay?’ (an example written for this lesson).

::: theorem Some features of Philippine English {#rule-philippine}
1. **Rhotic**, with many American features of vocabulary and spelling, reflecting its history.
2. For some speakers, especially of languages without these sounds, **/f/** and **/v/** close to [p] and [b], and **/θ ð/** as [t d].
3. **Fewer vowel contrasts**, especially between KIT and FLEECE and between FOOT and GOOSE.
4. **Syllable-based rhythm**, with stress sometimes placed differently from GA (*ceremony*, *establish* may have stress on a different syllable).
5. **Vocabulary**: *comfort room* or *CR* ‘public toilet’; *brownout* ‘power cut’; *open the light* ‘switch on the light’; *for a while* ‘please wait a moment’ (on the telephone); *barkada* ‘group of close friends’ (from Tagalog); *jeepney* ‘shared minibus taxi’.
:::

::: example Philippine English on the telephone {#ex-philippine}
A Filipino receptionist answers a call: *Good morning! For a while, ma'am, I'll check if Sir Mark is around.* A caller from London is briefly puzzled. Identify the Philippine English features and explain each.
::: solution
1. *For a while* — ‘please wait a moment’ ([[#rule-philippine]], point 5). In GB and GA, *for a while* means ‘for some time’ and is not used alone as a request.
2. *Sir Mark* — the title *Sir* with a given name, a respectful form of address for a superior in the Philippines, comparable to Indian English *Mr Ramesh* ([[world-englishes/south-asian-englishes#def-title-given]]). In Britain, *Sir* + given name is the form used for knights, so a British caller may briefly wonder whether the manager has been knighted.
3. *ma'am* — common and polite in Philippine English (as in American English), whereas in Britain *madam* is used mainly in shops and formal service.
4. *if … is around* — ‘is in the office’, normal in both Philippine and American English.

The receptionist's English is fluent and correct Philippine English; the puzzlement comes from differences in conventions, not from errors.
:::
:::

## English in ASEAN

The ten (now eleven) member states of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations speak many languages, and none of them has a majority in the region. The **ASEAN Charter**, which came into force in 2008, states in Article 34 that "The working language of ASEAN shall be English." English is therefore the language in which Indonesians, Thais, Vietnamese, Filipinos and Malaysians meet, negotiate and write their agreements — a lingua franca in the full sense of [[world-englishes/lingua-franca]]. Andy Kirkpatrick, in *English as a Lingua Franca in ASEAN* (2010), argued that the model for teaching English in the region should be the English used successfully by educated ASEAN speakers rather than a British or American norm; others hold that an Inner Circle standard remains the most useful target for learners who will also deal with the rest of the world. The question of models is taken up in [[world-englishes/accent-identity]].

::: warning Treating Singlish as broken English
✗ *Singlish is just English with mistakes and lah at the end.*
✓ *Singlish is a colloquial variety with its own grammar — copula absence, completive *already*, existential *got*, particles with specific meanings — used alongside Standard Singapore English by most educated Singaporeans.*

A learner who tries to "use Singlish" by adding *lah* to every sentence will be using it wrongly, because the particles have meanings and positions ([[#rule-particles]]). And an outsider who tells a Singaporean that their colloquial speech is "wrong" is ignoring the fact that the same speaker can and does use the standard when the setting calls for it.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Familiar grammar.** Several Singlish features correspond closely to Chinese: *got* to 有 (*Got many people* 有很多人), *already* to 了 (*I eat already* 我吃了), *can* to 可以 (*Can or not?* 可以不可以), *one* to 的 (*He very stubborn one* 他很固执的), copula absence with adjectives to sentences such as 他很忙. Chinese learners often produce similar forms by transfer; in Singapore they have become stable features of a colloquial variety. That does not make them standard in formal writing, in Singapore or elsewhere.
- **Particles.** Mandarin and Cantonese have rich systems of sentence-final particles (吧, 啊, 呢, 嘛; Cantonese 啦, 咯, 喎), and Singlish particles such as *lah* and *lor* are thought to come partly from these. Chinese speakers can therefore understand their function — expressing attitude rather than content — more easily than many other learners.
- ***Open the light.*** Philippine English *open the light* and Chinese learners' *open the light* (from 开灯) are the same form, arrived at independently. In the Philippines it is standard; in GB and GA the usual form is *turn on* or *switch on the light*.
:::

::: history
English in Southeast Asia has been studied systematically since the 1970s. John Platt and Heidi Weber's *English in Singapore and Malaysia* (1980) and Platt's work on the Singapore continuum were early descriptions; Anthea Fraser Gupta's *The Step-Tongue* (1994) studied children who grew up speaking Singapore English; David Deterding's *Singapore English* (2007) described its pronunciation; and Ee Ling Low, Esther Grabe and Francis Nolan's measurement of its rhythm (2000) became a standard reference in phonetics. For the Philippines, Maria Lourdes Bautista and Kingsley Bolton's *Philippine English: Linguistic and Literary Perspectives* (2008) brought together decades of research. The ASEAN Charter, in force from 2008, made English the working language of the region.
:::

## Where this leads

African and Caribbean Englishes, in which English also lives beside other languages and colloquial varieties, are the subject of [[world-englishes/african-caribbean-englishes]]. The question of how colloquial varieties such as Singlish relate to creoles is taken up in [[world-englishes/pidgins-creoles]]. English between speakers of different first languages in ASEAN and elsewhere is the subject of [[world-englishes/lingua-franca]], and the rhythm of Singapore English is set beside that of GB and GA in [[connected-speech/rhythm]].

::: summary
- In Singapore, a bilingual policy made English the medium of all schools by 1987; by the 2020 census it was the most common home language ([[world-englishes/models#def-dynamic]]).
- **Standard Singapore English** and **Singlish** are used by the same speakers for different settings; they have been modelled as a continuum (Platt 1975), as diglossia (Gupta 1994) and as a cultural choice (Alsagoff 2007) ([[#def-sse]]).
- Singlish grammar has copula absence, completive *already*, existential *got*, *can*, nominal *one*, omitted subjects and objects and topic-first order, many matching Chinese and Malay patterns ([[#rule-singlish]]).
- Singlish particles (*lah*, *lor*, *leh*, *meh*, *hor*, *what*) express attitude and have specific meanings ([[#rule-particles]]).
- Singapore English has a more even, syllable-based rhythm, measured by Low, Grabe and Nolan (2000) ([[#rule-singapore-sounds]]).
- Malaysia replaced English with Malay as the main medium of education after independence; the Philippines kept English beside Filipino, with American-influenced features and Taglish mixing ([[#rule-philippine]]).
- English is the working language of ASEAN (Charter, Article 34).
:::

::: exercise Rhythm {level=2}
Explain in three or four sentences what the pairwise variability index measured by Low, Grabe and Nolan (2000) shows about Singapore English, and why it is a better kind of evidence than a listener's impression.
::: solution
The index compares the duration of each vowel with the next and averages the differences. A high value means that long and short vowels alternate strongly, as in British English, where full vowels alternate with reduced ones; a lower value, as in Singapore English, means successive vowels are more similar in length — a more syllable-based rhythm. It is better evidence than impression because it is measured from recordings, can be repeated by other researchers and allows varieties to be compared on the same scale.
:::
