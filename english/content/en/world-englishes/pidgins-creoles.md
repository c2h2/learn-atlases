In Port Moresby, the capital of Papua New Guinea, a sign outside a clinic reads *Haus sik*. A notice in a shop says *Yu no ken smok long hia* ‘you cannot smoke here’. A child asks her mother *Yu save long dispela man?* ‘do you know this man?’, and her mother answers *Mi no save* ‘I don’t know’. Almost every word comes from English — *house*, *sick*, *you*, *no*, *can*, *smoke*, *along*, *here*, *this fellow*, *man* — except *save* ‘know’, which comes ultimately from Portuguese *saber*. Yet an English speaker who has not learned the language understands very little, because the words are put together by a grammar that is not English. This is **Tok Pisin**, one of the official languages of Papua New Guinea and the first language of a growing number of its people.

Languages like Tok Pisin arise when people who share no language must communicate, usually in situations of trade, labour or colonisation. Some remain simple contact languages; some become the mother tongues of whole communities. This chapter defines **pidgins** and **creoles**, explains the main theories of how creoles arise, and describes three English-lexifier languages in detail — Tok Pisin, Nigerian Pidgin and Jamaican Creole — before turning to a language with a special place in the history of English in China: **Chinese Pidgin English**.

## Pidgins and creoles

::: definition Pidgin {#def-pidgin}
A **pidgin** is a contact language that develops when speakers of different languages need to communicate regularly for limited purposes, such as trade or work, and do not learn each other's languages. It is nobody's first language. Its vocabulary comes mostly from one language, the **lexifier** (often the language of the more powerful group); its grammar is reduced compared with that of the languages in contact, with few or no inflections.
:::

::: definition Creole {#def-creole}
A **creole** is a language that has developed from a contact situation and has become the **first language** of a community. Compared with a pidgin, a creole has a full vocabulary and a complete grammar, used for every purpose of life. Most of its vocabulary usually comes from the lexifier; its grammar is shaped by the other languages in contact (the **substrate**), by the lexifier (the **superstrate**) and by processes of language creation. Jamaican Creole, Haitian Creole (French-lexifier) and Hawai'i Creole are creoles.
:::

The difference between a pidgin and a creole is a difference of **use**, not of origin: a pidgin becomes a creole when children grow up speaking it as their first language, or when it is used for so many purposes that it develops a full grammar. Linguists describe a typical sequence of stages, though not every contact language passes through all of them.

::: theorem The life cycle of a contact language {#rule-life-cycle}
Following Robert Hall (1966) and later work, a contact language may develop through these stages:

1. **Jargon** — an unstable, individual way of communicating with words from one language and little grammar, which varies from speaker to speaker.
2. **Stable pidgin** — shared conventions of vocabulary and grammar develop, and the pidgin can be learned as a language.
3. **Expanded pidgin** — the pidgin is used for more purposes (in towns, religion, the media), and its grammar and vocabulary grow, though it may still be mostly a second language.
4. **Creole** — the language becomes the first language of a community. (A pidgin may also become a creole directly, without expansion, if children acquire it.)
5. **Decreolisation** — where the creole remains in contact with its lexifier, as in Jamaica, some speakers shift towards the lexifier, producing a **post-creole continuum** ([[world-englishes/african-caribbean-englishes#def-continuum]]).

At any stage the language may also die out, if the contact situation ends.
:::

::: proof
The sequence reflects what each stage is used for. A jargon serves a few transactions and needs only a few words. When the same groups meet regularly, conventions stabilise and newcomers learn them as a system: a stable pidgin. Expansion follows when the language takes on new functions — the language of a town where many groups live, of a church, a newspaper, a radio station — because new functions need new words and new grammatical means (tense and aspect markers, ways of forming relative clauses). Creolisation happens when children acquire the language as their first, and the language must then serve every purpose of a human life. The sequence is a model, not a law: Hawai'i Creole developed in a few generations on plantations, and some creoles show no sign of having passed through a stable pidgin. That is one reason the origins of creoles are debated.
:::

### Typical features

Pidgins and creoles from different parts of the world, with different lexifiers, share many features, and the reasons for this similarity are at the centre of creole studies. Features often found include: no inflection for tense, person or number on verbs; **pre-verbal particles** for tense, mood and aspect (*bin* or *did* for past, *go* or *bai* for future, *a*, *dey* or *stap* for progressive); invariant pronouns, often with a plural formed by a particle; plurals marked by a separate word rather than an ending; **multifunctional** words (Tok Pisin *long* is a general preposition ‘to, at, in, with, about’); and **periphrasis**, where a phrase does the work of a word in the lexifier (*gras bilong het* ‘grass of head’, ‘hair’). The *Atlas of Pidgin and Creole Language Structures* (APiCS; Michaelis et al., eds., 2013) and eWAVE record such features across dozens of contact languages.

## How creoles arise

Why do creoles in the Caribbean, West Africa, the Indian Ocean and the Pacific look so alike, even when their lexifiers differ? Four main explanations have been proposed, and most linguists today combine elements of several.

::: theorem Theories of creole genesis {#rule-genesis}
1. **Substrate theories**: a creole's grammar comes largely from the first languages of its creators (for Caribbean creoles, West African languages such as Fon, Ewe, Twi and Igbo), with vocabulary from the lexifier. Claire Lefebvre's work on Haitian Creole and Fongbe (1998) argued for **relexification**: speakers kept the grammar of their language and replaced its words.
2. **Superstrate theories**: a creole's features come largely from the non-standard, spoken varieties of the lexifier that its creators heard, such as the regional English of sailors and overseers. Salikoko Mufwene's **founder principle** (2001) holds that the varieties spoken by the earliest population had a lasting influence, and that a creole is selected from a **feature pool** of all the varieties in contact.
3. **Universalist theories**: creoles are similar because children creating a language fall back on universal properties of the human language faculty. Derek Bickerton's **Language Bioprogram Hypothesis** (*Roots of Language*, 1981), based on Hawai'i Creole, argued that the first generation of children born on the plantations created a creole from an unstable pidgin, and that its tense–mood–aspect system reflected an innate default.
4. **Gradualist theories**: creoles formed over several generations as successive groups of adults learned the language, not in a single generation of children.
:::

::: proof
Each theory explains some facts better than others. Substrate theories explain why Caribbean creoles share specific structures with West African languages — the Jamaican pronoun *unu* ‘you (plural)’ is from Igbo, and serial verb constructions match those of Kwa languages. Superstrate theories explain why many "creole" features — habitual *does*, double negatives, *-s*-less verbs — also occur in non-standard English dialects of Britain. Universalist theories explain similarities between creoles whose substrates and lexifiers differ. Gradualist theories fit the historical evidence that plantation populations were renewed by continuing arrivals of adults: archival research by Sarah Roberts (1998) showed that Hawai'i Creole was established over more than one generation, which weakened Bickerton's single-generation claim. A related debate concerns whether creoles form a special type of language. John McWhorter ("The world's simplest grammars are creole grammars", *Linguistic Typology*, 2001) argued that creoles, being young, lack the accumulated complexity of older languages; Michel DeGraff ("Against creole exceptionalism", *Language*, 2003) argued that the idea of creoles as a separate, simpler type has no scientific basis and reflects the history of how colonised peoples' languages were viewed. The debate continues; both sides agree that creoles are full languages.
:::

## Tok Pisin

Tok Pisin (‘talk pidgin’) grew out of a Pacific pidgin English used on ships, trading stations and plantations in the nineteenth century, including plantations in Samoa and Queensland to which workers from New Guinea were recruited. In New Guinea itself, where more than 800 languages are spoken, it became the lingua franca of plantations, missions, towns and eventually national politics. It is one of the official languages of Papua New Guinea, with English and Hiri Motu (and, since 2015, Papua New Guinean Sign Language), and it is used in Parliament, on radio and in the weekly newspaper *Wantok*, founded in 1970. In towns, many children now grow up speaking Tok Pisin as their first language: an expanded pidgin is becoming a creole.

::: theorem Some features of Tok Pisin {#rule-tok-pisin}
1. **Pronouns** with inclusive and exclusive ‘we’: *mi* ‘I’, *yu* ‘you’, *em* ‘he, she, it’, *yumi* ‘we (including you)’, *mipela* ‘we (not including you)’, *yupela* ‘you (plural)’, *ol* ‘they’.
2. ***-pela*** on adjectives and some other words: *bikpela haus* ‘big house’, *dispela man* ‘this man’.
3. **Transitive *-im*** on verbs with an object: *Mi lukim yu* ‘I see you’; *Em i ritim buk* ‘she is reading a book’.
4. **The predicate marker *i*** after a third-person subject: *Em i go* ‘he goes, he went’.
5. **Pre-verbal and post-verbal markers**: *bai* future (*Bai mi go* ‘I will go’), *bin* past, *stap* continuing action (*Em i wok i stap* ‘she is working’), *pinis* completed action (*Mi kaikai pinis* ‘I have eaten’).
6. **Prepositions** *bilong* (possession, purpose) and *long* (almost every other relation): *haus bilong mi* ‘my house’; *Em i go long taun* ‘she went to town’.
7. **Periphrasis**: *gras bilong het* ‘hair’, *haus sik* ‘hospital’, *haus kuk* ‘kitchen’.
:::

The development of *bai* is a famous example of a pidgin becoming a creole. Gillian Sankoff and Suzanne Laberge (1973) recorded that the future marker, from English *by and by*, had once been a full adverb, *baimbai*, placed at the start of a sentence; in the speech of children who spoke Tok Pisin as their first language it had become a short, unstressed particle, *bai* or even *ba*, placed directly before the verb. An optional word had become a grammatical marker — exactly the kind of change by which languages create tense systems.

::: example Analysing Tok Pisin {#ex-tok-pisin}
Translate and analyse: (a) *Mipela i bin wokim dispela haus.* (b) *Bai yumi go long maket.* (c) *Ol pikinini i pilai i stap.*
::: solution
Use [[#rule-tok-pisin]].

1. (a) *mipela* ‘we (exclusive)’ + *i* predicate marker + *bin* past + *wok-im* ‘make, build’ (*work* + transitive *-im*) + *dispela* ‘this’ + *haus* ‘house’: ‘We (not including you) built this house.’
2. (b) *bai* future + *yumi* ‘we (inclusive)’ + *go* + *long* ‘to’ + *maket* ‘market’: ‘We (you and I) will go to the market.’ The inclusive pronoun shows that the speaker includes the listener.
3. (c) *ol* plural marker before the noun + *pikinini* ‘children’ + *i* + *pilai* ‘play’ + *i stap* ‘continuing’: ‘The children are playing.’

Each sentence is built from English words, but its grammar — inclusive and exclusive pronouns, the transitive suffix, the predicate marker, aspect particles after the verb — is Tok Pisin's own, and much of it matches the languages of Melanesia rather than English.
:::
:::

## Nigerian Pidgin

**Nigerian Pidgin**, also called **Naija**, developed from the English-based contact language of the West African coast and is now used by tens of millions of Nigerians of every background as a lingua franca, especially in the south, in cities and in the army, markets and popular music. For many people in cities such as Warri and Port Harcourt it is a first language, so it can be described as an expanded pidgin that has creolised for part of its community. The BBC began a news service in Pidgin for West Africa in 2017.

::: theorem Some features of Nigerian Pidgin {#rule-naija}
1. **Pre-verbal markers**: *dey* for progressive and habitual (*I dey go* ‘I am going’), *don* for completed action (*I don chop* ‘I have eaten’), *go* for future (*I go come* ‘I will come’), *bin* for past.
2. ***Na*** as a copula and focus marker: *Na me* ‘it’s me’; *Na him do am* ‘he’s the one who did it’.
3. **Object pronoun *am*** for ‘him, her, it’: *I see am* ‘I saw him/her/it’.
4. **Plural *dem*** after the noun: *di pikin dem* ‘the children’.
5. **Question words**: *wetin* ‘what’, *wey* ‘which, who’ (also a relative marker), *how far?* ‘how are things?’
6. **Words** from English, Portuguese and Nigerian languages: *chop* ‘eat’, *pikin* ‘child’, *sabi* ‘know’ (Portuguese *saber*), *wahala* ‘trouble’ (from Hausa), *abeg* ‘please’ (from Yoruba).
:::

::: example Analysing Nigerian Pidgin {#ex-naija}
Translate: (a) *Wetin you dey do?* (b) *Abeg, no wahala.* (c) *Na Ngozi go cook di food.* (d) *Di pikin dem don sleep.*
::: solution
1. (a) *wetin* ‘what’ + *you* + *dey* progressive + *do*: ‘What are you doing?’
2. (b) *abeg* ‘please’ + *no* + *wahala* ‘trouble’: ‘Please, don’t worry / no problem.’
3. (c) *na* focus + *Ngozi* + *go* future + *cook di food*: ‘It’s Ngozi who will cook the food.’
4. (d) *di pikin dem* ‘the children’ (plural *dem*) + *don* completive + *sleep*: ‘The children have gone to sleep.’

The patterns are parallel to those of Tok Pisin and Jamaican Creole — pre-verbal markers, a plural word, a general copula — although the three languages developed far apart. This is the kind of similarity that the theories in [[#rule-genesis]] try to explain.
:::
:::

## Jamaican Creole

**Jamaican Creole** developed on the sugar plantations of seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Jamaica, among enslaved Africans speaking many West African languages and a minority of English speakers. It is the first language of most Jamaicans and exists in a continuum with Standard Jamaican English ([[world-englishes/african-caribbean-englishes#def-continuum]]). It has a standard phonemic spelling, devised by Frederic Cassidy in the 1960s and promoted by the Jamaican Language Unit of the University of the West Indies, although many writers use spellings closer to English.

::: theorem Some features of Jamaican Creole {#rule-jamaican}
1. **Pronouns**: *mi* ‘I, me’, *yu* ‘you’, *im* ‘he, she, it’, *wi* ‘we’, *unu* ‘you (plural)’ (from Igbo), *dem* ‘they’.
2. **Pre-verbal markers**: *a* progressive (*Mi a nyam* ‘I am eating’), *did*, *ben* or *en* past (*Im did nyam* ‘he ate, had eaten’), *a go* future (*Mi a go nyam* ‘I’m going to eat’).
3. **Three ways of saying ‘be’**: *a* in equative sentences (*Im a di tiicha* ‘she is the teacher’), *de* for location (*Im de a yaad* ‘she is at home’), and nothing before adjectives (*Di pikni sik* ‘the child is sick’).
4. **Plural *dem*** after the noun: *di bwoy dem* ‘the boys’.
5. ***Fi*** ‘to, for’ before a verb or noun: *Mi waan fi go* ‘I want to go’; *a fi mi* ‘it’s mine’.
:::

::: example A sentence of Jamaican Creole {#ex-jamaican}
Analyse: *Unu did a talk bout di pikni dem wen mi kom in.*
::: solution
1. *unu* — ‘you (plural)’, from Igbo ([[#rule-jamaican]], point 1).
2. *did a talk* — past marker *did* + progressive *a* + verb: ‘were talking’. The two markers combine in a fixed order, past before progressive, as tense and aspect auxiliaries do in English (*were talking*) — but as separate invariant words.
3. *bout* — ‘about’.
4. *di pikni dem* — ‘the children’, with plural *dem* (point 4).
5. *wen mi kom in* — ‘when I came in’: *mi* is the subject form too, and *kom* has no past ending, because past time is already clear from *did* in the main clause.

Translation: ‘You (all) were talking about the children when I came in.’ The grammar is regular and economical: tense and aspect are marked once, by particles, and the plural once, by *dem*.
:::
:::

### The origins of African American English

The question of creoles reaches into the history of the United States. **Gullah**, spoken on the coast and islands of South Carolina and Georgia, is an English-lexifier creole related to the creoles of the Caribbean. Whether African American English ([[world-englishes/british-american#def-aae]]) also developed from a creole has been debated for decades. The **creolist** position, argued by J. L. Dillard (*Black English*, 1972) and in a more cautious form by John Rickford (1999), points to features such as copula absence and pre-verbal aspect markers, which resemble those of Caribbean creoles, and to the plantation history. The **anglicist** position, supported by Shana Poplack and Sali Tagliamonte's studies of communities descended from African Americans who emigrated to Nova Scotia and Samaná in the early nineteenth century (Poplack, ed., 2000; Poplack and Tagliamonte 2001), argues that early African American English was close to the non-standard English of the time, and that its distinctive features developed later. Many researchers now accept that both inheritance from British dialects and contact with creoles played a part, in proportions that differed from place to place.

## Chinese Pidgin English

The first English-lexifier contact language in East Asia developed in the eighteenth century at Canton (Guangzhou), where British and other foreign merchants traded with Chinese merchants. From 1757 the Qing government limited European trade to Canton, under what is called the Canton System, which lasted until the First Opium War (1839–1842). Foreign traders were not normally allowed to learn Chinese, and few Chinese merchants learned English formally; communication took place in a pidgin with mostly English vocabulary and some Portuguese and Malay words, inherited from earlier trade. After 1842 it spread with the treaty ports to Shanghai and other cities, and to Chinese communities abroad. It declined in the early twentieth century as English was taught in schools and as the old trading relationships ended.

The word **pidgin** itself is generally believed to come from this language: it represents a Chinese Pidgin English pronunciation of *business*, and *pidgin English* meant ‘business English’. In Shanghai the pidgin was called 洋泾浜英语 (*Yangjingbang English*), after the Yangjingbang creek that marked the border between the British and French concessions, and *洋泾浜* is still used in Chinese for any broken or pidgin form of a foreign language.

::: theorem Some features of Chinese Pidgin English {#rule-cpe}
1. ***piecee*** (from *piece*) as a general **classifier** between a number and a noun: *one piecee man* ‘one man’, modelled on Chinese classifiers such as 个.
2. ***-side*** for place and direction: *topside* ‘above, in authority’, *Chinaside* ‘in China’.
3. ***belong*** as a copula and possessive marker: *my belong* ‘mine’.
4. ***catchee*** (from *catch*) ‘get, obtain’, ***savvy*** ‘know’ (Portuguese *saber*), ***maskee*** ‘never mind’ (from Portuguese), ***chop-chop*** ‘quickly’, ***joss*** ‘god, idol’ (Portuguese *deus*).
5. **Chinese word order** in some phrases, such as *look-see* ‘have a look’.
:::

Glossaries of the pidgin were printed for Chinese merchants, with each English word written in Chinese characters that showed its pronunciation in Cantonese. Several expressions have passed into general English: *chop-chop*, *look-see*, *no can do*, and possibly *long time no see*, whose origin in the pidgin is often claimed but not proven ([[collocation-and-register/false-friends-chinglish]]). The history and descriptions are in Kingsley Bolton's *Chinese Englishes* (2003) and in Umberto Ansaldo, Stephen Matthews and Geoff Smith's study of China Coast Pidgin texts (2010). Its place in the history of English in China is discussed in [[world-englishes/english-in-china]].

::: example Interpreting Chinese Pidgin English {#ex-cpe}
A nineteenth-century glossary records phrases like the following (the forms here are illustrative, built from documented words). Translate them and identify the features: (a) *Two piecee ship come.* (b) *Maskee, my savvy.* (c) *He belong topside man.*
::: solution
1. (a) ‘Two ships have come.’ — *piecee* as classifier ([[#rule-cpe]], point 1); no plural ending on *ship*, because the number and classifier show plurality, as in Chinese 两只船.
2. (b) ‘Never mind, I know.’ — *maskee* ‘never mind’ and *savvy* ‘know’, both from Portuguese (point 4); *my* as a general first-person pronoun.
3. (c) ‘He is an important man, a man in authority.’ — *belong* as copula (point 3) and *topside* ‘high, in authority’ (point 2).

The words are mostly English, the classifier pattern is Chinese, and several words came from Portuguese, which had been the earlier European trade language of the region: a typical mixture for a trade pidgin.
:::
:::

::: warning Pidgin as an insult
✗ *Her English is just pidgin.* (meaning ‘bad English’)
✓ *Nigerian Pidgin is a language with its own grammar, used by tens of millions of people.*

In everyday English *pidgin* is often used loosely to mean any broken or imperfect speech, and Chinese *洋泾浜* has a similar everyday sense. In linguistics a pidgin is a particular kind of contact language with conventions shared by its speakers ([[#def-pidgin]]); an individual learner's imperfect English is not a pidgin, and a real pidgin such as Nigerian Pidgin or Tok Pisin is not "bad English" but a separate language. Using the word as an insult confuses the two and disparages the languages and their speakers.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Inclusive and exclusive ‘we’.** Tok Pisin *yumi* ‘we, including you’ and *mipela* ‘we, not including you’ match a distinction that Northern Mandarin makes with 咱们 (inclusive) and 我们 (which can be exclusive). Standard English *we* has only one form.
- **Classifiers.** The Chinese Pidgin English classifier *piecee* (*one piecee man*) is a direct reflection of Chinese classifiers (一个人). Learners of English often have to learn the opposite: English has no classifier with most countable nouns (*one man*), but needs one with uncountable nouns (*a piece of advice*).
- **Familiar structures.** Many features of pidgins and creoles — no verb inflection, tense and aspect shown by separate words, plurals shown by a number or a plural word — resemble Chinese. This is a coincidence of typology, not of history (Chinese played a part only in Chinese Pidgin English), and it shows that the English system of inflections is only one of the ways a language can work.
:::

::: history
Hugo Schuchardt, writing in the 1880s, is usually regarded as the founder of creole studies; at a time when creoles were dismissed as corrupt forms of European languages, he described them as languages and asked how they had arisen. Robert Hall's *Pidgin and Creole Languages* (1966) set out the life cycle; a conference held at Mona, Jamaica, in 1968 and published as *Pidginization and Creolization of Languages* (Hymes, ed., 1971) brought the field together. Bickerton's *Roots of Language* (1981) proposed the bioprogram, John Holm's *Pidgins and Creoles* (1988–1989) surveyed the languages, and Mufwene's *The Ecology of Language Evolution* (2001) and the *Atlas of Pidgin and Creole Language Structures* (2013) shaped current research.
:::

::: summary
- A **pidgin** is a contact language with a reduced grammar that is nobody's first language; a **creole** is a contact language that has become a community's first language ([[#def-pidgin]], [[#def-creole]]).
- Contact languages may pass through jargon, stable pidgin, expanded pidgin and creole stages, and creoles in contact with their lexifier may form a post-creole continuum ([[#rule-life-cycle]]).
- Theories of creole genesis stress the substrate, the superstrate (Mufwene's founder principle), universals (Bickerton's bioprogram) or gradual development; whether creoles form a special type is debated (McWhorter 2001; DeGraff 2003) ([[#rule-genesis]]).
- Tok Pisin has inclusive and exclusive pronouns, *-pela*, transitive *-im*, the predicate marker *i* and aspect particles ([[#rule-tok-pisin]]); Nigerian Pidgin has *dey*, *don*, *go*, *na* and plural *dem* ([[#rule-naija]]); Jamaican Creole has *a*, *did*, *de*, *unu* and *fi* ([[#rule-jamaican]]).
- The origin of African American English — creole or British dialect — is debated; both probably contributed.
- **Chinese Pidgin English** developed in eighteenth-century Canton, gave English the word *pidgin* and expressions such as *chop-chop*, and died out in the twentieth century ([[#rule-cpe]]).
:::

(b) Habitual *does* is found both in Caribbean creoles and in dialects of south-west England. (c) Creoles with different lexifiers and substrates in the Indian Ocean and the Caribbean have similar tense–aspect systems. (d) Plantation records show adults arriving continuously for decades while the creole formed.
::: solution
(a) substrate (b) superstrate (c) universalist (d) gradualist

::: exercise The word pidgin {level=2}
Explain in three or four sentences where the word *pidgin* probably comes from, and why its everyday use as an insult is misleading.
::: solution
*Pidgin* is generally believed to represent the Chinese Pidgin English pronunciation of *business*, from the trade language of eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Canton, so *pidgin English* meant ‘business English’. In linguistics it names a particular kind of shared contact language with its own conventions. Used as an insult for any imperfect English, it confuses an individual's learner language with a language system, and it disparages languages such as Nigerian Pidgin and Tok Pisin, which are used by millions of people for every purpose.
:::
:::

::: exercise Compare three languages {level=3}
Make a table comparing Tok Pisin, Nigerian Pidgin and Jamaican Creole for (a) how the progressive is marked, (b) how the future is marked, (c) how the plural of nouns is marked, (d) one word not from English. Then write 100–150 words on what the similarities suggest and which theory of [[#rule-genesis]] they support.
::: solution
| | Tok Pisin | Nigerian Pidgin | Jamaican Creole |
|---|---|---|---|
| progressive | *i stap* after the verb | *dey* before the verb | *a* before the verb |
| future | *bai* before the verb | *go* before the verb | *a go* before the verb |
| plural | *ol* before the noun | *dem* after the noun | *dem* after the noun |
| non-English word | *kaikai* (Polynesian), *save* (Portuguese) | *wahala* (Hausa), *sabi* (Portuguese) | *unu* (Igbo), *nyam* (West African) |

A good paragraph notes that all three mark tense and aspect with separate invariant words rather than inflections, and show plural with a separate word; that the West African languages share specific forms (*dem* after the noun) that Tok Pisin does not, which points to a shared West African and Atlantic history (substrate and diffusion); and that the general similarity across all three, despite different substrates, is what universalist theories try to explain. Most linguists combine explanations.
:::

::: exercise A short explanation {level=3}
A classmate says that Jamaican Creole is "just English without grammar". Write a reply of 150–200 words using the analysis of at least one Jamaican sentence from this chapter to show that it has grammar of its own.
::: solution
A good reply is polite and concrete. For example: *I can see why it looks like English — most of the words come from English. But the grammar is different, and it's very regular. Take "Unu did a talk bout di pikni dem": "unu" is a plural "you" (from Igbo, which English lacks), "did" marks the past and "a" the progressive, in a fixed order, and "dem" after "pikni" makes it plural. Jamaican even has three different words where English has "be": "a" for "is the teacher", "de" for location, and nothing before adjectives. A speaker who said "Di pikni dem sik" for one child, or put "a" before "did", would be making a mistake in Jamaican. That's what it means to have a grammar.* The reply should make at least two accurate grammatical points.
:::
