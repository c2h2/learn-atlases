Wang Lei works for a company with offices in Manchester and Chicago. On Friday afternoon she receives two emails. The first, from Manchester, says: *The team are meeting on Monday — could you post the revised programme to everyone at the weekend? Ring me if there's a problem with the colour printer.* The second, from Chicago, says: *The team is meeting Monday — could you mail the revised program to everyone on the weekend? Call me if there's a problem with the color printer.* Wang Lei understands both perfectly. She also notices that almost every line differs: a verb agreement, a preposition, three words, two spellings, and the absence of *on* before *Monday*.

British and American English are the two standard varieties that most learners meet first, and the two models taught in this atlas. This chapter describes how they differ — in spelling, vocabulary, grammar and pronunciation — and how much the differences matter. It also makes a point that is easy to forget: "British English" and "American English" are themselves names for standard varieties within two countries full of regional and social dialects, one of which, African American English, is described at the end of the chapter. Oscar Wilde had a character in *The Canterville Ghost* (1887) observe that the English "have really everything in common with America nowadays, except, of course, language". The line is a joke; the reality, as we shall see, is the reverse.

## A common core

::: definition Common core {#def-common-core}
The **common core** of English is the large body of grammar and vocabulary shared by all standard varieties (Quirk et al. 1985). British English (BrE) and American English (AmE) differ in a small number of spellings, in a few hundred everyday words, in some grammatical preferences and in pronunciation; almost everything else — the structure of clauses and phrases, the tense and aspect system, the articles, the great majority of words — is the same.
:::

::: theorem How the differences are distributed {#rule-distribution}
1. **Writing** shows the fewest differences: an edited American text and an edited British text differ mainly in spelling, a few words and a few grammatical preferences, and are fully intelligible to each other's readers.
2. **Vocabulary** differs most in **everyday** domains — transport, food, housing, school, shopping — and least in scientific, academic and technical English.
3. **Grammar** differs mostly in **frequency**, not in possibility: most "American" forms are also found in Britain, and most "British" forms in America, but one variety prefers them.
4. **Pronunciation** differs most: GB and GA can be told apart in almost every sentence.
:::

::: proof
The distribution follows from history. The two varieties separated in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, after the grammar of Early Modern English was largely in place, so the core grammar is shared. Words for everyday things changed independently on each side of the Atlantic, especially for things that did not exist before the separation (*petrol*/*gas*, *motorway*/*freeway*, [[core-vocabulary/everyday-topics]]). Spelling was standardised separately, with Noah Webster's dictionaries setting the American conventions. Pronunciation changed most, because sound change is continuous and unconscious: the loss of /r/ after vowels in southern England and the lengthening of the BATH vowel happened after the colonies were founded ([[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet#rule-rhotic]]). The academic vocabulary is shared because it was largely built from Latin and Greek after both varieties had their own printers, scientists and universities in close contact.
:::

## Spelling

The main American spellings go back to Noah Webster. His *American Spelling Book* (from 1783) taught generations of American children to read, and his *Compendious Dictionary of the English Language* (1806) and *An American Dictionary of the English Language* (1828) fixed spellings such as *color*, *center* and *traveler*. Webster wanted spelling that was simpler, closer to pronunciation and distinctly American. Some of his proposals were adopted, others were not, and British spelling, which had been codified in Samuel Johnson's *Dictionary* (1755), kept most of its older forms.

| pattern | BrE | AmE | notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| -our / -or | *colour, honour, labour* | *color, honor, labor* | BrE *-or* in *error, actor, horror* |
| -re / -er | *centre, metre, theatre* | *center, meter, theater* | BrE *meter* = a measuring device |
| -ise / -ize | *organise* or *organize* | *organize* | both are British; see the warning below |
| -yse / -yze | *analyse, paralyse* | *analyze, paralyze* | no choice in BrE |
| doubled *l* | *travelled, cancelled, jeweller* | *traveled, canceled, jeweler* | AmE doubles only with stress on the last syllable: *compelled* |
| single *l* | *enrol, fulfil, skilful* | *enroll, fulfill, skillful* | |
| -ce / -se | *defence, offence, licence* (n.) | *defense, offense, license* | BrE *license* is the verb |
| -ogue / -og | *catalogue, dialogue* | *catalog*, *dialogue* or *dialog* | |
| -mme / -m | *programme* | *program* | BrE *program* for computers |
| single words | *cheque, tyre, kerb, grey, plough, mould, sceptical, aluminium* | *check, tire, curb, gray, plow, mold, skeptical, aluminum* | *aluminium* also differs in pronunciation |
| ae, oe | *anaemia, manoeuvre, foetus* | *anemia, maneuver, fetus* | |

::: theorem Choosing a spelling {#rule-spelling}
1. Both British and American spellings are standard; neither is a mistake in itself.
2. **Be consistent** within a text: choose one variety and keep to it. Mixing *colour* and *center* in one essay looks careless, not international.
3. Follow the conventions of the **publisher, institution or exam** if it has them; many journals and companies have a house style.
4. Proper names keep their own spelling whatever the variety of the text: the *World Health Organization*, *Pearl Harbor*, the *Labour Party*, *Lincoln Center*.
:::

::: warning "-ize is American"
✗ *-ize is American spelling, so a British text must use -ise.*
✓ Both *-ise* and *-ize* are British. *-ize* is the older form in many words and is used by Oxford University Press and some international organisations ("Oxford spelling": *organize*, *realize*, but *analyse*). American English uses *-ize*.

Some words take only *-ise* in both varieties, because the ending is not the Greek-derived suffix: *advertise*, *advise*, *comprise*, *compromise*, *despise*, *exercise*, *revise*, *supervise*, *surprise*, *televise*. Writing ✗ *advertize* or ✗ *surprize* is an error in both varieties.
:::

::: example Making a text consistent {#ex-spelling}
A student's essay, written for a British university that asks for British spelling, contains this sentence. Correct it.

*The program of the center focuses on behavior, and its travelers analyze the colour of the theater's décor.*
::: solution
Change each American spelling to its British form and check every word against the table.

1. *program* → *programme* (not about computers);
2. *center* → *centre*;
3. *behavior* → *behaviour*;
4. *travelers* → *travellers*;
5. *analyze* → *analyse* (the *-yse* words have no *-yze* option in BrE);
6. *colour* — already British;
7. *theater's* → *theatre's*.

✓ *The programme of the centre focuses on behaviour, and its travellers analyse the colour of the theatre's décor.*

The original mixed both varieties: one British spelling (*colour*) in an otherwise American sentence. A spellchecker set to "English (UK)" would have caught most of these; it would not have caught *program*, because that is a British spelling in another sense.
:::
:::

## Vocabulary

Vocabulary differences fall into three kinds: different words for the same thing, the same word for different things, and words used in only one variety.

| meaning | BrE | AmE |
|---|---|---|
| a building for living in, one floor | *flat* | *apartment* |
| a machine between floors | *lift* | *elevator* |
| fuel for cars | *petrol* | *gas*, *gasoline* |
| a large goods vehicle | *lorry* | *truck* |
| a line of waiting people | *queue* | *line* |
| a fast road between cities | *motorway* | *highway*, *freeway*, *interstate* |
| household waste | *rubbish* | *garbage*, *trash* |
| the season after summer | *autumn* | *fall* (also *autumn*) |
| a portable phone | *mobile (phone)* | *cell phone*, *cell* |
| a document listing your career | *CV* | *résumé* (CV for academic posts) |
| a time away from work | *holiday* | *vacation* |
| thin fried potato pieces, hot | *chips* | *French fries*, *fries* |
| thin fried potato slices, in a packet | *crisps* | *chips* |

The words that cause most confusion are those used in both varieties with **different meanings**:

- *pants*: BrE ‘underpants’; AmE ‘trousers’.
- *pavement*: BrE ‘the path at the side of a road’ (AmE *sidewalk*); AmE ‘the road surface’.
- *first floor*: BrE the floor above the ground floor; AmE the ground floor.
- *football*: BrE the game Americans call *soccer*; AmE American football.
- *public school*: in England, an old and expensive private school; in the US, a school paid for by the state.
- *revise*: BrE also ‘study again for an exam’; AmE only ‘change and improve a text’.
- *table* (a proposal): BrE ‘present it for discussion’; AmE ‘postpone discussing it’ — exactly opposite.
- *quite*: with gradable adjectives, BrE *quite good* often means ‘fairly good, but not very’; AmE *quite good* usually means ‘very good’.

Dates are another everyday difference. *3/4/2026* is 3 April in Britain (day/month/year) and March 4 in the United States (month/day/year). International writing avoids the problem by writing the month as a word (*3 April 2026*, *April 3, 2026*) or by using the ISO order (2026-04-03).

::: example The same words, different messages {#ex-quite}
A British manager writes on a report: *Quite good. Could we table this at Thursday's meeting?* An American colleague reads it as high praise and assumes the report will not be discussed. What has gone wrong?
::: solution
Two words have different meanings in the two varieties.

1. *Quite good* — in British usage, with a falling intonation, *quite* with a gradable adjective often means ‘moderately’; the manager meant ‘acceptable, but it could be better’. In American usage *quite good* is usually ‘very good’.
2. *table* — in BrE ‘put on the agenda for discussion’; in AmE ‘set aside, postpone’. The manager wants to discuss the report on Thursday; the colleague thinks it has been shelved.

In international writing it is safer to avoid both: *Good, but I have a few suggestions. Could we discuss it at Thursday's meeting?* The general principle — avoid words whose meaning depends on the variety when writing for mixed readers — is part of writing for an international audience ([[world-englishes/lingua-franca]]).
:::
:::

## Grammar

The grammatical differences are fewer and, as [[#rule-distribution]] says, mostly matters of preference. The reference works that compare them include Biber et al.'s *Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English* (1999), which counts forms in British and American corpora, John Algeo's *British or American English?* (2006) and Gunnel Tottie's *An Introduction to American English* (2002).

::: theorem Main grammatical differences {#rule-grammar}
1. **Collective nouns.** With *team*, *government*, *family*, *committee*, *staff* and names of teams or companies, BrE often uses a plural verb when the group is seen as individuals (*The team are training*; *England are winning*); AmE almost always uses a singular verb (*The team is training*), though a plural pronoun may follow (*The team is training for their final*).
2. **Possession.** BrE uses both *have got* and *have* (*Have you got a pen?*, *Do you have a pen?*); AmE prefers *have* with *do*-support, and uses *have got* mainly in informal speech.
3. **Past participle of *get*.** AmE has *gotten* for ‘obtained, become’ (*She's gotten a new job*; *It's gotten cold*) beside *got* for possession (*I've got two sisters*); BrE has only *got*.
4. **Present perfect.** BrE strongly prefers the present perfect with *just*, *already* and *yet* (*Have you eaten yet?*); AmE also uses the past simple there (*Did you eat yet?*).
5. **The mandative subjunctive.** After verbs such as *insist*, *suggest*, *recommend*, AmE uses the base form (*They insisted that he be present*). BrE also uses it, increasingly, and in addition *should* (*that he should be present*) or an ordinary tense (*that he was present*).
6. **Prepositions and articles in fixed phrases.** BrE *at the weekend*, *in hospital*, *at university*, *Monday to Friday*, *different from/to*, *write to someone*; AmE *on the weekend*, *in the hospital*, *in college*, *Monday through Friday*, *different from/than*, *write someone*. AmE often drops *on* before days: *See you Monday.*
7. **Irregular verbs.** BrE often has *-t* forms (*learnt*, *dreamt*, *burnt*, *spoilt*) beside *-ed*; AmE prefers *-ed*. AmE has *dove* beside *dived*, *fit* beside *fitted* (*The suit fit him*).
8. ***Shall*** is rare in AmE except in fixed uses (*Shall we?*, legal texts); in BrE it remains common in offers and suggestions (*Shall I open the window?*), though it is declining there too.
:::

::: proof
These patterns appear in corpus comparisons, and they are stated as tendencies because both varieties allow most of the forms. Geoffrey Leech and colleagues, in *Change in Contemporary English* (2009), compared matched corpora of written British and American English from 1961 and from the early 1990s. They found that the mandative subjunctive, which had become rare in Britain, was becoming more common again — one of several changes in which British usage moved towards American, a trend they called **Americanisation** — and that modal verbs such as *shall* and *must* were declining in both varieties. Some differences are also shrinking in the other direction: linguists have recorded the spread of British expressions in American English, such as *gap year* and *queue*, described by Lynne Murphy in *The Prodigal Tongue* (2018). For a learner the practical lesson is that a "British" or "American" form is rarely wrong in the other variety; it is just less usual there.
:::

::: example British or American? {#ex-identify}
Which variety is each sentence most probably from? Give the feature that tells you.

(a) *The committee have not yet reached a decision.* (b) *I've gotten used to the cold.* (c) *Did you finish your homework yet?* (d) *She's in hospital, but she should be home at the weekend.* (e) *We recommend that every applicant submit two references.*
::: solution
1. (a) **British**: a plural verb with a collective noun ([[#rule-grammar]], point 1).
2. (b) **American**: *gotten* as the participle of *get* in the sense ‘become’ (point 3).
3. (c) **American**: past simple with *yet* (point 4); BrE would say *Have you finished your homework yet?*
4. (d) **British**: *in hospital* without an article and *at the weekend* (point 6).
5. (e) **Either**, though more typical of American writing: the mandative subjunctive *submit* (not *submits*) after *recommend that* (point 5) is standard in AmE and increasingly common in formal BrE.

Notice that none of the sentences is wrong in the other variety; (b) and (c) would simply sound American to a British listener, and (a) and (d) British to an American.
:::
:::

::: quiz
In which sentence does the choice of verb form show British rather than American usage?
- [ ] *My family is from Guangzhou.*
- [x] *The government are divided on the question.*
- [ ] *I don't have any money with me.*
- [ ] *She's already left.*
::: solution
A plural verb with a collective noun (*the government are*), showing the government as a group of individuals, is typical of British English; American English would use *is*. The other sentences are normal in both varieties: *My family is* is the usual choice in both, *I don't have* is common in modern BrE too, and *She's already left* is standard in both, although AmE also allows *She already left*.
:::
:::

## Pronunciation

The differences between General British and General American were introduced in [[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet]] and [[sounds-of-english/long-vowels-diphthongs]]. They fall into two kinds: **systematic** differences that affect whole lexical sets, and **word-by-word** differences that must be learned one at a time.

::: theorem Systematic differences between GB and GA {#rule-gb-ga}
1. **Rhoticity.** GA pronounces /r/ after vowels (*car* /kɑːr/, *nurse* /nɜːrs/); GB does not (/kɑː/, /nɜːs/), except before a vowel (linking /r/).
2. **BATH.** Words such as *bath*, *grass*, *dance*, *can't* have /ɑː/ in GB and /æ/ in GA.
3. **LOT, THOUGHT, CLOTH.** GB has a rounded /ɒ/ in *lot*; GA has an unrounded /ɑː/, the vowel of *father*. Many American speakers (especially in the West and in Canada) also merge LOT with THOUGHT, so that *cot* and *caught* sound the same.
4. **GOAT.** GB /əʊ/, GA /oʊ/.
5. **T-tapping.** In GA, /t/ (and /d/) between vowels after a stressed syllable is a tap [ɾ]: *water*, *city*, *getting*; so *latter* and *ladder* can sound alike.
6. **Glottalling.** In GB and in many British accents, /t/ before a consonant is often a glottal stop [ʔ] (*not now*, *football*), and in London and elsewhere increasingly between vowels too; in GA, glottalling is found mainly before /n/ (*button*, *certain*).
7. **Yod-dropping.** After /t/, /d/, /n/ in stressed syllables, GB has /j/ and GA usually none: *new* GB /njuː/, GA /nuː/; *tune* /tjuːn/ (often /tʃuːn/), GA /tuːn/; *student* /ˈstjuːdnt/, GA /ˈstuːdnt/.
8. **Unstressed syllables.** In words ending in *-ary*, *-ery*, *-ory*, GA keeps a full vowel with secondary stress where GB reduces or drops one: *secretary* GB /ˈsekrətri/, GA /ˈsekrəteri/; *laboratory* GB /ləˈbɒrətri/, GA /ˈlæbrətɔːri/.
:::

::: proof
Most of these changes can be dated. The loss of post-vocalic /r/ spread in southern England in the eighteenth century, after the American colonies were settled from many parts of Britain, including the rhotic west and north ([[history-of-english/spread-of-english]]); the eastern seaboard of the United States, in close contact with London, also became partly non-rhotic, but rhotic speech came to dominate American English in the twentieth century. The BATH lengthening was a southern English change of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries that never reached most of America. The tap and the American unrounding of LOT are American developments. Each change applies to a whole set of words because sound change is regular: a speaker with a tap in *water* has one in every word with the same environment ([[listening-and-conversation/accents#rule-differences]]). The reductions in *-ary* words and the patterns of glottalisation and tapping are the same processes of connected speech described in [[connected-speech/elision-assimilation]], applied differently in each accent.
:::

Some words differ individually and simply have to be learned:

| word | GB | GA |
|---|---|---|
| *schedule* | /ˈʃedjuːl/ (also /ˈskedʒuːl/) | /ˈskedʒuːl/ |
| *tomato* | /təˈmɑːtəʊ/ | /təˈmeɪtoʊ/ |
| *either* | /ˈaɪðə/, /ˈiːðə/ | /ˈiːðər/, /ˈaɪðər/ |
| *vase* | /vɑːz/ | /veɪs/, /veɪz/ |
| *garage* | /ˈɡærɑːʒ/, /ˈɡærɪdʒ/ | /ɡəˈrɑːʒ/ |
| *ballet* | /ˈbæleɪ/ | /bæˈleɪ/ |
| *advertisement* | /ədˈvɜːtɪsmənt/ | /ˌædvərˈtaɪzmənt/ |
| *herb* | /hɜːb/ | /ɜːrb/ |
| *leisure* | /ˈleʒə/ | /ˈliːʒər/ |
| *mobile* (adj.) | /ˈməʊbaɪl/ | /ˈmoʊbl/ |
| *z* (the letter) | /zed/ | /ziː/ |

::: example Transcribing in both models {#ex-transcribe}
Give the GB and GA pronunciations of *The new student can't start the course until autumn*, word by word where they differ, and name the rule behind each difference.
::: solution
Work through the sentence with [[#rule-gb-ga]].

1. *new*: GB /njuː/, GA /nuː/ — yod-dropping (point 7).
2. *student*: GB /ˈstjuːdnt/, GA /ˈstuːdnt/ — yod-dropping again.
3. *can't*: GB /kɑːnt/, GA /kænt/ — the BATH set (point 2).
4. *start*: GB /stɑːt/, GA /stɑːrt/ — rhoticity (point 1).
5. *course*: GB /kɔːs/, GA /kɔːrs/ — rhoticity.
6. *autumn*: GB /ˈɔːtəm/, GA /ˈɔːtəm/ — the same, but with a tap in GA [ˈɔːɾəm] (point 5); and an American speaker might well say *fall* instead.

*The*, *until* and the vowels of the other words are the same in both, apart from small differences of quality. A sentence of ten words contains six differences, which is why the two accents are recognisable at once — and yet every word is the same word.
:::
:::

## Neither variety is one variety

"British English" and "American English" in this chapter mean the **standard** varieties and the reference accents GB and GA. Most people in either country do not speak exactly like this. Britain has some of the most varied accents in the English-speaking world for its size; the accents of Scotland, Wales and Ireland are described in [[world-englishes/other-first-language-varieties]], and those of England — from Cornwall to Newcastle — in Wells's *Accents of English* (1982, vol. 2). American regional speech was mapped in William Labov, Sharon Ash and Charles Boberg's *Atlas of North American English* (2006), which describes large regional differences in vowels, among them the **Northern Cities Shift** of cities such as Chicago and Detroit and the vowel systems of the South.

One American variety deserves a section of its own, because it has been studied more than any other and misjudged more than most.

::: definition African American English {#def-aae}
**African American English** (AAE; also African American Vernacular English, AAVE, for its vernacular forms) is the variety of English spoken by many, though not all, African Americans, with features of grammar, pronunciation and vocabulary that are shared across the United States. Linguists have described it in detail since the 1960s — notably William Labov, John Rickford (*African American Vernacular English*, 1999) and Lisa Green (*African American English: A Linguistic Introduction*, 2002) — and agree that it is a **rule-governed** variety with a grammar as systematic as that of any other.
:::

::: theorem Some rules of African American English {#rule-aae}
1. **Copula absence.** *Is* and *are* may be absent: *She nice*, *They working*. Labov (1969) showed that this happens where Standard English can **contract** the copula (*She's nice* → *She nice*), and not where it cannot: at the end of a clause (*I know where he is*, never ✗ *I know where he*), or with *am* (*I'm* is kept).
2. **Habitual *be*.** Invariant *be* marks an action or state that happens regularly: *She be working late* ‘she usually works late’, contrasting with *She working late* ‘she is working late (now)’.
3. **Stressed *BIN*.** Stressed *been* marks a state or action that began long ago and continues: *She BIN married* ‘she has been married for a long time’.
4. **Completive *done*.** *He done finished the report* ‘he has (already) finished the report’.
5. **Negative concord.** Negatives agree throughout a clause, as in many other varieties of English and in many languages: *Nobody don't know nothing about it.*
6. **Third-person singular.** Present-tense verbs often have no *-s*: *She work downtown.*
:::

::: proof
The evidence for rule-governedness is that speakers use these forms in regular, predictable ways, and that some combinations never occur. Labov's analysis of copula absence (published in *Language* in 1969) is the classic case: in recordings of speakers in New York, *is* and *are* were absent in exactly the environments where Standard English allows contraction, and present in the others, so the "missing" copula follows a rule that links it to Standard English contraction. The contrast between *She working* and *She be working* expresses an aspectual distinction — habitual versus ongoing — that Standard English does not mark grammatically at all. In "The logic of nonstandard English" (1969) Labov showed that arguments in the vernacular could be as logical as those in the standard. Negative concord, which many people call illogical, was normal in Chaucer's English and is the rule in French, Spanish and Russian. None of this means that AAE is used in every situation; many speakers move between AAE and Standard English according to setting, as speakers of other varieties do.
:::

The public reception of AAE shows how strongly attitudes and facts can diverge. In 1979 a federal court in Ann Arbor, Michigan, ruled that a school had failed to take account of its pupils' home language in teaching them to read. In December 1996 the school board of Oakland, California, passed a resolution recognising "Ebonics" as the language of many of its pupils, and was widely ridiculed in the press; in January 1997 the Linguistic Society of America passed a resolution stating that the variety is "systematic and rule-governed like all natural speech varieties". The debate about how schools should respond — teaching Standard English by contrast with the home variety, or ignoring the home variety — continues. Its consequences outside school, including in courtrooms, are discussed in [[world-englishes/accent-identity]]. The historical origins of AAE, and the debate about whether it was once a creole, are discussed in [[world-englishes/pidgins-creoles]].

::: example Reading African American English {#ex-aae}
Explain the meaning of each sentence and say which rule of [[#rule-aae]] it shows. (a) *My brother be at the library after school.* (b) *She BIN knowing him.* (c) *They done ate already.* (d) Why would a speaker of AAE not say ✗ *That's where she*?
::: solution
1. (a) ‘My brother is usually at the library after school’ — habitual *be* (rule 2). Without *be*, *My brother at the library* would mean ‘he is there now’.
2. (b) ‘She has known him for a long time’ — stressed *BIN* (rule 3). Note that *know* in the progressive is normal here; the meaning is of a long-standing state.
3. (c) ‘They have already eaten’ — completive *done* (rule 4).
4. (d) The copula can be absent only where Standard English could contract it (rule 1). Standard English cannot contract *is* at the end of a clause (✗ *That's where she's*), so AAE keeps it: *That's where she is.* The absence of a form follows a rule, not chance.
:::
:::

::: quiz
What does the AAE sentence *He be cooking on Sundays* mean?
- [ ] He is cooking right now, on a Sunday.
- [x] He usually cooks on Sundays.
- [ ] He cooked last Sunday.
- [ ] He will cook next Sunday.
::: solution
Invariant *be* marks habitual aspect: something that happens regularly ([[#rule-aae]], point 2). An ongoing action now would be *He cooking*. The sentence contrasts with Standard English *He cooks on Sundays*, which expresses habit through the simple present.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Which variety did you learn?** Many Chinese learners have learned a mixture: British spelling from older textbooks, American pronunciation from films and recordings, and vocabulary from both. This is normal. In writing, choose one spelling system and keep to it ([[#rule-spelling]]); in speech, mixing hardly matters.
- **A familiar situation.** Chinese has similar differences between regions. Mainland China and Taiwan use different character sets (simplified and traditional) and sometimes different words for the same thing: 出租车 and 計程車 ‘taxi’, 软件 and 軟體 ‘software’. The same word can even have different meanings: 土豆 is ‘potato’ on the mainland but usually ‘peanut’ in Taiwan. Like the British and American forms, these are differences between standard varieties, not errors.
- **Exams.** International English exams generally accept either British or American spelling and usage, provided the candidate is consistent; check the current rules of the exam you are taking rather than relying on what a classmate remembers.
:::

::: history
The two standards took shape in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Samuel Johnson's *Dictionary of the English Language* (1755) codified British spelling. In the new United States, Noah Webster argued that an independent nation needed its own language; his *American Spelling Book* (first published in 1783 as part of *A Grammatical Institute of the English Language*), his *Compendious Dictionary* (1806) and his *American Dictionary of the English Language* (1828) established American spelling. In 1919 H. L. Mencken's *The American Language* described American English as a variety in its own right. Through the twentieth century, radio, film and later television brought each variety to the other's audience. Leech et al. (2009) documented the influence of American on British English in the late twentieth century, and Labov, Ash and Boberg's *Atlas of North American English* (2006) mapped the variety of American speech.
:::

## Where this leads

The other first-language varieties — of Ireland, Scotland, Wales, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and South Africa — are described in [[world-englishes/other-first-language-varieties]]. How to choose a pronunciation model, and whether a model must be British or American at all, is discussed in [[world-englishes/accent-identity]]. The British and American vocabulary of everyday topics is practised in [[core-vocabulary/everyday-topics]], and the history of the separation in [[history-of-english/spread-of-english]].

::: summary
- British and American English share a large **common core**; differences are in spelling, everyday vocabulary, grammatical preferences and, above all, pronunciation ([[#def-common-core]], [[#rule-distribution]]).
- American spelling (*color*, *center*, *traveled*, *defense*) goes back mainly to Webster (1806, 1828); both *-ise* and *-ize* are British; be consistent within a text ([[#rule-spelling]]).
- Some words have different meanings in the two varieties (*pants*, *first floor*, *table*, *quite*); avoid them in writing for mixed readers.
- Grammatical differences are mostly preferences: collective nouns, *have got*, *gotten*, the present perfect with *yet*, the subjunctive, prepositions and *-t* past forms ([[#rule-grammar]]).
- GB and GA differ systematically in rhoticity, BATH, LOT, GOAT, tapping, glottalling, yod-dropping and unstressed *-ary* syllables ([[#rule-gb-ga]]).
- Neither country has one English. **African American English** is a rule-governed variety with features such as habitual *be* and copula absence where Standard English can contract (Labov 1969; [[#def-aae]], [[#rule-aae]]).
:::

::: exercise GB or GA? {level=1}
Each transcription is in GB or GA. Say which, and name the feature that tells you: (a) /kɑːr/, (b) /bɑːθ/, (c) /nuːz/ (*news*), (d) /hɒt/, (e) /ɡoʊ/.
::: solution
(a) GA — rhotic /r/. (b) GB — BATH with /ɑː/. (c) GA — yod-dropping after /n/ (GB /njuːz/). (d) GB — rounded LOT vowel /ɒ/ (GA /hɑːt/). (e) GA — GOAT as /oʊ/ (GB /ɡəʊ/).

::: exercise Make it American {level=2}
Rewrite this British message for an American reader, changing spelling, vocabulary and grammar where American usage differs.

*Have you got the timetable for the autumn term? I've just rung the centre, but the lift's broken, so I'll post it to you at the weekend.*
::: solution
✓ *Do you have the schedule for the fall semester? I just called the center, but the elevator's broken, so I'll mail it to you on the weekend.*

Changes: *Have you got* → *Do you have*; *timetable* → *schedule*; *autumn term* → *fall semester* (American universities usually have semesters; *fall term* is also possible); *I've just rung* → *I just called* (past simple with *just* is usual in AmE; *ring* ‘telephone’ is British); *centre* → *center*; *lift* → *elevator*; *post* → *mail*; *at the weekend* → *on the weekend*. Answers that keep *I've just called* are also correct: the present perfect is standard in AmE too.
:::
:::

::: exercise Explain the misunderstanding {level=2}
An American visitor in London is told that the café is *on the first floor*. She waits on the ground floor for ten minutes. Explain what happened, and suggest a wording that works for both varieties.
::: solution
In British usage the *ground floor* is at street level and the *first floor* is the one above it; in American usage the street-level floor is the *first floor*. The visitor was on the American first floor, one floor below the café. A wording that works for both: *The café is one floor up* or *upstairs, one floor above the entrance*.
:::
:::

::: exercise Rules of AAE {level=2}
For each sentence, say whether it follows the rules of African American English described in [[#rule-aae]], and explain why. (a) *She tired.* (b) *I know where she.* (c) *He be late for work.* (d) *I tired.*
::: solution
(a) **Yes**: copula absence where Standard English can contract (*She's tired*). (b) **No**: Standard English cannot contract *is* at the end of a clause (*I know where she is*), so AAE keeps it. (c) **Yes**: habitual *be* — ‘he is usually late for work’. (d) **No**: *am* is not deleted in AAE; the usual form is *I'm tired*. Rules 1 and 2 predict all four judgements.
:::
:::

::: exercise A consistent text {level=3}
Write an email of 120–150 words to a new colleague describing your first week at a new job or university, entirely in **either** British **or** American English. Include at least two spellings, two vocabulary items and one grammatical feature that differ between the varieties, and underline (or list afterwards) each one with its equivalent in the other variety.
::: hint
Collective nouns, *have got*, *gotten*, *at/on the weekend* and the present perfect with *just* are good grammatical choices.
:::
::: solution
Answers vary. A good answer in British English might include *programme* (AmE *program*), *organise* or *organize* (both BrE; AmE *organize*), *flat* (AmE *apartment*), *lift* (AmE *elevator*), *The team are very friendly* (AmE *is*), *at the weekend* (AmE *on*). The answer should be consistent throughout — no *color* in a British text — and the list of equivalents should be accurate.
:::
:::

::: exercise A short report {level=3}
A classmate says: *American English is a simplified, careless form of British English.* Write a reply of 150–200 words that corrects this view politely, using at least three pieces of evidence from this chapter (for example the history of spelling, the age of some American forms, grammatical changes in British English, or the regularity of American pronunciation).
::: solution
A model answer: *I see why it might seem that way — American spellings like "color" are shorter, for example. But the differences are not carelessness. Many American spellings were deliberate choices by Noah Webster, whose dictionaries of 1806 and 1828 aimed at a simpler, more consistent spelling. Some American forms are actually older than the British ones: "gotten" was normal in British English too until it fell out of use there, and many American accents kept the /r/ after vowels that southern English lost in the eighteenth century. Influence also runs both ways: corpus studies (Leech et al. 2009) show British English adopting American patterns such as the subjunctive "that he be present". And American pronunciation is as regular as British — a speaker who taps the "t" in "water" taps it in every similar word. Both are standard varieties with their own histories.* Any answer that uses three accurate pieces of evidence and a polite tone is good.
:::
