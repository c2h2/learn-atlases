Professor Helen Clarke, newly arrived at a university in Hyderabad, receives this email from a second-year student:

> *Respected Madam, I am having two doubts regarding the assignment. Kindly clarify whether we should submit the hard copy also. I tried calling your office yesterday night but it was not reachable. Please revert at the earliest. Thanking you, R. Priya*

To Professor Clarke, who has taught only in Britain, some of this seems unusual: *doubts* where she expected *questions*, *revert* where she expected *reply*, *yesterday night* for *last night*, *I am having* with a noun of thought, and a signature in which the initial comes before the given name. Her Indian colleagues read it as a polite, correct, well-written student email. Both readings are accurate descriptions of different standards. Every one of the forms that surprised her is common among educated speakers in India and appears in Indian newspapers, offices and universities; several are recorded in dictionaries of Indian English.

South Asia — India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal and their neighbours — has more users of English than any other region outside the Inner Circle. This chapter describes how English came there, the roles it plays today, and the main features of Indian English, the most studied of the South Asian varieties, with notes on the others. The history before 1800 is told in [[history-of-english/spread-of-english]].

## English in South Asia

The English East India Company was founded in 1600 and gradually took control of much of the subcontinent in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries; after 1858 India was ruled directly by the British Crown. The decisive language policy was the **Minute on Indian Education** of 2 February 1835, in which Thomas Babington Macaulay argued that public money should support education in English rather than in Sanskrit, Arabic or Persian. The governor-general adopted the policy, and the universities founded at Calcutta, Bombay and Madras in 1857 taught in English. English became the language of administration, law, higher education and, eventually, of the Indian nationalist movement itself, whose leaders had been educated in it.

After independence in 1947 each new state had to decide what to do with English.

- **India.** The Constitution (1950) made Hindi in the Devanagari script the official language of the Union and allowed English to continue for official purposes for fifteen years. When the date approached, there were large protests in Tamil Nadu and other non-Hindi-speaking states against the replacement of English by Hindi, and the Official Languages Act 1963 (amended in 1967) allowed English to continue alongside Hindi without a time limit. Education policy since the 1960s has followed the **three-language formula**: a regional language, Hindi and English (with variations by state). In the 2011 census about 260,000 people reported English as their first language, but about 83 million reported it as a second language and about 46 million as a third, together roughly a tenth of the population.
- **Pakistan.** Urdu is the national language and English an official language; the Constitution of 1973 envisaged replacing English with Urdu, but English remains the language of government, the higher courts, the armed forces and much higher education.
- **Bangladesh.** The Bengali Language Movement, whose demonstrators were killed in Dhaka on 21 February 1952, made Bengali a symbol of national identity; after independence in 1971 Bengali became the sole official language. English is widely taught and used in business and higher education, but has no official status. UNESCO's International Mother Language Day, 21 February, commemorates the 1952 movement.
- **Sri Lanka.** The Official Language Act of 1956 ("Sinhala Only") replaced English with Sinhala as the official language; after decades of conflict, the Thirteenth Amendment to the Constitution (1987) made Tamil an official language too and described English as a **link language**.

::: definition Link language {#def-link}
A **link language** is a language used for communication between speakers of different first languages within a country or region. In India, with hundreds of languages and two official languages of the Union, English serves as a link language between states (especially between the Hindi-speaking north and the Dravidian-speaking south), in the national government, the higher courts, the armed forces, science and business. Its role as a link language is the main reason English has kept its place, despite the original intention to replace it.
:::

::: theorem The functions of English in India {#rule-functions}
Kachru (1983, 1986) described four functions of English in India, which also fit much of South Asia:

1. **Instrumental** — as a medium of learning, especially in higher education, science and technology.
2. **Regulative** — in administration, law and the courts.
3. **Interpersonal** — as a link language between people of different language backgrounds, and as a marker of education and modernity.
4. **Imaginative or innovative** — in literature, journalism, advertising and film, where writers use English creatively in local ways.
:::

::: proof
Kachru's point was that English in India is not a foreign language used for contact with the outside world, but a language with internal functions — functions within Indian society that no other single language performs across the whole country. This is what places India in the Outer Circle ([[world-englishes/models#def-circles]]) and what drives the nativisation of Indian English in Schneider's sense ([[world-englishes/models#def-dynamic]]): a language used daily for internal purposes, by speakers of many first languages, develops local norms. The literature written in Indian English is the clearest evidence of the fourth function: R. K. Narayan's novels set in the imaginary town of Malgudi (from 1935), Raja Rao's *Kanthapura* (1938), Salman Rushdie's *Midnight's Children* (1981) and Arundhati Roy's *The God of Small Things* (1997) are among the best-known works of world literature in English.
:::

The place of English in South Asia is still argued about, and the arguments are not simple. Some see English as a colonial inheritance that divides an English-educated elite from the majority, and argue for more teaching in home languages; India's National Education Policy of 2020, for example, recommends that the home language or regional language be the medium of instruction until at least the fifth year of school. Others, including some campaigners for groups historically excluded from education, argue that English offers a route to opportunity that regional languages do not, and that access to good English teaching should be widened, not reduced. Parents' demand for English-medium schools has grown strongly in many states. The debate concerns access and equality as much as language, and both sides appeal to the same value — fair opportunity for all children.

## Indian English pronunciation

There is no single Indian accent of English: a speaker whose first language is Tamil, one whose first language is Hindi and one whose first language is Bengali will have noticeably different accents. But educated speakers share many features, and some linguists describe an emerging **General Indian English** that is widely used in national broadcasting and is understood across the country. The description below follows Wells (1982, vol. 3) and Pingali Sailaja's *Indian English* (2009).

::: theorem Features of Indian English pronunciation {#rule-indian-sounds}
1. **Retroflex /t/ and /d/**: made with the tongue tip curled back, [ʈ] and [ɖ] (*time*, *daughter*), because Indian languages contrast retroflex and dental stops and English /t d/ sound closer to the retroflex ones.
2. **/θ/ and /ð/** as dental stops, often with aspiration for /θ/: [t̪ʰ], [d̪] (*thin*, *this*).
3. **/v/ and /w/** often both [ʋ], a sound between the two (*very well*).
4. **FACE and GOAT** as single long vowels [eː], [oː] (*late*, *go*).
5. **Rhoticity** varies; many speakers pronounce /r/ after vowels, often as a tap.
6. **Fewer reduced vowels**: unstressed syllables often keep a full vowel, so the rhythm is closer to syllable-timed than in GB or GA ([[connected-speech/rhythm]]).
7. **Aspiration**: /p t k/ are often unaspirated at the start of stressed syllables, because Indian languages use aspiration to distinguish separate phonemes.
:::

::: example Predicting a pronunciation {#ex-indian-sounds}
How might a speaker of General Indian English pronounce *We have to wait till Thursday* differently from GB [wi həv tə ˈweɪt tɪl ˈθɜːzdeɪ]? Use [[#rule-indian-sounds]].
::: solution
Go word by word:

1. *We* — [ʋiː]: /w/ as [ʋ] (point 3).
2. *have* — [hæʋ], probably with a full vowel rather than the weak [həv] (points 3 and 6).
3. *to* — [ʈuː] or [ʈu], retroflex and with a full vowel (points 1 and 6).
4. *wait* — [ʋeːʈ]: [ʋ], the single vowel [eː] for FACE, and retroflex [ʈ] (points 1, 3, 4).
5. *till* — [ʈɪl] (point 1).
6. *Thursday* — [ˈt̪ʰərsɖeː]: dental aspirated [t̪ʰ] for /θ/, a pronounced /r/, retroflex [ɖ] and [eː] (points 1, 2, 4, 5).

Six words, about a dozen differences — and every one follows a regular correspondence. A listener familiar with GB will understand the sentence easily once the correspondences are known, because the stress falls on the same words and syllables ([[listening-and-conversation/accents#rule-adapt]]).
:::
:::

## Indian English grammar

Most of the grammar of educated Indian English is the common core. The differences are few, but some are frequent enough to be noticed, and they are systematic. They are described in Mesthrie and Bhatt's *World Englishes* (2008), in Sailaja (2009), and in the *Electronic World Atlas of Varieties of English* (eWAVE; Kortmann, Lunkenheimer and Ehret, eds.), which records how common each of more than two hundred grammatical features is in each of dozens of varieties.

::: theorem Some grammatical features of Indian English {#rule-indian-grammar}
1. **The progressive with stative verbs**: *I am having two doubts*; *She is knowing the answer*. Verbs such as *have*, *know*, *understand* and *want*, which GB and GA rarely use in the progressive, often take it.
2. **Invariant tags**: *isn't it?* and *no?* after any clause: *You are coming tomorrow, isn't it?*; *He has left, no?*
3. **Focus particles *only* and *itself***: *I was there only* ‘I was right there’; *Today itself* ‘this very day, today’.
4. **The present perfect with a past time expression**: *I have met him yesterday*, where GB and GA require the past simple.
5. **Wh-questions without inversion** in informal speech: *What you are doing?*; and inversion kept in embedded questions: *I asked him where is the office.*
6. **Reduplication** for emphasis or distribution: *small small pieces* ‘many small pieces’; *hot hot tea* ‘very hot tea’.
7. ***Yesterday night***, *today morning*, beside *last night*, *this morning*.
:::

::: proof
Several of these features can be traced to the grammar of Indian languages, a process called **substrate influence** or transfer. Hindi and other Indian languages use a progressive form with some verbs of state, have tag particles that do not vary with the main clause (Hindi *na*), place focus particles after the word they focus (Hindi *hī*, closely matching *only* and *itself*), and use reduplication widely. Others — invariant tags, the progressive with stative verbs, the present perfect with past adverbials — are found in many second-language varieties around the world, which suggests that they also reflect general tendencies of language learning, such as regularising irregular patterns. Their status in India is not uniform: features 1, 2 and 7 are common in educated speech and writing, while 4 and 5 are more typical of informal speech and are often avoided in edited writing. This is the situation Bamgbose described ([[world-englishes/models#rule-innovation]]): some local forms are widely accepted, others are in an uncertain position between innovation and error.
:::

::: example Analysing a conversation {#ex-indian-grammar}
Identify the Indian English features in this conversation between two colleagues in Pune, and give a GB or GA equivalent for each.

**Anil:** *You are coming for the meeting today itself, no?*
**Deepa:** *Yes, but I am not understanding the agenda. Small small changes are there.*
**Anil:** *I have sent it yesterday only.*
::: solution
1. *today itself* — focus particle *itself* (point 3): ‘today, this very day’.
2. *no?* — invariant tag (point 2): GB/GA *aren't you?*
3. *I am not understanding* — progressive with a stative verb (point 1): GB/GA *I don't understand*.
4. *Small small changes* — reduplication (point 6): ‘many small changes’.
5. *are there* — an existential construction with *there* at the end, common in Indian English: GB/GA *There are many small changes*.
6. *I have sent it yesterday* — present perfect with a past time expression (point 4): GB/GA *I sent it yesterday*.
7. *yesterday only* — focus particle *only* (point 3): ‘just yesterday, as recently as yesterday’.

The conversation is entirely natural among speakers of Indian English. In an email to international colleagues, both speakers would probably write *I sent it yesterday* and *I don't understand the agenda*, choosing more widely shared forms for a mixed readership.
:::
:::

## Vocabulary and style

Indian English vocabulary includes words borrowed from Indian languages, English words with new meanings, and new English formations. Many are standard in India and unknown elsewhere.

| word | meaning | type |
|---|---|---|
| *lakh* | 100,000 (written *1,00,000*) | borrowing |
| *crore* | 10 million (written *1,00,00,000*) | borrowing |
| *prepone* | bring forward to an earlier time | new formation, on the model of *postpone* |
| *timepass* | an activity done to pass the time | new formation |
| *co-brother* | the husband of one's wife's sister | new formation |
| *cousin-brother*, *cousin-sister* | a male or female cousin | new formation |
| *batchmate* | a classmate from the same year | new formation |
| *out of station* | away from one's town, travelling | new sense |
| *pass out* | graduate from a college or academy | new sense |
| *doubt* | a question, something one wants clarified | new sense |
| *revert* | reply | new sense |
| *tiffin* | a light meal, a packed lunch | older British word, kept in India |

Indian English correspondence also has its own conventions of politeness, many of them older British formulas that have been kept: *Respected Sir/Madam*, *Kindly do the needful* ‘please take the necessary action’, *Thanking you* as a closing, and *at the earliest* ‘as soon as possible’. These forms signal respect, and within India they are normal and correct ([[pragmatics/writing-to-people]]).

::: definition Title + given name {#def-title-given}
In India, and in several other varieties of English, it is normal and respectful to address someone with a **title followed by the given name**: *Mr Ramesh*, *Dr Priya*, *Professor Lakshmi*. In British and American English the title is followed by the family name (*Mr Kumar*, *Dr Raman*) and the given name is used alone only in informal address.
:::

The difference has a practical cause. Many people, especially in South India, do not have a family name in the British or American sense: a person may be known by a given name, with an initial for the father's name or a village name placed before it (*R. Priya*, as in the email at the start of this chapter). A family name may not exist to be used with a title. *Mr Ramesh* is therefore the natural respectful form, not a misunderstanding of English. In international settings, when names come from different systems, the safest practice is to ask how someone prefers to be addressed.

::: example Reading the student's email {#ex-email}
Go back to Priya's email at the start of this chapter. List every form that is characteristic of Indian English, and say for each whether it belongs to vocabulary, grammar or the conventions of correspondence.
::: solution
1. *Respected Madam* — correspondence: a respectful salutation, standard in India.
2. *I am having two doubts* — grammar (the progressive with a stative verb, [[#rule-indian-grammar]], point 1) and vocabulary (*doubt* ‘question’).
3. *Kindly clarify* — correspondence: *kindly* as a polite request marker, more frequent than in GB or GA.
4. *the hard copy also* — grammar: *also* placed at the end of the clause, a common Indian English position.
5. *yesterday night* — vocabulary (point 7).
6. *it was not reachable* — vocabulary: *reachable* of a telephone, common in Indian English.
7. *revert at the earliest* — vocabulary (*revert* ‘reply’) and correspondence (*at the earliest*).
8. *Thanking you* — correspondence: a closing formula.
9. *R. Priya* — naming: an initial before the given name ([[#def-title-given]]).

None of these is an error in Indian English. A reader who knows the conventions reads the email as carefully polite; Professor Clarke's surprise came only from comparing it with British conventions.
:::
:::

::: example Converting numbers {#ex-lakh}
An Indian newspaper reports that a new bridge will cost *₹350 crore* and serve *12 lakh* commuters a year. Express both numbers in the international system.
::: solution
Use the values in the table: 1 lakh = 100,000; 1 crore = 10,000,000 (100 lakh).

1. *₹350 crore* = 350 × 10,000,000 = ₹3,500,000,000 — **3.5 billion rupees**.
2. *12 lakh* = 12 × 100,000 = 1,200,000 — **1.2 million commuters**.

Notice also the digit grouping. In the Indian system, numbers are grouped first in three digits and then in twos: *12,00,000* for 1.2 million and *3,50,00,00,000* for 3.5 billion. In international writing, use the international grouping (1,200,000) or words (*1.2 million*), and give the Indian form only for Indian readers.
:::
:::

::: warning Correcting a standard form
✗ A teacher "corrects" an Indian student's *Respected Madam* to *Dear Madam* and tells her it is bad English.
✓ *Respected Madam* is standard in Indian English; *Dear Madam* is the usual British and American form. For an international reader, *Dear Professor Clarke* is the safest choice.

The two forms are both correct in their own varieties. What a learner needs to know is **which readers expect which form**. The same applies to *revert* ‘reply’: standard in India, but liable to be read as ‘return to a previous state’ by readers elsewhere. In international emails, *Could you let me know…?* or *I look forward to your reply* are understood everywhere ([[pragmatics/writing-to-people]]).
:::

::: quiz
An Indian colleague writes: *Kindly revert with your comments by Friday.* What is she asking for?
- [ ] That you undo your previous comments.
- [ ] That you go back to the earlier version of the document.
- [x] That you reply with your comments by Friday.
- [ ] That you send your comments to someone else.
::: solution
In Indian English *revert* means ‘reply, get back to someone’ (see the table above). In GB and GA, *revert* usually means ‘return to a previous state’ (*revert to the old system*), which is why international readers sometimes misread it.
:::
:::

## Pakistan, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka

**Pakistani English** shares many features with Indian English — retroflex stops, [ʋ], invariant tags, the progressive with stative verbs — and has vocabulary from Urdu, Punjabi and other languages of Pakistan. Tariq Rahman's *Pakistani English* (1990) was an early description. **Bangladeshi English** is less used for internal purposes, because Bengali performs most official functions; it is closer to an Expanding Circle situation in some respects, while sharing South Asian features of pronunciation. **Sri Lankan English** has a long literary tradition and its own features, such as the tag *no?*, the discourse particle *aiyo* (expressing dismay or sympathy) and vocabulary from Sinhala and Tamil; it has been an established variety since the nineteenth century, and its vocabulary is recorded in Michael Meyler's *A Dictionary of Sri Lankan English* (2007). In all four countries, English is spoken with a range from the highly educated standard to more local forms, and speakers move along this range according to setting, as they do everywhere.

::: note English and Chinese
- **Forms of address.** Chinese speakers address teachers and senior colleagues with the family name and a title: 王老师, 李经理. Translated word by word, this gives *Teacher Wang* or *Manager Li*, which is unusual in GB and GA (*Ms Wang*, *Mr Li*). Indian English *Mr Ramesh* is the mirror image: title + **given** name. Each pattern is natural in its own system; when writing internationally, use title + family name, or ask.
- **Doubts and questions.** Chinese learners sometimes write *I have a question* where Indian English has *I have a doubt*; both mean the same thing, and both will be understood in India. Outside South Asia, *doubt* may suggest that you think something is wrong, so *question* is the safer word.
- **Listening to Indian English.** The features that make Indian English hard for some Chinese listeners — retroflex stops, [ʋ], fewer weak syllables — are regular. The fuller vowels can even make words easier to recognise once the consonants are familiar. Listen to several Indian speakers, not one.
- **Two large Asian countries.** India and China have very different relationships with English. In India English has internal official functions and is a first or second language for millions; in China it is mainly a foreign language, though a very widely studied one ([[world-englishes/english-in-china]]).
:::

::: history
Descriptions of Indian English began as glossaries for British readers. *Hobson-Jobson* (1886), by Henry Yule and Arthur Coke Burnell, collected Anglo-Indian words and phrases with their histories — *bungalow*, *shampoo*, *pyjamas*, *tiffin* — many of which passed into general English. Serious linguistic description began after independence: Braj Kachru's essays, collected in *The Indianization of English* (1983), argued that Indian English was a variety with its own norms; Wells (1982) and later Sailaja (2009) described its pronunciation; and eWAVE (from 2011) placed its grammar beside that of other varieties. Indian writers in English, from Narayan and Rao in the 1930s to Rushdie and Roy, made it a language of world literature.
:::

::: summary
- English came to South Asia with the East India Company and became the language of education after Macaulay's Minute (1835); after independence it was kept, in different roles, in India, Pakistan, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka.
