Zhou Min is staying with friends in Manchester. On the way out, her friend Laura says two things about the same person, using the same verb:

**Laura:** *Tom must be at home — his car's outside.*

**Laura:** *And Tom must stay at home this week. The doctor said so.*

The two sentences have the same structure — subject, *must*, a bare infinitive — but they do two quite different jobs. The first is a **conclusion**: Laura has not seen Tom, but the car is evidence, and she is reasoning from it. In Chinese this would be something like 汤姆一定在家. The second is an **obligation**: someone with authority has told Tom what he has to do, roughly 汤姆这周必须待在家里. Chinese uses two different words here, 一定 and 必须; English uses one.

This double life is typical of the modal verbs. *You may leave* gives permission; *It may rain* is a guess about the weather. *She should apologise* gives advice; *The train should be here soon* is an expectation. To understand English modality you need to know what the verbs look like and how they behave in sentences, and then to see that their meanings fall into two broad families. This chapter sets out both. The following chapters take the meanings one at a time.

The first modal verbs — *can* and *could* for ability and permission, *must* and *have to* for obligation, *should* for advice, and the difference between *mustn't* and *don't have to* — were introduced in [[core-grammar/first-modals]]. This course assumes them and goes further.

## The modal verbs and their relatives

::: definition Modality and modal verbs {#def-modality}
**Modality** is the expression of a speaker's attitude to the truth of what they say (how possible, probable or certain it is) or to the action they talk about (whether it is necessary, allowed, advisable or possible for someone). The **central modal verbs** of English are the nine auxiliaries *can*, *could*, *may*, *might*, *must*, *shall*, *should*, *will* and *would*. Four of them come in pairs, a present and a historically past form: *can/could*, *may/might*, *shall/should*, *will/would*. *Must* has no partner.
:::

Around this core is a ring of words that behave like modals in some ways but not in others. Quirk et al. (1985) call *ought to*, *used to*, *need* and *dare* the **marginal modals**; *need* and *dare* behave as modals mainly in negatives and questions (*You needn't wait*, *How dare you?*). Further out are the **semi-modals** (Biber et al. 1999; Quirk et al. call many of them semi-auxiliaries): multi-word expressions that share the meanings of the modals but are built with ordinary verbs such as *be* and *have*.

| group | members | example |
|---|---|---|
| central modals | *can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, would* | *She **might** be late.* |
| marginal modals | *ought to, used to, need, dare* | *You **needn't** worry.* *How **dare** he!* |
| semi-modals | *have to, have got to, need to, be able to, be going to, be supposed to, be allowed to, had better* | *We **have to** leave.* *You'**d better** hurry.* |

The groups are not sealed off from each other. *Ought to* has the grammar of a modal (*You ought not to…*, BrE also *Ought we to…?*) but includes *to*; *had better* is followed by a bare infinitive like a modal, but it is made with *had*; *need* is a modal in *Need I come?* and an ordinary verb in *Do I need to come?*. What all of them share is meaning: each tells us something about possibility, necessity, permission, ability, willingness or prediction.

::: theorem The grammar of the central modals {#rule-modal-grammar}
The central modals share these properties (Quirk et al. 1985; Huddleston & Pullum 2002):

1. They are followed by a **bare infinitive** (without *to*): *She can **swim**.*
2. They take ***not*** directly and form questions by **inversion**, without *do*: *You **mustn't** go.* ***Should** I call?*
3. They can stand alone in **short answers, tags and ellipsis**: *"Can you come?" "Yes, I **can**."* *You'll help, **won't** you?*
4. They have **no *-s***, no *-ing* form, no infinitive and no participle: ✗ *to can*, ✗ *maying*, ✗ *has musted*.
5. In standard English **only one** central modal can occur in a verb phrase: ✗ *will can*, ✗ *might should*. A semi-modal fills the gap: *will **be able to***, *might **have to***.
6. A modal comes **first** in the verb phrase, and the perfect, progressive and passive follow it in that order: *must **have been being** repaired* is possible, if rare.
:::

::: proof
Properties 2 and 3 are the four "NICE" properties of auxiliaries — Negation, Inversion, Code (standing for a whole verb phrase in ellipsis) and Emphasis — which every auxiliary has (Huddleston & Pullum 2002 use these tests). Property 4 is special to modals and has a historical cause: most of them are **preterite-present** verbs, old past-tense forms that came to have present meaning, and English past forms never took a third-person *-s* (see [[core-grammar/first-modals]]). Property 5 follows from property 4: because a modal has no infinitive, nothing can come after another modal and still be a modal. Some varieties do combine modals — Scottish English and the English of the southern United States have "double modals" such as *might could* — but these are dialect forms, not standard English (Swan 2016). Property 6 is the order of the verb phrase described in [[tense-and-aspect/time-tense-aspect#rule-verb-phrase]], with the modal in first position.
:::

Because a modal has no tense of its own in the ordinary sense, English uses the infinitive after it to add aspect and voice:

| after the modal | form | example |
|---|---|---|
| simple infinitive | *be*, *go* | *She must **be** tired.* |
| progressive infinitive | *be* + *-ing* | *He might **be sleeping**.* |
| perfect infinitive | *have* + past participle | *They can't **have left** yet.* |
| perfect progressive infinitive | *have been* + *-ing* | *You must **have been waiting** for ages.* |
| passive infinitive | *be* + past participle | *The form should **be signed** in ink.* |
| perfect passive | *have been* + past participle | *The letter may **have been lost**.* |

The perfect infinitive is how modals look back in time (*must have left*); it is the subject of [[modality/past-modals]].

::: warning A "past" modal is not a past tense
✗ *Yesterday I should go to the bank, so I left work early.* ✓ *Yesterday I **had to** go to the bank, so I left work early.*

✗ *Last year she might speak French quite well.* ✓ *Last year she **could** speak French quite well.* (past ability)

✗ *When I phoned, he would be out.* ✓ *When I phoned, he **was** out.*

*Could*, *might*, *should* and *would* began as past forms, but today they mostly express **tentativeness or hypothesis**, not past time: *Could you help me?* is a request about now, and *It might rain* is a guess about the future. Of the four, only *could* (general past ability) and *would* (past habit, future in the past) still have regular past-time uses. For past obligation use *had to*; for a conclusion about the past use a perfect infinitive (*She must have known*).
:::

::: example Fixing the form {#ex-form}
Each sentence breaks one rule of [[#rule-modal-grammar]]. Find the rule and correct it. (a) *You will can see the sea from your room.* (b) *Does she must wear a uniform?* (c) *He might to call later.* (d) *They should finished the report yesterday.* (e) *It mays be true.* (f) *I'd like to can play the guitar.*
::: solution
(a) Two central modals cannot combine (rule 5): *You **will be able to** see the sea from your room.* (Or simply *You can see the sea…*, since *can* already covers the future here.)

(b) Modals form questions by inversion, not with *do* (rule 2): ***Must** she wear a uniform?* or, more usually, ***Does** she **have to** wear a uniform?* — *have to* is an ordinary verb and does take *do*.

(c) A bare infinitive follows the modal (rule 1): *He might **call** later.*

(d) After a modal, the past is shown with a perfect infinitive: *They should **have finished** the report yesterday.* (This means they did not finish it: see [[modality/past-modals]].)

(e) No *-s* (rule 4): *It **may** be true.*

(f) A modal has no infinitive (rule 4): *I'd like to **be able to** play the guitar.*
:::
:::

## Two families of meaning

Go back to Laura's two sentences about Tom. The first is about **what Laura believes**: it rates how likely a proposition is to be true. The second is about **what Tom has to do**: it imposes a requirement on an action. Linguists name these two families with terms borrowed from philosophy, where von Wright (1951) had distinguished several "modes" of modality.

::: definition Epistemic modality {#def-epistemic}
**Epistemic modality** (from Greek *epistēmē* ‘knowledge’) expresses the speaker's judgement of **how likely it is that something is true**: possible, probable, almost certain, certain, impossible. *She **may** be right.* *That **must** be the postman.* *It **can't** be true.* *They **should** be home by now.* *It **will** be his sister — she always calls at this time.*
:::

::: definition Deontic and dynamic modality {#def-deontic}
**Deontic modality** (from Greek *deon* ‘what is binding’) expresses **obligation, permission and prohibition**: what someone must, may or must not do, usually because of a rule or a person with authority, often the speaker. *You **must** register by Friday.* *You **may** leave now.* *Visitors **can't** park here.* *You **should** apologise.*

**Dynamic modality** expresses **ability and willingness** that belong to the subject itself, with no rule or authority involved: *She **can** speak four languages.* *He **won't** tell me his name.* (Palmer 1979, 2001; Huddleston & Pullum 2002.)
:::

Deontic and dynamic meanings are often grouped together as **root** modality (Coates 1983) or **intrinsic** modality (Quirk et al. 1985), and epistemic modality is then called **extrinsic**. The terms differ from grammar to grammar, but the main division is the same in all of them: on one side, judgements about **truth**; on the other, the forces — rules, abilities, wishes — that act on **events**.

| modal | root (deontic or dynamic) meaning | epistemic meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *must* | obligation: *You **must** sign here.* | confident conclusion: *You **must** be tired.* |
| *should* | advice, what is right: *You **should** rest.* | expectation: *The parcel **should** arrive today.* |
| *may* | permission (formal): *You **may** begin.* | possibility: *It **may** snow tonight.* |
| *might* | very tentative permission (rare): ***Might** I ask a question?* | possibility: *She **might** know.* |
| *can* | ability, permission: *I **can** swim.* *You **can** go.* | impossibility, in negatives and questions: *That **can't** be right.* |
| *could* | past ability, polite request: *I **could** read at four.* ***Could** you help?* | possibility: *The keys **could** be in the car.* |
| *will* | willingness: *I'**ll** carry it for you.* | prediction, confident assumption: *It'**ll** be dark soon.* *That'**ll** be Mum.* |
| *would* | past willingness: *She **wouldn't** listen.* | hypothesis, tentative assumption: *That **would** be his brother, I suppose.* |

::: theorem Telling epistemic from root meanings {#rule-tests}
When a modal could have either meaning, these signs usually decide (Coates 1983; Palmer 2001):

1. **Paraphrase.** Epistemic meanings can be rephrased with *it is possible / likely / certain that…*: *She may be right* = ‘It is possible that she is right’. Root meanings are rephrased with *it is necessary for…*, *…is allowed to*, *…is able to*: *You may begin* = ‘You are allowed to begin’.
2. **What follows the modal.** A **perfect** or **progressive** infinitive, or a **state verb** such as *be*, *know* or *have* (possession), usually signals epistemic meaning: *He must **be** tired*, *She must **be working** late*, *They must **have left***. An **action verb** done on purpose by the subject usually signals root meaning: *You must **leave** now.*
3. **Who controls the event.** Root meanings need a subject who can act (or be made to act); epistemic meanings work with any subject, including *it* and *there*: *There **may** be a problem.*
4. **Context.** Evidence and reasoning (*his car's outside*) point to epistemic meaning; rules, authority and advice point to root meaning.
:::

::: proof
The signs follow from what each kind of meaning does. An obligation or permission is aimed at a future action that someone can choose to do, so it goes naturally with a dynamic verb and an agent. A judgement of likelihood can be made about anything — a state, an activity in progress, an event already over — so it goes with state verbs, progressives and perfects. These are tendencies, not laws: *You must have finished by six* (deontic: a deadline) has a perfect infinitive, and *She may leave early* can be permission or possibility. Coates (1983) found many corpus examples where both readings are present and the context does not choose between them, and she argued that the meanings of each modal form a gradient rather than separate boxes. Sweetser (1990) explains why one verb serves both families: the epistemic meaning applies the force of the root meaning to reasoning. Evidence "forces" Laura to the conclusion that Tom is at home, just as the doctor forces Tom to stay there.
:::

::: example Which family? {#ex-classify}
Say whether each modal is epistemic or root (and if root, deontic or dynamic). Give a paraphrase. (a) *You must be Mr Park — we spoke on the phone.* (b) *Passengers must not leave bags unattended.* (c) *Ana can run ten kilometres in under an hour.* (d) *The figures may be slightly out of date.* (e) *You may use a dictionary in the exam.* (f) *Prices should fall later this year.* (g) *I won't sign this contract.*
::: solution
(a) Epistemic. *Be* is a state verb, and the speaker reasons from evidence (the phone call): ‘I'm sure you are Mr Park.’

(b) Deontic. A rule, an action the subject controls: ‘Passengers are not allowed to leave bags unattended.’ The negation applies to the action (see [[#rule-negation]]).

(c) Dynamic. Ana's own ability, no rule involved: ‘Ana is able to run ten kilometres…’

(d) Epistemic. *There* or *it* could replace the subject without strangeness, and the meaning is ‘It is possible that the figures are slightly out of date.’

(e) Deontic. Permission from the examiner: ‘You are allowed to use a dictionary.’

(f) Epistemic. An expectation based on what the speaker knows: ‘It is probable that prices will fall.’ Prices cannot be advised to fall.

(g) Dynamic (willingness): ‘I refuse to sign this contract.’
:::
:::

In which sentence is *must* epistemic?
- [ ] *All visitors must sign in at reception.*
- [ ] *I must remember to call the bank.*
- [x] *You must have been very worried.*
- [ ] *Students must submit two essays.*
::: solution
*Must have been* has a perfect infinitive and a state verb, and it expresses the speaker's conclusion: ‘I'm sure you were very worried.’ The other three are obligations — two from rules, one (*I must remember*) from the speaker.

- [ ] permission
- [x] possibility
- [ ] ability
- [ ] obligation
::: solution
The paraphrase ‘It is possible that it is cheaper to take the train’ works; ‘It is allowed to be cheaper’ does not. The subject is *it*, which cannot be given permission.

## Strength: a scale of modality

The modals do not only differ in kind; they also differ in **strength**. Within each family they can be arranged on a rough scale. Halliday's functional grammar (*An Introduction to Functional Grammar*, 2nd edition, 1994) groups them into three values: high (for example *must* and *have to*), median (*will*, *would*, *should*) and low (*may*, *might*, *can*, *could*).

| strength | epistemic (how likely?) | deontic (how necessary?) |
|---|---|---|
| strong | *must*, *can't*, *will* | *must*, *have to*, *mustn't* |
| medium | *should*, *ought to*, *would* | *should*, *ought to*, *had better* |
| weak | *may*, *might*, *could* | *may*, *can*, *could* (permission, suggestion) |

The scale is approximate. The chapters on deduction ([[modality/deduction]]) and obligation ([[modality/obligation-necessity]]) refine it, and the scale changes with context and intonation: *You **could** at least say thank you* is not weak at all.

::: example Ordering by strength {#ex-strength}
A doctor is talking to a patient about the result of a blood test. Put the five sentences in order from the weakest claim to the strongest, and say what each tells the patient. (a) *The result will be ready on Monday.* (b) *The result might show a slight infection.* (c) *The result should be ready on Monday.* (d) *The result could take longer if the lab is busy.* (e) *You must come back to discuss it.*
::: solution
Sentences (a)–(d) are epistemic and can be ranked together; (e) is deontic and belongs to the other scale.

1. (b) *might* and (d) *could* — weak possibility: the doctor does not expect an infection, or a delay, but cannot rule them out. *Might* and *could* are about equally weak here.
2. (c) *should* — medium: the doctor expects the result on Monday, on the basis of the normal routine, but leaves room for a delay.
3. (a) *will* — strong: a confident prediction, stated almost as fact.

(e) *must* — strong deontic: the doctor makes the return visit an obligation. A weaker version would be *You should come back*, and a much weaker one *You might like to come back* (a polite suggestion: see [[modality/modality-politeness]]).

The order of (c) and (a) shows a useful difference: *should* tells the patient that the doctor is relying on things going normally, *will* that she has no doubt.
:::
:::

## Negation and modals

Negation is where the modals cause most trouble, because *not* can attach to two different things: the modal meaning itself, or the event after it.

::: theorem What *not* negates {#rule-negation}
In a negative modal sentence, the negation applies **either to the modality** or **to the main verb**, and each modal has its own pattern (Quirk et al. 1985; Huddleston & Pullum 2002; Swan 2016):

| negative | negates | meaning | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *can't*, *cannot* | the modality | ‘not possible’, ‘not allowed’ | *That **can't** be true.* *You **can't** park here.* |
| *needn't*, *don't have to* | the modality | ‘not necessary’ | *You **needn't** come.* |
| *mustn't* | the main verb | ‘necessary not to’ | *You **mustn't** tell anyone.* |
| *shouldn't*, *oughtn't to* | the main verb | ‘advisable not to’ | *You **shouldn't** worry.* |
| *may not* (deontic) | the modality | ‘not allowed’ (formal) | *You **may not** use your phone.* |
| *may not*, *might not* (epistemic) | the main verb | ‘possible that … not’ | *She **may not** know.* |
| *won't* | the modality (willingness) or the main verb (prediction) | ‘refuse(s) to’; ‘will not happen’ | *He **won't** listen.* *It **won't** rain.* |
:::

::: proof
The patterns become clear when you paraphrase. *You mustn't tell anyone* = ‘It is necessary that you do **not** tell anyone’: the obligation is positive, the action negative. *You needn't come* = ‘It is **not** necessary that you come’: the obligation itself is denied. That is why the two negatives, which look like near-synonyms, are opposites in practice — the contrast of 不能 and 不必 discussed in [[core-grammar/first-modals#rule-mustnt]]. With *may*, the two readings split by meaning: deontic *You may not leave* denies the permission (‘you are not allowed’), but epistemic *She may not know* asserts a possibility of not knowing (‘perhaps she doesn't know’). Huddleston and Pullum (2002) call these **external** and **internal** negation: negation outside the modal and negation inside it. In speech, epistemic *may not* and *might not* are not contracted to *mayn't* (now very rare) and are only sometimes contracted to *mightn't*, mainly in British English.
:::

::: warning *Mustn't* for "not necessarily"
✗ *You mustn't bring a laptop — we'll provide one.* ✓ *You **don't have to** / **needn't** bring a laptop — we'll provide one.*

✗ *He's not answering, but he mustn't be out — maybe he's asleep.* ✓ *…but he **isn't necessarily** out* or *…but he **may not** be out.*

✗ *She mustn't be at home — the lights are off.* ✓ *She **can't** be at home — the lights are off.*

*Mustn't* means ‘it is necessary not to’. For ‘not necessary’ use *don't have to* or *needn't*; for an impossible conclusion use *can't* (see [[modality/deduction]]).
:::

::: example Paraphrasing negatives {#ex-negatives}
Paraphrase each sentence with *it is (not) necessary / possible / allowed*, and say whether *not* negates the modality or the main verb. (a) *You mustn't feed the animals.* (b) *We needn't decide today.* (c) *He may not have received my email.* (d) *Candidates may not leave the room during the first hour.* (e) *That can't be Jun — he's in Beijing.*
::: solution
(a) ‘It is necessary that you do not feed the animals’ — negates the main verb.

(b) ‘It is not necessary for us to decide today’ — negates the modality.

(c) ‘It is possible that he has not received my email’ — negates the main verb (epistemic *may not*).

(d) ‘Candidates are not allowed to leave the room…’ — negates the modality (deontic *may not*, formal).

(e) ‘It is not possible that that is Jun’ — negates the modality.
:::
:::

::: quiz
*The museum may not be open on Mondays.* Which paraphrase is correct?
- [ ] The museum is not allowed to open on Mondays.
- [x] Perhaps the museum is not open on Mondays.
- [ ] The museum is certainly closed on Mondays.
- [ ] The museum does not need to open on Mondays.
::: solution
The subject is a building and the verb a state (*be open*), so *may* is epistemic, and epistemic *may not* means ‘it is possible that … not’. The first option would need a deontic reading, which is odd with a museum as subject.
:::
:::

## How often, and where

The modals are not used equally. In the large corpus counts of the *Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English* (Biber et al. 1999), *will*, *would* and *can* are the most frequent central modals, and modals as a group are commonest in conversation. *May* is much more frequent in academic prose than in conversation, and there it is used almost always for possibility rather than permission, while conversation prefers *might* and *could* for possibility and *can* for permission. Coates (1983) found the same pattern in British data: deontic *may* is a formal, rather old-fashioned use.

Over the second half of the twentieth century, several central modals became less frequent in written English. Leech et al. (2009), comparing matched corpora of British and American writing from 1961 and the early 1990s, found that *must*, *shall*, *may* and *ought to* declined most clearly, while semi-modals such as *have to* and *need to* became more frequent. Their explanation includes a general trend in writing towards the style of speech ("colloquialisation") and a move away from expressions that sound authoritarian. The decline is uneven across meanings, and it does not make the old modals wrong or unusual: *must* and *may* are still very common, especially in writing. The details belong to the chapters on obligation ([[modality/obligation-necessity]]) and on *shall* ([[modality/will-would-shall]]).

::: note English and Chinese
- **Same position, different division of meaning.** Chinese modal verbs (能愿动词 or 助动词 — 能, 会, 可以, 可能, 应该, 应当, 必须, 得, 要, 敢, 肯, 愿意) also stand before the main verb and do not change for person, so the English form causes few problems. The difficulty is that the two languages divide the meanings differently (Li & Thompson 1981 describe the Mandarin auxiliary verbs).
- **One English verb, two Chinese words.** Epistemic *must* is usually 一定 or 肯定 (他一定在家 → *He must be at home*), but deontic *must* is 必须 (你必须在家 → *You must stay at home*). 必须 is never epistemic: ✗ 他必须很累 does not mean *He must be very tired*.
- **One Chinese word, two English meanings.** 会 is dynamic (我会游泳 → *I can swim*) and epistemic (明天会下雨 → *It will rain tomorrow*). 应该 is deontic (你应该道歉 → *You should apologise*) and epistemic (他应该已经到了 → *He should be there by now*). Here Chinese and English match well: *should* has both meanings too.
- **可能 is only epistemic.** 他可能不来 → *He may not come / He might not come.* It never gives permission, so 你可能走了 cannot mean *You may go*; permission is 可以 (你可以走了 → *You can go now* / formal *You may go*).
- **Negation.** Chinese puts 不 before the modal (不能, 不会, 不应该, 不必, 不可能), and the meaning comes from the word chosen, not from the position of *not*. English *mustn't* and *needn't* look alike but mean 不能/不准 and 不必; *can't be* is 不可能是, not ✗ *mustn't be*.
- **Past.** Chinese adds 已经 or 了 to the main verb (他一定已经走了); English uses a perfect infinitive after the modal (*He must have left*), never a past-tense modal.
:::

::: history
The word *modal* comes from Latin *modus* ‘manner, measure’. In ancient and medieval logic a "mode" was a way in which a proposition could be true — necessarily or possibly — and Aristotle's discussion of necessity and possibility in *De Interpretatione* is the starting point of modal logic. Linguists took over the logicians' categories in the twentieth century. Von Wright's *An Essay in Modal Logic* (1951) separated epistemic modality (what is known) from deontic modality (what is obligatory or permitted), and John Lyons's *Semantics* (1977) discussed both at length in a linguistic setting. Palmer's *Modality and the English Modals* (1979) and Coates's *The Semantics of the Modal Auxiliaries* (1983) then described the English verbs in these terms, Coates with examples from British corpora; most later grammars, including Huddleston & Pullum (2002), use the same framework with some changes of name.
:::

::: summary
- Modality expresses the speaker's view of how likely something is, or of what is necessary, allowed, advisable or possible for someone ([[#def-modality]]).
- The nine central modals take a bare infinitive, form negatives and questions without *do*, have no *-s* or non-finite forms, and do not combine with each other in standard English; semi-modals (*have to*, *be able to*) fill the gaps ([[#rule-modal-grammar]]).
- *Could*, *might*, *should* and *would* are not simply past tenses; to look back, a modal takes a perfect infinitive (*must have left*).
- Epistemic modality judges truth (*She must be tired*); deontic modality expresses obligation and permission (*You must leave*); dynamic modality expresses ability and willingness (*She can swim*) ([[#def-epistemic]], [[#def-deontic]]).
- Paraphrases, the kind of verb that follows (state, progressive, perfect), the subject and the context tell the meanings apart ([[#rule-tests]]).
- Negation applies either to the modality (*can't*, *needn't*, deontic *may not*) or to the main verb (*mustn't*, *shouldn't*, epistemic *may not*) ([[#rule-negation]]).
- Chinese splits some English modals (epistemic 一定 and deontic 必须 for *must*) and joins others (会 for *can* and *will*).

## Exercises

::: exercise Central, marginal or semi-modal? {level=1}
Sort the words in italics into central modals, marginal modals and semi-modals. (a) *You **ought to** see this film.* (b) *She **might** be late.* (c) *We'**re supposed to** be there at six.* (d) *He **needn't** know.* (e) *I'**ll** call you.* (f) *They'**ve got to** finish by Friday.* (g) *How **dare** you!* (h) *You'**d better** take a coat.*
::: solution
Central: (b) *might*, (e) *will*. Marginal: (a) *ought to*, (d) *need* (as a modal: *needn't*), (g) *dare*. Semi-modals: (c) *be supposed to*, (f) *have got to*, (h) *had better*. (*Had better* is sometimes grouped with the marginal modals; either answer is defensible if you explain that it takes a bare infinitive like a modal but is built with *had*.)
:::
:::

::: solution

::: exercise Epistemic or root? {level=1}
Mark each modal E (epistemic) or R (root). (a) *It might snow tonight.* (b) *You can borrow my bike.* (c) *She must be over seventy.* (d) *I can't lift this box.* (e) *There should be enough food for everyone.* (f) *All employees should complete the safety course.* (g) *This can't be the right address.* (h) *Will you marry me?*
::: solution
(a) E; (b) R (deontic: permission); (c) E; (d) R (dynamic: ability); (e) E (expectation); (f) R (deontic); (g) E; (h) R (dynamic: willingness).
:::
:::

::: exercise Ambiguous modals {level=2}
Each sentence can be read in two ways. Give both readings and a context for each. (a) *He must be very careful.* (b) *You may go to the party.* (c) *She can't be serious about this job.* (d) *They should be in the office at nine.*
::: solution
(a) Epistemic: ‘I'm sure he is a careful person’ (*Look how neatly he packs — he must be very careful*). Deontic: ‘He is obliged to be careful’ (*The ice is thin — he must be very careful*).

(b) Deontic: ‘You are allowed to go’ (a parent to a child). Epistemic: ‘Perhaps you will go’ (*If you finish early, you may go to the party after all*).

(c) Epistemic: ‘It is impossible that she is serious’ (*She can't be serious about this job — she hates offices*). Deontic, less natural: ‘She is not allowed to be serious’ — almost only in a jokey context. A dynamic reading (‘She is unable to be serious’) is also possible: *She can't be serious about anything for more than a minute.*

(d) Deontic: ‘They are supposed to be in the office at nine’ (a rule). Epistemic: ‘I expect they will be in the office at nine’ (a prediction).
:::
:::

::: exercise Negatives {level=2}
Complete with *mustn't*, *needn't*, *can't* or *may not*, and give a paraphrase. (a) *You ___ shout — I can hear you perfectly well.* (b) *You ___ shout in the library.* (c) *The parcel ___ arrive before Friday; it depends on the post.* (d) *He ___ be the thief — he was with me all evening.* (e) *We ___ book in advance; there are always free tables.*
::: solution
(a) *needn't* — it is not necessary. (b) *mustn't* — it is necessary that you don't; prohibition. (*can't* is also possible: ‘you are not allowed’.) (c) *may not* — it is possible that it will not arrive. (d) *can't* — it is not possible that he is the thief. (e) *needn't* — it is not necessary.
:::
:::

::: exercise Choosing the strength {level=2}
Rewrite each sentence with a modal, changing the strength as asked. (a) *It is certain that the meeting has been cancelled.* (weaker: possible) (b) *Perhaps the shop is closed.* (stronger: almost certain) (c) *It would be a good idea to back up your files.* (stronger: necessary) (d) *You are required to show your passport.* (weaker: advisable)
::: solution
(a) *The meeting may / might / could have been cancelled.* (b) *The shop must be closed.* (or medium strength: *The shop will be closed* / *should be closed* — *should* suggests the speaker expects or hopes it is) (c) *You must / have to back up your files.* (d) *You should / ought to show your passport.*

::: exercise Translate {level=3}
Translate into English, choosing between epistemic and deontic modals. (a) 他一定很累了。 (b) 你一定要来参加我的婚礼！ (c) 学生必须在九点以前到校。 (d) 明天可能会下雪。 (e) 你不必现在回答。 (f) 这不可能是真的。 (g) 他应该已经到上海了。
::: hint
For 一定, decide first whether the sentence is a conclusion or an insistence. For 应该, decide whether it is advice or an expectation.
:::
::: solution
(a) *He must be very tired.* (epistemic) (b) *You must come to my wedding!* (deontic; also *You have to come…*) — 一定要 here is insistence, so *must* is right, but the meaning is root, not epistemic. (c) *Students must / have to be at school before nine.* (d) *It may / might snow tomorrow.* (e) *You don't have to / needn't answer now.* (f) *This can't be true.* (g) *He should be in Shanghai by now.* (or *He should have arrived in Shanghai by now.*)
:::

::: exercise Analyse a dialogue {level=3}
Read the dialogue. For each modal in bold, say whether it is epistemic, deontic or dynamic, and explain the clue you used.

**Kofi:** *The lights are on next door. They **must** be back from holiday.*

**Wang Lei:** *They **can't** be — they said two weeks, and it's only been one.*

**Kofi:** *Then someone **might** be in the house. **Should** we call the police?*

**Wang Lei:** *Wait. Their son **could** be staying there. He **can** get in — he has a key.*

**Kofi:** *You're right. But we **ought to** check, just in case. I'**ll** knock.*
::: solution
*must* — epistemic: a conclusion from evidence (*the lights are on*), state verb *be*. *can't* — epistemic: the impossibility of the same conclusion. *might* — epistemic: possibility; the subject *someone* is not being given permission. *Should* — deontic: asking for advice about an action the speakers can take. *could* — epistemic: possibility, with a progressive infinitive (*be staying*). *can* — dynamic: ability (he has a key; it also shades into permission, since the key was given to him). *ought to* — deontic: advice about an action. *'ll* — dynamic: willingness, a decision made now.
