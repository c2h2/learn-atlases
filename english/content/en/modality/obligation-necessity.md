Here are four notices that a student, Nguyen Thi Lan, finds on her first day at a British university.

- *All students **must** register online before 30 September.*
- *You **don't have to** buy the textbooks: copies are available in the library.*
- *Students **should** contact their personal tutor in the first week.*
- *Laboratory coats **must** be worn at all times. Food and drink **must not** be brought into the laboratory.*

And here is what her flatmate, Ellie, says that evening: *I've **got to** register tonight — I keep forgetting. You'**d better** do yours too. Oh, and you **need to** get a library card. You **ought to** go to the welcome party as well, it's fun.*

In a few sentences there are seven ways of saying that something is necessary, advisable or not necessary: *must*, *must not*, *don't have to*, *should*, *have got to*, *had better*, *need to* and *ought to*. They are not interchangeable. Some are stronger than others; some sound written and official, others spoken and friendly; some are more British, some more American. This chapter sorts them out.

The basic contrasts — *must* and *have to* for obligation, *should* for advice, and the opposition between *mustn't* (not allowed) and *don't have to* (not necessary) — were introduced in [[core-grammar/first-modals]]. Obligation and advice are deontic modality ([[modality/what-modals-do#def-deontic]]); the same verbs used for conclusions (*She must be tired*) are the subject of [[modality/deduction]].

## Strong obligation: *must*, *have to* and *have got to*

::: definition Obligation and necessity {#def-obligation}
**Obligation** is deontic necessity: an action is required because of a rule, a law, a person with authority, or the speaker's own judgement. **Necessity** in a wider sense also covers what is required by circumstances, with no one imposing it: *To get a visa you **need to** show a bank statement.* English expresses both mainly with ***must***, ***have to***, ***have got to*** and ***need to***.
:::

| form | grammar | register and variety | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *must* | central modal; present only | neutral to formal; common in rules and in writing | *Passengers **must** wear seatbelts.* |
| *have to* | ordinary verb + *to*: all tenses, *do* in questions | neutral; the usual spoken form, especially in AmE | *Do you **have to** work on Saturday?* |
| *have got to* | *have* + *got to*: present only | informal; mainly BrE | *I'**ve got to** go now.* |
| *need to* | ordinary verb + *to* | neutral; necessity from circumstances or needs | *You **need to** renew your visa.* |

::: theorem *Must* and *have to* {#rule-must-have-to}
1. Both express **strong obligation**, and in many sentences either is correct: *I **must** / **have to** finish this report today.*
2. In British English there is a tendency to use ***must*** when the obligation comes from the **speaker** — the speaker's own wishes or authority — and ***have to*** when it comes from **outside**: a rule, a law, a timetable, another person. *I **must** get my hair cut* (I think so). *I **have to** get my hair cut* (my school insists). *You **must** be here by eight* (I am telling you). *You **have to** be here by eight* (that is the rule).
3. In American English, ***have to*** is the usual form in speech for both kinds of obligation, and *must* sounds strong or formal.
4. In **written rules and regulations** in both varieties, *must* is normal, often with a passive: *Applications **must** be received by 1 March.*
5. *Must* has no past, future or non-finite forms. Use ***had to*** for the past, ***will have to*** for the future (and for decisions about the future), and ***have to*** after other verbs and modals: *We **had to** queue for an hour.* *You'**ll have to** wait.* *I hate **having to** get up early.* *You **might have to** pay a fee.*
6. *Must* can refer to the future when the obligation exists now: *I **must** call her tomorrow.*
:::

::: proof
Rule 2 is described in Swan (2016), Quirk et al. (1985) and Leech (2004). It is a tendency, not a fixed law: Coates (1983) found in British data that *must* is often "subjective" (the speaker's authority) and *have to* more often "objective", but both kinds occur with both verbs, and many speakers use *have to* everywhere. Rule 3 reflects a real difference between the varieties: Biber et al. (1999) found *have to* far more common than *must* in American conversation, and *have got to* much more common in British than in American English. Over the twentieth century, the balance has moved in the same direction in both varieties: Leech et al. (2009), comparing matched corpora of written English from 1961 and the early 1990s, found that *must* declined clearly in British and American writing, while *have to* and especially *need to* became more frequent. They connect this with a wider move away from forms that sound authoritarian. *Must* has not become rare: it remains the normal word in rules and in formal writing.
:::

Two uses of *must* deserve a separate mention because they are not really obligation at all. In **warm insistence**, *must* turns an invitation or a recommendation into an enthusiastic one: *You **must** come and visit us!* *You **must** try the dumplings here — they're amazing.* And ***have to*** is used in the same way in American and increasingly in British English: *You **have to** see this film.* Nobody is being forced; the speaker is showing strong feeling.

::: theorem *Have got to* {#rule-have-got-to}
1. ***Have got to*** has the same meaning as *have to* and is common in **informal British English**: *I'**ve got to** be at the airport at six.* ***Have** you **got to** work tomorrow?*
2. It is used only in the **present**. For the past use *had to* (not normally ✗ *had got to*); for the future *will have to*.
3. It is usually contracted (*I've got to*, *she's got to*), and in fast speech often sounds like *gotta*: GB /ˈɡɒtə/, GA /ˈɡɑːtə/ with the *t* usually flapped, [ˈɡɑːɾə]. In American informal speech *got to* and *gotta* are common without *have*: *I **gotta** go.* The written form *gotta* appears only in informal writing or to represent speech.
:::

::: warning Questions and negatives with *must* and *have to*
✗ *Do I must pay?* ✓ ***Must** I pay?* (formal, rare) / ***Do** I **have to** pay?* (usual)

✗ *She doesn't must come.* ✓ *She **doesn't have to** come.* (not necessary) / *She **mustn't** come.* (not allowed)

✗ *Yesterday I must stay late.* ✓ *Yesterday I **had to** stay late.*

✗ *I have to can speak English for this job.* ✓ *I **have to be able to** speak English for this job.*

An answer to *Must I…?* in the negative is *No, you **needn't** / **don't have to***, not *No, you mustn't* — which would forbid the action.
:::

::: example *Must* or *have to*? {#ex-must-have-to}
Complete with *must* or a form of *have to*. Where both are possible, say which a British speaker would probably choose and why. (a) *I ___ remember to call Grandma — it's her birthday.* (b) *In Singapore you ___ pay a large fine for dropping litter.* (c) *When I was at school we ___ wear a uniform.* (d) *All visitors ___ report to reception.* (sign) (e) *Sorry, I can't come tonight. I ___ work late.* (f) *You'll ___ book early if you want a good seat.*
::: solution
(a) *must* (or *have to*) — the obligation comes from the speaker's own sense of what is right; a British speaker would probably say *must*.

(b) *have to* — the obligation comes from the law; the speaker is reporting a rule, not imposing it.

(c) *had to* — past: *must* has no past form.

(d) *must* — the language of written rules.

(e) *have to* (or *'ve got to*) — the obligation comes from outside (the job); this is also the natural spoken form in American English.

(f) *have to* — after *will*, only *have to* is possible.
:::
:::

## No obligation, and prohibition

::: theorem *Don't have to*, *don't need to*, *needn't* and *mustn't* {#rule-no-obligation}
1. ***Don't have to***, ***don't need to*** and ***needn't*** all mean that something is **not necessary**. *Don't have to* and *don't need to* are used in both varieties; ***needn't*** is mainly **British** and slightly formal: *You **needn't** bring anything.* (American English: *You **don't have to** / **don't need to** bring anything.*)
2. ***Mustn't*** (*must not*) means that something is **necessary not to do**: prohibition. In British English it is common in speech and writing: *You **mustn't** tell anyone.* In American English *must not* is mainly written (rules, instructions), and in speech prohibition is usually ***can't***, ***don't***, ***aren't allowed to*** or ***are not supposed to***: *You **can't** park here.*
3. *Need* as a modal (*needn't*, *Need I…?*) is used almost only in **negatives and questions**; in affirmative statements, *need to* is the normal form: ✗ *You need come.* ✓ *You **need to** come.*
:::

::: proof
The meanings follow from the scope of the negation ([[modality/what-modals-do#rule-negation]]): *needn't* and *don't have to* negate the necessity, while *mustn't* negates the action. Rules 1 and 2 on variety are given in Swan (2016) and in the *Longman Grammar* (Biber et al. 1999), which found modal *need* largely restricted to British English and mostly to negative contexts. Leech et al. (2009) found modal *need* (*needn't*) declining in written English, while *need to* rose strongly in the same period, especially in American English. *Need to* has a slightly different flavour from *have to*: it often presents an action as necessary for the hearer's own benefit or for a practical goal (*You need to drink more water*), and in workplaces it is often a polite way of giving an order (*We need to get this finished by Friday*).
:::

::: example Reading the notices {#ex-notices}
Go back to the notices and to Ellie's advice at the start of the chapter. For each modal form, say (i) how strong it is, (ii) where the obligation comes from, and (iii) why that form suits the text.
::: solution
**Notices.** *must register* — strong obligation imposed by the university; *must* is the normal word for written rules. *don't have to buy* — no obligation; the notice tells students they have a choice. *should contact* — advice, medium strength; the university recommends but does not require. *must be worn*, *must not be brought* — strong obligation and prohibition, in the passive because the rule is about coats and food, not about a particular person.

**Ellie.** *'ve got to register* — strong obligation from outside (the university), in informal British speech. *'d better do yours* — strong advice about a particular situation, with a hidden warning (you'll forget, or miss the deadline). *need to get a library card* — practical necessity: Lan needs one to use the library. *ought to go to the welcome party* — advice, medium strength, friendly; Ellie could equally have said *should*.

The contrast shows register at work: the notices use *must*, the passive and impersonal subjects; the flatmate uses *have got to*, *had better* and *need to*, with *you* and *I*.
:::
:::

The past of these negatives raises one more distinction — between *didn't need to* (it was not necessary, whether or not it was done) and *needn't have* (it was done, but it was not necessary). That belongs to modal perfects and is treated in [[modality/past-modals]].

::: quiz
*Must I fill in this form?* — *No, you ___.* Which answer means ‘it isn't necessary’?
- [ ] *mustn't*
- [x] *needn't*
- [ ] *shouldn't*
- [ ] *can't*
::: solution
*Needn't* (or *don't have to*) denies the necessity. *Mustn't* and *can't* would forbid filling in the form, and *shouldn't* would advise against it.
:::

## Advice and recommendation: *should*, *ought to* and *had better*

::: theorem *Should* and *ought to* {#rule-should-ought}
1. ***Should*** and ***ought to*** express **advice**, **recommendation** and what is **morally right** or **expected**: *You **should** see a doctor.* *We **ought to** tell her the truth.* *Children **should** be in bed by nine.* They are weaker than *must* and *have to*: the speaker recommends the action but does not make it compulsory.
2. *Should* and *ought to* mean almost the same, but ***should*** is much more frequent, especially in American English. *Ought to* is rare in questions and negatives; where it is used, the forms are *ought not to* / *oughtn't to* (BrE) and, in questions, BrE ***Ought** we **to**…?* — but *Should we…?* is far more usual.
3. To give negative advice, English often prefers ***I don't think you should…*** to *I think you shouldn't…*, as with other verbs of opinion.
4. Neither has a past form for past obligation; for advice that was not followed use ***should have*** / ***ought to have*** + past participle ([[modality/past-modals]]).
:::

::: proof
Swan (2016), Quirk et al. (1985) and Huddleston & Pullum (2002) treat *should* and *ought to* as near-synonyms. Some speakers feel *ought to* as slightly more objective — based on duty or rules rather than on the speaker's own opinion — but the difference is small and not consistent. The frequency difference is large: Biber et al. (1999) found *should* many times more common than *ought to*, and Leech et al. (2009) found *ought to* among the modals that declined most clearly in written English between 1961 and the early 1990s. A learner can safely use *should* everywhere and recognise *ought to* when reading.
:::

::: theorem *Had better* {#rule-had-better}
1. ***Had better*** + bare infinitive gives **strong advice about a particular situation**, usually with an implied warning that something bad will happen otherwise: *You'**d better** leave now, or you'll miss the train.* *We'**d better not** tell Mum.*
2. It refers to the **present or the future**, not the past, although it is built with *had*: *You'd better hurry* = now.
3. It is usually contracted (*you'd better*), and in casual speech the *'d* may disappear (*You better hurry*), though this is not written in standard English.
4. It is not used for **general** advice or rules: ✗ *People had better eat more vegetables.* ✓ *People **should** eat more vegetables.*
5. Because of the warning, *had better* can sound like a threat when said to a superior or a stranger: *You'd better give me a refund* is aggressive. To someone in authority use *should* or a polite suggestion ([[modality/modality-politeness]]).
:::

::: warning *Had better* forms
✗ *You had better to go.* ✓ *You **had better go**.*

✗ *You hadn't better be late.* ✓ *You'**d better not** be late.*

✗ *We had better have called earlier.* (for a past regret) ✓ *We **should have called** earlier.*

✗ *Students had better do homework every day.* (general advice) ✓ *Students **should** do homework every day.*
:::

::: theorem *Be supposed to* {#rule-supposed}
***Be supposed to*** expresses what is **expected** or **required by a rule or arrangement** — often something that does not actually happen: *We'**re supposed to** be there at eight* (that's the arrangement). *You'**re** not **supposed to** park here* (it's against the rules). *The train **was supposed to** arrive at six* (but it didn't). It is very common in conversation, where it often replaces *should* and *mustn't*.
:::

::: example Choosing the right strength {#ex-strength}
Hassan has a job interview tomorrow. His friend gives him advice. Rewrite each idea with the most suitable form: *must*, *have to*, *need to*, *should*, *had better*, *be supposed to*, *don't have to* or *mustn't*. Explain your choice. (a) Arrive on time — the company is strict about it, and late candidates are not interviewed. (b) Wearing a tie is optional; most people there don't. (c) Look at the company's website tonight: a good idea. (d) It's raining, and the bus is often late: leave early or risk missing the interview. (e) The letter says to bring two copies of your passport photo; people often forget. (f) Don't, under any circumstances, criticise your last employer.
::: solution
(a) *You **have to** arrive on time.* — an external rule from the company. (*must* is also possible; *need to* if the friend is stressing the practical necessity.)

(b) *You **don't have to** wear a tie.* — not necessary.

(c) *You **should** look at the company's website tonight.* — advice.

(d) *You'**d better** leave early.* — advice about a particular situation, with a warning.

(e) *You'**re supposed to** bring two copies of your passport photo.* — what the arrangement requires; *supposed to* fits because people often don't do it.

(f) *You **mustn't** criticise your last employer.* — a strong prohibition from the speaker (BrE). An American speaker might say *Don't ever criticise…* or *You can't criticise…*.
:::
:::

## A scale of strength

The forms of this chapter can be placed on a rough scale, from strongest to weakest:

1. *must*, *have to*, *have got to* — strong obligation (and *mustn't* — strong prohibition)
2. *need to* — necessity, often presented as practical
3. *had better* — strong advice for a particular situation
4. *be supposed to* — what a rule or arrangement expects
5. *should*, *ought to* — advice, what is right
6. *could*, *might* (*You could ask…*, *You might want to…*) — suggestions ([[modality/ability-possibility#rule-hypothetical]])
7. *don't have to*, *needn't*, *don't need to* — no obligation

The scale is only a guide. Intonation and context can strengthen or weaken any form, and the same form can be strong from a teacher and weak from a friend. Huddleston & Pullum (2002) describe *must* and *have to* as "strong" modality and *should* and *ought to* as "medium"; Halliday's "high", "median" and "low" values ([[modality/what-modals-do]]) make a similar division.

::: quiz
Which is the strongest?
- [ ] *You should apply early.*
- [ ] *You could apply early.*
- [x] *You have to apply early.*
- [ ] *You ought to apply early.*
::: solution
*Have to* expresses obligation; *should* and *ought to* are advice; *could* is a suggestion.
:::
:::

## Obligation in formal writing

Formal and official texts express obligation in ways that conversation rarely uses.

::: theorem Obligation in formal and official English {#rule-formal-obligation}
1. **Passive *must*** with an impersonal subject: *All bags **must be** left at the entrance.*
2. **Lexical verbs and adjectives** instead of modals: *Candidates **are required to** bring photo ID.* *Attendance **is compulsory** / **mandatory**.* *Staff **are obliged to** report accidents.* *It **is essential** / **necessary** **that** forms are completed in full.* (See [[modality/modality-without-modals]].)
3. **The mandative subjunctive or *should*** in *that*-clauses after verbs and adjectives of demanding and recommending — *insist*, *demand*, *recommend*, *suggest*, *essential*, *important*: *The committee recommends **that** the policy **be** reviewed* (subjunctive: base form for all persons). *…recommends that the policy **should be** reviewed* (mainly BrE). *…recommends that the policy **is** reviewed* (BrE, informal to neutral). American English strongly prefers the subjunctive; British English uses all three (Quirk et al. 1985; Swan 2016), and corpus studies found the subjunctive becoming more frequent in British writing in the later twentieth century (Leech et al. 2009).
4. ***Shall*** in legal and contractual documents states obligations: *The tenant **shall** pay the rent monthly.* This use is discussed in [[modality/will-would-shall]].
5. ***Be to*** for official instructions: *You **are to** report to Room 4 at nine.* (See [[tense-and-aspect/future-forms#rule-other-future]].)
:::

::: example Rewriting a rule for two audiences {#ex-rewrite}
A company's policy document says: *Employees are required to complete the online safety training within one month of their start date. Failure to do so may result in suspension of system access. Completion of the optional module on fire safety is recommended.* Rewrite it as a short spoken explanation from a manager to a new colleague.
::: solution
A model:

*So, you **have to** do the online safety training in your first month — if you don't, they **might** cut off your access to the system. There's also a fire-safety module. You **don't have to** do it, but you **should**, really — it only takes half an hour.*

Changes: *are required to* → *have to* (spoken obligation); *may result in* → *might* (spoken possibility, with a person as agent: *they might cut off*); *optional* → *don't have to*; *is recommended* → *should*. The spoken version uses personal subjects (*you*, *they*) instead of nouns such as *employees*, *failure* and *completion*.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **必须, 得 and 一定要.** 必须 is the usual equivalent of *must* or *have to* in rules (学生必须穿校服 → *Students must / have to wear a uniform*). Colloquial 得 (děi) is close to *have to* or BrE *have got to*: 我得走了 → *I have to / I've got to go*. 一定要 adds insistence and corresponds to *must* in invitations and strong recommendations: 你一定要来！ → *You must come!*
- **应该 and 要.** 应该 corresponds to *should* or *ought to* (你应该多休息 → *You should rest more*). 要 is very wide: it can mean *need to* (你要小心 → *You need to be careful*, or *You should be careful*), *want* or *will*. When 要 means obligation, decide from the context how strong it is.
- **不用, 不必 and 不能, 不许, 别.** 不用 and 不必 are *don't have to* or *needn't*; 不能, 不许, 不准 and imperative 别 are *mustn't*, *can't* or *don't*. The most frequent learner error is to translate 不必 as ✗ *mustn't*.
- **最好 and *had better*.** 你最好早点出发 is often translated *You'd better leave early*, and in many contexts that fits. But 最好 is softer and more general than *had better*, which carries a warning and can sound pushy; when the speaker is simply giving friendly advice, *You should leave early* or *It's best to leave early* is often closer.
- **No form for past obligation in the modal.** Chinese uses the same 必须 or 得 for any time, with time words to show the past (昨天我得加班). English must change the verb: *Yesterday I **had to** work late*, never ✗ *Yesterday I must work late.*
:::

::: history
Several of the obligation verbs began with quite different meanings. *Must* comes from Old English *mōste*, the past tense of *mōtan* ‘be allowed to’, which in the course of Middle English came to mean ‘be obliged to’ and lost its past meaning. *Ought* is the old past tense of *owe* (Old English *āgan* ‘own, possess’, past *āhte*): to say what one ought to do was originally to say what one owed. *Have to* developed from sentences in which *have* meant possession and was followed by an object and an infinitive — *I have a letter to write* — and was gradually reinterpreted as *I have to write a letter*; standard accounts of this kind of change are in Hopper and Traugott's *Grammaticalization* (2nd edition, 2003), and Manfred Krug's *Emerging English Modals* (2000) traces the rise of *have got to* and *gotta*. The most recent chapter of the story is the twentieth-century decline of *must* in written English and the rise of *have to* and *need to* measured by Leech et al. (2009).
:::

## Where this leads

The epistemic uses of *must*, *should* and *have to* (*It must be true*, *It has to be a mistake*) are the subject of [[modality/deduction]]. Past obligation and regrets — *should have*, *needn't have*, *didn't need to* — are in [[modality/past-modals]], and *shall* in rules and offers in [[modality/will-would-shall]]. Softer ways to tell someone what to do — *You might want to…*, *It might be worth…* — are in [[modality/modality-politeness]], and the cultural side of directness in [[pragmatics/directness-across-cultures]].

::: summary
- *Must*, *have to* and *have got to* express strong obligation; BrE tends to use *must* for the speaker's own judgement and *have to* for outside rules; AmE uses *have to* for both; written rules use *must* ([[#rule-must-have-to]]).
- *Must* has no past or future: use *had to* and *will have to*. *Have got to* is informal, mainly British and present only ([[#rule-have-got-to]]).
- *Don't have to*, *don't need to* and BrE *needn't* mean ‘not necessary’; *mustn't* means ‘necessary not to’; in American speech prohibition is usually *can't* or *not allowed to* ([[#rule-no-obligation]]).
- *Should* and the less common *ought to* give advice; *had better* gives strong advice for a particular situation, with a warning ([[#rule-should-ought]], [[#rule-had-better]]).
- *Be supposed to* expresses what is expected or required, often something that does not happen ([[#rule-supposed]]).
- Formal English uses passive *must*, *be required to*, *compulsory*, the mandative subjunctive and legal *shall* ([[#rule-formal-obligation]]).
- Corpus studies show *must*, *ought to* and *needn't* declining and *have to* and *need to* rising in written English since 1961 (Leech et al. 2009).
:::

Neutral: *I have to go.* BrE informal: *I've got to go.* (c) Formal written rules (both varieties). Spoken: *You have to send your application by email.* (d) Informal British. Both varieties: *Do you have to work tomorrow?* (e) Formal, both varieties, more typical of AmE. BrE alternative: *…recommends that he should be appointed.*
:::

::: exercise Correct the mistakes {level=2}
(a) *I must to finish this today.* (b) *Last year we must take five exams.* (c) *You hadn't better forget.* (d) *Do we must bring our own food?* (e) *You don't need bring anything.* (f) *She has got to leave early yesterday.*
::: solution
(a) *I must finish this today.* (b) *Last year we had to take five exams.* (c) *You'd better not forget.* (d) *Do we have to bring our own food?* (or formal *Must we bring…?*) (e) *You don't need to bring anything.* / *You needn't bring anything.* (f) *She had to leave early yesterday.*
:::

::: exercise *Supposed to* {level=2}
Rewrite with *be supposed to*. (a) *The rule is that you leave your shoes at the door, but nobody does.* (b) *The meeting was planned for 3 p.m., but it started at 3.40.* (c) *It's against the rules to eat in the library.* (d) *Everyone says this film is very good.*
::: solution
(a) *You're supposed to leave your shoes at the door, but nobody does.* (b) *The meeting was supposed to start at 3 p.m., but it started at 3.40.* (c) *You're not supposed to eat in the library.* (d) *This film is supposed to be very good.* (Here *be supposed to* means ‘is said to’, a reporting use, not obligation.)
:::

::: exercise Translate {level=3}
Translate into natural English, choosing the strength and register carefully. (a) 我得去接孩子了。 (b) 你不必为这件事道歉。 (c) 所有乘客必须系好安全带。 (d) 你最好别告诉他，不然他会生气的。 (e) 按规定我们应该六点到，但大家都迟到了。 (f) 你一定要尝尝这家的饺子！
::: hint
(e) describes an arrangement that was not kept; (f) is enthusiasm, not a real obligation.
:::
::: solution
(a) *I have to / I've got to go and pick up the kids.* (b) *You don't have to / needn't apologise for this.* (c) *All passengers must fasten their seatbelts.* (written notice; spoken: *You have to wear your seatbelt*) (d) *You'd better not tell him, or he'll be angry.* (*had better* fits because there is a warning) (e) *We were supposed to be there at six, but everyone was late.* (f) *You must / You have to try the dumplings here!*

::: exercise Write a set of guidelines {level=3}
Write guidelines (100–150 words) for new members of a club, society or team you know. Write them twice: first as a formal notice, then as a friendly spoken explanation. Use at least five different forms from this chapter.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

*Formal:* *Members **must** sign the attendance sheet at every session. Equipment **must** be returned to the store room after use. Members **are required to** pay the annual fee by 31 October. Guests **may** attend one session free of charge. Members **are not required to** take part in competitions, but are encouraged to do so.*

*Spoken:* *OK, so you **have to** sign in every time — that's the main thing. And you'**re supposed to** put the rackets back afterwards, though people forget. You'**ll need to** pay the fee by the end of October, so you'**d better** do it soon. You **can** bring a friend once for free. You **don't have to** play in matches, but you **should** — they're great fun.*

Check: formal *must*, passive *must be returned*, *are required to*, *may*, *are not required to*; spoken *have to*, *be supposed to*, *need to*, *had better*, *can*, *don't have to*, *should*.
