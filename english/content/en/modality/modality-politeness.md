Zhang Wei, a first-year master's student in Manchester, sends this email to a lecturer he has never met:

*Dear Professor Brown,*

*I want you to send me the slides of last week's lecture. I was ill and could not attend. You must reply before Friday because I have an assignment.*

*Zhang Wei*

Every sentence is grammatically correct, and the message is clear. Yet Professor Brown is likely to find it rude, and Zhang Wei — who was being direct and clear, as he would be in many Chinese emails to a teacher, where the polite pronoun 您 and a respectful greeting already show respect — would be surprised to learn this. Compare a version that a British or American lecturer would probably receive well:

*Dear Professor Brown,*

*I'm sorry to trouble you. I was ill last week and missed your lecture on Tuesday. **Would** it be possible for me to have a copy of the slides? I have an assignment due next week, so I **would** be very grateful if you **could** send them by Friday, if that's convenient.*

*Best wishes,*
*Zhang Wei*

The request is the same. What has changed is mostly **modality**: *I want you to* has become *Would it be possible…?*, *You must reply* has become *I would be very grateful if you could…*. The modal verbs *would* and *could*, together with conditionals and other softeners, put distance between the request and the person who receives it, and that distance is what English speakers hear as politeness. This chapter explains why, and shows how to use modality to make requests, suggestions, offers, refusals and disagreement as direct as the situation needs.

The everyday request and offer forms are introduced in [[everyday-english/requests-offers]], and *would*, *could* and *shall* in [[modality/will-would-shall]]. The theory of politeness — face, and the strategies speakers use to protect it — is the subject of [[pragmatics/politeness-face]]; here we focus on the grammar that carries it.

## Why modality makes language polite

::: definition Face and imposition {#def-face}
**Face** is the public self-image that every person wants to keep (Goffman 1967; Brown & Levinson 1987). Brown and Levinson distinguish **positive face**, the wish to be liked and approved of, and **negative face**, the wish to be free to act without being imposed on. A request, an order, advice or a suggestion **imposes** on the hearer's negative face, because it asks them to do something. Politeness is the set of ways in which speakers reduce such threats.
:::

::: definition Conventional indirectness {#def-indirect}
A request is **conventionally indirect** when its literal form asks a question about the hearer's ability or willingness, or about whether something is possible, but everyone understands it as a request: ***Can** you pass the salt?* ***Could** you open the window?* ***Would** you mind waiting?* In a large comparative study of requests in several languages, Blum-Kulka, House and Kasper (1989) found this kind of request — they called it a "query preparatory" — to be the most frequent strategy in English.
:::

::: theorem Three kinds of distance {#rule-distance}
English makes a request or suggestion more polite by adding **distance** of three kinds, often together:

1. **Modal distance** — a modal verb instead of a statement or imperative, and the "remote" form instead of the present form: *Open the window* → ***Can** you open the window?* → ***Could** you open the window?* *Will you…?* → ***Would** you…?*
2. **Hypothetical distance** — a conditional or hypothetical framing: ***Would** you mind **if** I opened the window?* *I'**d be grateful if** you **could**…* *It **would** help **if**…*
3. **Past-tense distance** — a past tense that does not refer to the past: *I **was wondering** if you could help me.* *I **wanted** to ask you something.* ***Did** you want a drink?*

Each kind of distance makes the request easier to refuse, because it presents it as hypothetical, tentative or already in the past.
:::

::: proof
The explanation is the same one that accounts for the "past" modals in general ([[modality/what-modals-do]]): *could*, *would* and *might* are remote forms, and remoteness from the present can be remoteness in time, in reality or in social terms. *Can you help?* asks about the hearer's real ability now; *Could you help?* asks about a hypothetical ability, and so leaves the hearer more room to say no without contradicting a fact. The same remote past tense appears in unreal conditionals and wishes ([[complex-sentences/unreal-past]]): *I wish I knew*, *If I had time*. Swan (2016) and Leech (*The Pragmatics of Politeness*, 2014) describe these as ways of being "tentative"; Brown and Levinson (1987) list hedging, questioning instead of asserting, and being "pessimistic" about whether the hearer will comply among the strategies of negative politeness.
:::

## Requests

The forms of request can be placed on a scale from most direct to least direct. Blum-Kulka et al. (1989) distinguish nine strategies; the main ones in English, with the modality that carries them, are:

| strategy | example | modality |
|---|---|---|
| imperative | *Send me the file.* | none |
| obligation statement | *You must / have to / need to send me the file.* | deontic necessity |
| want statement | *I want you to send me the file.* / *I'd like you to send me the file.* | volition (*'d like* softer) |
| suggestion formula | *How about sending me the file?* / *Why don't you send me the file?* | — |
| query about ability or willingness | ***Can** / **Could** / **Will** / **Would** you send me the file?* | dynamic modality |
| query about possibility or permission | ***Would** it be possible to send me the file?* / ***Could** I have the file?* | possibility, permission |
| conditional | *I'**d** be grateful if you **could** send me the file.* | hypothetical |
| hint | *I don't seem to have the file.* | none (the request is not said) |

::: theorem Choosing the level of directness {#rule-directness}
How direct a request should be depends on three things (Brown & Levinson 1987):

1. **Power** — is the hearer in a position of authority over you (a teacher, a manager, an official)? More power on the hearer's side calls for more indirectness.
2. **Distance** — how well do you know each other? Strangers and new contacts need more indirectness than friends and family.
3. **Imposition** — how big is the request? *Could you pass the salt?* is small; *I was wondering if you could lend me £500* is large.

As a rule of thumb, in British and American English:

- with **friends and family** and for small things: *Can you…?*, imperatives with *please* or a tag (*Pass the salt, would you?*);
- with **colleagues, strangers, service staff**: *Could you…?*, *Can I have…?*, *Would you mind…?*;
- with **people in authority**, for **larger requests**, and in **formal emails**: *Would it be possible…?*, *I was wondering if you could…*, *I would be grateful if you could…*, *Could you possibly…?*
:::

::: proof
Brown and Levinson's three variables explain most of the choices native speakers make, although each culture, and each family and workplace, weighs them differently ([[pragmatics/directness-across-cultures]]). The scale is not a ladder of "more polite is better": an over-polite request to a close friend (*I was wondering whether you might possibly pass the salt*) sounds cold, sarcastic or comic, because it treats the friend as a stranger. The aim is a fit between the form and the relationship.
:::

::: warning *I want*, *you must* and *please* + imperative in requests
✗ *I want you to check my essay.* (to a teacher) ✓ ***Would** you be able to look at my essay?* / ***Could** you possibly check my essay?*

✗ *You must reply before Friday.* (to someone you are asking a favour) ✓ *I'**d** be grateful if you **could** reply by Friday.*

? *Please send me the form.* (in an email to a stranger) ✓ ***Could** you please send me the form?*

*Please* + imperative is fine in instructions and in requests the hearer is expected to fulfil (*Please sign here*, *Please find attached…*), but on its own it does not make a favour polite. English relies more on modal questions than on *please*.
:::

::: example Rewriting an email {#ex-email}
Rewrite this email from a student to a university office so that it is polite but still clear. Explain each change.

*Hello. I need a letter that says I am a student here, for my visa. Give it to me by Monday. Also, tell me where the office is. Thanks.*
::: solution
A model:

*Dear Sir or Madam,*

*I am a master's student in the School of Engineering (student number 2604117). **Would it be possible** to have a letter confirming that I am registered as a student here? I need it for my visa application. **I would be grateful if** it **could** be ready by Monday, as my appointment is on Tuesday. **Could you also** let me know where I can collect it?*

*Thank you for your help.*

*Best wishes,*
*Li Ming*

Changes: *I need a letter* → *Would it be possible to have a letter…?* (a query about possibility instead of a want statement; *I need it* keeps the reason). *Give it to me by Monday* → *I would be grateful if it could be ready by Monday* (hypothetical distance; the passive avoids telling the reader what to do; a reason is given). *tell me where…* → *Could you also let me know…?* (a modal question). The greeting and closing are formal, and identifying details make the request easy to fulfil — which is itself polite.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which request is most suitable for asking your manager, whom you don't know well, for a day off next week?
- [ ] *Give me Friday off.*
- [ ] *I want Friday off.*
- [ ] *Can I have Friday off?*
- [x] *Would it be possible for me to take Friday off?*
::: solution
The hearer has power and the request is fairly large, so a query about possibility with *would* is the best fit. *Can I…?* is acceptable in a relaxed workplace but more casual. The imperative and the want statement are too direct.

## Suggestions and advice

Advice imposes too: it implies that the hearer's plan could be better. English softens it with the same tools.

::: theorem Softening suggestions and advice {#rule-suggestions}
From more direct to less direct:

1. *You **must** / **should** / **need to**…* — deontic: an order or firm advice.
2. *You'**d better**…* — strong, with a warning; can sound like a threat ([[modality/obligation-necessity#rule-had-better]]).
3. ***Maybe** / **Perhaps** you **should**…* — advice softened by an adverb.
4. *If I were you, I'**d**…* — advice framed as the speaker's own hypothetical choice.
5. *You **could**… / You **might** try…* — a suggestion: one possibility among others.
6. *You **might want to**… / You **may want to**…* — very polite; the decision is left to the hearer.
7. *It **might be** worth* + *-ing* / *Have you thought about* + *-ing*? / ***Would** it help to…?* — indirect: the suggestion is presented as a question or an evaluation.

In feedback from teachers, editors and managers, forms 5–7 are common, and they are not weak: *You might want to check your figures* often means ‘your figures are wrong’ (Swan 2016).
:::

::: example Softening feedback {#ex-feedback}
A teaching assistant has written these comments on a student's essay. Rewrite them so that they are clear but not harsh, using at least four different forms from [[#rule-suggestions]].

(a) *Your introduction is too long. Cut it.* (b) *You must give a source for this figure.* (c) *This paragraph is confusing. Rewrite it.* (d) *Use more recent studies.* (e) *Your conclusion repeats the introduction.*
::: solution
Model answers:

(a) *Your introduction is quite long — **you might want to** shorten it so that you get to your argument sooner.*

(b) ***Could** you add a source for this figure?* (A source is required, so a direct form — *You need to give a source for this figure* — is also acceptable: the point is not optional.)

(c) *I found this paragraph a little hard to follow. **It might be worth** splitting it into two.*

(d) ***Have you thought about** including some more recent studies? Several have appeared since 2020.*

(e) ***Perhaps you could** use the conclusion to say what your findings mean, rather than repeating the introduction.*

Note that the softened versions still say exactly what is wrong; they add a reason or a concrete suggestion. Hedging the evaluation (*a little hard to follow*, *quite long*) and speaking for oneself (*I found…*) also reduce the threat ([[modality/hedging-boosting]]).
:::
:::

## Offers, invitations, refusals and disagreement

::: theorem Modality in offers, invitations and refusals {#rule-offers}
1. **Offers**: ***Would** you like…?* (neutral and polite), ***Can** I get you…?* (informal, service), ***Shall** I…?* (BrE), *I **could**… if you like* (tentative), *Let me…* (confident, friendly). (See [[modality/will-would-shall#rule-shall]].)
2. **Invitations**: ***Would** you like to…?*, *We **were wondering** if you'd like to…*, and warm insistence with *must*: *You **must** come and stay with us!* (not an order: [[modality/obligation-necessity#rule-must-have-to]]).
3. **Refusals** usually avoid a bare *no*. They combine an expression of regret or appreciation, a reason, and modal distance: *I'm afraid I **won't be able to** make it.* *I'**d love to**, but I **can't** this week.* *I **might not** have time, I'm afraid.* *Won't be able to* is softer than *can't*, because it presents the refusal as caused by circumstances rather than chosen.
4. **Disagreement** is softened with tentative *would*, negative questions and hedges: *I'**m not sure** I'**d** agree.* *I **would have thought** it was the other way round.* ***Couldn't** it also be argued that…?* ***Wouldn't** it be better to…?*
:::

::: example Saying no politely {#ex-refusal}
Your colleague Pedro asks: *Could you cover my shift on Saturday?* You can't, because you are going to your cousin's wedding. Write three refusals: (a) to Pedro, a close friend, (b) to Pedro, a colleague you don't know well, (c) to your manager, who asks the same question. Explain the differences.
::: solution
(a) *Sorry, mate, I can't — it's my cousin's wedding. Ask Jun?* — Informal; a short apology, *can't*, a reason, and a helpful suggestion. Directness is fine between friends.

(b) *Ah, I'm really sorry, I won't be able to — I've got a family wedding on Saturday. I hope you find someone.* — *won't be able to* presents the refusal as caused by circumstances; the apology is stronger; a good wish replaces a suggestion.

(c) *I'm afraid I won't be able to this Saturday — I'm going to my cousin's wedding, which was arranged months ago. I'd be happy to help another weekend if that would be useful.* — More distance (*I'm afraid*), a fuller reason that shows the refusal is not personal, and a conditional offer with *would* to protect the relationship.

In all three, the speaker says no clearly. Politeness is in the framing, not in leaving the hearer unsure whether the answer is yes or no.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which response to an invitation is a polite refusal?
- [ ] *No, I don't want to.*
- [ ] *Maybe.*
- [x] *I'd love to, but I'm afraid I won't be able to make it on Saturday.*
- [ ] *I must not come.*
::: solution
The third combines appreciation (*I'd love to*), a softener (*I'm afraid*) and modal distance (*won't be able to*), and is still clear. *Maybe* leaves the host uncertain; *I don't want to* is blunt; *I must not come* sounds as if the speaker is forbidden.

## When politeness misfires

Politeness forms are conventions, and conventions can be bent.

- **Over-politeness.** A very indirect form for a small request between equals can sound sarcastic or cold: *Would you be so kind as to pass the salt?* at a family dinner is a joke — or a complaint.
- **Polite forms used as orders.** In some contexts *Will you…?* and *Would you…?* are orders with a polite surface, especially with falling intonation and stress: *Would you **please** be quiet!* *Will you stop doing that!*
- **Pointed suggestions.** *You might want to…* from a superior is often an instruction. A learner who hears it as optional advice may miss the message.
- **Insufficient distance.** As in Zhang Wei's email, a correct but direct form can be heard as a demand. Because the grammar is correct, native speakers may judge the writer, not the language — which is why these conventions are worth learning explicitly.

::: example What went wrong? {#ex-misfire}
Each exchange caused a small misunderstanding. Explain what happened in terms of modality and directness, and suggest a better choice.

(a) A new employee, Daniela, receives a comment from her manager on a draft: *You might want to have another look at the budget section.* She decides the comment is optional and leaves the section unchanged. The manager is annoyed.

(b) At a family dinner, Tariq says to his brother: *I wonder if I might trouble you for the salt.* His brother asks him what is wrong.

(c) In a shop, Mei says to the assistant: *I want this one in a bigger size.* The assistant looks surprised.
::: solution
(a) *You might want to…* has the form of a tentative suggestion, but from a manager about a draft it is a conventional, polite way of giving an instruction: the budget section has a problem. Daniela read the modality literally. The manager could have been clearer (*Could you revise the budget section? The totals don't match*), and Daniela could have asked (*Is there something specific you'd like me to change?*).

(b) The request uses modal and hypothetical distance (*I wonder if I might trouble you*) suitable for a stranger or a formal occasion. Between brothers, for a tiny imposition, this much distance signals that something is wrong — irritation, sarcasm or a joke. *Pass the salt, would you?* or *Can you pass the salt?* fits the relationship.

(c) *I want* is a want statement, among the most direct request forms after the imperative. In service encounters English speakers usually use *Could I have…?*, *Have you got this in a bigger size?* or *Do you have…?*. The assistant was not offended so much as surprised by the bluntness. (This is a common transfer from 我要这个, which is perfectly polite in a Chinese shop.)
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **您 and English *you*.** Chinese can show respect with the pronoun 您 and with titles (张老师, 王经理). English has one pronoun, *you*, for everyone, and expresses much of the same respect through modality, conditionals and indirect questions. An English email that is "correct" but contains no modal distance can therefore seem less polite than the writer intended.
- **请 is not *please*.** 请 + verb (请坐, 请发给我) is a polite way to invite or ask in Chinese. Its word-for-word English equivalent, *please* + imperative, is polite enough for instructions (*Please take a seat*) but often too direct for a favour (*Please send me the file* → *Could you send me the file?*).
- **麻烦你, 能不能, 可不可以.** 麻烦你帮我看一下 → *Could you have a look at this for me?* / *Sorry to bother you, but could you…?* 能不能 and 可不可以 correspond well to *Could you…?* and *Can I…?*.
- **吧 and 一下.** Chinese softens requests with particles and diminutive phrases: 你帮我看看吧, 等一下. English uses *just*, *a moment*, *a bit* and tags: *Could you just have a look?* *Wait a moment, would you?*
- **我想 and *I want*.** 我想请你帮个忙 is polite in Chinese, but *I want you to help me* is not in English. Use *I'd like to ask you a favour* or *Could I ask you a favour?*
- **Norms vary.** What counts as polite differs between regions, generations, workplaces and families in China and in English-speaking countries; Gu (1990) argued that Brown and Levinson's idea of negative face does not fit traditional Chinese notions of 礼貌. The forms in this chapter describe common conventions in British and American English, not a ranking of cultures.
:::

::: history
Politeness became a central topic of linguistics in the 1970s. The sociologist Erving Goffman's essay "On face-work" (reprinted in *Interaction Ritual*, 1967) introduced the idea of face. Robin Lakoff's paper "The logic of politeness" (1973) proposed rules such as "don't impose" and "give options", and Geoffrey Leech's *Principles of Pragmatics* (1983) set out maxims of politeness such as tact and generosity. Penelope Brown and Stephen Levinson's essay of 1978, reissued as *Politeness: Some Universals in Language Usage* (1987), explained indirect requests as strategies for protecting face and claimed that the strategies are found in all languages. Their claim of universality was challenged by researchers working on Chinese and Japanese, among them Gu Yueguo (1990). The Cross-Cultural Speech Act Realization Project, published as *Cross-Cultural Pragmatics: Requests and Apologies* (Blum-Kulka, House and Kasper, 1989), compared requests in several languages and showed how central the modal question (*Could you…?*) is to English.
:::

Polite disagreement in seminars and meetings is practised in [[presentations-and-discussion/seminars-discussion]] and [[presentations-and-discussion/meetings-negotiation]].

::: summary
- Requests, advice and suggestions threaten the hearer's negative face; English reduces the threat mainly with modality ([[#def-face]]).
- Conventionally indirect requests ask about ability, willingness or possibility (*Could you…?*, *Would you mind…?*) and are the commonest English request form ([[#def-indirect]]).
- Distance comes from modals (*could*, *would*), conditionals (*I'd be grateful if…*) and the remote past (*I was wondering…*) ([[#rule-distance]]).
- Choose the level of directness by power, social distance and the size of the imposition; more indirect is not always better ([[#rule-directness]]).
- Soften suggestions with *could*, *might want to*, *it might be worth*, *if I were you*; in feedback these often carry firm messages ([[#rule-suggestions]]).
- Refuse with regret, a reason and *won't be able to*; disagree with *I'm not sure I'd agree* or *Wouldn't it…?* ([[#rule-offers]]).
- English *you* has no polite form like 您; *please* + imperative is less polite than 请 + verb suggests. Politeness norms vary within every culture.
:::

*have you got a minute?* — a preliminary question that checks availability before asking (reduces imposition). *I was wondering if you could* — past-tense and modal distance. *I'd like to, but* — appreciation before a refusal (*would*). *I'm afraid* — regret. *a bit* (twice) — minimises the problem. *Would tomorrow be OK?* — a counter-offer as a hypothetical question. *might be a bit late* — a hedged disagreement (*might*, *a bit*). *Could you possibly* — modal distance plus *possibly*. *half an hour* — a specific, limited request (reduces imposition). *I suppose I could* — tentative agreement (*suppose*, *could*). *Shall we say…?* — a suggestion as a question (BrE).

Note that Ms Park is Kwame's manager, but she still uses indirect forms; politeness from people with power is common in British and American workplaces, and it does not make the request optional.
