Here is part of a weather forecast and part of a news report.

*Heavy rain is **likely** in the north tonight, and there is a **strong chance** of thunderstorms. Temperatures will **probably** stay below ten degrees.*

*The minister **appears** to have resigned. According to colleagues, she was **reportedly** unhappy with the new policy, although she has **apparently** made no public statement.*

There is only one modal verb in these five sentences (*will*). Yet every one of them expresses modality: *likely*, *a strong chance*, *probably*, *appears*, *reportedly* and *apparently* all tell us how sure the writer is, or where the information comes from. Modal verbs are the grammatical centre of modality, but in real texts they share the work with adverbs, adjectives, nouns and lexical verbs. In academic and journalistic writing these other forms are often more frequent than the modals, because they allow a writer to state confidence precisely, to put it in a prominent position, or to report someone else's view.

This chapter surveys these "lexical" ways of expressing modality, their grammar — where learners make most of their mistakes — and how to choose between them. It builds on the scale of certainty in [[modality/deduction]]; the next chapter, [[modality/hedging-boosting]], puts them to work in academic writing.

## Four word classes

::: definition Lexical modality {#def-lexical-modality}
**Lexical modality** is modal meaning expressed by ordinary words rather than by modal auxiliaries: **adverbs** (*certainly*, *probably*, *perhaps*), **adjectives** (*possible*, *likely*, *necessary*), **nouns** (*possibility*, *chance*, *need*) and **lexical verbs** (*seem*, *appear*, *tend*, *suggest*, *require*). Like the modals, they express both epistemic meanings (how likely something is) and deontic meanings (what is necessary or allowed).
:::

| meaning | adverb | adjective | noun | verb |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| certain | *certainly*, *definitely*, *undoubtedly* | *certain*, *sure*, *bound to* | *certainty*, *no doubt* | *know*, *show*, *prove* |
| probable | *probably*, *presumably*, *likely* (AmE) | *likely*, *probable* | *likelihood*, *probability*, *a good chance* | *expect*, *think*, *believe* |
| possible | *possibly*, *perhaps*, *maybe* | *possible* | *possibility*, *chance* | *suggest*, *wonder whether* |
| seeming, evidence | *apparently*, *evidently*, *seemingly* | *apparent*, *evident* | *appearance*, *evidence* | *seem*, *appear*, *look*, *indicate* |
| generally true | *usually*, *generally*, *typically* | *typical* | *tendency* | *tend to* |
| necessary | *necessarily* | *necessary*, *essential*, *compulsory* | *need*, *necessity*, *obligation* | *require*, *need*, *oblige* |
| allowed | — | *allowed*, *permitted* | *permission* | *allow*, *permit*, *let* |

## Adverbs

::: theorem Modal adverbs and their position {#rule-adverbs}
1. Most modal adverbs (*probably*, *certainly*, *definitely*, *possibly*, *presumably*, *clearly*) usually go in **mid-position**: after *be* or the first auxiliary, and before a main verb: *She is **probably** right.* *They will **certainly** object.* *He **probably** knows.*
2. With a **negative auxiliary**, they usually come **before** it: *She **probably won't** come.* *He **certainly doesn't** want to.* (✗ *She won't probably come* is much less natural.) *Definitely* can also follow the negative, but then the meaning changes (see below).
3. ***Perhaps*** and ***maybe*** usually go at the **front**: ***Perhaps** she's right.* ***Maybe** they missed the bus.* *Maybe* is typical of conversation; *perhaps* is used in speech and writing and is the usual choice in formal and academic texts.
4. Any of them can go at the front for emphasis, followed by a comma in writing: ***Clearly**, the plan has failed.*
5. ***Likely*** as an adverb on its own (*She'll **likely** be late*) is mainly **American**; British English prefers *probably*, *very likely* or *most likely* (Swan 2016).
:::

::: proof
The positions follow the general rule for adverbs that comment on the whole clause (Quirk et al. 1985 call them disjuncts; Biber et al. 1999 call them stance adverbials). In mid-position they sit next to the auxiliary, where English marks tense, negation and modality, so they naturally go before a negative auxiliary: the speaker's judgement (*probably*) applies to the whole negative statement (*she won't come*), not the other way round. The difference in rule 2 is a difference of scope: *I definitely won't go* means ‘I'm certain I won't go’, while *I won't definitely go* means ‘it is not certain that I'll go’.
:::

Two adverbs need special care.

***Surely*** does not simply mean *certainly*. It expresses the speaker's belief and invites agreement, and often shows surprise that something might not be true: ***Surely** you're not leaving already?* (I can't believe you are.) *There must be a mistake, **surely**.* For plain certainty use *certainly* or *definitely* (Swan 2016).

***Apparently***, ***evidently***, ***reportedly*** and ***allegedly*** are **evidential**: they show where the information comes from. *Apparently* usually means ‘I have heard / it seems’, not ‘obviously’: ***Apparently** the shop is closing* (someone told me). *Allegedly* and *reportedly* are typical of news, where the writer does not take responsibility for a claim: *He **allegedly** took the money.*

::: warning *Surely*, *apparently* and adverb position
✗ *I will surely pass the exam — I've worked very hard.* ✓ *I'**ll definitely** / **I'm sure I'll** pass the exam.*

✗ *Apparently, the earth goes round the sun.* (as if this were a rumour) ✓ ***Obviously** / **Of course**, the earth goes round the sun.*

✗ *He doesn't probably know.* ✓ *He **probably doesn't** know.*

*Surely* asks for agreement or shows surprise; *apparently* reports what you have heard.
:::

::: theorem Modal verbs with adverbs {#rule-harmonic}
A modal verb and a modal adverb of the **same strength** reinforce each other: *He **may possibly** be there.* *She **must certainly** have known.* *It'**ll probably** rain.* Lyons (1977) called such combinations **harmonic**. Combinations of **different** strength are uncommon and usually sound contradictory (? *It **must possibly** be true*), except where the adverb modifies a different part of the meaning (*You **should certainly** apply* = ‘I certainly recommend that you apply’).
:::

::: example Reading a message for evidence and attitude {#ex-message}
Hu Jing receives this message from a colleague. For each adverb in bold, say whether it expresses the writer's certainty, the source of the information, or a request for agreement, and what Hu Jing can conclude.

*Hi Jing — **apparently** the client meeting has moved to Thursday. It's **probably** because their director is travelling. I'll **definitely** be there, but **perhaps** you could bring the slides? **Surely** they won't change it again!*
::: solution
***apparently*** — source: the writer has heard this from someone else. Hu Jing should perhaps check before changing her plans.

***probably*** — certainty (medium): the writer is guessing the reason.

***definitely*** — certainty (strong): a firm commitment.

***perhaps*** — here it softens a request rather than expressing doubt about a fact (‘could you bring the slides?’). Modality in requests is the subject of [[modality/modality-politeness]].

***Surely*** — a request for agreement mixed with mild exasperation: the writer hopes, but is not sure, that the date will not change again. It does not mean ‘certainly’.
:::
:::

## Adjectives

::: theorem Patterns with modal adjectives {#rule-adjectives}
1. ***It is*** + adjective + ***that***-clause: *It is **possible / likely / probable / certain / unlikely** that the meeting will be cancelled.*
2. Subject + ***be*** + adjective + ***to***-infinitive, only with **likely**, **unlikely**, **certain**, **sure** and **bound**: *The meeting **is likely to** be cancelled.* *She **is sure to** win.* *Prices **are bound to** rise.*
3. ***Possible*** and ***probable*** do **not** take pattern 2: ✗ *He is possible to win.* ✗ *The meeting is probable to be cancelled.* Use pattern 1, a modal or *likely*: *It is possible that he will win* / *He may win* / *He is likely to win*.
4. ***It is possible for*** + person + ***to***-infinitive expresses what someone can do (dynamic or deontic, not epistemic): *Is it **possible for** me **to** change my seat?* *It isn't **possible for** children **to** enter alone.*
5. ***Sure*** and ***certain*** have two meanings. *She is **sure to** win* = ‘I (the speaker) am sure she will win’. *She is **sure (that)** she will win* = ‘she is confident’. *Bound to* has only the first meaning: *She's **bound to** win.*
6. Deontic adjectives — ***necessary***, ***essential***, ***vital***, ***important***, ***compulsory*** — take *it is … for … to* or *it is … that*: *It is **necessary for** all staff **to** attend.* *It is **essential that** the form **be** / **is** / **should be** signed.* (See [[modality/obligation-necessity#rule-formal-obligation]].) Not ✗ *You are necessary to attend.*
:::

::: proof
Pattern 2 is often called "raising": the subject of the *that*-clause (*the meeting* in *It is likely that the meeting will be cancelled*) becomes the subject of the main clause (*The meeting is likely to be cancelled*). Only a few adjectives allow it, and the list has to be learned; Quirk et al. (1985), Huddleston & Pullum (2002) and Swan (2016) give the same small set. *Possible* and *probable* are close in meaning to *likely*, but not in grammar, and this is one of the most frequent errors of learners whose language has a single word for both a modal adverb and an adjective ([[#ex-adjectives]] shows several). The two meanings of *sure* and *certain* in rule 5 come from the same contrast: with the *to*-infinitive the certainty belongs to the speaker, with a *that*-clause it belongs to the subject.
:::

::: example Correcting adjective patterns {#ex-adjectives}
Correct the sentences that are wrong and explain. (a) *Our team is possible to win the cup.* (b) *It is likely that the train will be late.* (c) *The new law is probable to pass.* (d) *Students are necessary to register by Friday.* (e) *He is bound to be late — he always is.* (f) *Is it possible to me to leave early?*
::: solution
(a) *Possible* does not take a subject + *to*-infinitive: *It is possible that our team will win the cup* / *Our team could win the cup* / *Our team is likely to win the cup* (stronger).

(b) Correct.

(c) *Probable* does not take this pattern either: *The new law is likely to pass* / *It is probable that the new law will pass* / *The new law will probably pass.*

(d) *Necessary* cannot have a person as subject in this sense: *It is necessary for students to register by Friday* / *Students must / need to register by Friday.*

(e) Correct: *bound to* = ‘certain to’ (the speaker's certainty).

(f) The person is introduced by *for*: *Is it possible for me to leave early?* (or *Could I leave early?*)
:::
:::

## Nouns

::: theorem Patterns with modal nouns {#rule-nouns}
1. ***There is a possibility / chance / likelihood that*** + clause: *There is a **possibility that** the flight will be delayed.* *There's a good **chance** (that) we'll finish early.*
2. ***The chances are (that)…*** (informal) and ***in all likelihood*** (formal): ***The chances are** she's already left.* ***In all likelihood**, the vote will be close.*
3. ***Chance of*** + *-ing* or noun means **probability**; ***chance to*** + infinitive means **opportunity**: *What are the **chances of** winning?* (how likely) / *I had a **chance to** visit Kyoto* (an opportunity). ***Possibility of*** + *-ing* is also probability: *the **possibility of** finding life on Mars*. ✗ *the possibility to* is common in learner English and in some international business English, but *opportunity to* or *possibility of* is standard.
4. ***No doubt*** (= ‘probably, I expect’) and ***without doubt*** / ***undoubtedly*** (= ‘certainly’) differ in strength: ***No doubt** you've heard the news* is a confident assumption, not proof.
5. Deontic nouns: ***There's no need to*** + infinitive (= *don't have to*): *There's **no need to** apologise.* *be under **an obligation** to*, *have **permission** to*, *a **requirement** that*.
:::

::: proof
Nouns let a writer turn modality into the subject or object of a sentence and then describe it: *a **small** possibility*, *a **strong** likelihood*, *a **real** chance*. This is why they are frequent in academic and official writing (Biber et al. 1999), where they also allow a claim to be made without naming who holds it. The difference between *chance of* and *chance to* (rule 3) is given in learner's dictionaries and in Swan (2016). Rule 4 surprises many learners: *no doubt* sounds stronger than it is, and in speech it often means little more than ‘I suppose’.
:::

## Verbs

::: theorem Lexical verbs with modal meaning {#rule-verbs}
1. ***Seem*** and ***appear*** + *to*-infinitive or ***it seems / appears that***: the speaker reports an impression or inference, without full commitment. *The results **seem to** confirm the theory.* ***It appears that** the email was never sent.* With a perfect infinitive they look back: *She **seems to have** left.* With *there*: ***There seems to be** a problem.*
2. ***Look*** (***as if / as though***, ***like***) is the informal, visual equivalent: *It **looks as if** it's going to rain.* (*look like* + clause is common, especially in AmE, but informal.)
3. ***Tend to*** + infinitive turns a generalisation into a tendency: *Young people **tend to** use social media more.* It is a key academic hedge ([[modality/hedging-boosting]]).
4. ***Suggest***, ***indicate***, ***show*** and ***prove*** report what evidence supports, in rising order of strength: *The data **suggest** / **indicate** / **show** that…*. (*Prove* is rarely used of empirical data.)
5. **Mental-state verbs** — ***think***, ***believe***, ***suppose***, ***expect***, ***assume***, ***doubt***, informal BrE ***reckon*** and informal AmE ***guess*** — mark a statement as the speaker's opinion: ***I think** she's right.* ***I doubt** (whether) they'll agree.*
6. With *think*, *believe*, *suppose* and *expect*, English usually puts the negative on the **mental verb**, not in the clause: ***I don't think** he's coming* is more natural than *I think he isn't coming* (Quirk et al. 1985; Huddleston & Pullum 2002).
7. **Deontic verbs**: ***require***, ***oblige***, ***force*** (necessity) and ***allow***, ***permit***, ***let*** (permission): *The rules **require** all players **to** wear helmets.* *Visitors are not **permitted to** take photographs.*
:::

::: example *Seem* and *appear* {#ex-seem}
Rewrite each sentence with *seem* or *appear* + *to*-infinitive, choosing the right form of the infinitive. (a) *It seems that the computer is broken.* (b) *It appears that the fire started in the kitchen.* (c) *It seems that they are enjoying themselves.* (d) *It seems that there is a mistake in the bill.* (e) *It appears that she has been working here for years.*
::: solution
(a) *The computer seems to be broken.* — simple infinitive for a present state.

(b) *The fire appears to have started in the kitchen.* — perfect infinitive for a past event.

(c) *They seem to be enjoying themselves.* — progressive infinitive for an activity in progress.

(d) *There seems to be a mistake in the bill.* — *there* is kept as the subject.

(e) *She appears to have been working here for years.* — perfect progressive infinitive.

The forms of the infinitive are the same as after a modal verb ([[modality/what-modals-do#rule-modal-grammar]]): *seem to have left* matches *must have left*.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which is the most natural way to say that you believe he is not coming?
- [ ] *I think he isn't coming.*
- [x] *I don't think he's coming.*
- [ ] *I think he doesn't come.*
- [ ] *I not think he's coming.*
::: solution
With *think*, English normally puts the negative on the mental verb. *I think he isn't coming* is grammatical but less usual, and sounds more emphatic. The third option uses the wrong tense for a future arrangement, and the fourth is not English.

## Choosing a form

The same degree of certainty can be expressed in many ways:

- *The project **may** be delayed.* (modal verb)
- ***Perhaps** the project will be delayed.* (adverb)
- *It is **possible** that the project will be delayed.* (adjective)
- *There is a **possibility** that the project will be delayed.* (noun)
- *The project **seems likely** to be delayed.* (verb and adjective)

They are not all equally natural everywhere. The choice depends on three things.

1. **Register.** Conversation uses modal verbs and *maybe*, *probably*, *I think*; academic and official writing uses more adjectives, nouns and verbs such as *suggest* and *appear* (Biber et al. 1999).
2. **Focus.** Front position (*Perhaps…*, *It is possible that…*) makes the modality prominent; a modal verb or mid-position adverb keeps it in the background.
3. **Source.** *It is said that*, *reportedly*, *apparently* and *seem* attribute the judgement to someone else or to appearances; *I think* and *in my view* make it personal; *it is likely that* makes it impersonal.

::: example One claim, four registers {#ex-registers}
Express the idea ‘there is a good probability that remote working will become more common’ (a) in conversation with a friend, (b) in a news report, (c) in an academic essay, (d) as a personal opinion in a job interview.
::: solution
(a) *I reckon more people will probably work from home in future.* / *Loads more people are going to be working from home, I bet.* — Mental verb (*reckon*, BrE informal), adverb *probably*, informal vocabulary.

(b) *Remote working is likely to become more common, according to a new survey.* — Adjective *likely* with the raising pattern, and the source attributed.

(c) *It is likely that remote working will become increasingly common.* / *Remote working appears likely to become more widespread.* — Impersonal *it is likely that*, and *appears* to distance the writer further. The modality is in the most prominent position.

(d) *I think it's very likely that remote working will become more common, and that's one reason I'm interested in this role.* — *I think* makes it personal, *very likely* gives the strength, and the speaker links the claim to herself.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **可能 is a verb, an adverb and an adjective — *possible* is only an adjective.** 他可能赢 → *He **may** win* / *He **could** win* / ***Perhaps** he'll win* / *It's **possible** (that) he'll win*. Not ✗ *He is possible to win*: the most common learner error in this area. Similarly 他很可能赢 → *He is (very) likely to win*, not ✗ *He is very possible to win*.
- **好像, 似乎, 看起来.** 他好像病了 → *He **seems** to be ill* / *He **looks** ill* / ***It looks as if** he's ill*. 似乎 is closer to *seem* and *appear*, 看起来 to *look*.
- **据说, 听说.** 据说他要辞职 → ***Apparently*** *he's going to resign* / *He **is said to** be resigning* / *He is **reportedly** resigning* (news). *Apparently* is the natural spoken choice.
- **往往, 一般.** 年轻人往往更喜欢网购 → *Young people **tend to** prefer online shopping*. *Tend to* is often a better translation than *usually* or *always* in academic writing.
- **我觉得……不.** 我觉得他不会来 → *I **don't think** he'll come*. Chinese places the negative in the clause; English usually moves it to *think*.
- **没有必要 and 必要.** 没有必要道歉 → *There's **no need to** apologise* / *You **don't need to** apologise*. ✗ *It is no necessary to* is a frequent error; *necessary* also cannot take a person as subject (✗ *You are necessary to…*).
- **肯定, 一定 (adverbs) and *definitely*.** 我肯定去 → *I'll **definitely** go*, not ✗ *I will surely go*; *surely* asks for agreement or expresses surprise.
:::

::: history
For a long time grammars treated modality as a property of the modal verbs, and the other ways of expressing it were left to dictionaries. Michael Perkins's *Modal Expressions in English* (1983) was one of the first books to describe modal adverbs, adjectives, nouns and verbs together with the modal auxiliaries, and Lyons's *Semantics* (1977) had already noticed how modal verbs and adverbs combine "harmonically" (*may possibly*). Corpus linguistics then showed how frequent these forms are. Douglas Biber and Edward Finegan's article "Styles of stance in English" (1989) and the *Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English* (1999) grouped them under **stance** — the expression of a speaker's attitude and certainty — and counted them across registers. Leo Hoye's *Adverbs and Modality in English* (1997) studied the combinations of modal verbs and adverbs in detail.
:::

## Where this leads

The forms of this chapter are the main tools of hedging and boosting in academic writing ([[modality/hedging-boosting]]), and of indirect, polite language ([[modality/modality-politeness]]). Reporting verbs and stance in academic reading are treated in [[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]], and the register differences between conversation and academic writing in [[collocation-and-register/register]].

::: summary
- Modality is expressed not only by modal verbs but also by adverbs, adjectives, nouns and lexical verbs, with both epistemic and deontic meanings ([[#def-lexical-modality]]).
- Modal adverbs usually go in mid-position, before a negative auxiliary (*She probably won't come*); *perhaps* and *maybe* go first; adverb *likely* alone is mainly AmE ([[#rule-adverbs]]).
- *Surely* invites agreement or shows surprise; *apparently* reports what you have heard.
- Same-strength modal verbs and adverbs combine harmonically: *may possibly*, *must certainly* ([[#rule-harmonic]]).
- Only *likely*, *unlikely*, *certain*, *sure* and *bound* take subject + *be* + adjective + *to*: never ✗ *He is possible to win* ([[#rule-adjectives]]).
- *Chance of* = probability, *chance to* = opportunity; *no doubt* means ‘probably’; *there's no need to* = *don't have to* ([[#rule-nouns]]).
- *Seem*, *appear*, *tend*, *suggest* and *indicate* distance the writer from a claim; with *think*, the negative usually goes on the mental verb ([[#rule-verbs]]).
- Choose the form by register, focus and source.
:::

::: exercise Analyse a news text {level=3}
Find every expression of modality in this short news text, say which word class it belongs to, and how strong it is.

*Officials said yesterday that the bridge is likely to remain closed for at least a month. Engineers appear to have found cracks in two of its supports, although the extent of the damage is still uncertain. There is a strong possibility that traffic will be diverted through the city centre, which will almost certainly cause delays. Local businesses are reportedly worried.*
::: solution
*is likely to* — adjective (raising pattern): probable. *appear to have found* — verb: an inference from appearances, with the source kept at a distance. *uncertain* — adjective: not known. *There is a strong possibility that* — noun with an intensifying adjective: fairly probable. *will* — modal verb: prediction. *almost certainly* — adverb with a hedge (*almost*): very probable. *reportedly* — evidential adverb: the information comes from other people, not the reporter. Only one modal verb (*will*) appears among seven expressions of modality, which is typical of news writing.
