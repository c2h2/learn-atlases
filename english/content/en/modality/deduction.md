Chen Jie comes home late on a rainy evening. There is a wet umbrella by the door and the smell of cooking from the kitchen. Her flatmate Amira is in the living room.

**Chen Jie:** *Is Sam back?*

**Amira:** *He **must** be — that's his umbrella. And he **must** be cooking: I can smell garlic.*

**Chen Jie:** *It **can't** be Sam cooking. He never cooks. It **might** be his brother — he said he **might** come round.*

**Amira:** *Well, his brother **should** be here by now, if his train was on time. Either way, dinner **will** be ready soon, I hope.*

Neither speaker has seen who is in the kitchen. Everything they say is a **conclusion** from evidence — the umbrella, the smell, what they know about Sam and his brother — and each conclusion comes with a degree of confidence: *must* (I'm sure), *can't* (I'm sure it isn't), *might* (perhaps), *should* (I expect so). This is epistemic modality, the family of meanings introduced in [[modality/what-modals-do#def-epistemic]]. The weak end of the scale — *may*, *might* and *could* for possibility — was treated in [[modality/ability-possibility]]. This chapter adds the strong end and the middle, and puts the whole scale together.

## The scale of certainty

::: definition Deduction {#def-deduction}
A **deduction** (in this course, also called a **conclusion** or **inference**) is a judgement about what is true, made by reasoning from evidence or from what the speaker knows, rather than from direct observation. English expresses deductions mainly with the epistemic modals ***must***, ***can't***, ***couldn't***, ***will***, ***should*** and ***ought to***, together with ***may***, ***might*** and ***could*** for possibility.
:::

| speaker's confidence | positive | negative |
|---|---|---|
| fact: no modal | *He **is** at home.* | *He **isn't** at home.* |
| sure, from evidence | *He **must** be at home.* | *He **can't** / **couldn't** be at home.* |
| sure, from what usually happens | *That'**ll** be him.* | *That **won't** be him.* |
| expects so | *He **should** / **ought to** be home by now.* | *He **shouldn't** be late.* |
| possible | *He **may** / **might** / **could** be at home.* | *He **may not** / **might not** be at home.* |

A sentence with a modal is, paradoxically, **less** certain than one without. *He is at home* states a fact; *He must be at home* tells the listener that the speaker has not checked, but has good reasons. Palmer (2001) distinguishes three kinds of epistemic judgement that this table reflects: speculative (*may*, possibility), deductive (*must*, a conclusion from evidence) and assumptive (*will*, a conclusion from general knowledge).

## *Must* and *can't*

::: theorem *Must* and *can't* for conclusions {#rule-must-cant}
1. ***Must*** expresses a **confident positive conclusion**: the speaker sees no other explanation for the evidence. *You've been travelling all day — you **must** be exhausted.* *There **must** be a mistake.*
2. The negative of epistemic *must* is ***can't*** (or ***cannot***), and in a past or more tentative context ***couldn't***: *He **can't** be hungry — he's just eaten.* *It **couldn't** be the same person.*
3. Epistemic ***mustn't*** is **not used** in British English for negative conclusions: ✗ *He mustn't be hungry.* In American English, ***must not*** (usually uncontracted) is sometimes used this way: *He **must not** be home — the lights are off* (Swan 2016).
4. Epistemic *must* and *can't* are usually about the **present** (or, with a perfect infinitive, the past: [[modality/past-modals]]). For confident predictions about the **future**, English normally uses *will*, *be bound to* or *be sure to*: *She'**s bound to** win* (not ✗ *She must win*, which sounds like an order).
5. In **questions**, epistemic *must* is rare; speakers ask with *can* (often in surprise), *could* or *Do you think…?*: ***Can** that really be true?* ***Could** it be a virus?* *Do you think she's ill?*
:::

::: proof
The logic of rules 1–3 is the scope of negation ([[modality/what-modals-do#rule-negation]]). The opposite of ‘it is certainly true’ is ‘it is certainly not true’ — the necessity of a negative, which in principle *mustn't* could express. But *mustn't* is already used for prohibition, and English fills the place with *can't*, which says ‘it is not possible that…’. Logically the two say the same thing: if something is necessarily false, it is impossible. Quirk et al. (1985), Coates (1983), Huddleston & Pullum (2002) and Swan (2016) all describe *can't* as the usual negative of epistemic *must*; Swan notes the American use of *must not*. Rule 4 reflects the fact that a deduction is based on evidence available now: evidence can show what is true now or what has happened, but the future is not yet there to be deduced, so a confident view of it is a prediction. *Must* with a future meaning is understood as obligation, which is why ✗ *You must pass the exam* (meaning ‘I'm sure you'll pass’) misleads: it sounds like a parent's demand.
:::

::: warning *Mustn't be* and *must* for the future
✗ *She mustn't be at home — her car isn't there.* ✓ *She **can't** be at home — her car isn't there.*

✗ *Don't worry, you must pass — you've worked so hard.* ✓ *Don't worry, you'**re bound to** / **you'll definitely** pass — you've worked so hard.*

✗ *It mustn't be easy to learn Arabic.* ✓ *It **can't** be easy to learn Arabic.* (= I'm sure it isn't easy.)

Both errors often come from translating 一定不 and 一定会 word for word: see the note on English and Chinese below.
:::

::: theorem Deductions about actions in progress {#rule-progressive}
For conclusions about what is happening **now**, use the modal with a **progressive infinitive** (*be* + *-ing*): *Listen — the neighbours **must be having** a party.* *She **can't be working** — it's Sunday.* *He **might be sleeping**.* A simple infinitive with an action verb usually gives a root meaning instead: *She must work* is obligation, *She must be working* a conclusion.
:::

::: example From evidence to conclusion {#ex-evidence}
Make a deduction with *must*, *can't* or *might* (with a simple or progressive infinitive) for each piece of evidence, and explain the strength. (a) Lin has a plaster on his arm and can't hold his pen. (b) The phone has been ringing for a long time and nobody answers. (c) Mrs Ito is seventy, but this woman looks about thirty. (d) Two of your colleagues are whispering and looking at the calendar near your birthday. (e) Your friend's light is on at 3 a.m.
::: solution
(a) *He **must** have hurt his arm* or *His arm **must** be broken.* — The evidence is strong; there is no other likely explanation. (*must have hurt* uses a perfect infinitive: see [[modality/past-modals]].)

(b) *There **can't** be anyone at home* or *They **must** be out.* — Strong: if someone were in, they would answer. A more cautious speaker might say *They **might** be in the garden.*

(c) *She **can't** be Mrs Ito.* — Strong negative conclusion: impossible, given what we know. (Not ✗ *She mustn't be Mrs Ito.*)

(d) *They **might** be planning a surprise for me.* — Only possible: there could be other reasons. A confident speaker would say *They **must** be planning something.*

(e) *She **must** be studying for her exam* or *She **might** be watching a film.* — The progressive infinitive shows an activity in progress now; *must* if you know she has an exam, *might* if you are only guessing.
:::
:::

::: quiz
*You've just come back from a ten-hour flight. You ___ tired.*
- [ ] *mustn't be*
- [x] *must be*
- [ ] *should to be*
- [ ] *can be*
::: solution
The speaker draws a confident conclusion from the evidence (the long flight): *must be*. *Can be* would be a generalisation (‘you are sometimes tired’), and *mustn't be* is not used for conclusions in British English.

## *Have to* and *have got to* in conclusions

*Have to* and *have got to*, the usual semi-modals of obligation, are also used for confident conclusions, especially in American English and in emphatic speech: *This **has to** be the best pizza in town.* *There's **got to** be a better way.* *You'**ve got to** be joking!* (Biber et al. 1999; Swan 2016). Often they sound a little more emphatic or emotional than *must*, as if the speaker is insisting on the conclusion. The negative is still *can't*: *That **can't** be right* — not ✗ *That doesn't have to be right*, which means ‘it is not necessarily right’.

## *Will* and *would*: assumptions

::: theorem *Will* and *would* for assumptions {#rule-will-assumption}
1. ***Will*** expresses a confident **assumption about the present**, based on what the speaker knows about how things usually are rather than on evidence in front of them: *(The doorbell rings.) That'**ll** be the delivery — I ordered some books.* *Don't phone now — they'**ll** be having dinner.* *As you **will** know, the meeting has been moved* (formal: ‘I assume you know’).
2. The negative is ***won't***: *Don't bother ringing Mei — she **won't** be up yet.*
3. ***Would*** makes the assumption more tentative, mainly in British English: *"Who's that man with Wang Fang?" "That **would** be her uncle, I think."*
:::

::: proof
*Will* is best known as a way of talking about the future ([[tense-and-aspect/future-forms]]), but its basic meaning is a confident prediction, and a prediction can be about the present too: what the speaker would expect to find if they checked. The difference from *must* is the source of the confidence. *Must* reasons from present evidence (*His car's here — he must be home*); *will* reasons from general knowledge or routine (*It's eight o'clock — he'll be home*). Palmer (2001) calls these deductive and assumptive judgements. Swan (2016) and Leech (2004) also describe this "predictive" or "assumptive" *will*. The use is common in British English and less frequent, though not unknown, in American English.
:::

## *Should* and *ought to*: expectations

::: theorem *Should* and *ought to* for expectations {#rule-should-expectation}
1. ***Should*** and ***ought to*** express what the speaker **expects** to be true if things are normal or go as planned: *The parcel **should** arrive tomorrow.* *She left an hour ago, so she **ought to** be there by now.* *This **shouldn't** take long.*
2. The confidence is **medium**: stronger than *may* or *might*, weaker than *must* or *will*. The speaker leaves room for something unexpected.
3. Because *should* means ‘if all goes as it ought to’, it is used most naturally for expected outcomes that are **neutral or welcome**. For unwelcome outcomes speakers usually prefer *will probably* or *is likely to*: *The roads **will probably** be busy* rather than ? *The roads should be busy* (Swan 2016).
4. Epistemic *should* is often **ambiguous** with deontic *should*: *They should be at the airport by six* can mean ‘I expect they are’ or ‘they are supposed to be’. Context decides.
:::

::: proof
The two meanings of *should* are closely related, and this explains rule 3. Deontic *should* says what is right or expected by some standard; epistemic *should* says what is expected if the world behaves as it is supposed to. A speaker who says *The parcel should arrive tomorrow* is relying on the post doing its job. It is odd to rely on the normal course of events to bring something bad, so ? *The exam should be very difficult* sounds strange as a prediction (although it is fine as a deontic statement about what the examiners intend). Coates (1983) and Huddleston & Pullum (2002) both describe the "medium" strength of epistemic *should*, and Coates notes how often deontic and epistemic readings merge in it.
:::

::: example Two meanings of *should* {#ex-should}
Say whether *should* is epistemic (expectation) or deontic (what is right or required) in each sentence, or whether both readings are possible. Give a paraphrase. (a) *You should apologise to her.* (b) *The new bridge should open in May.* (c) *The guests should be here at seven.* (d) *Two hours should be enough for the exam.* (e) *Children should be seen and not heard* (an old saying).
::: solution
(a) Deontic: ‘It would be right for you to apologise.’ An apology is an action the hearer controls.

(b) Epistemic: ‘I expect the bridge will open in May, if the work goes to plan.’ A bridge cannot be advised. (A deontic reading — ‘the contract says it must open in May’ — is possible in a planning meeting.)

(c) Both. Epistemic: ‘I expect the guests to arrive at seven.’ Deontic: ‘The guests are supposed to arrive at seven’ (that is what the invitation said). In conversation, a host looking at the clock means the first; a host writing instructions means the second.

(d) Epistemic: ‘I expect two hours will be enough.’ *Be enough* is a state, not an action.

(e) Deontic: a (now old-fashioned) rule about how children ought to behave.

The tests of [[modality/what-modals-do#rule-tests]] work here too: a state verb or an inanimate subject points to the epistemic reading, an action the subject controls to the deontic one.
:::
:::

::: example *Must*, *will* or *should*? {#ex-must-will-should}
Choose the best modal and explain why. (a) *It's 7.15. The film started at seven, so it (must / should) be on by now.* (b) *(Phone rings.) That (will / should) be my sister — she always calls on Sundays.* (c) *I've checked the train times. If we leave at ten, we (must / should) arrive before lunch.* (d) *Look at his face — he (must / will) be furious.* (e) *There are footprints in the snow; someone (must / should) have come this way.*
::: solution
(a) *must* — a confident deduction from the time and the start of the film. (*should* is possible if the speaker is thinking ‘if it started on time’.)

(b) *will* — an assumption from routine (she always calls on Sundays), not from present evidence.

(c) *should* — an expectation that depends on the trains running normally.

(d) *must* — a conclusion from present evidence (his face).

(e) *must* — a conclusion from evidence; *must have come* looks back at the past.
:::
:::

::: quiz
*It's only nine o'clock. Rosa ___ be asleep already — she never goes to bed before midnight.*
- [ ] *mustn't*
- [ ] *shouldn't*
- [x] *can't*
- [ ] *might not*
::: solution
The speaker is sure that Rosa is not asleep, so the strong negative *can't* is right. *Might not* would leave it open, *shouldn't* would express only an expectation, and *mustn't* is not used this way in British English.

::: quiz
*"There's someone at the door." "That ___ be the plumber — he said he'd come at ten."*
- [x] *'ll*
- [ ] *can*
- [ ] *mustn't*
- [ ] *may not*
::: solution
*That'll be the plumber* is a confident assumption based on what the speaker knows (the plumber's promise). *Must* would also be possible. *Can* does not express a specific conclusion, and the two negatives contradict the reason given.

## Not necessarily: *needn't be* and *don't have to be*

The negative scale has one more step. To say that a conclusion **does not follow** — that something is not necessarily true — English uses ***not necessarily***, and also ***needn't*** (mainly British) or ***doesn't have to***: *An expensive wine **isn't necessarily** a good one.* *A low score **needn't** mean that a student is weak.* *Being quiet **doesn't have to** mean being shy.* These deny the necessity, not the possibility, so they are weaker than *can't*: *A quiet person can't be shy* (impossible — and false), *A quiet person needn't be shy* (not necessarily shy — true).

::: example Correcting a paragraph {#ex-correct}
A student wrote this about a photograph of a street scene. Correct the modal errors and explain.

*This photo must be taken in the 1950s, because all the cars are old. It mustn't be in China, because the cars drive on the left; it must be in Britain or maybe Japan. The man in the hat can be a policeman. It must rain soon, because the sky is very dark.*
::: solution
*This photo **must have been taken** in the 1950s* — a conclusion about the past needs a perfect (passive) infinitive.

*It **can't** be in China* — the negative of epistemic *must* is *can't*; *mustn't* means prohibition. (*It can't have been taken in China* also works.)

*It **must** be in Britain or maybe Japan* — the speaker offers two options, so *must* is too strong for either one; better *It **could** be Britain or Japan* or *It **must** be somewhere where people drive on the left, perhaps Britain or Japan.*

*The man in the hat **may** / **might** / **could** be a policeman* — a specific possibility, not *can*.

*It **looks as if** it was about to rain* — epistemic *must* is not used for future events, and in any case the photo shows the past. A dark sky is evidence of possible rain, not proof, so a form such as *looks as if* or *was probably about to* matches the evidence better than *must*.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **一定, 肯定 and *must*.** 他一定很累 → *He must be very tired.* This is the most direct match between the two languages, and Chinese speakers usually use epistemic *must* well in the positive.
- **一定不 is not *mustn't*.** 他一定不在家 means ‘I'm sure he isn't at home’ → *He **can't** be at home* or *He **definitely isn't** at home*. A word-for-word translation (✗ *He mustn't be at home*) gives a prohibition.
- **一定会 and the future.** 你一定会成功 → *You'**ll definitely** succeed* or *You'**re bound to** succeed*. ✗ *You must succeed* turns encouragement into a demand.
- **不会 and 不可能.** 他不会知道的 and 他不可能知道 both deny the possibility → *He **can't** know* or, as an assumption, *He **won't** know*. 不会 is often closer to *won't* (an assumption based on what the speaker knows), 不可能 to *can't* (impossible).
- **应该 for expectations.** 他应该已经到了 → *He should be there by now* (or *He should have arrived by now*). English *should* covers this epistemic meaning just as 应该 does.
- **大概, 也许, 恐怕.** 大概 is usually *probably*, 也许 *maybe* or *might*; 恐怕 (‘I'm afraid’) marks an unwelcome possibility: 恐怕他不来了 → *I'm afraid he might not come* / *I don't think he's coming*. English often uses adverbs here rather than modals ([[modality/modality-without-modals]]).
:::

::: history
Deduction with *must* is one of the oldest topics in the study of modality, because it links grammar to logic. In logic, ‘necessarily *p*’ and ‘not possibly not *p*’ are equivalent, and so are ‘not necessarily *p*’ and ‘possibly not *p*’; English *must*, *can't*, *needn't* and *may not* express these four combinations. Linguists noticed that English epistemic *must* is in fact weaker than logical necessity: *He must be at home* is less certain than *He is at home*. Lyons (1977) used examples like this to argue that epistemic modals mark the speaker's own, subjective judgement, and Palmer (*Mood and Modality*, 1986; 2nd edition 2001) separated the deductive judgement of *must* from the assumptive judgement of *will* and the speculative judgement of *may*. Coates (1983), working with British corpora, found that epistemic *must* is used mainly with state verbs and progressives and is rare in questions — the patterns given in this chapter.
:::

## Where this leads

Deductions about the past — *must have left*, *can't have known*, *should have arrived* — are the subject of [[modality/past-modals]]. The same scale of certainty is expressed by adverbs and adjectives (*certainly*, *probably*, *it is likely that*) in [[modality/modality-without-modals]], and in academic writing it becomes the art of hedging ([[modality/hedging-boosting]]). How readers detect a writer's confidence is practised in [[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]].

::: summary
- Deductions are conclusions from evidence or knowledge, expressed on a scale of certainty; a modal makes a statement less certain than a plain fact ([[#def-deduction]]).
- *Must* = a confident positive conclusion; its negative is *can't* or *couldn't*, not *mustn't* (AmE sometimes *must not*) ([[#rule-must-cant]]).
- Epistemic *must* is about the present or (with *have* + participle) the past; for confident future predictions use *will*, *be bound to* or *be sure to*.
- Use a progressive infinitive for conclusions about actions in progress: *She must be working* ([[#rule-progressive]]).
- *Will* and *won't* express assumptions from general knowledge (*That'll be the plumber*); *would* is more tentative ([[#rule-will-assumption]]).
- *Should* and *ought to* express medium-strength expectations, mostly about neutral or welcome outcomes ([[#rule-should-expectation]]).
- *Needn't*, *doesn't have to* and *not necessarily* deny that a conclusion follows; they are weaker than *can't*.
- 一定不 is *can't*, not *mustn't*; 一定会 is *will definitely* or *be bound to*, not *must*.
:::

::: exercise A short dialogue {level=3}
Write a dialogue of 8–12 lines between two people who find a mysterious object (a key, a letter, a box) and try to work out what it is and whose it is. Use at least six different epistemic forms, including *must*, *can't*, *might*, *should* and *will*, and at least one progressive infinitive.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

**Ana:** *Look — a key under the doormat. It **must** be for this flat.*

**Bilal:** *It **can't** be. Our keys are silver, and this one's gold.*

**Ana:** *Then it **might** be for the shed in the garden.*

**Bilal:** *The shed doesn't have a lock. It **could** be for the post box.*

**Ana:** *That **won't** be it — the post box key is on my keyring.*

**Bilal:** *Maybe the old tenants left it. They **must** have forgotten it when they moved.*

**Ana:** *Mrs Lopez upstairs knows them. She **should** be home by now — let's ask her.*

**Bilal:** *Listen — she **must** be watching TV. I can hear it from here.*

Check: *must* (twice, plus *must have* and *must be watching*), *can't*, *might*, *could*, *won't*, *should*; one progressive infinitive (*be watching*).
