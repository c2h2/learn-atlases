Mariam and her colleague Zhao Lei are talking the morning after a team dinner.

**Mariam:** *You weren't at the dinner last night! You **should have** come — it was great.*

**Zhao Lei:** *I know. I **could have** come, but I was so tired. Anyway, nobody told me where it was.*

**Mariam:** *Really? Tom **must have** forgotten to send you the address. He **can't have** sent it to everyone.*

**Zhao Lei:** *Or it **might have** gone to my junk folder. I **needn't have** worried about the dress code, though — I bought a new shirt specially!*

Every sentence in Mariam and Zhao Lei's exchange uses the same pattern, **modal + *have* + past participle**, and every one looks back at the past. Yet they do very different things. *Must have forgotten* and *can't have sent* are **conclusions** about what happened. *Should have come* is a **criticism** of something that did not happen. *Could have come* describes a **chance that was not taken**. *Needn't have worried* says that something was done but was **unnecessary**.

Chinese uses quite different means for these meanings — 一定是忘了, 你本来应该来的, 我本来可以来, 其实不用担心 — so the English pattern has to be learned as a system. This chapter sets out the form, the two families of meaning it carries, and the details of each modal. It assumes the present-tense meanings from [[modality/ability-possibility]], [[modality/obligation-necessity]] and [[modality/deduction]], and the present perfect from [[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect]].

## Form

::: definition Modal perfect {#def-modal-perfect}
A **modal perfect** is a modal verb followed by a **perfect infinitive**: *have* + past participle (*must have gone*, *should have told*, *could have been*). Since modals have no past tense of their own, the perfect infinitive is the main way of combining a modal meaning with **past time**. A progressive form (*must have been sleeping*) and a passive form (*should have been told*) are also possible.
:::

| modal | positive | negative | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *must* | *must have* + pp | (*can't have* + pp) | *She **must have** left early.* |
| *can't*, *couldn't* | — | *can't / couldn't have* + pp | *He **can't have** seen us.* |
| *may*, *might* | *may / might have* + pp | *may not / might not have* + pp | *They **might have** missed the bus.* |
| *could* | *could have* + pp | *couldn't have* + pp | *I **could have** won.* |
| *should*, *ought to* | *should / ought to have* + pp | *shouldn't have* + pp | *You **should have** asked.* |
| *will*, *would* | *will / would have* + pp | *won't / wouldn't have* + pp | *They'**ll have** landed by now.* |
| *need* | — | *needn't have* + pp (mainly BrE) | *You **needn't have** paid.* |

::: theorem Pronunciation and spelling of modal perfects {#rule-weak-form}
1. In normal speech *have* after a modal is unstressed and reduced to /əv/ or even /ə/: *should have* /ˈʃʊd əv/, *could have* /ˈkʊd əv/, *must have* /ˈmʌst əv/. In writing that represents speech it is contracted: *should've*, *could've*, *would've*, *might've*, *must've*.
2. The reduced *have* sounds the same as the weak form of *of*, which is why the spelling ✗ *should of*, ✗ *could of* is common in informal writing. It is not standard and is marked as an error in edited English.
3. The perfect infinitive after a modal can occur with a **definite past time expression**, unlike the present perfect: *She must have left **at six*** (✓), but ✗ *She has left at six* ([[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect#rule-pp-past]]).
:::

::: proof
Rule 3 is explained by the gap in the modal system. The present perfect is a choice: a speaker who wants to name a past time uses the past simple instead. A modal has no past simple, so *must have left* is the only way to combine certainty with a past event, whether or not the time is named. Huddleston & Pullum (2002) describe the perfect after a modal as a past tense in disguise for this reason. The perfect infinitive can also have its ordinary perfect meaning, with the time running up to now or to a point in the future: *They **should have** finished by now* (up to now), *I'**ll have** finished by Friday* (up to a future point; see [[tense-and-aspect/future-forms#rule-future-perfect]]).
:::

::: warning *Should of*, *must had*, *should told*
✗ *You should of told me.* ✓ *You **should have** (should've) told me.*

✗ *He must had forgotten.* ✓ *He **must have** forgotten.*

✗ *I should told you yesterday.* ✓ *I **should have told** you yesterday.*

✗ *She must left early.* ✓ *She **must have left** early.*

After a modal, the past is always *have* + past participle. *Had* never follows a modal, and the participle cannot follow the modal directly.
:::

## Two families of meaning

The modal perfects divide into the same two families as the present modals ([[modality/what-modals-do#def-epistemic]]), but with an important twist.

::: theorem What modal perfects mean {#rule-two-uses}
1. **Epistemic (deduction about the past).** The speaker judges **now** how likely it is that something **happened**: *She must have left* (I'm sure she did). *He might have called* (perhaps he did). The speaker does not know what happened.
2. **Counterfactual (root meanings in the past).** The speaker says what was necessary, advisable or possible **then**, and the sentence implies that the opposite actually happened: *You should have called* (but you didn't). *I could have won* (but I didn't). *You needn't have paid* (but you did). The speaker knows what happened.
:::

::: proof
The twist is in the second family. A present root modal looks forward to an action that may or may not happen: *You should call* leaves the matter open. A past root modal looks back at a situation that is already settled, and the speaker would normally say plainly what happened if it had gone as expected (*You called — good*). Choosing *should have called* therefore implies that it did not happen. Quirk et al. (1985) and Swan (2016) describe this implication for *should have*, *could have*, *might have* and *needn't have*. It is an implication rather than a strict part of the meaning: *You should have taken the train — and you did, luckily* is possible. Which family a sentence belongs to depends on the modal and the context, just as for the present modals; *could have* and *might have* belong to both.
:::

## Deductions about the past

::: theorem Epistemic modal perfects {#rule-past-deduction}
1. ***Must have*** + pp: a confident conclusion that something happened: *The streets are wet — it **must have rained** in the night.*
2. ***Can't have*** / ***couldn't have*** + pp: a confident conclusion that something did **not** happen: *She **can't have heard** you — she had her headphones on.* (Not ✗ *mustn't have heard* in British English; American English sometimes uses *must not have*, as in the present: [[modality/deduction#rule-must-cant]].)
3. ***May have***, ***might have***, ***could have*** + pp: it is possible that something happened: *I **may have left** my phone in the taxi.* *He **could have taken** the wrong road.*
4. ***May not have*** / ***might not have*** + pp: it is possible that something did **not** happen: *They **might not have received** the invitation.*
5. ***Should have*** / ***ought to have*** + pp can express an expectation about something that ought to be complete by now: *The parcel **should have arrived** by now* — often with the suggestion that the speaker is beginning to worry.
6. ***Will have*** + pp: a confident assumption based on routine or general knowledge: *It's ten o'clock — the meeting **will have started**.* *You **won't have heard** the news yet.*
:::

::: example What happened? {#ex-deduction}
Read the situation and make a deduction with a modal perfect. Explain the strength. (a) Mehdi's car is not in the car park, and his coat is gone. (b) Your friend says she saw you in Paris last week, but you were in Hangzhou. (c) Sofia hasn't replied to your email, which you sent yesterday. (d) The plates are clean and the cake has disappeared. (e) It's 9 p.m. and your brother's plane was due to land at 8.30.
::: solution
(a) *He **must have gone** home.* — Two pieces of evidence point the same way; the speaker is confident.

(b) *She **can't have seen** me. It **must have been** someone else.* — The speaker knows she was in Hangzhou, so the friend's claim is impossible.

(c) *She **may / might not have seen** it yet* or *It **might have gone** to her junk folder.* — Only possible: there are several explanations.

(d) *Someone **must have eaten** the cake.* — Confident. (Or, with humour, *The children **must have been** hungry.*)

(e) *His plane **will have landed** by now* (assumption from the timetable), or *His plane **should have landed** by now* (expectation — and perhaps a hint of worry if he hasn't called).
:::
:::

::: quiz
*I can't find my keys. I ___ them at the office.*
- [ ] *must leave*
- [x] *might have left*
- [ ] *should have left*
- [ ] *might left*
::: solution
The speaker does not know where the keys are, so this is a deduction about the past, with *might have* + past participle. *Should have left* would mean that leaving them there was the right thing to do (and was not done). *Might left* is not a possible form.

## Criticism and regret: *should have* and *ought to have*

::: theorem *Should have* and *shouldn't have* {#rule-should-have}
1. ***Should have*** / ***ought to have*** + pp says that something was the right thing to do, but was **not done**. With *you* it is usually a criticism; with *I* or *we* it is a regret: *You **should have told** me you were ill.* *I **should have studied** harder.* *We **ought to have booked** a table.*
2. ***Shouldn't have*** / ***ought not to have*** + pp says that something was wrong, but **was done**: *You **shouldn't have shouted** at him.* *I **shouldn't have eaten** so much.*
3. *You shouldn't have!* is also a conventional, warm response to a gift: ‘It was too kind of you (to buy this)’.
4. In British English, ***might have*** and ***could have*** + pp can be a **reproach**, usually with stress on the modal or on the main verb: *You **might** have told me!* *You **could** at least have said thank you.* (= You should have, and you didn't — I'm annoyed.)
:::

::: proof
Rule 1 is the counterfactual implication of [[#rule-two-uses]] applied to advice: the advice comes too late, so it can only be a criticism or a regret. Regret is also often expressed with *I wish I had…* or *If only I had…* (see [[complex-sentences/unreal-past]]). The reproach in rule 4 grows out of the meaning of possibility: *You might have told me* literally says that telling me was possible — and implies that, since it was possible and easy, not doing it was a failure. Swan (2016) and Quirk et al. (1985) describe the use as typical of British English; it depends heavily on intonation.
:::

::: warning Past obligation and criticism
✗ *Yesterday I should go to the dentist, so I left early.* ✓ *Yesterday I **had to** go to the dentist, so I left early.* (an obligation that was met)

✗ *You should tell me yesterday.* ✓ *You **should have told** me yesterday.* (a criticism: you didn't)

*Had to* reports a past obligation (and usually implies that the action was done). *Should have* reports advice that was not followed.
:::

## Missed chances: *could have* and *might have*

::: theorem Counterfactual *could have* and *might have* {#rule-could-have}
1. ***Could have*** + pp says that something was **possible** or someone was **able** to do it, but it **did not happen**: *I **could have been** a doctor, but I chose music.* *You **could have been** killed!* (It was possible, but luckily it didn't happen.) *We **could have walked** — it was only ten minutes.*
2. ***Couldn't have*** + pp says that something was **impossible**: *I **couldn't have done** it without you* (= you made it possible). *The weather **couldn't have been** better* (= it was perfect).
3. ***Might have*** + pp is also used for possibilities that did not happen: *If you'd asked, she **might have helped**.* *It was a dangerous road — we **might have had** an accident.*
4. With ***could have*** and ***might have***, the same words can be epistemic or counterfactual: *She could have taken the train* means either ‘perhaps she took it’ (we don't know) or ‘she had the option, but she didn't’. Context decides.
:::

::: proof
Rule 2 shows a common use of *couldn't have* with comparatives and *without*: *It couldn't have been easier* is a strong way of saying ‘it was extremely easy’. The ambiguity in rule 4 follows from the two families: *could have* combines possibility, which can be epistemic or not, with past time. Speakers resolve it with context, often with *but* (counterfactual) or *perhaps* (epistemic).

The use of ***may have*** for counterfactual possibility — *Without the seatbelt, he **may have died*** — is common in modern English, especially in news writing, but many usage guides recommend *might have* when it is known that the event did not happen, because *may have* suggests that we do not know whether he died. Swan (2016) notes both the recommendation and the actual usage. In careful writing, *might have* avoids any ambiguity.
:::

::: example Criticism, regret or missed chance? {#ex-counterfactual}
For each sentence, say what actually happened and what the speaker feels. (a) *You shouldn't have left the door unlocked.* (b) *I could have gone to university in Canada.* (c) *She ought to have warned us about the traffic.* (d) *You might have offered to help!* (e) *We couldn't have chosen a better day for the wedding.* (f) *I should have listened to my mother.*
::: solution
(a) The door was left unlocked. Criticism.

(b) The speaker did not go to university in Canada, although it was possible. A neutral statement of a missed chance, perhaps with a little regret.

(c) She did not warn us. Criticism.

(d) The listener did not offer to help. Reproach, with annoyance (British, stressed *might*).

(e) We chose a perfect day. Strong satisfaction: *couldn't have* + comparative means ‘it was the best possible’.

(f) The speaker did not listen. Regret.
:::
:::

::: example Reviewing a project {#ex-review}
A team leader, Ngozi, is writing a short review of a product launch that went badly. Her notes say: *(1) Not sure why the website crashed — perhaps too many visitors, perhaps the new server. (2) Load testing was not done; that was a mistake. (3) We had the option of a soft launch for one city, but didn't take it. (4) We printed 5,000 leaflets; the event was online, so they were not needed. (5) I'm sure the press release went out late — journalists only wrote about it the next day.* Turn each note into a sentence with a modal perfect, and explain the choice.
::: solution
(1) *The website **may have crashed** because of the number of visitors, or the new server **might have been** the problem.* — Epistemic: the cause is not known, so the speaker offers possibilities.

(2) *We **should have done** load testing.* — Counterfactual: it was not done, and that was a mistake. In a report, *should have* expresses a lesson learned without blaming a person.

(3) *We **could have launched** in one city first.* — A missed chance: the option existed but was not used.

(4) *We **needn't have printed** 5,000 leaflets.* — The leaflets were printed, unnecessarily. (In American English: *We didn't need to print 5,000 leaflets*, with the context making clear that they were printed.)

(5) *The press release **must have gone out** late.* — A confident deduction from evidence (the date of the articles).

The review mixes the two families: (1) and (5) are about what happened, which Ngozi is not sure of; (2), (3) and (4) judge decisions whose outcome she knows.
:::
:::

## Unnecessary actions: *needn't have* and *didn't need to*

::: theorem *Needn't have* and *didn't need to* {#rule-neednt-have}
1. ***Needn't have*** + pp means that something **was done**, but it **was not necessary**: *You **needn't have bought** me a present* (but you did). *I **needn't have worried** — the exam was easy.*
2. ***Didn't need to*** + base form and ***didn't have to*** + base form mean that something was **not necessary**; they usually suggest that it was **not done**, but they do not say so: *I **didn't need to** go to work, so I stayed in bed.* *She **didn't have to** pay, because it was free.* With stress, they can also report an unnecessary action: *I **didn't need** to buy it, but I wanted it.*
3. *Needn't have* is mainly **British**. American English usually uses *didn't need to*, *didn't have to* or *shouldn't have bothered*, with context showing that the action was done: *You **didn't have to** bring anything!*
:::

::: proof
The two forms differ in what they put in the past. *Didn't need to* puts the necessity in the past: at that time there was no need. *Needn't have done* puts the action in the past and judges it from now: it happened, and it turns out that it was not needed. Quirk et al. (1985), Huddleston & Pullum (2002) and Swan (2016) describe the contrast in the same terms. Because modal *need* is rare in American English (and declining in British writing: Leech et al. 2009), *needn't have* is the less frequent form of the two everywhere, but it is the only short way to say ‘it was done unnecessarily’.
:::

::: example *Needn't have* or *didn't need to*? {#ex-neednt}
Complete each sentence and explain. (a) *We ___ (take) a taxi — the station was only five minutes away. We wasted twenty pounds!* (b) *The hotel had towels, so I ___ (pack) any.* (c) *You ___ (wash) the dishes — I was going to do them. But thanks!* (d) *Luckily, I ___ (cook) last night; my sister brought dinner.*
::: solution
(a) *needn't have taken* — we took the taxi, and it was unnecessary. (AmE: *We didn't need to take a taxi*, with context making clear that we did.)

(b) *didn't need to pack* — it was not necessary, and the context suggests the speaker didn't pack any. (If the speaker had packed towels, *I needn't have packed any* would fit.)

(c) *needn't have washed* — the dishes were washed. (AmE: *You didn't have to wash the dishes!*)

(d) *didn't need to cook* (or *didn't have to cook*) — not necessary, and not done.
:::
:::

::: quiz
*"I brought an umbrella, but it didn't rain."* Which sentence reports this?
- [ ] *I didn't need to bring an umbrella, so I didn't.*
- [x] *I needn't have brought an umbrella.*
- [ ] *I mustn't have brought an umbrella.*
- [ ] *I couldn't have brought an umbrella.*
::: solution
The umbrella was brought, and it was not needed: *needn't have brought*. The first option says the speaker did not bring one. *Mustn't have* is not used, and *couldn't have* would mean it was impossible.
:::

## *Would have* and other modals in conditionals

*Would have* + pp is the modal perfect of the third conditional: *If I had known, I **would have** come.* It also stands alone, with the condition understood: *I **would have** helped you* (if you had asked — but you didn't). *Could have* and *might have* appear in the same position: *If we'd left earlier, we **might have** caught the train.* Conditionals and mixed conditionals are treated in full in [[complex-sentences/conditionals]], and the use of modal perfects in reported speech (*She said she might have lost it*) in [[complex-sentences/reported-speech]].

::: note English and Chinese
- **No perfect infinitive.** Chinese marks the past in these sentences with 了, 已经, 是……的 or a time word, while the modal stays the same: 他一定已经走了 / 他一定是走了 → *He must have left*. A direct translation tends to produce ✗ *He must left* or ✗ *He must leave already*. Remember: modal + *have* + past participle.
- **本来, 早该 and *should have*.** 你本来应该告诉我的 and 你早该告诉我 → *You should have told me.* The words 本来 (‘originally, by rights’) and 早 (‘earlier’) signal the counterfactual meaning that English carries in the perfect infinitive alone.
- **本来可以 and *could have*.** 我本来可以去北京读书 → *I could have studied in Beijing* (but I didn't). 差点儿 and 险些 often correspond to *could have* + pp or *nearly*: 我差点儿被车撞了 → *I could have been hit by a car* / *I was nearly hit by a car*.
- **其实不用 and *needn't have*.** 你其实不用买礼物的 → *You needn't have bought a present* (BrE) / *You didn't have to buy a present* (both varieties).
- **早知道 and regret.** 早知道就不买了 → *I shouldn't have bought it* or *If I'd known, I wouldn't have bought it*. The second translation uses the third conditional ([[complex-sentences/conditionals]]).
- **一定不 in the past.** 他一定没看见 → *He can't have seen it*, not ✗ *He mustn't have seen it*.
:::

::: history
In Old English the past forms of the modals were real past tenses. *Sceolde*, the ancestor of *should*, meant ‘had to, was obliged to’, and *mihte*, the ancestor of *might*, meant ‘was able to’. Over the Middle and Early Modern English periods these forms came more and more to express tentativeness and hypothesis instead of past time, and the perfect infinitive took over the job of placing a modal judgement in the past — the system described in this chapter. Historical grammars such as David Denison's *English Historical Syntax* (1993) trace the development of the modals in detail. The spelling *of* for unstressed *have* (*would of*) is a modern consequence of the weak form /əv/: the two words sound the same, and only spelling keeps them apart.
:::

## Where this leads

Regrets with *wish* and *if only*, which often appear beside *should have*, are in [[complex-sentences/unreal-past]]; the third conditional and mixed conditionals in [[complex-sentences/conditionals]]. Modal perfects as hedges in academic writing — *This may have been caused by…*, *The sample might have been too small* — are discussed in [[modality/hedging-boosting]].

::: summary
- A modal perfect is modal + *have* + past participle; it can take a definite past time expression, and in speech *have* is reduced to /əv/ — never written *of* ([[#def-modal-perfect]], [[#rule-weak-form]]).
- Epistemic modal perfects are deductions about the past: *must have* (sure it did), *can't/couldn't have* (sure it didn't), *may/might/could have* (perhaps), *will have* (assumption), *should have* (expected by now) ([[#rule-past-deduction]]).
- Counterfactual modal perfects imply the opposite happened: *should have* = right, but not done; *shouldn't have* = wrong, but done ([[#rule-two-uses]], [[#rule-should-have]]).
- *Could have* and *might have* express missed chances; *couldn't have* + comparative means ‘it was the best possible’; in careful writing prefer *might have* to *may have* for things that did not happen ([[#rule-could-have]]).
- *Needn't have* = done, but unnecessary (mainly BrE); *didn't need to* / *didn't have to* = not necessary, usually not done ([[#rule-neednt-have]]).
- Chinese uses 本来, 早该, 已经 and 是……的 where English uses the perfect infinitive.
:::

::: exercise Translate {level=3}
Translate into English. (a) 你本来应该早点告诉我的。 (b) 他一定是坐错车了。 (c) 我们其实不用这么早出发。（但我们已经出发了） (d) 她不可能看过这封信。 (e) 我差点儿就错过了飞机。 (f) 早知道你也来，我就等你了。
::: hint
(c) is about something that was done; (f) is a conditional.
:::
::: solution
(a) *You should have told me earlier.* (b) *He must have taken the wrong bus / train.* (c) *We needn't have left so early.* (AmE *We didn't need to leave so early.*) (d) *She can't / couldn't have read this letter.* (e) *I nearly missed the plane.* / *I could have missed the plane.* (*nearly* is closer to 差点儿, since it reports how close it was) (f) *If I'd known you were coming too, I would have waited for you.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Write about a past event {level=3}
Write a paragraph (100–140 words) about a time when something went wrong — a trip, an exam, a meeting. Explain what happened, make at least two deductions about causes you are not sure of, express at least two regrets or criticisms, and mention one chance that was missed and one thing that was done unnecessarily.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

*Last spring my friends and I went camping, and almost everything went wrong. When we arrived, the tent poles were missing. Someone **must have taken** them out of the bag to dry, and we **can't have checked** before we left — or perhaps they **might have fallen** out in the car. We **should have made** a list. It also rained all night; I **should have checked** the forecast, and we **shouldn't have chosen** a site so close to the river. We **could have stayed** in a cheap hotel in the village, but we were too proud. The funny thing is that I **needn't have bought** new hiking boots — we never went hiking at all.*

Check: deductions (*must have taken*, *can't have checked*, *might have fallen*), regrets and criticisms (*should have made*, *should have checked*, *shouldn't have chosen*), a missed chance (*could have stayed*), an unnecessary action (*needn't have bought*).
