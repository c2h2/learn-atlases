Three students write the same sentence for the conclusion of a short research project. They surveyed 120 secondary school pupils in one city about their use of social media and their sleep.

- **Student A:** *This study proves that social media causes sleep problems in teenagers.*
- **Student B:** *It might perhaps be possible to suggest that social media could possibly have some kind of effect on sleep in some cases.*
- **Student C:** *The results suggest that heavy use of social media in the evening may contribute to shorter sleep among the pupils surveyed.*

Their tutor gives A a low mark for **overclaiming**: one survey of 120 pupils in one city cannot *prove* anything about teenagers in general, and a survey cannot show cause. B also gets a low mark, for the opposite reason: the sentence is so cautious that it says nothing, and the reader cannot tell what the student actually found. C's sentence is the one that a journal editor would accept. It makes a clear claim, and it marks exactly how far the evidence goes: *suggest* (not *prove*), *may contribute to* (not *causes*), *heavy use … in the evening* (not all use), *the pupils surveyed* (not all teenagers).

The difference between A, B and C is mostly a matter of modality. Writers **hedge** a claim to make it weaker or narrower, and **boost** it to make it stronger. Doing this well is one of the clearest signs of an experienced academic writer, and doing it badly — in either direction — is one of the commonest problems in student writing in English, whatever the writer's first language. This chapter uses the tools of the previous chapters ([[modality/deduction]], [[modality/modality-without-modals]]) to show how.

## Hedges and boosters

::: definition Hedge {#def-hedge}
A **hedge** is a word or phrase that reduces the writer's commitment to a claim or narrows its scope: *may*, *might*, *could*, *suggest*, *appear*, *tend to*, *probably*, *possibly*, *likely*, *approximately*, *in most cases*, *to some extent*, *in this sample*. The term was introduced into linguistics by Lakoff (1972) for words that make meanings "fuzzier"; Hyland (1998) used it for all the ways academic writers present claims as less than fully certain.
:::

::: definition Booster {#def-booster}
A **booster** is a word or phrase that increases the writer's commitment to a claim and shows confidence: *clearly*, *certainly*, *undoubtedly*, *in fact*, *indeed*, *demonstrate*, *show*, *establish*, *it is evident that*, *there is no doubt that*. Hyland (2005) treats hedges and boosters as two sides of the same choice: how strongly a writer stands behind a statement.
:::

Writers hedge for three reasons, which often work together (Hyland 1998; Myers 1989):

1. **Accuracy.** A claim should be exactly as strong as the evidence for it. If the data show a correlation in one sample, the honest claim is about a correlation in one sample.
2. **Protection.** A hedged claim is harder to refute. If later research finds exceptions, *X tends to Y* is still true; *X always causes Y* is not.
3. **Respect for the reader.** Academic writing is a conversation with other researchers. A hedged claim leaves room for the reader to judge, and a writer who disagrees with another scholar usually does so with hedges (*this interpretation may underestimate…*) rather than flat contradiction. This is a kind of politeness ([[modality/modality-politeness]]).

## The tools of hedging

| tool | examples |
|---|---|
| modal verbs | *may*, *might*, *could*, *would*, *should* (expectation) |
| lexical verbs | *suggest*, *indicate*, *appear*, *seem*, *tend to*, *assume*, *speculate* |
| adverbs | *possibly*, *probably*, *perhaps*, *likely* (AmE), *apparently*, *generally*, *largely*, *relatively* |
| adjectives and nouns | *possible*, *likely*, *potential*, *a possibility*, *a tendency* |
| approximators | *approximately*, *about*, *around*, *roughly*, *some*, *to some extent*, *in part* |
| quantifiers and frequency | *many*, *most*, *some* (instead of *all*); *often*, *usually* (instead of *always*) |
| limiting phrases | *in this sample*, *under these conditions*, *in the cases studied*, *on the basis of these data* |
| attribution | *according to Wang (2019)*, *it has been argued that*, *X claims that* |
| personal judgement | *we believe*, *in our view*, *I would argue that* |

::: theorem What to hedge, and what not to {#rule-what-to-hedge}
1. **Hedge** interpretations, explanations of results, generalisations beyond the data, claims about cause, predictions, and claims that go against other researchers: *These differences **may** reflect…*, *This **suggests** that…*, *The effect **appears to** be stronger in…*.
2. **Do not hedge** the facts of your own procedure and data, definitions, and established knowledge: *We interviewed 40 teachers* (not ✗ *We may have interviewed…*). *Water boils at 100 °C at sea level* (not ✗ *Water possibly boils…*).
3. **Hedge the right part of the claim.** A claim can be narrowed in several places: its **certainty** (*may*, *probably*), its **quantity** (*most*, *some*), its **frequency** (*often*, *tends to*), its **scope** (*in this sample*, *among younger users*), and its **strength of relation** (*is associated with* rather than *causes*; *contributes to* rather than *determines*).
4. In general, **one hedge per claim** is enough; two are possible if they narrow different parts (*heavy use **may** contribute to shorter sleep **among the pupils surveyed***). Several hedges on the same part make the writer sound unsure or evasive.
:::

::: proof
Rules 1 and 2 follow from the purpose of hedging: it marks the distance between evidence and claim. Where there is no distance — in reporting what the writer did, or in stating what everyone in the field accepts — a hedge would be inaccurate, and it would make readers wonder why the writer is uncertain about their own procedure. Rule 3 is the most useful in practice. Swales and Feak (*Academic Writing for Graduate Students*, 3rd edition, 2012) teach the same strategy of qualifying claims: most overclaims can be fixed by narrowing just one element — often the verb of cause or the scope — rather than by adding *may* to everything. Rule 4 comes from the same source and from Hyland's corpus studies, which show experienced writers using hedges precisely, not in clusters.
:::

::: example Finding the hedges {#ex-find}
Underline (or list) the hedges and boosters in this passage from a discussion section, written for this course, and say what each one does.

*The results clearly show that participants in the mixed-language group recalled more words than those in the English-only group. This difference may be explained by the richer associations that two languages provide, although it is also possible that the mixed-language materials were simply more interesting. Since the sample was relatively small and consisted entirely of university students, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, they indicate that bilingual materials could be a useful tool in vocabulary teaching.*
::: solution
***clearly show*** — boosters. The writer is confident about the result itself (a measured difference), which is appropriate if the statistics support it.

***may be explained by*** — hedge on certainty: an interpretation, not a fact.

***it is also possible that*** — hedge (adjective): offers an alternative explanation, which makes the first one less certain and shows fairness.

***simply*** — not a hedge; it downplays the alternative explanation.

***relatively small*** — hedge on degree, part of a statement of limitation.

***should be interpreted with caution*** — a limiting statement about the whole study; *should* is deontic advice to the reader.

***indicate*** — hedge (lexical verb): weaker than *show*, stronger than *suggest*.

***could be a useful tool*** — hedge on certainty for a practical implication, which goes furthest beyond the data.

The pattern is typical: boosting the result, hedging the interpretation, and hedging most of all the implication.
:::
:::

::: example Fixing an overclaim {#ex-overclaim}
Rewrite Student A's sentence from the start of the chapter so that it matches the evidence (a survey of 120 pupils in one city, which found that pupils who used social media for more than two hours in the evening reported sleeping less). Show each change.

*This study proves that social media causes sleep problems in teenagers.*
::: solution
1. *proves* → ***suggests*** (or *indicates*): one survey supports a claim; it does not prove it.
2. *causes* → ***is associated with*** or ***may contribute to***: a survey shows a relationship, not a cause. *Is associated with* states what was found; *may contribute to* offers a hedged causal interpretation.
3. *social media* → ***heavy evening use of social media***: the finding concerned more than two hours in the evening, not all use.
4. *sleep problems* → ***shorter sleep***: the survey measured reported sleep time, not clinical problems.
5. *in teenagers* → ***among the pupils surveyed*** or ***among secondary school pupils in this city***: the sample limits the scope.

Result: *The findings suggest that heavy evening use of social media may contribute to shorter sleep among secondary school pupils.* (or, more cautiously: *…is associated with shorter reported sleep among the pupils surveyed.*)

Five changes are needed, but only one of them (*may*) is a modal verb. Most of the precision comes from verbs and from narrowing the scope.
:::
:::

::: warning Hedges in the wrong places
✗ *In this study, we may have interviewed 30 nurses.* ✓ *In this study, we interviewed 30 nurses.* (your own procedure)

✗ *It could be possible that this might perhaps be one possible factor.* ✓ *This may be one factor.* (one hedge is enough)

✗ *This proves that all students prefer online learning.* ✓ *This suggests that many of the students surveyed preferred online learning.* (overclaim)

✗ *Maybe the results are because of the small sample.* ✓ *The results may reflect the small sample.* (*maybe* is too informal for academic writing)
:::

## Boosters and their limits

::: theorem Using boosters {#rule-boosters}
1. Use boosters where the evidence is strong or the point is agreed: *The data **clearly** show a rise in…*, *It is **well established** that…*, *This **demonstrates** that…*.
2. Use them to mark **contrast with an expectation**: *Contrary to earlier reports, the effect was **in fact** larger among older participants.*
3. Avoid **absolute** words unless they are literally true: *always*, *never*, *all*, *every*, *prove*, *undeniable*, *everyone knows*. One counter-example refutes them.
4. Avoid **emotional** boosters in academic writing: *extremely*, *totally*, *absolutely*, *amazing*, *huge*. Use precise words or numbers instead: *a large effect* or *a 40% increase* rather than *a huge effect*.
5. Boosters such as *obviously* and *of course* can sound condescending to readers who did not find the point obvious; use them sparingly.
:::

::: proof
Hyland's studies of research articles (1998, 2005) found boosters much less frequent than hedges, and found them concentrated where writers claim solidarity with readers or contrast their results with previous work. A booster is a promise: the writer tells the reader that the claim will stand up. If the evidence does not support the promise, the booster damages the writer's credibility more than a missing hedge would. *Prove* is a special case: in mathematics and logic a proof is possible, but empirical research gathers evidence that supports or weakens a claim, so most fields avoid *prove* for their own results.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is the most appropriate for the discussion section of a study of 50 language learners?
- [ ] *Our results prove that learners always benefit from feedback.*
- [x] *Our results indicate that learners who received regular feedback made fewer errors.*
- [ ] *Our results might possibly perhaps suggest some benefit.*
- [ ] *Maybe feedback is good for learners.*
::: solution
The second sentence makes a clear claim and limits it to what was observed. The first overclaims (*prove*, *always*), the third overhedges until there is no claim at all, and the fourth is too informal and too vague for academic writing.
:::

## Reporting verbs: a scale

The verbs used to report evidence and other writers' claims carry modality of their own. Arranged roughly from weak to strong:

- *speculate*, *hypothesise*, *propose* — an idea, not yet supported
- *suggest*, *imply* — the evidence points this way
- *indicate*, *argue*, *claim* — the evidence or the writer supports it more firmly (*claim* often signals that the reporter is not convinced)
- *show*, *find*, *demonstrate*, *establish*, *confirm* — the evidence is strong (*show* and *find* are the most frequent neutral choices)

Choosing among them is a way of hedging or boosting **someone else's** claim. *Li (2020) **shows** that…* tells the reader that you accept Li's conclusion; *Li (2020) **claims** that…* suggests that you do not. Reporting verbs are practised in [[academic-writing/citation-referencing]] and [[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]].

::: quiz
A writer reports: *Garcia (2018) claims that homework has no effect on achievement.* What does *claims* suggest about the writer's view?
- [ ] The writer fully accepts Garcia's conclusion.
- [x] The writer is reporting Garcia's view without accepting it, and may go on to question it.
- [ ] Garcia proved the point experimentally.
- [ ] Garcia was only speculating, without any data.
::: solution
*Claim* reports a firm assertion by the source but keeps the writer at a distance from it; it often comes before a counter-argument. To signal acceptance the writer would use *shows* or *found*; for an idea without data, *speculates* or *proposes*.
:::
:::

::: example Removing overhedging {#ex-overhedge}
A student wrote this paragraph in a report on a library survey. Rewrite it so that it is clear but still accurate.

*It might perhaps be suggested that it is possible that students could possibly use the library somewhat less than they perhaps did in the past. The survey seems to appear to show that maybe about 60% of the students may use it less than once a month.*
::: solution
A clear version:

*The survey suggests that students use the library less than they did in the past. About 60% of respondents reported using it less than once a month.*

Changes:

1. The first sentence had six hedges on one claim (*might perhaps be suggested*, *it is possible that*, *could possibly*, *somewhat*, *perhaps*). One hedging verb, *suggests*, is enough, because the comparison with the past is an interpretation.
2. In the second sentence, the 60% is a fact about the student's own data and needs no hedge on certainty: *about* (an approximator) is fine if the figure is rounded, but *seems to appear to*, *maybe* and *may* are wrong here.
3. *reported using* limits the claim accurately: the survey measured what students said, not what they did.

If the student also wants to note a limitation, it belongs in a separate sentence: *Since the survey relied on self-reports, actual use may differ.*
:::
:::

## Hedging in speech

Conversation and presentations have their own hedges: *sort of*, *kind of*, *a bit*, *I think*, *I mean*, *I'd say*, *more or less*, *or something*, *I'm not sure, but…*. They soften opinions, make disagreement friendlier and protect the speaker from sounding too sure (Lakoff's original examples, *sort of* and *kind of*, were spoken). In a seminar, *I'd say the main problem is…* and *I'm not sure I agree — wouldn't it be…?* are more usual than *The main problem is…* and *You're wrong*. These spoken forms are informal, and they do not belong in academic writing. Seminar language is practised in [[presentations-and-discussion/seminars-discussion]].

::: example From seminar to essay {#ex-seminar}
In a seminar, Aisha says: *I'd say online reviews are sort of unreliable, to be honest — I mean, loads of them are fake, I think.* Turn her point into one or two sentences for an academic essay, keeping her degree of confidence.
::: solution
A model: *Online reviews may be an unreliable guide to quality, since a considerable proportion of them appear to be fake.* If she has a source: *…since a considerable proportion of them are reported to be fake (Author, year).*

Changes: *I'd say* and *I think* (personal, spoken) become a hedge on the claim itself (*may be*); *sort of unreliable* (spoken hedge) becomes *an unreliable guide to quality* (precise); *to be honest* and *I mean* (spoken discourse markers) disappear; *loads of them* (informal quantity) becomes *a considerable proportion* — or, better, an exact figure from a cited study; *are fake, I think* becomes *appear to be fake*. The strength stays roughly the same: Aisha was fairly, but not completely, sure.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Chinese academic writing hedges too.** Chinese has its own hedging devices: 可能, 或许, 也许, 似乎, 在一定程度上, 初步表明, 大致, 笔者认为. So the idea of hedging is not new to Chinese writers. What differs are the conventions of particular genres, and the English forms.
- **Evidence from corpus studies.** Comparing abstracts of applied linguistics articles, Hu and Cao (2011) found that abstracts in English-medium journals used more hedges than those in Chinese-medium journals. Hyland and Milton (1997) found that Cantonese-speaking school leavers in Hong Kong, writing in English, relied on a smaller range of devices and expressed certainty less precisely than British school leavers. Such studies describe groups of texts, not individual writers, and they say nothing about ability: they show which conventions a writer needs to learn.
- **证明 is not always *prove*.** In Chinese academic prose 证明 is used more widely than English *prove*: 研究证明 is often best translated *studies have shown* or *research indicates*, not *studies have proved*.
- **一定, 肯定, 必然 in arguments.** Translated word for word, these become boosters (*certainly*, *definitely*, *inevitably*) that may be stronger than the writer intends. Consider *is likely to*, *tends to* or a plain statement.
- **Avoid *maybe* and *I think* in essays.** 也许 and 我认为 translate easily into *maybe* and *I think*, which are spoken forms. In academic English use *perhaps*, *possibly*, *it seems that*, *this suggests*, or (where the field allows the first person) *I would argue*.
:::

::: history
The word *hedge* in this sense comes from George Lakoff's paper "Hedges: a study in meaning criteria and the logic of fuzzy concepts", read at the Chicago Linguistic Society in 1972, which was about words such as *sort of*, *technically* and *strictly speaking* that change how strictly a category applies (*A whale is technically a mammal*). Applied linguists soon extended the term to all the ways writers qualify claims. Greg Myers (1989) analysed hedges in scientific articles as politeness towards other scientists, and Ken Hyland's *Hedging in Scientific Research Articles* (1998), based on a corpus of biology articles, showed how hedges express both accuracy and respect for readers. Hyland's *Metadiscourse* (2005) set hedges beside boosters and other markers of stance. Most academic writing textbooks now include a unit on hedging; Swales and Feak's *Academic Writing for Graduate Students*, first published in 1994, is one widely used example.
:::

## Where this leads

Hedging and boosting run through the academic writing courses: the tone of academic prose in [[academic-writing/academic-style]], the limits of a study in [[academic-writing/discussion-conclusions]], abstracts in [[academic-writing/abstracts-titles]], and judging other writers' claims in [[academic-reading/claims-and-evidence]] and [[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]]. The links between hedging and politeness are followed up in [[modality/modality-politeness]] and in the course *Pragmatics* ([[pragmatics/politeness-face]]).

::: summary
- Hedges reduce commitment or narrow a claim; boosters increase commitment. Good academic writing matches the strength of each claim to its evidence ([[#def-hedge]], [[#def-booster]]).
- Writers hedge for accuracy, for protection, and out of respect for readers.
- Hedge interpretations, generalisations, causes, predictions and disagreements; do not hedge your own procedure, your data or established facts ([[#rule-what-to-hedge]]).
- Hedge the right element — certainty, quantity, frequency, scope or strength of relation — and usually only one element per claim.
- Use boosters where the evidence is strong; avoid *prove*, *always*, *never* and emotional intensifiers ([[#rule-boosters]]).
- Reporting verbs form a scale (*suggest* < *indicate* < *show*) and hedge or boost other writers' claims.
- Spoken hedges (*sort of*, *I think*, *maybe*) are informal and do not belong in academic prose.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Hedge or not? {level=1}
Which of these sentences from a research paper should be hedged? (a) *We collected data from 85 hospitals.* (b) *Longer shifts cause more medical errors.* (c) *The mean age of participants was 34.* (d) *This pattern will continue in the next decade.* (e) *The difference is due to better training.*
::: solution
(a) No — procedure. (b) Yes — a causal claim: *Longer shifts may contribute to / are associated with more errors.* (c) No — data. (d) Yes — a prediction: *This pattern is likely to continue.* (e) Yes — an interpretation: *The difference may be due to / may reflect better training.*
:::

Mark each word or phrase H (hedge) or B (booster). (a) *demonstrate* (b) *tend to* (c) *undoubtedly* (d) *to some extent* (e) *indicate* (f) *in fact* (g) *approximately* (h) *it is clear that*
::: solution
(a) B (b) H (c) B (d) H (e) H (weaker than *show*) (f) B (g) H (h) B

Put the verbs in order from the weakest to the strongest claim: *show*, *suggest*, *speculate*, *indicate*, *demonstrate*.
::: solution
*speculate* < *suggest* < *indicate* < *show* < *demonstrate*. (*show* and *demonstrate* are close; *demonstrate* often implies a deliberate, conclusive test.)

::: exercise Narrow the claim {level=2}
Rewrite each overclaim by hedging one element. Say which element you changed. (a) *Bilingual children are better at problem solving.* (b) *Online classes reduce student motivation.* (c) *Chinese students prefer written exams.* (d) *Exercise prevents depression.*
::: solution
Possible answers: (a) *Bilingual children **tend to** perform better on some problem-solving tasks.* (frequency and scope) (b) *Online classes **may** reduce motivation **for some students**.* (certainty and scope) (c) ***Many of the** Chinese students **surveyed** preferred written exams.* (quantity and scope — and note that a generalisation about a nationality needs particular care) (d) *Regular exercise **is associated with a lower risk** of depression.* (strength of relation)
:::
:::

::: exercise Remove overhedging {level=2}
Rewrite with one hedge only. (a) *It may possibly be the case that the drug could perhaps have some effect.* (b) *The results might seem to suggest a possible link.* (c) *Perhaps it is maybe likely that prices will probably rise.*
::: solution
(a) *The drug may have some effect.* (b) *The results suggest a possible link.* / *The results suggest a link.* (c) *Prices are likely to rise.* / *Prices will probably rise.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Choose the strength {level=2}
For each piece of evidence, write one sentence with a suitable reporting verb and modal or adverb. (a) Three large experiments all found the same result. (b) One small pilot study found a weak correlation. (c) A researcher proposes an idea without data. (d) A survey of 2,000 people found that 70% supported the policy.
::: solution
Possible answers: (a) *These experiments **clearly show** that…* / *There is strong evidence that…* (b) *A pilot study **suggests** that there **may** be a weak relationship between…* (c) *Smith **speculates** / **proposes** that…* (d) *The survey **found** that 70% of respondents supported the policy* (a fact about the data: no hedge needed), *which **suggests** that the policy **is likely to** have broad support* (an inference: hedged).
:::
:::

::: exercise Revise a paragraph {level=3}
Revise this paragraph from a student's discussion section. It contains overclaims, overhedging and informal hedges.

*Our survey of 60 students at one university proves that all students hate group work. Maybe this is because they kind of think it is unfair. It might perhaps possibly also be because of the teachers. We may have used a questionnaire with 20 questions. In the future, universities will stop using group work.*
::: hint
Find the facts first (they need no hedge), then the interpretations (they need one hedge each), then the prediction.
:::
::: solution
A model revision:

*We surveyed 60 students at one university, using a questionnaire with 20 questions. The responses suggest that many of these students had negative views of group work. One possible reason is that they perceived it as unfair; the way group work was organised by teachers may also have played a part. Since the sample was small and drawn from a single institution, these findings may not apply more widely. Further research could examine whether similar attitudes are found elsewhere.*

Changes: the method is stated plainly (*may have used* → *used*); *proves that all students hate* → *suggest that many of these students had negative views* (verb, quantity, scope, register); *Maybe … kind of think* → *One possible reason is that they perceived* (informal hedges replaced by an academic one); *might perhaps possibly also be because of* → *may also have played a part* (one hedge); the unsupported prediction is replaced by a statement of limitations and a suggestion for further research.
:::
:::

::: exercise Write an abstract sentence set {level=3}
Imagine a small study of your own (for example, how 30 classmates use a vocabulary app). Write four sentences: (1) what you did, (2) what you found, (3) what it may mean, (4) a limitation. Use no hedge in (1), at most an approximator in (2), one hedge in (3) and one in (4).
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

*(1) We asked 30 second-year students to record their use of a vocabulary app for four weeks. (2) Students who used the app on at least five days a week learned about twice as many new words as those who used it less often. (3) This suggests that frequent, short practice may be more effective than occasional long sessions. (4) However, the more frequent users may also have been more motivated, which could explain part of the difference.*

Check: (1) plain past simple; (2) one approximator (*about*); (3) *suggests* and *may* — two hedges that work on one claim, which is acceptable because *suggests* marks the inference and *may* the comparison; a stricter version would use only one; (4) *may* (and *could*) for an alternative explanation.
:::
