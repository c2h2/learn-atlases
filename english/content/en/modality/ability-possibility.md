Two short puzzles show why ability and possibility need a chapter of their own.

First, a story. *The fire spread very quickly, but all the guests **were able to** get out of the hotel.* Most native speakers would not say ✗ *all the guests could get out* here, although *could* is "the past of *can*" and *can* means ability. Yet *I **could** smell smoke* is perfectly natural, and so is *Nobody **could** get out*.

Second, the weather. A Chinese student, Liu Yang, writes to a friend: ✗ *Bring a coat — it can rain tomorrow.* The friend understands, but it sounds wrong. *It **may** rain tomorrow*, *It **might** rain* or *It **could** rain* are all fine. And yet *It **can** rain a lot in Shanghai in June* is correct.

The rules behind these puzzles are clear once two distinctions are made: between **general** ability and **one achievement** on a particular occasion, and between possibility **in general** and possibility in **a particular situation**. This chapter sets out *can*, *could* and *be able to* for ability, the permission uses that grow out of them, and *may*, *might*, *could* and *can* for possibility. The basic uses of *can* and *could* were introduced in [[core-grammar/first-modals#rule-can]]; the two families of modal meaning are explained in [[modality/what-modals-do]].

## Ability

::: definition Ability {#def-ability}
**Ability** is a kind of dynamic modality: it says what the subject is capable of doing, because of skill, knowledge, physical capacity or circumstances. It is expressed mainly by ***can***, ***could*** and ***be able to***, and also by *know how to* (learned techniques), *manage to* and *succeed in* (achievements).
:::

The forms divide the work between them, because *can* has only two forms and *be able to* has all of them.

| time | general ability | one achievement |
|---|---|---|
| present | *She **can** play the violin.* / *She **is able to** play…* (more formal) | *I **can** see you now — wave again!* |
| past | *At five she **could** play simple tunes.* / *was able to* | *She **was able to** / **managed to** finish the piece.* |
| future | *By June she'**ll be able to** play it.* | *You'**ll be able to** see the castle from the bridge.* |
| perfect | *She'**s** never **been able to** sing.* | *I **haven't been able to** reach him today.* |
| after another verb | *I'd love **to be able to** play.* *I hate **not being able to** help.* | |

::: theorem *Could* and *was able to* in the past {#rule-could-past}
1. For **general ability** in the past — a skill or capacity that lasted — use ***could*** or ***was able to***: *My grandfather **could** speak Russian.* *By the age of ten she **could** / **was able to** swim a kilometre.*
2. For **one achievement** on a particular occasion, use ***was able to***, ***managed to*** or ***succeeded in*** + *-ing*, not affirmative *could*: *The road was flooded, but we **were able to** / **managed to** get through.* ✗ *we could get through*.
3. In the **negative**, *couldn't* is used for both: *I **couldn't** swim until I was twelve.* *We **couldn't** get through — the road was flooded.*
4. With verbs of **perception and understanding** — *see*, *hear*, *smell*, *taste*, *feel*, *understand*, *remember*, *guess* — affirmative *could* is normal for a particular occasion too: *I **could** hear voices downstairs.* *She **could** tell he was lying.*
5. With ***only***, ***hardly***, ***almost*** and ***all*** (*All I could do was…*), which make the sentence close to a negative, *could* is also possible for one occasion: *We **could** only see the top of the mountain.*
:::

::: proof
Swan (2016) and Quirk et al. (1985) give the same rule, and the reason lies in what *could* reports. Affirmative *could* says that the ability existed; it does not say that it was used. *I could swim when I was five* tells us about a capacity, not about any particular swim. When we report one successful action, we need a form that says the action actually happened, and *was able to* and *managed to* do that (*managed to* also suggests difficulty). The negative is different: if the ability was missing, the action did not happen, so *couldn't* already reports the outcome. Perception verbs fit the same logic from the other side: with *see* or *hear*, having the capacity at that moment and using it are the same thing, so *I could hear voices* reports what happened. The rule is a strong tendency rather than an absolute law, and speakers sometimes use *could* for one achievement, especially when the sentence also stresses the ability (*At last I could breathe again*), but *was able to* is always safe.
:::

::: warning *Could* for a single success
✗ *I lost my passport, but I could get a new one the same day.* ✓ *…but I **was able to** / **managed to** get a new one the same day.*

✗ *After three hours we could find the hotel.* ✓ *After three hours we **found** / **managed to find** the hotel.*

✓ *We looked for hours, but we **couldn't** find the hotel.* (negative: *could* is fine)

✓ *From the hotel we **could** see the whole bay.* (perception verb: *could* is fine)

Often the plain past simple is the best choice: *After three hours we found the hotel.*
:::

::: example Telling a story {#ex-story}
Complete the story with *could*, *couldn't*, *was/were able to* or *managed to*. More than one answer may be possible; explain each choice.

*When Mehmet was young he (1) ___ run very fast, and he won every race at school. But last year, at forty, he entered a half-marathon. At the twelfth kilometre he (2) ___ feel his legs getting heavy. He (3) ___ keep up with the leaders, but he did not give up, and in the end he (4) ___ finish in just under two hours. Afterwards he (5) ___ hardly walk.*
::: solution
(1) *could* (or *was able to*) — general ability over a period of his life.

(2) *could* — a perception verb (*feel*): affirmative *could* is normal for one occasion.

(3) *couldn't* (or *wasn't able to*) — a negative: *couldn't* works for one occasion.

(4) *was able to* or *managed to* — one successful achievement. Not *could*. *Managed to* stresses the effort, which suits the story.

(5) *could* — *hardly* makes the sentence nearly negative, so *could* is natural. (*was hardly able to* is possible but heavy.)
:::
:::

::: theorem *Can* with verbs of perception {#rule-can-perception}
With ***see***, ***hear***, ***smell***, ***taste***, ***feel***, and with ***understand*** and ***remember***, English often uses *can* where the action is happening now, because these verbs rarely take the progressive: *I **can** hear someone at the door* (more natural than *I hear…*, and not ✗ *I'm hearing…*). *Can you see the red building?* This use is especially common in British English; American English uses it too, and also uses the plain present more often (*I hear someone at the door*) (Swan 2016).
:::

Two other ways of talking about ability are worth knowing. ***Know how to*** stresses a learned technique: *Do you know how to change a tyre?* It is not used for natural capacities (✗ *I know how to see*). And ***be capable of*** + *-ing* is used for what someone is able to do in principle, often in an evaluative way: *She is capable of running the whole department.* *He's capable of anything* (a criticism).

::: quiz
*The exam was hard, but in the end I ___ answer all the questions.* Which is best?
- [ ] *could*
- [x] *was able to*
- [ ] *can*
- [ ] *could be able to*
::: solution
This reports one successful achievement on a particular occasion, so *was able to* (or *managed to*) is needed. Affirmative *could* would suggest only that the ability existed. *Could be able to* combines two forms of ability for no reason.
:::
:::

## Permission

Permission is deontic: someone with authority allows an action. The basic forms — *Can I…?*, *Could I…?*, *May I…?* — are introduced in [[core-grammar/first-modals]] and [[everyday-english/requests-offers]]. What matters at this level is the difference of register, and the past.

::: theorem Forms of permission {#rule-permission}
1. ***Can*** is the ordinary, neutral form, for giving, refusing and asking permission: *You **can** leave your bags here.* ***Can** I use your charger?*
2. ***Could*** is more tentative in requests for permission: ***Could** I leave a little early today?* In statements, *could* means general permission in the **past**: *As children we **could** stay up late at weekends.*
3. ***May*** is formal: ***May** I come in?* *Visitors **may** take photographs.* *You **may not** smoke in this building.* In conversation it now sounds polite or distant; in notices and regulations it is normal.
4. ***Might*** for permission (***Might** I suggest…?*) is very formal and rare, and is mainly British.
5. For permission on **one occasion in the past**, and in all forms *can* lacks, use ***be allowed to***: *Yesterday we **were allowed to** leave early* (✗ *we could leave early*). *Will we **be allowed to** bring food?* *She's never **been allowed to** drive her father's car.*
:::

::: proof
The pattern in 5 mirrors the ability rule ([[#rule-could-past]]): *could* reports that the permission existed, and *was allowed to* reports that the action took place because of it. The rise of *can* at the expense of *may* is documented in corpus studies: Coates (1983) found that deontic *may* was largely restricted to formal contexts, and Leech et al. (2009) found that *may* became less frequent in written English between 1961 and the early 1990s. The school rule that *Can I…?* asks about ability and only *May I…?* asks permission was never a description of how English is used; both are standard, and they differ in formality (Swan 2016; Huddleston & Pullum 2002).
:::

::: example Permission in three registers {#ex-permission}
A university library is changing its rules: from next term, students will be allowed to bring drinks with lids into the reading rooms, but food will still not be allowed. Express the change (a) in a formal notice, (b) in an email from a librarian to students, (c) in conversation between two students. Explain the choice of forms.
::: solution
(a) *From 1 October, readers **may** bring covered drinks into the reading rooms. Food **may not** be consumed in any part of the library.* — Formal notices use deontic *may* and *may not*, often with the passive and impersonal subjects (*readers*).

(b) *From next term you'**ll be allowed to** bring drinks into the reading rooms, as long as they have a lid. Food still **isn't allowed**, I'm afraid.* — *Be allowed to* is needed for the future (*can* has no future form, though *you can bring drinks from next term* is also natural, since *can* often refers to the future). The tone is neutral and friendly.

(c) *Guess what — we **can** take coffee into the library next term! — Really? What about food? — No, you still **can't** eat in there.* — *Can* and *can't* are the ordinary conversational forms.

The rule is the same in all three; only the register changes. A reader who wrote *You may bring drinks* in (c) would sound oddly formal, and *You can't eat* in a notice would sound informal (though many modern notices do use it).
:::
:::

## Possibility

Possibility is where the modal meanings change families. *I can swim* is dynamic; *You can go* is deontic; but *It may rain* is epistemic, a judgement about what is true or will be true. Between them is a kind of possibility that is not quite either.

::: definition General possibility and specific possibility {#def-possibility}
**General possibility** (Leech's "theoretical possibility") is what is possible in principle, or sometimes happens: *Snakes **can** be dangerous.* *It **can** take weeks to get a visa.* It means roughly ‘it is sometimes the case’, and is expressed by ***can*** (past ***could***).

**Specific possibility** (Leech's "factual possibility") is a judgement that something may be true, or may happen, in a particular situation: *That snake **may** be dangerous — don't touch it.* *The visa **might** take weeks.* It is epistemic, and is expressed by ***may***, ***might*** and ***could*** (Leech 2004; Swan 2016).
:::

::: theorem *Can* for general possibility {#rule-can-general}
1. *Can* expresses what is **sometimes** true or **possible in principle**: *Winters in Harbin **can** be extremely cold.* *Children **can** be very cruel.* *This medicine **can** cause drowsiness.*
2. In the past, ***could*** has the same meaning: *My grandmother **could** be very strict* (she was sometimes strict).
3. In **affirmative** statements about a **particular** present or future situation, *can* is not used for possibility: ✗ *Take a coat — it can be cold tonight.* ✓ *…it may / might / could be cold tonight.*
4. In **negatives** and **questions**, *can* is the normal epistemic form: *That **can't** be right.* ***Can** it really be true?* (See [[modality/deduction]].)
:::

::: proof
The difference between rule 1 and rule 3 is the difference between a generalisation and a judgement. *Winters can be extremely cold* says that some winters are; it is close to *are sometimes*. *It may be cold tonight* is about one particular night and rates the chance that it will be cold. Quirk et al. (1985) and Leech (2004) describe this contrast, and it explains the second puzzle at the start of the chapter: *it can rain tomorrow* sounds like a generalisation about tomorrow, which makes no sense. Rule 4 is the exception that makes the system complete: English has no other short negative for impossibility, so *can't* fills that place in epistemic meaning.
:::

::: theorem *May*, *might* and *could* for specific possibility {#rule-may-might}
1. ***May***, ***might*** and ***could*** all express a **specific possibility** about the present or the future: *She **may** be at lunch.* *The flight **might** be delayed.* *The keys **could** be in your other coat.*
2. Many speakers feel *might* and *could* as slightly less certain than *may*; others feel no difference. *May* is more frequent in formal writing, *might* and *could* in conversation (Biber et al. 1999; Swan 2016).
3. With ***well***, the possibility becomes a probability: *She **may well** be right* (= ‘it is quite likely’). *It **could well** rain.*
4. **Negatives** differ: *may not* and *might not* mean ‘possibly not’ (*He **might not** know*), but ***couldn't*** means ‘impossible’, like *can't*: *He **couldn't** be the thief — he was in hospital.*
5. **Questions** about possibility usually use *could* or *might*, or a paraphrase: ***Could** this be the reason?* ***Might** they have got lost?* *Do you think she's ill?* Epistemic *may* is unusual in direct questions: ? *May she be ill?*
:::

::: proof
The negatives follow the pattern of negation explained in [[modality/what-modals-do#rule-negation]]. *May not* and *might not* negate the main verb: *He might not know* = ‘it is possible that he does **not** know’. *Couldn't* negates the possibility itself: *He couldn't be the thief* = ‘it is **not** possible that he is the thief’. This is why *It may not be true* (perhaps it isn't) and *It couldn't be true* (it is impossible) are far apart, although *may* and *could* are close in the affirmative. The near-absence of epistemic *may* in questions is noted by Coates (1983) and Huddleston & Pullum (2002); a speaker who asks is not yet in a position to judge, so the question is usually put with *could*, *might* or *Do you think…?*.
:::

::: warning *Can* for a specific possibility, and *couldn't* for *might not*
✗ *Don't wait for me. I can be late.* ✓ *Don't wait for me. I **may** / **might** be late.*

✗ *Call Ana — she can know the answer.* ✓ *Call Ana — she **might** know the answer.*

✗ *I'm not sure he'll come. He couldn't come.* ✓ *I'm not sure he'll come. He **might not** come.*

*Couldn't* rules the possibility out; *might not* and *may not* leave it open.
:::

::: example *Can*, *may*, *might* or *could*? {#ex-possibility}
Choose the best form or forms and explain. (a) *Driving in fog (can / may) be very dangerous.* (b) *Where's Priya? — She (can / might) be in the library.* (c) *The meeting (may / can) go on until seven, so don't book dinner for six.* (d) *I'm not sure the shop is open. It (may not / couldn't) open on Sundays.* (e) *That (can't / mustn't) be Dev's car — his is blue.* (f) *Leave early: the roads (can / could) be busy tonight.*
::: solution
(a) *can* — a general truth about driving in fog (sometimes it is dangerous). *May* is also possible in formal writing with the same general meaning, but *can* is the usual choice.

(b) *might* — a guess about where Priya is now: a specific possibility. *May* and *could* also work. *Can* does not.

(c) *may* — a specific possibility about one meeting.

(d) *may not* — ‘perhaps it doesn't open on Sundays’. *Couldn't* would mean it is impossible, which contradicts *I'm not sure*.

(e) *can't* — an impossible conclusion. *Mustn't* is not used for this in British English (see [[modality/deduction]]).

(f) *could* — tonight is a specific situation. *Can* would turn the sentence into a generalisation about the roads, which does not fit *tonight*; but *Leave early: the roads can be busy in the evening* would be fine.
:::
:::

## Hypothetical possibility, suggestions and concessions

Three further uses of *could*, *might* and *may* grow out of possibility.

::: theorem Hypothetical *could* and *might*; concessive *may* {#rule-hypothetical}
1. ***Could*** and ***might*** express what would be possible **if** circumstances were different, even when the *if* is not said: *I **could** lend you the money* (if you need it). *With a bit more practice, you **might** win next time.* *It **could** be worse!* Full conditionals are treated in [[complex-sentences/conditionals]].
2. ***Could*** and ***might*** make **suggestions**: *You **could** try the library.* *We **might** go for a walk later.* *You **might** want to check that again* (a polite, sometimes pointed, suggestion: see [[modality/modality-politeness]]).
3. ***May*** and ***might*** + ***but*** make **concessions**: the speaker accepts one point in order to make a stronger one. *He **may** be clever, **but** he doesn't work.* *The hotel **might** be cheap, **but** it's miles from anywhere.* Here *may* does not express doubt: the speaker usually accepts that he is clever (Quirk et al. 1985; Swan 2016).
4. ***May/might as well*** means ‘there is no better alternative, so let's do it’: *The bus has gone — we **might as well** walk.*
:::

::: example What does *could* mean here? {#ex-could}
Say whether *could* expresses past ability, past permission, specific possibility, hypothetical possibility or a suggestion. (a) *You could ask your tutor for an extension.* (b) *When I lived in Tokyo I could read about a thousand characters.* (c) *That could be the answer to our problem.* (d) *I could never live in a city that big.* (e) *At my old school we could wear what we liked on Fridays.*
::: solution
(a) Suggestion — the speaker proposes an action; compare *Why don't you ask…?*

(b) Past ability — a general capacity at that time.

(c) Specific possibility (epistemic) — ‘perhaps that is the answer’.

(d) Hypothetical possibility — ‘I wouldn't be able to, even if I tried’. Also a statement about the speaker's dynamic ability (or willingness) in an imagined situation.

(e) Past permission — a general rule at the old school.
:::
:::

## Possibility and ability in Chinese

::: note English and Chinese
- **会, 能 and 可以 for ability.** 会 is used for learned skills (我会开车 → *I can drive*), 能 for capacity in a situation or to a degree (我能游一千米 → *I can swim a kilometre*; 他病了，今天不能来 → *He's ill, so he can't come today*), and 可以 for what is possible or allowed (这个房间可以住四个人 → *This room can sleep four*; 你可以走了 → *You can go now*). English uses *can* for all of them, so the problem is not choosing among them but noticing the past rules above.
- **Potential complements.** Chinese often expresses ability with a verb and a result complement: 看得见 / 看不见, 听得懂 / 听不懂, 打得开 / 打不开. In English these become *can* with the plain verb: *I can see it*, *I can't understand*, *I can't open it*. Do not translate the result separately (✗ *I can't see clearly it*): *I can't see it clearly* keeps *clearly* only if 清楚 is really there.
- **One achievement.** Chinese usually reports a success with a result complement and 了 (我终于找到了酒店), with no word for ability at all. English uses the plain past (*I found the hotel*) or *managed to* / *was able to*, but not *could*: ✗ *Finally I could find the hotel.*
- **可能 and *can*.** 可能 is the usual word for a specific possibility: 明天可能下雨 → *It may / might / could rain tomorrow*, not ✗ *It can rain tomorrow*. When Chinese uses 会 or 有时候会 for a general tendency (哈尔滨的冬天会非常冷), English uses *can*: *Winters in Harbin can be extremely cold.*
- **不可能 and 可能不.** 他不可能知道 → *He can't / couldn't know* (impossible). 他可能不知道 → *He may / might not know* (possibly not). The Chinese word order shows the difference clearly; in English it is carried by the choice of modal.
:::

::: history
The modern division of work between *can* and *may* is the result of a long shift. Old English *magan*, the ancestor of *may*, meant ‘be able, have power’ — the same root survives in the noun *might* ‘power’ (*with all his might*) — while *cunnan*, the ancestor of *can*, meant ‘know’. In Middle English *can* gradually took over the meaning of ability from *may*, and *may* moved towards permission and possibility; standard histories of English and the *Oxford English Dictionary* trace both changes. A similar change is still going on: in the twentieth century *can* has been taking over permission from *may* in everyday speech (Coates 1983; Leech et al. 2009). *Be able to*, which fills the gaps in the forms of *can*, uses the adjective *able*, borrowed from French in the Middle English period.
:::

## Where this leads

Possibility is the weak end of a scale of certainty; [[modality/deduction]] completes the scale with *must*, *can't*, *should* and *will*, and [[modality/past-modals]] adds *could have*, *might have* and *may have*. Suggestions and requests with *could* and *might* are treated in [[modality/modality-politeness]], and possibility expressed with adverbs and adjectives (*possibly*, *it is possible that*) in [[modality/modality-without-modals]].

::: summary
- *Can*, *could* and *be able to* express ability; *be able to* supplies the future, perfect and infinitive forms ([[#def-ability]]).
- For general past ability use *could* or *was able to*; for one successful action use *was able to* or *managed to*, not affirmative *could*. *Couldn't* is fine in both cases, and so is *could* with verbs of perception ([[#rule-could-past]]).
- *Can* is the neutral form of permission, *could* more tentative, *may* formal; for permission on one past occasion use *was allowed to* ([[#rule-permission]]).
- *Can* expresses general possibility (‘sometimes true’); *may*, *might* and *could* express specific possibility in a particular situation ([[#def-possibility]], [[#rule-can-general]], [[#rule-may-might]]).
- *May not* and *might not* mean ‘possibly not’; *can't* and *couldn't* mean ‘impossible’ ([[#rule-may-might]]).
- *Could* and *might* also express hypothetical possibility and suggestions, and *may … but* makes a concession ([[#rule-hypothetical]]).
- Chinese 可能 corresponds to *may*, *might* or *could*, not to *can*; potential complements (看得见, 听不懂) correspond to *can* and *can't*.
:::

::: exercise Permission in the past {level=2}
Complete with *could*, *couldn't* or *was/were allowed to*, and explain. (a) *At my primary school we ___ use calculators.* (b) *After the interview I ___ see the director for a few minutes.* (c) *In the 1990s you ___ smoke on many flights.* (d) *The journalist ___ ask one question before the guards stopped her.*
::: solution
(a) *couldn't* (or *weren't allowed to*) — negative, general rule. (b) *was allowed to* — one occasion. (c) *could* (or *were allowed to*) — a general rule in the past. (d) *was allowed to* — one occasion.
:::
:::

::: exercise Correct the mistakes {level=2}
Correct the sentences that are wrong. (a) *I'm not sure, but the answer can be in chapter 4.* (b) *He may be rich, but he isn't generous.* (c) *Yesterday I could finally pass my driving test.* (d) *Can you hear that noise?* (e) *I know how to speak Spanish because my mother is from Mexico.* (f) *Prices can rise again next month.*
::: solution
(a) *…the answer **may / might / could** be in chapter 4.* (b) Correct (concessive *may*). (c) *Yesterday I **finally passed** / **was finally able to pass** / **managed to pass** my driving test.* (d) Correct. (e) *I **can** speak Spanish…* — *know how to* suits techniques rather than languages learned at home. (f) *Prices **may / might / could** rise again next month.* (*can* only for a general statement: *Prices can rise very quickly in a crisis*.)
:::
:::

::: exercise Translate {level=3}
Translate, paying attention to the type of possibility or ability. (a) 你说话太快，我听不懂。 (b) 经过三次尝试，他终于打开了那扇门。 (c) 他可能不知道开会的时间。 (d) 这不可能是他写的。 (e) 在这里，夏天有时候会非常潮湿。 (f) 我小时候会说一点上海话，现在不会了。
::: hint
(b) is one achievement; (e) is a general tendency; (c) and (d) differ in what is negated.
:::
::: solution
(a) *You're speaking too fast — I can't understand.* (b) *After three attempts, he finally managed to open / was able to open the door.* (or simply *finally opened the door*) (c) *He may / might not know what time the meeting is.* (d) *He can't / couldn't have written this.* (perfect infinitive, because the writing is in the past) (e) *Summers here can be very humid.* (f) *When I was a child I could speak a little Shanghainese, but I can't any more.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Write a short report {level=3}
A friend is visiting your city next month. Write a short message (80–120 words) giving advice. Use *can* for at least one general possibility, *may*, *might* or *could* for at least two specific possibilities, *be able to* once, and one concession with *may … but*.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

*Great news that you're coming! The weather in March can be very changeable, so bring a warm jacket and an umbrella. It might be sunny all week — but it could just as easily rain. You'll be able to get from the airport to my flat by metro in about forty minutes. The trains can be crowded in the rush hour, so try to land before five if you can. My street may be a bit hard to find the first time, so I'll send you a map. The old town may be full of tourists, but it's still the most beautiful part of the city. See you soon!*

Check: general *can* (*can be very changeable*, *can be crowded*); specific *might*, *could*, *may* (*might be sunny*, *could … rain*, *may be a bit hard*); *be able to* (*You'll be able to get*); concession (*may be full of tourists, but…*).
:::
