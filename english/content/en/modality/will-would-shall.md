*Will* is usually taught as "the future", and in [[tense-and-aspect/future-forms]] it appears as one future form among several. But look at what it does in these sentences:

- *It'**ll** be dark by six.* — a prediction
- *Leave it — I'**ll** wash up.* — an offer, a decision made now
- *The car **won't** start.* — a refusal, by a car
- *My cat **will** sit on the keyboard whenever I'm working.* — a habit
- *He **WILL** leave his socks on the floor!* — an annoying habit, with heavy stress
- *That'**ll** be Grandma on the phone.* — an assumption about now

Only the first is a plain statement about the future. *Will* is a modal verb, and like the other modals it has root meanings (willingness, refusal, habit) and epistemic meanings (prediction, assumption). Its partner *would* has as many uses again, from past habits (*We **would** play by the river every summer*) to hypothesis (*I **would** help if I could*) and politeness (***Would** you mind…?*). *Shall*, once a common verb, is now used mainly in a few corners of English — offers and suggestions in British English, and legal documents everywhere.

This chapter sorts out these three verbs as modals. Their basic future uses are in [[core-grammar/talking-about-the-future]] and [[tense-and-aspect/future-forms]]; conditionals are in [[complex-sentences/conditionals]]. The two families of modal meaning are explained in [[modality/what-modals-do]].

## *Will*

::: definition Volition and prediction {#def-volition}
The meanings of *will* fall into two groups (Quirk et al. 1985; Coates 1983). **Volition** (a kind of dynamic modality) is the subject's **willingness**, **intention** or **insistence**: *I'll help you.* *She won't listen.* **Prediction** (epistemic modality) is the speaker's confident expectation about the future, the present, or what generally happens: *It'll rain.* *That'll be the postman.* *Oil will float on water.*
:::

::: theorem The uses of *will* {#rule-will}
1. **Prediction** about the future: *Prices **will** rise next year.* *You'**ll** love this film.* (See [[tense-and-aspect/future-forms]].)
2. **Assumption** about the present: *Don't ring now — they'**ll** be eating.* (See [[modality/deduction#rule-will-assumption]].)
3. **Willingness, offers and promises**, often decided at the moment of speaking: *I'**ll** carry that for you.* *I'**ll** call you tomorrow, I promise.* *"The phone's ringing." "I'**ll** get it."*
4. **Requests**: ***Will** you close the door, please?* This is fairly direct; *Would you…?* and *Could you…?* are more polite ([[modality/modality-politeness]]).
5. **Refusal** with ***won't***, by people and by things: *She **won't** tell me her name.* *The window **won't** open.*
6. **Characteristic behaviour and habits**: *On Sundays he'**ll** often cook for the whole family.* *A cat **will** always land on its feet.* *Accidents **will** happen.*
7. **Insistence**, especially an annoying habit, with *will* stressed and not contracted: *She **WILL** keep interrupting me!* *"I'm going out." "You **WILL** not!"*
:::

::: proof
The two groups go back to the history of *will*. Old English *willan* meant ‘want, wish’, and the volition meanings (3–5, 7) keep this sense: *I'll help* is a statement of willingness, *won't* a refusal. The prediction meanings (1, 2, 6) developed from it, because what someone intends to do is likely to happen. Habit (6) is prediction applied to all occasions: given the chance, the cat *will* land on its feet. Coates (1983) found volition and prediction meanings mixed in many corpus examples, and Biber et al. (1999) found *will* to be one of the commonest modals in all registers. The refusal use of *won't* with things (*The car won't start*) treats the object as if it had a will of its own; it is fully standard.
:::

::: warning *Will* in requests and after *if*
✗ *If you will come early, we can talk.* ✓ *If you **come** early, we can talk.* (a condition: present tense)

✓ *If you'**ll** follow me, I'll show you to your room.* (= if you are willing — a polite request: *will* is correct)

✗ *Will you give me a glass of water?* (to a waiter — not wrong, but rather direct) ✓ *Could I have a glass of water, please?*

The rule against *will* after *if* applies to conditions about the future ([[tense-and-aspect/future-forms#rule-time-clauses]]), not to *will* meaning willingness.
:::

::: example Which meaning of *will*? {#ex-will}
Give the meaning of *will* or *won't* in each sentence (prediction, assumption, willingness/offer, request, refusal, habit, insistence). (a) *My laptop won't turn on.* (b) *You'll be Ms Okafor — please come in.* (c) *I'll lend you my notes if you like.* (d) *Babies will put anything in their mouths.* (e) *He WILL eat with his mouth open!* (f) *The new line will open in 2028.* (g) *Will you sign here, please?*
::: solution
(a) Refusal, by a thing.

(b) Assumption about the present: ‘I assume you are Ms Okafor.’ Common in formal greetings.

(c) Willingness: an offer.

(d) Characteristic behaviour: a general habit of babies.

(e) Insistence: an annoying habit (stressed *WILL*).

(f) Prediction about the future (or a plan stated as a prediction).

(g) Request — in this context polite enough, because it is a routine instruction from a clerk or receptionist.
:::
:::

## *Would*

*Would* is the past form of *will*, and some of its uses are simply past versions of the uses of *will*. But most of the time it does not refer to the past at all: it marks a situation as **hypothetical** or a request as **tentative**.

::: theorem The uses of *would* {#rule-would}
**Past uses**

1. **Future in the past** and **reported speech**: *She said she **would** call.* *I knew it **would** be difficult.* (See [[tense-and-aspect/future-forms#rule-future-in-past]] and [[complex-sentences/reported-speech]].)
2. **Past habit** (actions, not states): *When we were children, we **would** spend every summer at our grandparents' house.* (See [[tense-and-aspect/past-habits]].)
3. **Past refusal** with ***wouldn't***, also for a single occasion: *The car **wouldn't** start this morning.* *He **wouldn't** say what was wrong.*
4. **Typical or annoying past behaviour**, with *would* stressed: *He **WOULD** say that — he always takes her side.* *"She blamed me." "She **would**!"*

**Hypothetical and tentative uses**

5. **Hypothesis** and conditionals: *I **would** buy a house if I had the money.* *It **would** be nice to see you.* *What **would** you do?* (See [[complex-sentences/conditionals]].)
6. **Polite requests and offers**: ***Would** you open the door, please?* ***Would** you mind waiting?* ***Would** you like some tea?*
7. **Wishes and preferences**: *I'**d like** a coffee.* *I'**d rather** walk.* *I'**d prefer** to stay in.*
8. **Tentative opinions**: *I **would** think so.* *I'**d say** it's about ten kilometres.* *I **wouldn't** have thought that was necessary.*
:::

::: proof
The hypothetical uses come from the same historical change as *could*, *might* and *should* ([[modality/what-modals-do]]): an old past form is used to mark distance, but distance from reality or from directness instead of distance in time. Swan (2016) and Quirk et al. (1985) list the same uses. The past habit use (2) is limited to repeated **actions**; for past states, English uses *used to* or the past simple: ✗ *I would have long hair*, ✓ *I **used to** have long hair*. A story of past habits usually starts with *used to* and then continues with *would*, because *would* needs a past time that has already been set ([[tense-and-aspect/past-habits]]). Tentative opinions (8) are especially common in British speech; *I would think so* is less committed than *I think so*.
:::

::: warning Overusing *would*
✗ *When I lived in Xi'an, I would have a small flat near the old city wall.* ✓ *…I **had** / **used to have** a small flat…* (a state, not a repeated action)

✗ *If I would have more time, I would learn the piano.* ✓ *If I **had** more time, I would learn the piano.*

✗ *I would like that you help me.* ✓ *I **would like you to** help me.*

✗ *Yesterday I would go to the cinema, but it was closed.* ✓ *Yesterday I **was going to** go to the cinema, but it was closed.*

In standard English *would* does not normally appear in the *if*-clause of a conditional. Forms such as *If I would have known* are heard in informal American speech (Swan 2016), but they are not accepted in writing.
:::

::: example *Would* in a text {#ex-would}
Read the paragraph and give the use of each *would*.

*When I was a student, my flatmate Rahul (1) **would** cook a huge pot of dal every Sunday. I (2) **'d** always say I wasn't hungry, and then I (3) **'d** eat three bowls. He told me once that he (4) **would** open a restaurant one day. I (5) **would** never have believed then that he (6) **would** really do it. Last month he invited me to the opening. (7) **Would** I go again? Of course — I (8) **'d** go every week if I could.*
::: solution
(1) Past habit (a repeated action). (2), (3) Past habit. (4) Future in the past / reported speech (*he said: "I will open…"*). (5) Hypothetical in the past (*would have believed*: a counterfactual with an understood condition — ‘if you had told me’). (6) Future in the past (seen from *then*). (7) Hypothetical question. (8) Hypothetical (second conditional: *if I could*).

Note that the whole text is about the past, but only (1)–(4) and (6) refer to past time; (5), (7) and (8) are hypothetical.
:::
:::

::: quiz
*I asked him to help, but he ___.* Which is correct?
- [ ] *won't*
- [x] *wouldn't*
- [ ] *shouldn't*
- [ ] *mustn't*
::: solution
A refusal on one occasion in the past: *wouldn't*. *Won't* is a present or future refusal. *Shouldn't* and *mustn't* express advice and prohibition.

## *Shall*

::: theorem The uses of *shall* {#rule-shall}
1. **Offers and suggestions** in questions with *I* and *we*: ***Shall** I open the window?* ***Shall** we go?* *What **shall** we do this evening?* This use is **mainly British**. American English usually prefers ***Should** I…?*, *Do you want me to…?*, *Let's…* or *How about…?* (Swan 2016; Biber et al. 1999).
2. The tag after *let's*: *Let's start, **shall** we?* (in both varieties, though more British).
3. **Future** with *I* and *we*, in statements: *I **shall** be away next week.* This is now formal or old-fashioned British English; *will* ('*ll*) is normal in both varieties.
4. **Obligation in legal and official texts**: *The tenant **shall** keep the property in good repair.* *Each member state **shall** report annually.* Here *shall* means ‘is required to’, with any subject.
5. **Promises and determination** in literary or formal language: *You **shall** have your answer tomorrow.* *We **shall** not give up.*
:::

::: proof
The old school rule, first stated by John Wallis in 1653 and repeated in many later grammars, was that *shall* expresses the plain future with *I* and *we* and *will* the plain future with other subjects, and that the pattern is reversed for promises and determination. Studies of usage have never found that speakers followed it consistently, and today the rule is of historical interest only. What corpus studies show is decline. Leech et al. (2009), comparing British and American written English of 1961 and the early 1990s, found *shall* to be the central modal that declined most steeply in both varieties; Biber et al. (1999) found it rare in conversation, especially in American English. Its strongest remaining uses are the British questions *Shall I…?* and *Shall we…?* and legal drafting. Even there it is contested: plain-language guidance for official writing, such as the US federal government's *Federal Plain Language Guidelines*, recommends *must* for obligations, because legal *shall* has been interpreted in several different ways.
:::

::: example *Shall* or something else? {#ex-shall}
Rewrite each sentence for an American speaker, and say which uses of *shall* are natural in both varieties. (a) *Shall I carry your bag?* (b) *What shall we have for dinner?* (c) *Let's take a break, shall we?* (d) *I shall be in touch soon.* (e) *The Supplier shall deliver the goods within 30 days.*
::: solution
(a) *Should I carry your bag?* / *Do you want me to carry your bag?* / *Let me carry your bag.*

(b) *What should we have for dinner?* / *What do you want for dinner?*

(c) *Let's take a break, OK?* / *Why don't we take a break?* (*shall we* is understood and sometimes used in American English, but sounds rather British or formal.)

(d) *I'll be in touch soon.* (This is also the normal British form; *I shall* sounds formal.)

(e) No change needed for a contract in either variety — legal *shall* is used in both. A plain-language version would be *The Supplier must deliver the goods within 30 days.*
:::
:::

::: theorem Special uses of *should* {#rule-putative-should}
Besides advice and expectation ([[modality/obligation-necessity]], [[modality/deduction]]), *should* has three uses that come from its history as the past of *shall* rather than from advice:

1. **"Putative" *should*** in *that*-clauses after expressions of emotion or judgement — *It's strange that…*, *I'm surprised that…*, *It's a pity that…*: *It's strange **that** she **should** say that* (= that she says / said that). The meaning is not obligation; *should* presents the situation as something to react to (Quirk et al. 1985). It is mainly British; the plain verb is always possible.
2. ***If … should*** and inverted ***Should…*** for an unlikely condition: *If you **should** see Anna, give her my love.* ***Should** you need any help, please call.* (formal) (See [[complex-sentences/conditionals]].)
3. ***Why should…?*** and ***How should I know?*** express indignation: *Why **should** I apologise? It wasn't my fault.*
:::

::: example A conversation with *will*, *would*, *shall* and *should* {#ex-mixed}
Complete the conversation with *will*, *won't*, *would*, *wouldn't*, *shall* or *should*, and give the meaning of each.

**Kemal:** *The printer (1) ___ work again. It's so annoying — it (2) ___ jam just when I'm in a hurry.*

**Li Na:** *(3) ___ I have a look?*

**Kemal:** *Thanks. Last week it (4) ___ print anything at all, and IT said they (5) ___ send someone, but nobody came.*

**Li Na:** *Typical. Well, if you (6) ___ hold this tray, I'll try to pull the paper out… There. It (7) ___ be fine now.*

**Kemal:** *Brilliant. It's strange that it (8) ___ work for you and not for me!*
::: solution
(1) *won't* — refusal by a thing.

(2) *will* (stressed, *WILL*) — annoying characteristic behaviour. (*always jams* is also possible.)

(3) *Shall* — an offer (BrE). AmE: *Should I have a look?* / *Want me to have a look?*

(4) *wouldn't* — past refusal on one occasion.

(5) *would* — future in the past, in reported speech (IT said: "We'll send someone").

(6) *'ll* / *will* — willingness in an *if*-clause: a polite request (‘if you are willing to hold…’). Here *will* after *if* is correct.

(7) *should* — expectation (‘if all goes normally’); *'ll* (*It'll be fine*) is also possible, as a more confident prediction.

(8) *should* — putative *should* after *It's strange that* (or simply *works*).
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **会 and *will*.** 会 expresses prediction (明天会下雨 → *It'll rain tomorrow*) and habitual tendency (他周末会给全家做饭 → *He'll cook for the whole family at weekends*), which match the epistemic and habit uses of *will*. But 会 does not express an offer or a decision made now: 我来拿 / 我去开门 → *I'll carry it* / *I'll get the door*. Chinese uses 来 or 去 (or nothing) where English uses volitional *will*.
- **就是不 and *won't*.** 他就是不听 → *He just won't listen.* 门打不开 → *The door won't open.* The refusal meaning of *won't* covers both people and things, which Chinese expresses with 不肯, 就是不 or a potential complement.
- **老是, 总是 and insistent *will*.** 他老是把袜子扔在地上 → *He's always leaving his socks on the floor* or, with irritation, *He WILL leave his socks on the floor!* The progressive with *always* is the more common choice for learners ([[tense-and-aspect/simple-and-progressive]]).
- **吧, 要不要 and *shall*.** 我们走吧 → *Let's go* or *Shall we go?* 要不要我帮你？ → *Shall I help you?* (BrE) / *Do you want me to help?* Note that ✗ *Will I help you?* is not an offer in standard English.
- **应当, 应 and legal *shall*.** Chinese laws and contracts state obligations with 应当 or 应 (and prohibitions with 不得); English legal texts traditionally use *shall* and *shall not*, and plain-language texts *must* and *must not*. In ordinary English, 应当 is *should*, not *shall*.
- **以前常常 and *would*.** 小时候我们常常在河边玩 → *When we were children, we would / used to play by the river.* Use *would* only for repeated actions, not for states (以前我有一只狗 → *I used to have a dog*, not ✗ *I would have a dog*).
:::

::: history
The meanings of *will* and *shall* have changed more than those of any other modals. Old English *willan* meant ‘wish, want’, and *sculan* (*sceal*) meant ‘owe, be obliged to’; both developed future meanings during the Middle English period. In 1653 John Wallis's *Grammatica Linguae Anglicanae* stated the rule that *shall* is the plain future with *I* and *we* and *will* with other persons, and the rule was repeated and elaborated for the next three centuries. H. W. and F. G. Fowler's *The King's English* (1906) gave it many pages, and noted that speakers in Scotland, Ireland and America did not follow it. Twentieth-century corpus studies found that *will* had taken over the future with all persons and that *shall* was becoming rare: Leech et al. (2009) found *shall* declining more steeply than any other central modal in British and American writing between 1961 and the early 1990s.
:::

## Where this leads

The future uses of *will* are compared with *be going to* and the present continuous in [[tense-and-aspect/future-forms]], and *would* for past habits with *used to* in [[tense-and-aspect/past-habits]]. Conditional *would* is treated in [[complex-sentences/conditionals]] and [[complex-sentences/unreal-past]]. *Would*, *could* and *will* in requests and offers are compared in [[modality/modality-politeness]]; for everyday phrases see [[everyday-english/requests-offers]].

::: summary
- *Will* expresses prediction and assumption (epistemic) and willingness, offers, refusal, habit and insistence (volition) ([[#def-volition]], [[#rule-will]]).
- *Won't* is a refusal by people or things (*The car won't start*); stressed *WILL* marks an annoying habit.
- *Would* is the past of *will* in reported speech, the future in the past, past habits (actions only) and past refusals; more often it is hypothetical or tentative: conditionals, polite requests, *would like*, *would rather* ([[#rule-would]]).
- *Would* does not normally go in the *if*-clause, and is not used for past states.
- *Shall* survives in British offers and suggestions (*Shall I…?*, *Shall we…?*), formal statements of the future, and legal obligation; American English prefers *Should I…?* or *Do you want me to…?* ([[#rule-shall]]).
- *Shall* declined more steeply than any other central modal in written English between 1961 and the early 1990s (Leech et al. 2009).
- *Should* also has putative, conditional and indignant uses: *It's strange that she should say that*; *Should you need help…*; *Why should I?* ([[#rule-putative-should]]).
:::
