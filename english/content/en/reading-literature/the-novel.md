George Eliot's *Middlemarch* (1871–72) is more than 300,000 words long. Read at 250 words a minute, it takes about twenty hours; most readers spend weeks on it. In that time they meet well over a hundred named characters, follow four main plots that cross and recross, and watch several marriages begin and change over about three years of story. A short story can be held in mind at once, and every detail of it read closely ([[reading-literature/short-story]]). A novel cannot. Its reader must remember, forget, notice patterns across hundreds of pages, and live for a while in a world that the book builds slowly.

That is why reading a novel is a different skill from reading a poem or a story, not just a longer one. It asks for the extensive reader's stamina ([[reading-skills/extensive-reading]]) and the close reader's attention at chosen moments ([[reading-literature/close-reading]]). This chapter explains what a novel is, how the English novel developed from the early eighteenth century to the modernists, and how to read one well, with passages from Jane Austen, Charles Dickens, Charlotte Brontë, George Eliot and Thomas Hardy, all in the public domain.

## What a novel is

::: definition Novel {#def-novel}
A **novel** is an extended work of prose fiction — conventionally more than about 40,000 words, and often far longer — that presents characters and events in enough breadth and depth to create a social world and to show change over time. Shorter works of prose fiction are **novellas** and **short stories** ([[reading-literature/short-story#def-short-story]]).
:::

The word comes from Italian *novella* ‘a new thing, a short tale’. In English, *novel* came in the eighteenth century to mean a long prose fiction about contemporary, ordinary life, in contrast with the **romance**, a story of heroes, marvels and distant times. The distinction is old-fashioned now, but it marks something that has stayed central to the English novel: an interest in how ordinary people live, in houses, streets, money, families and marriages.

::: theorem What a novel allows {#rule-novel-features}
Because of its length, a novel typically has:

1. **Several lines of action** — a main plot and subplots, often connected by parallels and contrasts between characters.
2. **Development over time**: characters change (or fail to change) across months or years, and the reader experiences this change at length.
3. **A social world**: classes, professions, places and institutions, presented in enough detail to seem real.
4. **Variation of pace and method**: scenes and summaries, dialogue and description, the narrator's commentary, letters, flashbacks ([[reading-literature/narrative#rule-order]]).
5. **Patterns across distance**: recurring images, places and situations that the reader notices over hundreds of pages and that carry the novel's themes.
:::

::: proof
These features are what length makes possible and what readers of novels have valued. Ian Watt, in *The Rise of the Novel* (1957), argued that the novel's distinctive quality was **formal realism**: the attempt to give a full and authentic account of individual experience, with particular people in particular times and places, in plain prose. Later critics have questioned his story (it leaves out women writers and earlier fiction; see Margaret Anne Doody, *The True Story of the Novel*, 1996), but his description of the novel's interest in ordinary, particular life remains useful. Point 5 is the reader's special task: a pattern that is obvious in a short poem may only become visible in a novel when an image on page 400 recalls one on page 12.
:::

The chart is a sequence, not a story of progress. A few developments matter for the reader.

**The first-person and the letter.** Daniel Defoe's *Robinson Crusoe* (1719) presented itself as the true account of a shipwrecked sailor, and gave readers lists, quantities and daily routine as no romance had. Samuel Richardson's *Pamela* (1740) and *Clarissa* (1747–48) were **epistolary novels**, told entirely in letters, which let readers follow characters' feelings as they happened, "to the moment", as Richardson said.

**The narrator who talks.** Henry Fielding's *Tom Jones* (1749) has a narrator who addresses the reader, opens each of its eighteen books with an essay, and makes a comic epic out of an English country life. The commenting narrator became a central feature of the Victorian novel.

**Austen and free indirect style.** Jane Austen's six novels (1811–17) are short by Victorian standards and narrow in setting — a few families in the country. Their achievement is in voice: a narrator whose irony can enter a character's thoughts and quietly judge them ([[reading-literature/narrative#def-fis]]).

**Serial publication.** Dickens's *The Pickwick Papers* came out in monthly parts in 1836–37 and was an enormous success. For the rest of the century, many novels first reached readers in instalments — in monthly numbers or in magazines — and only afterwards as books, often in the expensive three-volume form that the circulating libraries bought. Serial publication shaped the novels themselves: chapters end on suspense, large casts of characters keep many plots alive, and writers could respond to readers' reactions as they wrote.

**Modernism.** In the early twentieth century, writers such as Joseph Conrad, James Joyce and Virginia Woolf broke with the conventions of the Victorian narrator, moving the novel inside the minds of characters and fragmenting its time ([[reading-literature/literary-periods]]).

## Five passages

### Austen: dialogue as character

The first chapter of *Pride and Prejudice* (1813) follows its famous opening sentence ([[reading-literature/close-reading#ex-austen]]) with a conversation:

> *"My dear Mr. Bennet," said his lady to him one day, "have you heard that Netherfield Park is let at last?"*
>
> *Mr. Bennet replied that he had not.*

::: example Characterisation through dialogue {#ex-bennet}
What do these two short paragraphs show about Mr and Mrs Bennet, and how?
::: solution
**Mrs Bennet speaks in direct speech**, with an affectionate formal address (*My dear Mr. Bennet*) and an eager question about news. *At last* shows that she has been waiting for this; *let* (‘rented’) concerns property — and, the reader of the first sentence knows, a possible rich husband for one of her daughters. She is characterised by her own words.

**Mr Bennet's reply is in indirect speech**: *Mr. Bennet replied that he had not.* The narrator reports him in the flattest possible way, in six words. He gives her nothing. The contrast of forms — her lively direct speech, his reported minimum — shows the marriage at once: she talks, he withholds; she is eager, he is dry and, as the chapter goes on to show, amused at her expense.

**Narrator.** *Said his lady* is slightly mock-formal, and the narrator says no more than is needed. The method is **indirect characterisation** ([[reading-literature/narrative#def-character]]): the reader infers the characters from how they speak, and how the narrator chooses to present their speech. At the end of the chapter the narrator does sum them up directly, but the reader has already done most of the work.
:::
:::

### Dickens: setting as meaning

*Bleak House* (1852–53) is a novel about a lawsuit in the Court of Chancery that has gone on for so long that no one understands it, and that slowly ruins everyone connected with it. Its first chapter begins in the London fog:

> *Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping and the waterside pollutions of a great (and dirty) city. Fog on the Essex marshes, fog on the Kentish heights.*

A few paragraphs later the fog leads to its centre: *at the very heart of the fog, sits the Lord High Chancellor in his High Court of Chancery.*

::: example Setting that is also a metaphor {#ex-fog}
Analyse the syntax and the imagery of the fog paragraph. How does the setting connect to the novel's subject?
::: solution
**Syntax.** *Fog everywhere.* There is no verb. The paragraph continues in fragments — *Fog up the river … fog down the river … Fog on the Essex marshes* — phrases that name a place but never make a full clause. The repetition of *fog* at the head of each phrase spreads it over the whole country as the sentence spreads, and the lack of verbs makes the scene static, suspended, like a city where nothing moves.

**Imagery.** The fog moves from clean to dirty: *green aits and meadows* (an *ait* is a small island in a river) upstream; downstream, the river *rolls defiled* among ships and *pollutions*. The bracket *(and dirty)* is the narrator's sharp aside, undercutting *great*.

**Meaning.** The fog is a real London fog and a **metaphor** ([[reading-literature/figurative-language]]): the Court of Chancery is *at the very heart of the fog* because its procedures are obscure, it is impossible to see through, and it spreads its confusion everywhere, as the lawsuit spreads through the lives of the characters. The setting is not decoration; it introduces the novel's argument. A reader who notices the fog at the beginning will notice how often obscurity, dirt and contagion return later.
:::
:::

### Charlotte Brontë: the first-person voice

*Jane Eyre* (1847) is narrated by Jane herself, an orphan who becomes a governess. Its power comes largely from the force of her voice, which repeatedly claims equality with people who think themselves above her. In chapter 23, she speaks to her employer, Mr Rochester, whom she loves and believes is about to marry someone else:

> *Do you think, because I am poor, obscure, plain, and little, I am soulless and heartless? You think wrong!—I have as much soul as you,—and full as much heart!*

::: example Rhetoric in a character's speech {#ex-jane}
How does the syntax of Jane's speech create its force?
::: solution
**A list of four adjectives**: *poor, obscure, plain, and little*. Each names a way in which the world measures her as less than Rochester — money, social position, beauty, size. Listing them herself, without apology, takes the insult away from him: she names them before he can.

**A rhetorical question** (*Do you think … ?*), which accuses rather than asks ([[style-and-rhetoric/figures-of-speech]]). Its answer is given at once: *You think wrong!*

**Antithesis and parallelism**: *soulless and heartless* — *as much soul … as much heart*. The second half answers the first word for word, turning his assumed judgement upside down. *Full as much* is a slightly old-fashioned intensifier: ‘quite as much’.

**Punctuation**: the dashes and exclamation marks give the sentence the movement of speech under strong emotion.

**Effect.** Jane's speech claims spiritual equality across class, money and gender in the language of argument. Read in the context of the first-person narration, it is also the narrator's claim on the reader: this is a plain, poor woman's story, and she insists it matters as much as anyone's. The novel ends, famously, with Jane addressing the reader directly: *Reader, I married him.* The subject of the sentence is *I*.
:::
:::

### George Eliot: the narrator who reflects

The narrator of *Middlemarch* is omniscient ([[reading-literature/narrative#rule-narration]]) and often stops the story to reflect on what it means. In chapter 20, describing the young Dorothea Brooke weeping on her honeymoon in Rome, the narrator generalises:

> *If we had a keen vision and feeling of all ordinary human life, it would be like hearing the grass grow and the squirrel's heart beat, and we should die of that roar which lies on the other side of silence.*

And the novel's last sentence, about Dorothea:

> *… for the growing good of the world is partly dependent on unhistoric acts; and that things are not so ill with you and me as they might have been, is half owing to the number who lived faithfully a hidden life, and rest in unvisited tombs.*

::: example A commenting narrator {#ex-eliot}
What does the narrator do in these two passages that a first-person narrator like Jane Eyre could not easily do? What is the effect of *we* and *you and me*?
::: solution
**Generalising.** Both sentences step back from the individual character to a general truth about *all ordinary human life* and *the growing good of the world*. An omniscient narrator can move from a particular woman to humanity, because she stands outside the story.

**Including the reader.** *We*, *you and me*: the narrator draws the reader into the reflection. The first passage says that *we* — narrator, reader, all of us — are protected from the full suffering around us by a kind of deafness; the last says that *you and me* owe our well-being partly to people history does not remember. The reader is made part of the moral world of the novel.

**Imagery.** The first passage moves from the tiny (grass growing, a squirrel's heartbeat) to the overwhelming (*that roar*), and the paradox *the other side of silence* suggests that ordinary suffering is everywhere, unheard. The last sentence ends with *hidden life* and *unvisited tombs*: Dorothea's life will not be famous, and that is the point.

**Effect.** The commenting narrator gives the novel a moral breadth — it is not only about Dorothea but about how to value ordinary lives. Some modern readers find such commentary intrusive; others find it the heart of Eliot's achievement. Either way, it is a resource of the omniscient novel that first-person and limited narration give up.
:::
:::

### Hardy: the end of a tragedy

Thomas Hardy's *Tess of the d'Urbervilles* (1891) follows a young countrywoman destroyed by the men in her life and by social judgement; at the end she is hanged for killing the man who seduced her. The last paragraph begins:

> *"Justice" was done, and the President of the Immortals, in Æschylean phrase, had ended his sport with Tess.*

::: quiz
What is the main effect of the quotation marks around *Justice*?
- [ ] They show that a character is speaking.
- [x] They suggest that the narrator does not accept that what was done was really justice.
- [ ] They mark a word in a foreign language.
- [ ] They indicate the title of a book.
::: solution
The quotation marks are **scare quotes**: they distance the narrator from the word, implying that the legal process called itself justice but was not. The rest of the sentence supports this: Tess's fate is described as the *sport* (game) of a cruel divine power, in a phrase adapted from the Greek tragedian Aeschylus (*Æschylean*). The narrator's attitude is bitter irony ([[reading-skills/inference#rule-attitude-clues]]).
:::
:::

## Kinds of novel

::: definition Some kinds of novel {#def-novel-kinds}
- **Bildungsroman** (German ‘novel of formation’): follows a young person's development from childhood or youth to maturity (*Jane Eyre*, *Great Expectations*, Joyce's *A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man*, 1916).
- **Epistolary novel**: told in letters (*Pamela*, *Clarissa*; partly Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein*, 1818, and Bram Stoker's *Dracula*, 1897, which also uses diaries).
- **Gothic novel**: mystery, terror and the supernatural in old buildings and wild places (Horace Walpole's *The Castle of Otranto*, 1764; Ann Radcliffe; elements in *Jane Eyre* and *Wuthering Heights*).
- **Historical novel**: set in a past period, with historical events in the background (Walter Scott's *Waverley*, 1814; Dickens's *A Tale of Two Cities*, 1859).
- **Realist novel**: a detailed, probable representation of ordinary contemporary life and society (George Eliot, Anthony Trollope, much Victorian fiction).
:::

These labels overlap, and the best novels use several at once: *Jane Eyre* is a Bildungsroman with Gothic elements, a first-person narrative and a realist account of a governess's life. Genre names are useful because they tell you what expectations a novel plays with, not because they put it in a box.

## How to read a novel

::: theorem Reading a long novel {#rule-reading-novel}
1. **Choose the edition with care.** For older novels, an edition with notes explains words, money, laws and customs; check that the text is complete and not abridged.
2. **Keep a character list** with one line on each character and their relationships; many Victorian novels have dozens of named characters.
3. **Write a few lines after each chapter or session**: what happened, what changed, one passage worth rereading.
4. **Read closely at key moments** — the opening, first meetings, turning points, the ending — and read faster elsewhere.
5. **Track patterns**: recurring images (fog, windows, fire), places, parallel characters (two sisters, two suitors), and repeated situations. Note the page each time.
6. **Notice the narrator's methods**: who narrates, how much commentary there is, where free indirect style appears ([[reading-literature/narrative]]).
7. **Think about how the novel was first read**: in monthly parts, in three volumes, by readers who knew the social world it describes.
:::

::: proof
Each step answers a problem caused by length. A novel is too long to reread whole, so notes and lists replace the instant overview possible with a poem. Close reading at selected moments keeps the attention that the rest of this course teaches, without the impossible aim of reading every sentence of 300,000 words with equal care. Tracking patterns turns memory into evidence: a claim such as *fog in Bleak House stands for the law* becomes convincing when it can point to its recurrence. Knowing the original form of publication explains features that seem odd today — suspense at the end of every few chapters, very large casts, recaps of earlier events — which were designed for readers who waited a month between instalments.
:::

::: warning Reading a simplified version and writing about the original
✗ *Reading a graded or abridged version of Pride and Prejudice, then quoting it in an essay as Austen's words.*

✓ *Reading a graded version first for the story, if the original is too hard, then reading the original — at least the chapters you will write about — and quoting only from it.*

Graded readers and abridgements are excellent for extensive reading ([[reading-skills/extensive-reading#def-graded-reader]]), but their sentences are not the author's. In literary study, the words are the evidence: an essay about Austen's irony must quote Austen. Note also that some modern editions of older novels modernise spelling and punctuation; say which edition you quote.
:::

::: quiz
A reader of *Middlemarch* notices that the image of a window, or of looking out of a window, appears in several important scenes. What should she do?
- [ ] Ignore it: images in novels are decoration.
- [x] Note each occurrence with its page and context, and later ask what the scenes have in common.
- [ ] Decide immediately what the window symbolises and look for evidence for that meaning only.
- [ ] Look up "window" in a dictionary of symbols and use the meaning given there.
::: solution
Patterns across a novel are evidence, but their meaning depends on context ([[#rule-reading-novel]], point 5). Recording each occurrence first, and interpreting afterwards, avoids deciding the meaning too early. A dictionary of symbols may suggest possibilities, but the novel's own uses decide.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Serial forms in both traditions.** The great Chinese vernacular novels are organised in *hui* (回, chapters), each with a couplet title, and many chapters end on suspense with the storyteller's formula 欲知后事如何，且听下回分解 ‘if you want to know what happened next, listen to the next chapter’. Victorian serial novels ended their instalments on suspense for a similar reason: readers had to come back. Comparing *Hongloumeng* (《红楼梦》) with *Middlemarch* — two long novels of families, households and marriages — is a rewarding exercise.
- **The novel in translation into Chinese.** Many Chinese readers first met English novels through translations. Lin Shu (林纾) translated or adapted well over a hundred foreign novels into classical Chinese in the early twentieth century, including works by Dickens, with collaborators who knew the foreign languages. For the European realist novel and its reception in China, see the Chinese Atlas lesson on [the realist novel](/learn/chinese/lesson.php?c=world-literature&l=realism).
- **Names and forms of address.** Victorian novels use titles and surnames in ways that signal class and intimacy: *Mr. Bennet* to his wife, *Miss Bennet* for the eldest unmarried daughter, *Miss Elizabeth* for a younger one; first names only between family or close friends. Changes in how characters address each other often mark changes in their relationship.
- **Length.** Chinese learners used to intensive reading may try to read a Victorian novel with a dictionary on every page and give up. Use [[#rule-reading-novel]]: read most of the book at extensive speed, and slow down at the key passages.
:::

::: history
Long prose fiction existed in many cultures before the English novel — in Greek and Latin antiquity, in Japan (*The Tale of Genji*, early eleventh century), in Spain (*Don Quixote*, 1605 and 1615) and in China. The English novel's usual starting point is placed in the early eighteenth century, with Defoe's *Robinson Crusoe* (1719), Richardson's *Pamela* (1740) and Fielding's *Joseph Andrews* (1742) and *Tom Jones* (1749), though earlier writers such as Aphra Behn (*Oroonoko*, 1688) are now often included. Women were among the most widely read novelists from the start, and by the time of Fanny Burney's *Evelina* (1778) and Austen's novels (1811–17) the novel was the dominant form of fiction. Dickens's *Pickwick Papers* (1836–37) began the age of serial publication. The three-volume novel was the standard first form of publication until the circulating libraries stopped buying it in 1894. Ian Watt's *The Rise of the Novel* (1957) became the classic account of the form's origins.
:::

::: exercise Direct and reported speech {level=1}
In the *Pride and Prejudice* passage, Mrs Bennet's words are given in direct speech and Mr Bennet's in indirect speech. Rewrite Mr Bennet's reply in direct speech, and say what is lost or gained.
::: solution
*"I have not," said Mr. Bennet.* (or *"No, I haven't"*). In direct speech, Mr Bennet has a voice of his own and the exchange becomes more balanced. What is lost is the effect of the narrator's flat report, which shows how little he gives his wife and how little the narrator thinks his reply deserves.

::: exercise Serial endings {level=2}
Explain why many Victorian novels have chapters that end with an unanswered question or an unexpected arrival. Give two reasons connected with how they were published.
::: solution
(1) Many were published in monthly or weekly instalments, so the end of a number had to make readers buy the next one: suspense sold the next part. (2) Readers waited weeks between parts, so a strong ending kept the story alive in their minds; serial novels also tend to have recaps and large casts so that readers could pick up the threads. The structure of the novel is partly a result of its form of publication ([[#rule-reading-novel]], point 7).
:::
:::

::: exercise Patterns of imagery {level=2}
A student claims: *In Bleak House, the fog stands for the Court of Chancery.* What kind of evidence would make this claim convincing? What would weaken it?
::: solution
**Convincing evidence:** the opening chapter's explicit link (*at the very heart of the fog, sits the Lord High Chancellor*); later passages where fog, mud or obscurity appear near the law, lawyers or the lawsuit; characters who are confused or harmed by the case described in images of fog, darkness or blindness. Recording the places where the image recurs ([[#rule-reading-novel]], point 5) turns the claim from a guess into an argument.

**Weakening evidence:** fog that appears often in contexts that have nothing to do with the law, or the law described in other, unrelated images. A careful claim might say that the fog *is associated with* the court and with confusion in general, rather than that it simply *stands for* the court.
:::
:::

::: exercise The narrator's we {level=2}
Rewrite George Eliot's sentence *If we had a keen vision and feeling of all ordinary human life, it would be like hearing the grass grow and the squirrel's heart beat …* without *we* (for example, beginning *If a person had …*). What changes?
::: solution
*If a person had a keen vision and feeling of all ordinary human life, it would be like hearing the grass grow …* The sentence becomes an abstract statement about people in general. The original *we* includes the narrator and the reader in the claim: *we* are the ones who do not hear the roar. The change removes the reader's involvement and the moral appeal that the omniscient narrator makes ([[#ex-eliot]]).
:::
