For three centuries before Shakespeare, European love poets praised their ladies in the same terms: eyes like the sun, lips like coral, skin as white as snow, breath sweeter than perfume. The fourteen-line poem in which they did so — the sonnet — had been brought to perfection by the Italian poet Petrarch in the fourteenth century and imitated everywhere. Then, in a collection printed in 1609, came this:

> *My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun;*
> *Coral is far more red than her lips' red;*
> *If snow be white, why then her breasts are dun;*
> *If hairs be wires, black wires grow on her head.*
> *I have seen roses damask'd, red and white,*
> *But no such roses see I in her cheeks;*
> *And in some perfumes is there more delight*
> *Than in the breath that from my mistress reeks.*
> *I love to hear her speak, yet well I know*
> *That music hath a far more pleasing sound;*
> *I grant I never saw a goddess go;*
> *My mistress, when she walks, treads on the ground:*
> *And yet, by heaven, I think my love as rare*
> *As any she belied with false compare.*

(*Dun* ‘dull greyish brown’; *damask'd* ‘patterned, mingled red and white’; *reeks* ‘is breathed out’ — in Shakespeare's time the word did not necessarily mean ‘stinks’, though the modern sense makes the joke sharper; *she* ‘woman’; *belied* ‘misrepresented’.)

Shakespeare's Sonnet 130 is funny only if you know the form and its conventions. It keeps the sonnet's fourteen lines, its iambic pentameter and its rhyme scheme, and uses all of them to mock the clichés that the form usually carried — until the last two lines, which turn, as Shakespeare's sonnets usually do, and make a real declaration of love. The form is not a container for the content; it is part of the joke and part of the meaning.

This chapter introduces the main forms of English poetry: the ways lines are grouped into stanzas and linked by rhyme, the fixed forms such as the sonnet and the ballad, and the open forms — blank verse and free verse — that have no rhyme or no metre. It assumes the metrical terms of [[reading-literature/metre-rhythm]].

## Rhyme and stanza

::: definition Rhyme {#def-rhyme}
Two words **rhyme** when their final stressed vowels and everything after them sound the same, and the sounds before differ: *sun*/*dun*, *white*/*delight*, *despair*/*bare*.

- **Full** (perfect) rhyme: the sounds match exactly (*know*/*go*).
- **Masculine** rhyme falls on a final stressed syllable (*day*/*away*); **feminine** rhyme on a stressed syllable followed by unstressed ones (*viewest*/*renewest*).
- **Slant rhyme** (half rhyme, near rhyme): the sounds are similar but not identical (*soul*/*all*, *stone*/*frown*).
- **Eye rhyme**: words that look as if they rhyme but do not in pronunciation (*love*/*prove*, *come*/*home*); some were full rhymes in earlier English.
- **Internal rhyme**: a rhyme within a line.
:::

A **rhyme scheme** is described with letters: the first rhyme sound is *a*, the second *b*, and so on. The first quatrain of Sonnet 130 — *sun*, *red*, *dun*, *head* — rhymes *abab*.

::: definition Stanza {#def-stanza}
A **stanza** is a group of lines forming a unit of a poem, usually separated from the next by a space and usually repeating the same pattern of line lengths and rhyme. Common stanzas are the **couplet** (two lines rhyming *aa*), the **tercet** (three lines), the **quatrain** (four lines; *abab*, *abcb* or *abba*), and longer forms such as **rhyme royal** (seven lines of pentameter, *ababbcc*, used by Chaucer) and the **Spenserian stanza** (eight lines of pentameter and one alexandrine, *ababbcbcc*, from Spenser's *The Faerie Queene*, 1590–96).
:::

The **heroic couplet** — two rhyming lines of iambic pentameter, often closed at the end of the second line — was the dominant form of English verse from John Dryden to Alexander Pope. Its closed pairs suit wit, balance and argument:

> *True ease in writing comes from art, not chance,*
> *As those move easiest who have learn'd to dance.*

(Pope, *An Essay on Criticism*, 1711.) The couplet says what it does: it moves with practised ease, and its rhyme (*chance*/*dance*) clicks the thought shut.

::: theorem Describing the form of a poem {#rule-describe-form}
To describe a poem's form, answer these questions, in roughly this order:

1. **How many lines**, and are they grouped into stanzas? Of how many lines each?
2. **What metre**, if any ([[reading-literature/metre-rhythm#rule-scan]])? Are all lines the same length?
3. **What rhyme scheme**, if any? Full or slant rhymes? Masculine or feminine?
4. **Is it a fixed form** (sonnet, ballad, villanelle) or a stanza form with a name? If so, which, and does it follow the form strictly?
5. **Where does it turn** — change direction, tone or argument — and does the turn coincide with a division of the form?
6. **What does the form do?** How does it support, shape or work against the content?
:::

::: proof
Questions 1–4 are descriptive and can be checked by anyone: they establish the facts on which an interpretation must rest. Question 5 connects form with argument, because in many forms — the sonnet above all — the structural divisions are expected to carry turns of thought, and readers notice when they do or do not. Question 6 is the purpose of the exercise ([[reading-literature/close-reading#rule-three-questions]]): naming a form is never the end of an analysis. A sonnet that ignores its own divisions, or a cheerful poem in a mournful form, may be doing something significant precisely by departing from what the form leads us to expect.
:::

## The sonnet

::: definition Sonnet {#def-sonnet}
A **sonnet** is a poem of fourteen lines, in English usually in iambic pentameter, with a fixed rhyme scheme and a **turn** (Italian *volta*) — a change of direction in thought or feeling. Three main types are used in English:

- the **Petrarchan** (Italian) sonnet: an **octave** of eight lines rhyming *abbaabba*, followed by a **sestet** of six lines rhyming *cdecde*, *cdcdcd* or a similar pattern; the turn comes at or near the start of the sestet;
- the **Shakespearean** (English) sonnet: three quatrains and a couplet, *abab cdcd efef gg*; the turn usually comes in the couplet, sometimes at line 9;
- the **Spenserian** sonnet: three quatrains linked by rhyme and a couplet, *abab bcbc cdcd ee*.
:::

The English sonnet needs seven rhyme sounds; the Italian needs only four or five, which is easy in Italian, with its many rhyming endings, and harder in English. This is one reason the English form developed in the sixteenth century.

::: example The structure of Sonnet 130 {#ex-130}
Describe the form of Sonnet 130 with [[#rule-describe-form]], and explain how the form shapes the argument.
::: solution
**Lines and metre.** Fourteen lines of iambic pentameter, with no stanza breaks in the original printing.

**Rhyme.** *abab cdcd efef gg* (*sun*/*dun*, *red*/*head*; *white*/*delight*, *cheeks*/*reeks*; *know*/*go*, *sound*/*ground*; *rare*/*compare*): a Shakespearean sonnet.

**Argument by quatrain.** Each quatrain takes a group of conventional comparisons and denies them: lines 1–4, eyes, lips, breasts, hair (sight); lines 5–8, cheeks and breath (colour and scent); lines 9–12, voice and walk (sound and movement). Each quatrain covers more of the body and the senses, and each is a self-contained unit of mock-insult.

**The turn.** Line 13 begins *And yet*: the couplet reverses the direction. Everything before has said what she is *not*; the couplet says that the speaker's love is *as rare / As any she belied with false compare* — as precious as any woman praised with lying comparisons. The target was never the mistress; it was the poets who lie.

**What the form does.** The Shakespearean form, with its separable quatrains, suits a list of parallel denials, and its closing couplet suits a sudden reversal and a summary. The poem uses the form of the conventional love sonnet to criticise the conventional love sonnet, and then, in the form's own place for a conclusion, to write a sincere one.
:::
:::

John Milton's sonnet on his blindness, printed in his *Poems* of 1673, uses the Petrarchan form:

> *When I consider how my light is spent,*
> *Ere half my days, in this dark world and wide,*
> *And that one talent which is death to hide,*
> *Lodged with me useless, though my soul more bent*
> *To serve therewith my Maker, and present*
> *My true account, lest he returning chide;*
> *"Doth God exact day-labour, light denied?"*
> *I fondly ask; but Patience, to prevent*
> *That murmur, soon replies, "God doth not need*
> *Either man's work or his own gifts; who best*
> *Bear his mild yoke, they serve him best. His state*
> *Is kingly. Thousands at his bidding speed*
> *And post o'er land and ocean without rest:*
> *They also serve who only stand and wait."*

(*Talent* alludes to the parable in Matthew 25, in which a servant who buries the money (*talent*) his master entrusted to him is punished; *fondly* ‘foolishly’; *post* ‘travel fast’.)

::: example Where the turn falls {#ex-milton-sonnet}
Find the rhyme scheme of Milton's sonnet. Where is the turn, and how does it relate to the division between octave and sestet?
::: solution
**Rhyme scheme.** *spent*, *wide*, *hide*, *bent*, *present*, *chide*, *denied*, *prevent*: *abbaabba*. *Need*, *best*, *state*, *speed*, *rest*, *wait*: *cdecde*. A strict **Petrarchan** sonnet ([[#def-sonnet]]).

**The turn.** The octave states the speaker's anxious complaint: his sight is gone before half his life is over, his gift is useless, and he fears God's judgement — ending in the bitter question *Doth God exact day-labour, light denied?* The answer, from Patience, begins *in the middle of line 8*: *but Patience, to prevent / That murmur, soon replies*. The turn comes half a line **early**, and the sentence runs over the division between octave and sestet.

**Effect.** In a regular Petrarchan sonnet the turn waits for line 9. Here Patience interrupts the complaint before it can finish — *to prevent / That murmur* — and the syntax enacts it: the answer breaks into the octave. The sestet then answers in calmer, more balanced sentences, ending in the famous line made of monosyllables, *They also serve who only stand and wait*, which accepts stillness as a form of service. The departure from the form's expectation is part of the meaning: patience comes sooner than the speaker expects.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which rhyme scheme belongs to a Shakespearean sonnet?

- [x] *abab cdcd efef gg*
- [ ] *aabb ccdd eeff gg*
- [ ] *aba bcb cdc ded ee*
::: solution
Three quatrains and a couplet with seven rhyme sounds ([[#def-sonnet]]). The first is Petrarchan; the third is a series of couplets; the fourth is the interlinked tercets of *terza rima* closed by a couplet, which Shelley used in "Ode to the West Wind" (1820).
:::
:::

Not every sonnet follows a type. Percy Bysshe Shelley's "Ozymandias" (published in *The Examiner*, 1818) tells of a ruined statue of an ancient king in the desert:

> *I met a traveller from an antique land*
> *Who said: Two vast and trunkless legs of stone*
> *Stand in the desert. Near them, on the sand,*
> *Half sunk, a shattered visage lies, whose frown,*
> *And wrinkled lip, and sneer of cold command,*
> *Tell that its sculptor well those passions read*
> *Which yet survive, stamped on these lifeless things,*
> *The hand that mocked them and the heart that fed:*
> *And on the pedestal these words appear:*
> *"My name is Ozymandias, king of kings:*
> *Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair!"*
> *Nothing beside remains. Round the decay*
> *Of that colossal wreck, boundless and bare*
> *The lone and level sands stretch far away.*

::: example A broken form {#ex-ozymandias}
Work out the rhyme scheme of "Ozymandias". Is it Petrarchan or Shakespearean? How might its form relate to its subject?
::: solution
**Rhyme scheme.** *land* (a), *stone* (b), *sand* (a), *frown* (b — a slant rhyme with *stone*), *command* (a), *read* (c), *things* (d), *fed* (c), *appear* (e), *kings* (d), *despair* (e), *decay* (f), *bare* (e), *away* (f): *ababacdcedefef*.

**Type.** Neither. The octave begins like an English sonnet (*abab*) but adds a fifth *a* rhyme and then shifts; the sestet's pattern (*edefef*) borrows a rhyme (*e*) from the octave. There is a turn in about the usual Petrarchan place — line 9, *And on the pedestal these words appear* — when the description gives way to the king's boast, and a second, sharper turn in the middle of line 12: *Nothing beside remains.*

**Form and subject.** The poem is about a great monument that has fallen into fragments. Its form is a sonnet — a fixed, ordered structure — whose rhymes are displaced and interlocked irregularly, as if the structure has been shaken. Whether Shelley intended this effect cannot be known, and a careful essay would say *the form can be read as*, not *Shelley deliberately broke*. But the reading fits the poem: the king's command (*Look on my works … and despair!*) survives only as words, and the sonnet's rigid frame survives only in pieces. The final lines, long vowels and alliteration (*boundless and bare*, *lone and level*), stretch out like the sand.
:::
:::

## The ballad

::: definition Ballad and ballad stanza {#def-ballad}
A **ballad** is a narrative poem, originally a song, that tells a story — often of love, death, the supernatural or a dramatic event — in simple language, with dialogue, repetition and little comment. Traditional (folk) ballads were passed on orally and have no known author; **literary ballads** imitate them. The **ballad stanza** is a quatrain alternating lines of four and three beats, rhyming *abcb* (sometimes *abab*). When the lines are strictly iambic (8, 6, 8, 6 syllables), the same pattern is called **common metre**, the commonest metre of English hymns.
:::

Samuel Taylor Coleridge's "The Rime of the Ancient Mariner" (in *Lyrical Ballads*, 1798; revised for *Sibylline Leaves*, 1817) is the most famous literary ballad. Its revised opening:

> *It is an ancient Mariner,*
> *And he stoppeth one of three.*
> *"By thy long grey beard and glittering eye,*
> *Now wherefore stopp'st thou me?"*

(The 1798 text spelled the title and the first line with deliberately old spellings, *Rime of the Ancyent Marinere*.)

Four beats in lines 1 and 3, three in lines 2 and 4; *three* rhymes with *me* (*abcb*). The stanza starts *in medias res* ([[reading-literature/narrative#rule-order]]), moves at once into dialogue, and uses old forms (*stoppeth*, *thy*, *wherefore* ‘why’) to sound like a traditional song. Line 2 has more unstressed syllables than the strict iambic pattern — *and he* **STOP** *peth* **ONE** *of* **THREE** — typical of the ballad's four-beat rhythm ([[reading-literature/metre-rhythm]]).

Emily Dickinson (1830–86) wrote almost all her poems in variations of common metre, a form she knew from hymns, and made it strange with slant rhymes, dashes and compressed grammar. Very few of her poems were published in her lifetime; the first selection, edited by Mabel Loomis Todd and Thomas Wentworth Higginson, appeared in 1890, with her punctuation regularised and some words changed. The first stanza of a poem printed in the second series (1891):

> *Hope is the thing with feathers*
> *That perches in the soul,*
> *And sings the tune without the words,*
> *And never stops at all,*

Beats of 3, 3, 4, 3, with a slant rhyme (*soul*/*all*) in place of the full rhyme of a hymn. (Modern editions, based on her manuscripts, print the poem with her dashes; when you quote Dickinson, say which text you use.)

::: warning Calling every poem with rhymes a sonnet
✗ *This poem is a sonnet because it rhymes and is about love.*

✓ *This poem is a sonnet: it has fourteen lines of iambic pentameter rhyming abab cdcd efef gg, with a turn in the final couplet.*

A form is defined by its structure, not its subject. A sonnet may be about death, politics or a ruined statue; a love poem may be a ballad, a song or free verse. Count the lines, identify the metre and the rhyme scheme ([[#rule-describe-form]]) before you name the form. Equally, do not stop once you have named it: say what the form does.
:::

## Open forms: blank verse and free verse

::: definition Blank verse and free verse {#def-blank-free}
**Blank verse** is unrhymed iambic pentameter. It is the verse of Shakespeare's plays, Milton's *Paradise Lost*, Wordsworth's *The Prelude* and many long poems. **Free verse** (French *vers libre*) has neither regular metre nor regular rhyme; its lines vary in length and are shaped by the poet's sense of rhythm, syntax and emphasis.
:::

Do not confuse the two: blank verse has strict metre and no rhyme; free verse has neither. Blank verse is close enough to the rhythms of speech to carry drama and long argument, and its freedom from rhyme lets sentences run over the lines (as in the Milton passage of [[reading-literature/metre-rhythm#ex-milton]]). It is discussed further, with Shakespeare's practice, in [[reading-literature/drama-shakespeare]].

Free verse in English is usually dated from Walt Whitman's *Leaves of Grass* (1855), whose first poem (later called "Song of Myself") begins:

> *I celebrate myself,*
> *And what I assume you shall assume,*
> *For every atom belonging to me as good belongs to you.*

::: theorem What organises free verse {#rule-free-verse}
Free verse has no regular metre, but it is not unorganised. Look for:

1. **The line**: where lines break, and whether breaks fall at grammatical boundaries or cut across them; long and short lines.
2. **Repetition and parallelism**: the same word or structure at the start of lines (**anaphora**), lists, balanced clauses — often influenced by the King James Bible, especially the Psalms.
3. **Rhythm without metre**: clusters of stresses, runs of light syllables, passages that briefly become metrical.
4. **Sound**: alliteration, assonance, occasional rhyme.
5. **Visual form**: spacing, indentation, white space on the page.
:::

::: proof
These are the means poets writing in free verse have used in place of metre and rhyme. Whitman's long lines are built on parallelism and anaphora, which he learned partly from the English Bible; the Psalms in the King James translation (1611) are verse in Hebrew whose parallelism survives in English prose, a model for poetry without metre. Twentieth-century free verse often relied more on the line break and the image (the Imagists of 1912–17 called for "direct treatment of the thing"). Paul Fussell's *Poetic Meter and Poetic Form* (1979) and Charles O. Hartman's *Free Verse: An Essay on Prosody* (1980) discuss how free verse creates rhythm. Point 1 matters most: in free verse, the line break is the poet's chief formal decision, and it controls pace and emphasis.
:::

::: example Reading free verse {#ex-whitman}
Analyse Whitman's three lines using [[#rule-free-verse]].
::: solution
**Lines.** They grow longer: 3 words, 6 words, 11 words. Each line ends at a grammatical boundary, so there is no enjambment; the growing length enacts expansion — from *myself* to *you* to *every atom*.

**Parallelism and repetition.** Line 2 repeats *assume* in two clauses (*what I assume you shall assume*); line 3 balances *belonging to me* against *belongs to you*. Each line moves from *I*/*me* to *you*.

**Rhythm.** Line 1 is almost metrical (*i* **CEL** *e* *brate* *my* **SELF**, three beats); line 3 is long and uneven, with no regular pattern, but its stresses (**EV**-*ery* **AT**-*om* … **GOOD** … **YOU**) build to the final pronoun.

**Meaning.** The lines claim that the self being celebrated is not separate from the reader: what belongs to *me* belongs to *you*. The form serves that claim — each line begins with the self and ends with the other, and the lines expand outward. The form is free, but it is not random.
:::
:::

## Other forms

- **Ode**: a long, serious lyric addressed to a person, a thing or an idea, often in elaborate stanzas (Keats's odes of 1819, among them "To Autumn" and "Ode to a Nightingale").
- **Elegy**: a poem of mourning or meditation on death (Milton's "Lycidas", 1638; Gray's "Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard", 1751; Tennyson's *In Memoriam*, 1850).
- **Dramatic monologue**: a poem spoken by a character, not the poet, to a silent listener, revealing more than the speaker intends. Robert Browning's "My Last Duchess" (1842) begins *That's my last Duchess painted on the wall, / Looking as if she were alive*; the Duke's account of his dead wife gradually suggests that he had her killed (*I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together*). The dramatic monologue is the poetic cousin of the unreliable narrator ([[reading-literature/narrative#def-unreliable]]).
- **Villanelle**: nineteen lines on two rhymes, with two refrains repeated in a fixed pattern; a French form that became popular in English in the late nineteenth century and is best known today from twentieth-century examples.
- **Haiku** in English: a short poem modelled, loosely, on the Japanese form, often in three lines; English haiku cannot reproduce Japanese syllable counting exactly, since English and Japanese syllables differ.

::: quiz
A poem has long unrhymed lines of varying length, many beginning with the same words. It is most likely:
- [ ] blank verse
- [x] free verse
- [ ] a ballad
- [ ] a sonnet
::: solution
No rhyme and no regular line length (so no regular metre): free verse, organised here by anaphora ([[#rule-free-verse]]). Blank verse would be unrhymed but in regular iambic pentameter.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Fixed forms in both traditions.** The Chinese regulated poem (律诗) has eight lines in four couplets, with fixed tonal patterns, rhyme on the even lines and parallel couplets in the middle; the quatrain (绝句) has four. The English sonnet has fourteen lines and a turn; Chinese readers often compare the sonnet's movement with 起承转合 (introduction, development, turn, conclusion), which is a fair analogy for the Shakespearean form but should not be pressed too hard.
- **Rhyme.** Chinese verse usually rhymes on the even lines with a single rhyme throughout; English stanzas use several rhyme sounds in patterns (*abab*, *abba*). English rhyme depends on the stressed vowel and what follows it, not on the whole syllable, so *delight*/*white* rhyme although their first syllables differ.
- **Forms set to tunes.** The Chinese *ci* (词) was written to existing tune patterns (词牌), each with its own line lengths. The English ballad and common metre are similar: many hymns in common metre can be sung to the same tune, and Dickinson's poems can often be sung to hymn tunes. See the Chinese Atlas lesson on [the forms of the *ci*](/learn/chinese/lesson.php?c=poetry-metrics&l=ci-forms).
- **Free verse.** Modern Chinese poetry (新诗) broke with classical forms in the early twentieth century, partly under the influence of English and American free verse; Hu Shi's *Experiments* (《尝试集》, 1920) was the first collection of vernacular poetry. The questions of [[#rule-free-verse]] apply to both.
:::

::: history
The sonnet was invented in thirteenth-century Sicily and perfected by Petrarch (1304–74). Sir Thomas Wyatt and Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey, brought it into English in the 1530s and 1540s; their poems were printed after their deaths in *Tottel's Miscellany* (1557), where Surrey's sonnets already use the English pattern of three quatrains and a couplet. The 1590s saw a fashion for sonnet sequences, among them Sidney's *Astrophil and Stella* (printed 1591) and Spenser's *Amoretti* (1595); Shakespeare's *Sonnets* were printed in 1609. Milton used the Petrarchan form for political and personal subjects; Wordsworth revived it in the early nineteenth century. Thomas Percy's *Reliques of Ancient English Poetry* (1765) brought traditional ballads to the attention of educated readers, and Wordsworth and Coleridge's *Lyrical Ballads* (1798) adapted the form. Whitman's *Leaves of Grass* (1855) opened the way to free verse, which became common in English from the 1910s.
:::

::: summary
- Words **rhyme** when their final stressed vowel and what follows match; rhyme may be full, slant, masculine, feminine, eye or internal; a **rhyme scheme** is written with letters ([[#def-rhyme]]).
- A **stanza** is a repeated group of lines: couplet, tercet, quatrain, rhyme royal, Spenserian stanza; the **heroic couplet** is rhymed pentameter in pairs ([[#def-stanza]]).
- Describe form by lines, metre, rhyme, fixed form, turn — and then say what the form does ([[#rule-describe-form]]).
- The **sonnet** has fourteen lines and a turn: Petrarchan (*abbaabba cdecde*), Shakespearean (*abab cdcd efef gg*) or Spenserian; poets vary the form for effect ([[#def-sonnet]]).
- The **ballad** tells a story in quatrains of four and three beats rhyming *abcb*; **common metre** is its strict iambic form, used by hymns and by Dickinson ([[#def-ballad]]).
- **Blank verse** is unrhymed iambic pentameter; **free verse** has no regular metre or rhyme but is organised by line breaks, repetition, parallelism and sound ([[#def-blank-free]], [[#rule-free-verse]]).
:::

Compare it with the couplet of Sonnet 130.
::: solution
The three quatrains of Sonnet 73 each present an image of the speaker's ageing (autumn, twilight, a dying fire). The couplet turns from the speaker to the listener (*thou*) and draws a conclusion: because you see that I am near death, your love grows stronger, since you must soon lose me. Like the couplet of Sonnet 130, it turns and sums up; but in 130 the couplet reverses the argument (*And yet*), while in 73 it applies the argument to the listener. Both show the Shakespearean couplet as the form's place for a conclusion ([[#def-sonnet]]).
:::

::: exercise Ballad features {level=2}
List four features of the ballad ([[#def-ballad]]) that appear in the first stanza of "The Rime of the Ancient Mariner".
::: solution
(1) The ballad stanza: four lines of four and three beats, rhyming *abcb* (*three*/*me*). (2) It begins abruptly, in the middle of the action. (3) Dialogue without a speech tag (*By thy long grey beard …* — the wedding guest speaks). (4) Simple, old-fashioned diction (*stoppeth*, *thy*, *wherefore*), imitating a traditional song. Also: a narrative, not a reflection.
:::

::: exercise Blank or free? {level=2}
Is this passage, written for this lesson, blank verse or free verse? Explain. *The rain had stopped. The street was wet and bright, / and every window held a piece of sky.*
::: solution
**Blank verse.** Both lines are iambic pentameter (*the* **RAIN** | *had* **STOPPED** | *the* **STREET** | *was* **WET** | *and* **BRIGHT**; *and* **EV** | *ery* **WIN** | *dow* **HELD** | *a* **PIECE** | *of* **SKY**) and they do not rhyme (*bright*/*sky*). Free verse would have no regular metre.
:::

::: exercise Describe and interpret a form {level=3}
Choose a sonnet by Shakespeare, Milton, Wordsworth or Keats (all in the public domain). Describe its form using all six questions of [[#rule-describe-form]], and write a paragraph of 150–200 words on how the form shapes its meaning.
::: hint
Find the turn first. Does it fall where the form leads you to expect?
:::
::: solution
Answers will differ. A strong answer gives the line count, metre and rhyme scheme accurately; identifies the type (or the departure from types); locates the turn by the words that mark it (*but*, *yet*, *then*, a question, a change of tense or person); and argues, with short quotations, how the divisions of the form organise the poem's argument or feeling. It ends with what the form *does*, not with its name.
:::

::: exercise Write in a form {level=3}
Write either (a) a ballad stanza (*abcb*, four and three beats) beginning a story, or (b) a quatrain and couplet in iambic pentameter (*abab cc*) that turns in the couplet. Mark the beats and the rhyme scheme, and explain your turn or your opening in one or two sentences.
::: solution
Answers will differ. Check (a) that lines 1 and 3 have four beats and lines 2 and 4 three, that lines 2 and 4 rhyme, and that the stanza starts a story, ideally *in medias res* with dialogue; or (b) that each line has ten syllables with five beats, that the rhymes follow *abab cc*, and that the couplet changes direction (*And yet …*, *But …*) rather than merely repeating the quatrain.
