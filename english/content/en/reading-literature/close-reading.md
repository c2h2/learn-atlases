Here are four lines from William Blake's poem "London", first printed in his *Songs of Experience* (1794):

> *I wander thro' each charter'd street,*
> *Near where the charter'd Thames does flow,*
> *And mark in every face I meet*
> *Marks of weakness, marks of woe.*

A quick reader takes in the sense in a few seconds: someone walks through London and sees unhappy faces. That is true, and it is almost nothing. A slower reader notices that *charter'd* appears twice in two lines, and wonders why a street and a river should both be "chartered" — a word that means ‘granted rights by a written charter’, and also ‘hired out’, ‘owned’, ‘mapped’. She notices that *mark* is a verb in line 3 (‘notice’) and a noun in line 4 (‘sign, scar’), so that the speaker's act of noticing seems to leave marks of its own. She hears the heavy repetition of *marks of … marks of …* and of *every … every*, and the way the river, which might have been the one free and natural thing in the city, is "charter'd" too. Every one of these observations is on the page; together they turn a simple statement into an argument about a city where even the water belongs to someone.

This is **close reading**: reading slowly enough to notice how a text says what it says, and asking what difference those choices make. It is the foundation of this course: later chapters give you more things to notice; this one gives the method that turns noticing into understanding. It builds on the strategic reading of [[academic-reading/strategic-reading]] and the inferences of [[reading-skills/inference]], but its question is different: *why is this text written exactly like this, and not otherwise?*

## What close reading is

::: definition Close reading {#def-close-reading}
**Close reading** (细读, also 文本细读) is the careful, repeated reading of a short passage — a poem, a paragraph of prose, a speech from a play — with attention to its **language** (word choice, grammar, sound, rhythm, imagery) and its **structure** (order, repetition, contrast, turning points), in order to explain how these features produce the passage's meanings and effects. The result of close reading is an **interpretation**: a reasoned account of what the passage means, supported by evidence from its words.
:::

Close reading is about **how**, not only **what**, and it ends in an interpretation **supported by evidence**. It is not a list of devices (*alliteration in line 4*), and not a free response (*this poem makes me think of my own city*), but an argument about the text, grounded in the text.

Literary critics use the word **form** for everything about how a text is made — its words, sentences, sounds, lines, sections and order — and **content** for what it is about. The central idea of close reading is that form and content cannot be separated. "London" is about misery in a city; but its particular meaning — that the misery comes from ownership and control, that the speaker cannot escape seeing it, that it repeats everywhere — is carried by *charter'd*, by *mark*/*Marks* and by *every*. Change the form and you change the meaning.

::: definition Diction, syntax and tone {#def-diction}
**Diction** is a writer's choice of words: their register (formal or informal, old or new, technical or everyday), their connotations, and their sound. **Syntax** is the arrangement of words into phrases and sentences: their length, their order, and how they are joined. **Tone** is the attitude that the language conveys towards its subject and its reader — tender, ironic, angry, solemn, playful ([[reading-skills/inference#def-attitude-tone]]).
:::

Diction is where most close reading starts, because single words are easy to point to. But syntax often carries as much: a very long sentence can enact accumulation; a sudden short one can stop the reader; an inverted order (*Dull would he be of soul*) puts weight on the word that comes first.

::: theorem The three questions of close reading {#rule-three-questions}
For any feature you notice in a passage, ask:

1. **What is there?** Describe the feature precisely: the word, its grammar, its sound, its position, its pattern (*charter'd* occurs twice, as an adjective, in two consecutive lines).
2. **What does it do?** Say what meaning or effect it produces in context, considering more than one possibility (the word suggests ownership, control, mapping; repeated, it suggests that nothing escapes).
3. **Why does it matter?** Connect the effect to the passage as a whole: how does this feature support, complicate or change your sense of what the passage is about?

An observation that stops at question 1 is description; one that skips question 1 is unsupported opinion. Close reading needs all three.
:::

::: proof
These questions follow the practice set out in the standard guides to literary study: description of a feature, analysis of its effect, interpretation of its significance (see, for example, the entries on explication and close reading in Abrams and Harpham's *A Glossary of Literary Terms*). Question 1 keeps the reader honest, because every claim can be checked against the page. Question 2 is where reading becomes thinking: the same feature can have different effects in different contexts — alliteration may sound harsh in one poem and soothing in another — so a device is never meaningful on its own. Question 3 stops close reading from becoming a hunt for devices. A reader who notices thirty features but cannot say how any of them matters has not yet interpreted the text.
:::

## A method: four readings

Close reading is not one reading but several, each with a different aim. The following procedure works for a poem of up to about twenty lines or a paragraph of prose.

::: theorem Reading a passage closely {#rule-method}
1. **First reading: for the sense.** Read the whole passage once without stopping, aloud if you can. Then write one or two sentences saying what it is about, literally: who speaks, to whom, where, about what. Look up unknown words, and words whose meaning may have changed.
2. **Second reading: annotate.** Mark everything that catches your attention: unusual or repeated words, strong images, changes of tone or direction, sounds (rhyme, alliteration), long or short sentences, questions and exclamations, the first and last words of lines and sentences. Write brief notes in the margin.
3. **Third reading: look for patterns.** Group your annotations. Which words belong to the same field of meaning? What is repeated? What contrasts are set up? Where does the passage turn?
4. **Fourth reading: interpret.** State what you now think the passage means and how it achieves that meaning, in a sentence or two, and test the statement against the whole passage. If a detail does not fit, either your statement needs changing or the detail is a tension worth discussing.
:::

::: proof
Each stage protects against a typical failure. Without stage 1, readers interpret a misreading: an archaic word (*wherefore* ‘why’, *still* ‘always’) sends the interpretation in the wrong direction. Without stage 2, readers notice only what they expected to see. Stage 3 is where observations become evidence: a single word is suggestive, but a group of related words is a pattern a reader can argue from. Stage 4 applies the tests of a good inference ([[reading-skills/inference#rule-good-inference]]). The number four is not sacred, but the order matters: sense before devices, patterns before conclusions.
:::

Good annotations are short and precise: underline a word and write *repeated*, *verb or noun?*, *odd word*, *contrast with l. 2*, *turn*. Circle words that belong together and join them with a line. Put a question mark next to anything you do not understand: a question is often the start of the best part of an interpretation.

::: example Four readings of a stanza {#ex-london}
Apply the method of [[#rule-method]] to the second stanza of Blake's "London" (1794):

> *In every cry of every Man,*
> *In every Infants cry of fear,*
> *In every voice: in every ban,*
> *The mind-forg'd manacles I hear*
::: solution
**First reading (sense).** The speaker hears something — "mind-forg'd manacles" — in all the cries, voices and prohibitions of the people of the city. *Ban* means a prohibition or a public curse or proclamation; *manacles* are chains for the hands; *mind-forg'd* means ‘made (forged, like iron) by the mind’. Blake's engraved text has no apostrophe in *Infants*; a modern edition would print *Infant's*.

**Second reading (annotations).**
- *every* five times in three lines.
- *In …* begins three lines: the sentence piles up places before it reaches its main clause.
- The main verb comes last, in the last word of the stanza: *I hear*. The subject and verb are delayed and inverted (normal order: *I hear the mind-forg'd manacles in every cry …*).
- *cry* twice; *voice*; *hear*: all about sound, not sight. Stanza 1 was about seeing (*mark in every face*).
- *mind-forg'd manacles*: alliteration on /m/ (and *Man* in line 1); a compound that joins something mental (*mind*) and something physical (*forg'd*, *manacles*).
- *ban*: a word of law and authority among the cries.

**Third reading (patterns).**
- **Accumulation**: *every* and the repeated *In* create a list that seems endless; there is no exception.
- **Sound**: the stanza moves from seeing to hearing; the city's misery is now heard everywhere.
- **Confinement**: *manacles* links back to *charter'd* in stanza 1. Both are about restraint: legal ownership, iron chains.
- **Source of the restraint**: the manacles are *mind-forg'd*. They are made by minds, not by smiths.

**Fourth reading (interpretation).** The stanza makes the speaker's claim universal through its syntax — the list of *every … every* delays the main verb until the speaker has gathered all the city's sounds — and then locates the cause of suffering in the human mind: the chains that bind these people are made of ideas, laws and habits of thought (*ban* suggests that some of these are official), not of iron. This develops stanza 1, where *charter'd* showed restraint in the streets and the river; now it is heard in every voice. Whether the minds that forge the manacles are those of the rulers or of the sufferers themselves is left open, and that openness is worth discussing in an essay.
:::
:::

Notice what the interpretation does *not* do. It does not say "Blake uses repetition" as if that were the point; it does not leap to biography or history before the words have been read; and it marks the place where the text stays open — whose minds? — instead of forcing a single answer.

## Things to notice

The second reading is easier if you know what kinds of features to look for. The list below is a checklist, not a recipe: no passage uses all of these, and the point is never to tick boxes but to find the features that matter in this passage.

::: theorem What to look for {#rule-checklist}
1. **Words.** Unusual, repeated or striking words; words with more than one meaning (puns, ambiguity); the register of the words (plain, formal, archaic, technical, colloquial); groups of words from one field of meaning (law, religion, nature, money).
2. **Grammar and syntax.** Long and short sentences; lists; inversion; questions, commands and exclamations; the tense and the person (*I*, *you*, *we*, *he*); active or passive; who is the grammatical subject — who acts and who is acted on.
3. **Sound.** Rhyme; alliteration (repeated initial consonants), assonance (repeated vowels); harsh or smooth sounds; rhythm and metre ([[reading-literature/metre-rhythm]]).
4. **Imagery and figures.** Pictures and sensations the words create; metaphors, similes, personification and symbols ([[reading-literature/figurative-language]]).
5. **Structure.** How the passage begins and ends; where it turns (*but*, *yet*, a new stanza, a change of tense); contrasts and parallels; what comes first and what last; in verse, the line and the stanza ([[reading-literature/poetic-forms]]).
6. **Voice.** Who is speaking; to whom; with what attitude; whether the speaker can be trusted ([[reading-literature/narrative]]).
:::

::: proof
These categories are the levels at which language can be analysed — vocabulary, grammar, sound, meaning and organisation — plus the literary category of voice. They overlap (an inversion is a matter of syntax and of emphasis), and they run from the easiest features to point to towards those that need a view of the whole. The list widens attention during annotation; the three questions of [[#rule-three-questions]] then decide which features are worth discussing.
:::

### Diction and connotation

A word chosen by a writer is chosen from among alternatives. Asking "what else could the writer have said?" is one of the most useful habits in close reading. Blake could have written *crowded street* or *busy street*; he wrote *charter'd*. The difference shows what the poem is interested in.

The **connotations** of a word — the associations and feelings it carries beyond its basic meaning ([[collocation-and-register/connotation]]) — are especially important in literature, where a single word may carry a whole attitude. So is **register** ([[collocation-and-register/register]]): a legal word in a love poem, or a slang word in a solemn one, creates an effect precisely because it does not belong.

::: warning Treating old words as modern words
Many English words have changed their meanings. A reader who assumes the modern meaning will misread the text.

✗ *In Shakespeare's line "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?", Juliet is asking where Romeo is.*

✓ *Wherefore means ‘why’: Juliet is asking why Romeo has to be a Montague, the son of her family's enemy.*

Other words to watch in older texts: *still* often means ‘always, constantly’; *nice* can mean ‘precise’ or ‘foolish’; *silly* can mean ‘simple, innocent’; *presently* ‘immediately’; *anon* ‘soon’; *awful* ‘inspiring awe’; *gay* ‘joyful, bright’. A historical dictionary such as the *Oxford English Dictionary*, or the notes of a good edition, will tell you what a word meant at the time.
:::

### Sound

Sound matters in prose as well as in verse. Read the opening of John Keats's "To Autumn" (written 1819, published 1820) aloud:

> *Season of mists and mellow fruitfulness,*
> *Close bosom-friend of the maturing sun;*
> *Conspiring with him how to load and bless*
> *With fruit the vines that round the thatch-eves run;*

The repeated /m/ of *mists and mellow* and *maturing*, the long vowels of *Close*, *load*, *round*, and the soft *s* and *f* sounds slow the reading down, which suits a season of ripeness and slow warmth. But sounds do not have fixed meanings: the /m/ that sounds full here could sound dull in another poem. The claim must always be about what a sound does *in this context*.

::: quiz
Which statement about the Keats lines is the best example of close reading?
- [ ] Keats uses alliteration in line 1.
- [ ] The lines make me feel calm, because I like autumn.
- [x] The soft, repeated /m/ sounds and long vowels slow the reading, which suits a description of ripeness and slow warmth.
- [ ] The poem is about autumn.
::: solution
Only the third option does all three things of [[#rule-three-questions]]: it names a feature (repeated /m/, long vowels), says what it does (slows the reading) and connects it to the meaning of the whole (ripeness, slowness). The first option is description only; the second is a personal response with no evidence from the text; the fourth is a summary.
:::
:::

### Syntax and structure

Read the octave (first eight lines) of William Wordsworth's sonnet "Composed upon Westminster Bridge, September 3, 1802", as printed in his *Poems, in Two Volumes* (1807):

> *Earth has not any thing to shew more fair:*
> *Dull would he be of soul who could pass by*
> *A sight so touching in its majesty:*
> *This City now doth like a garment wear*
> *The beauty of the morning; silent, bare,*
> *Ships, towers, domes, theatres, and temples lie*
> *Open unto the fields, and to the sky;*
> *All bright and glittering in the smokeless air.*

(*Shew* is an older spelling of *show*; *doth … wear* is an older form of *wears*.)

::: example Syntax and effect {#ex-westminster}
Look only at the syntax of these eight lines. Find (a) the inversion in line 2, (b) the long list in line 6, and (c) the way the sentences run across the ends of lines. What does each contribute?
::: solution
**(a) Inversion.** The normal order would be *He who could pass by a sight so touching in its majesty would be dull of soul.* Wordsworth begins with *Dull*. The judgement comes first and with full weight, as a challenge: anyone who is not moved is dull. Line 1, a superlative put negatively, is just as defiant — and the subject is surprising: a city, praised by a poet who usually found beauty in nature.

**(b) The list.** *Ships, towers, domes, theatres, and temples* — five nouns, without adjectives, from different parts of city life (trade, government, religion, entertainment). The list is the grammatical subject of *lie*, and the verb comes only after all of them. The effect is of a wide view taken in at one glance, everything held together in a single moment.

**(c) Run-on lines.** *This City now doth like a garment wear* runs across the line end to its object, *The beauty of the morning*, and *Ships … lie* runs on to *Open unto the fields*. The sense keeps flowing over the line endings, so the octave reads as one unbroken view. Lines that run on in this way are called **enjambed** ([[reading-literature/metre-rhythm]]).

**Putting it together.** The syntax enacts the experience: strong claims at the start, then an accumulating, unbroken description in which the city lies open to the country and the sky. *Smokeless air* explains why: it is early morning, before the day's fires are lit, and for a moment the city is as clear as nature.
:::
:::

## Interpretation and its limits

Can an interpretation be right? Critics have argued about this for a century; here are the main positions.

::: definition Interpretation, ambiguity and the intentional fallacy {#def-interpretation}
An **interpretation** is a reasoned account of a text's meaning. A word, phrase or passage is **ambiguous** when it supports more than one meaning at once; William Empson's *Seven Types of Ambiguity* (1930) argued that such multiple meanings are often the source of a poem's richness, not a fault. The **intentional fallacy** is the name W. K. Wimsatt and Monroe Beardsley gave, in an essay of 1946, to the view that the meaning of a literary work is what its author intended: they argued that the work itself, not evidence about the author's intention, is the standard for judging an interpretation.
:::

Not everyone accepts Wimsatt and Beardsley's argument. Some critics hold that a text means what its author meant (E. D. Hirsch, *Validity in Interpretation*, 1967); others that meaning is made by readers and their communities (Stanley Fish, *Is There a Text in This Class?*, 1980); others that it depends on historical context ([[reading-literature/writing-about-literature]] introduces these approaches). But close reading as a practice survives all these debates, because every approach still has to point to the words of the text. For a student, a useful working position is this:

::: theorem What makes an interpretation convincing {#rule-convincing}
An interpretation of a passage is convincing when it:

1. **accounts for the details** — it explains the features of the passage that a careful reader notices, especially the strange ones;
2. **is consistent with the whole passage**, and does not depend on ignoring a line or a word;
3. **respects the language of the time** — it does not depend on a modern meaning of a word that the text could not have had;
4. **is more economical than its rivals** — it explains more with fewer extra assumptions.

Several interpretations of a passage may be convincing at once, especially where the text is ambiguous; many more will not be.
:::

::: proof
These are the criteria that critics of different schools actually appeal to when they disagree: a rejected reading is usually shown to ignore a detail (criteria 1 and 2), to be anachronistic (3) or to need an unnecessary assumption (4). They do not determine a single correct reading, which is why literary study remains a field of argument, but they make it possible to say that some readings are better supported than others. Criterion 3 does not forbid new readings of old texts — a feminist or postcolonial reading may notice what earlier readers ignored — only misreading their words.
:::

::: example Testing two interpretations {#ex-test}
Two students read Wordsworth's line *And all that mighty heart is lying still!* (the last line of the Westminster Bridge sonnet, describing London in the early morning). Student A writes: *The city is dead; the poem is an elegy for London.* Student B writes: *The city is asleep, and the poet feels the stillness of its enormous life for a moment.* Use [[#rule-convincing]] to compare them.
::: solution
**Details.** The line before is *Dear God! the very houses seem asleep*. *Asleep* supports B directly, and *mighty heart* suggests great life, resting. A's reading must ignore *asleep*.

**Whole passage.** The octave praises the city's beauty (*Earth has not any thing to shew more fair*). An elegy would mourn; the octave celebrates. B is consistent with the whole; A is not.

**Economy.** B needs no extra assumption. A must assume that the poem secretly means the opposite of what it says, without evidence.

**But A has noticed something.** A heart that is *lying still* does recall death, and the whole poem is about a moment that will pass when the city wakes. A better essay might combine the two: the city is asleep, but the image of a still heart gives the calm a faint shadow of mortality, which makes the moment more precious. This is an ambiguity worth discussing ([[#def-interpretation]]), not a reason to call the poem an elegy.
:::
:::

## Close reading prose

Everything so far applies equally to prose. Prose has no lines or rhymes, but it has sentence length and rhythm, paragraph structure, repetition and sound. Here is the first paragraph of Jane Austen's *Pride and Prejudice* (1813), a single sentence:

> *It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife.*

::: example Irony in a single sentence {#ex-austen}
Read Austen's sentence closely. Is it true? Who believes it? What does its form suggest about the novel's attitude?
::: solution
**What is there.** The sentence has the grammar of a general law: the present tense (*It is*), a grand abstract noun (*a truth*), an intensifying adverb (*universally*), a passive with no agent (*acknowledged* — by whom?), and a modal of logical necessity (*must be*). Its diction is formal, even philosophical.

**What it does.** The grand form clashes with the small, social content: marriage and money. And the logic is reversed: in Austen's society it was women without fortune who were "in want of" rich husbands, and their families who assumed that every rich single man must want a wife. The next paragraph shows exactly this: a neighbourhood that considers a newly arrived rich young man "the rightful property" of one or other of its daughters.

**Why it matters.** The sentence is **ironic** ([[reading-skills/inference]]): it states, in the voice of universal wisdom, a belief that is really the self-interest of a particular group, and lets the reader see through it. The passive *acknowledged* hides who does the acknowledging; *universally* exaggerates. Taken at face value, it hides the novel's tone: amused, sceptical, interested in the gap between what people say and what they want. The narrator's irony is examined further in [[reading-literature/narrative]] and [[reading-literature/the-novel]].
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **A familiar practice with a different emphasis.** Chinese literary education has a long tradition of attentive reading: the commentators who annotated novels and plays line by line in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, such as Jin Shengtan (金圣叹) on *Water Margin*, practised a form of close reading (评点), and school 赏析 asks students to explain the 表达效果 of words and techniques. The habit transfers well. What often does not transfer is the expected answer: an English essay rewards an argument specific to *this* passage, not a set formula (*生动形象地表现了……*, *运用了比喻的修辞手法，生动地写出了……*). A sentence that would fit any poem is not close reading.
- **Words that have no single equivalent.** Many of the most important words in a passage — *charter'd*, *mark*, *still* — carry two meanings at once. A Chinese gloss usually chooses one. When a word is central, check every sense in an English dictionary rather than relying on a translation, and ask whether the text uses more than one.
- **Grammar as meaning.** Inversion, the agentless passive and tense are often used for effect in English literature. Because Chinese marks these differently (no tense inflection; a 被 passive that is often adversative), they are easy to overlook. Ask of each sentence: *who is the subject? what is the tense? is anything inverted?*
- **Reading aloud.** English verse and much literary prose depend on stress and rhythm ([[connected-speech/rhythm]]). Reading aloud, even quietly, makes sound patterns audible that silent reading misses.
:::

::: history
Close reading as a named method grew out of an experiment. At Cambridge in the 1920s, I. A. Richards gave students poems without the names of their authors and asked them to comment freely. His analysis of their responses, *Practical Criticism* (1929), showed how often educated readers misread the plain sense or responded to stock ideas rather than to the words. Richards's student William Empson published *Seven Types of Ambiguity* in 1930. In the United States, the critics later called the New Critics made close attention to "the text itself" the centre of literary teaching: Cleanth Brooks and Robert Penn Warren's textbook *Understanding Poetry* (1938) trained generations of students, and Brooks's *The Well Wrought Urn* (1947) argued that the meaning of a poem cannot be fully restated in a paraphrase. Later theories challenged the New Critics' exclusion of history, authors and readers, but kept close reading as a basic skill.
:::

::: summary
- **Close reading** is the slow, repeated reading of a short passage, attending to its language and structure, in order to explain how it produces its meanings and effects ([[#def-close-reading]]).
- Form and content cannot be separated: change the words, the syntax or the sound and the meaning changes.
- For every feature, ask **what is there**, **what it does** and **why it matters** ([[#rule-three-questions]]).
- Read at least four times: for the sense, to annotate, to find patterns, to interpret ([[#rule-method]]).
- Look at words, syntax, sound, imagery, structure and voice ([[#rule-checklist]]); ask what else the writer could have said.

- An interpretation is convincing when it accounts for the details, fits the whole passage, respects the language of the time and is economical ([[#rule-convincing]]); ambiguity can support more than one reading.

(d) He gave an *awful* account of the creation of the world. (e) The servant arrived *presently*, as soon as he was called.
::: solution
(a) ‘why’; (b) ‘always, constantly’; (c) ‘soon’; (d) ‘inspiring awe, solemn’ (not ‘very bad’); (e) ‘immediately’ (in modern English, *presently* usually means ‘soon’ in BrE or ‘now, currently’ in AmE).

::: exercise What else could the writer have said? {level=1}
For each word in italics, suggest one alternative the writer could have used, and say what the original word adds. (a) Blake: *I wander thro' each charter'd street* (instead of *wander*). (b) Wordsworth: *This City now doth like a garment wear / The beauty of the morning* (instead of *garment*). (c) Keats: *Close bosom-friend of the maturing sun* (instead of *bosom-friend*).
::: solution
Model answers; others are possible if they are argued.
(a) *walk*: *wander* suggests movement without a fixed purpose, so the speaker seems to see everything by chance, wherever he goes — which makes the misery seem everywhere.
(b) *cloak* or *dress*: *garment* is general and slightly formal; above all, a garment is worn and can be taken off, which suggests that the beauty of the morning is temporary — the city will put on its smoke later.
(c) *companion* or *ally*: *bosom-friend* is intimate and physical (*bosom* ‘breast’), suggesting closeness, warmth and even affection between the season and the sun; *Close* intensifies it.
:::

::: exercise Annotate a passage {level=2}
Annotate the sestet of Wordsworth's sonnet (1807) using [[#rule-checklist]]. List at least six observations.

> *Never did sun more beautifully steep*
> *In his first splendour, valley, rock, or hill;*
> *Ne'er saw I, never felt, a calm so deep!*
> *The river glideth at his own sweet will:*
> *Dear God! the very houses seem asleep;*
> *And all that mighty heart is lying still!*
::: solution
Possible observations:
1. **Inversion** in lines 1 and 3 (*Never did sun …*, *Ne'er saw I*), echoing the inversion of the octave (*Dull would he be*).
2. **Negatives** used to make superlatives: *Never … more beautifully*, *Ne'er … never*, as in line 1 of the poem (*Earth has not any thing …*).
3. **Comparison with nature**: the sun has never steeped *valley, rock, or hill* more beautifully — the city is set against the landscapes Wordsworth usually praised, and wins.
4. **Personification**: the sun (*his first splendour*), the river (*his own sweet will*), the houses (*asleep*), the city (*mighty heart*).
5. **Exclamations**: *a calm so deep!*, *Dear God!*, *lying still!* — the speaker's emotion breaks through.
6. **Sound**: long vowels and soft consonants in *glideth at his own sweet will*; *seem asleep*, *lying still* slow the end.
7. **Freedom**: *at his own sweet will* — the river moves freely (compare Blake's *charter'd Thames*).
8. **Structure**: the poem ends on stillness, after the movement of *glideth*.
:::

A model, from observation 7: *The river in Wordsworth's sestet "glideth at his own sweet will": the phrase gives the Thames a will of its own, and the long vowels and soft consonants make the line move slowly and smoothly. In the early morning, before the city's business begins, even the river seems free, part of a moment of calm in which the city belongs to no one. A reader who knows Blake's "charter'd Thames" may notice how differently the two poets see the same river.*
A good answer names the feature precisely, says what it does in context, and connects it to the passage as a whole.

::: exercise Two interpretations {level=2}
A student writes: *In "London", the speaker is happy, because he is wandering, which is a relaxing activity.* Use [[#rule-convincing]] to explain why this interpretation is weak.
::: solution
It fails criterion 1 and 2: it is based on one word (*wander*) and ignores the details that most readers notice — *marks of weakness, marks of woe*, *cry of fear*, *manacles*. It is inconsistent with the whole passage, whose diction is full of suffering and restraint. It also assumes that *wander* must mean relaxation, when in context it suggests movement without purpose, so that the speaker meets misery wherever he goes. A stronger interpretation explains *wander* as part of the poem's claim that suffering is everywhere.

::: exercise Close reading a prose sentence {level=3}
Read this sentence, the opening of Charles Dickens's *A Tale of Two Cities* (1859), and write a close reading of about 150 words. Consider syntax, repetition and contrast.

> *It was the best of times, it was the worst of times, it was the age of wisdom, it was the age of foolishness, it was the epoch of belief, it was the epoch of incredulity, it was the season of Light, it was the season of Darkness, it was the spring of hope, it was the winter of despair …*
::: hint
Look at the pattern of each pair of clauses. What figure of rhetoric is it ([[style-and-rhetoric/figures-of-speech]])? What does the length of the sentence do? The sentence continues well beyond this point.
:::
::: solution
A model:

*Dickens's opening is built from paired clauses, each pair identical in structure and opposite in meaning: best and worst, wisdom and foolishness, belief and incredulity, Light and Darkness. This antithesis is repeated so many times that the contrast stops seeming like a balance and becomes a contradiction that cannot be resolved: the same period was both things at once. The repetition of "it was" gives the sentence the rhythm of a list or a chant, and its great length delays any main point, so that the reader feels the confusion of an age that cannot be summed up. The capitalised Light and Darkness give the contrast an almost religious force. Before any character appears, the novel announces its subject: a world divided, on the edge of revolution.*

A good answer names the antithesis and the repetition, explains their effect, and connects them to the novel's subject (the French Revolution, the two cities).
:::
