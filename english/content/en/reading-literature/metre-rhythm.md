Read this line aloud, slowly, from Thomas Gray's "Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard" (1751):

> *The curfew tolls the knell of parting day*

Then read this paraphrase of it: *The evening bell rings to mark the end of the day.* The two say much the same thing. But the first has a regular movement that the second lacks: weak, strong, weak, strong, five times over — *the* **CUR**-*few* **TOLLS** *the* **KNELL** *of* **PART**-*ing* **DAY**. The slow, even beat suits a bell tolling at dusk, and it carries the reader into a poem about death and memory at a walking pace. Nothing in the paraphrase is wrong, but the music has gone.

That regular movement is **metre**. English poetry from Chaucer to the early twentieth century was mostly written in metre, and much is still written in it today; even free verse defines itself against it. To read English poetry well — and to hear Shakespeare's verse in [[reading-literature/drama-shakespeare]] — you need to be able to hear where the beats fall, name the pattern, and notice when a poet departs from it. This chapter shows how. It builds on the stress and rhythm of speech in [[connected-speech/sentence-stress]] and [[connected-speech/rhythm]], where you met the first line of Shakespeare's Sonnet 18 as an example of iambic pentameter.

## Stress and metre

English metre is built on **stress**: the greater prominence of some syllables than others ([[connected-speech/word-stress-patterns]]). In ordinary speech, content words (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, most adverbs) carry stress on one syllable, and function words (articles, prepositions, auxiliaries, pronouns) are usually unstressed. Poetry in English arranges stressed and unstressed syllables into regular patterns.

::: definition Metre, scansion and foot {#def-metre}
**Metre** (AmE *meter*) is a regular pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables that organises the lines of a poem. **Scansion** is the analysis of a line to show its metre: marking which syllables carry the beats and dividing the line into feet. A **foot** is the repeated unit of the metre, usually one stressed syllable with one or two unstressed syllables.
:::

Note that this is not quite the "foot" of [[connected-speech/rhythm#def-foot]]. There, a foot of speech rhythm always begins with a stress and runs to the next one. A metrical foot is a unit of the abstract pattern, and in the commonest English metre it begins with the *unstressed* syllable.

In this chapter, scansions are shown in two ways: the stressed syllables in bold capitals with feet divided by |, as in *the* **CUR** | *few* **TOLLS**; and, where useful, with the traditional marks × (unstressed, or "offbeat") and / (stressed, or "beat"), written out as `× / × / × / × / × /`.

::: theorem The main feet of English verse {#rule-feet}
| foot | pattern | adjective | word example | line example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **iamb** | × / | iambic | *be*-**LIEVE**, *a*-**WAY** | *the* **CUR** · *few* **TOLLS** · *the* **KNELL** … |
| **trochee** | / × | trochaic | **GAR**-*den*, **TY**-*ger* | **TY**-*ger* · **TY**-*ger* · **BURN**-*ing* · **BRIGHT** |
| **anapaest** (AmE *anapest*) | × × / | anapaestic | *in*-*ter*-**VENE** | *and his* **CO** · *horts were* **GLEAM** · *ing in* **PUR** · *ple and* **GOLD** |
| **dactyl** | / × × | dactylic | **MER**-*ri*-*ly*, **YES**-*ter*-*day* | **HALF** *a league* · **HALF** *a league* |
| **spondee** | / / | spondaic | **HEART**-**BREAK** | (only as a variation) |
| **pyrrhic** | × × | pyrrhic | — | (only as a variation) |

The **iamb** is by far the commonest foot in English verse. (In the table, · divides the feet.) Iambs and anapaests are called **rising** feet (they end on the beat); trochees and dactyls **falling** feet.
:::

::: proof
The names come from Greek and Latin verse, which was built on syllable **length** (quantity), not stress. English poets and critics adopted the names from the sixteenth century onwards for patterns of stress, which is a different principle; the borrowing works well enough for the commonest patterns and less well for others, which is one reason why some modern metrists, such as Derek Attridge (*The Rhythms of English Poetry*, 1982; *Poetic Rhythm: An Introduction*, 1995), prefer to describe English verse as a sequence of **beats** and **offbeats** rather than feet. The iamb's dominance reflects the language: English phrases very often begin with an unstressed function word followed by a stressed content word (*the* **DOG**, *to* **GO**, *in* **TIME**), so a rising pattern fits ordinary speech easily. Spondees and pyrrhics cannot form a whole line in English, because speech does not produce long runs of equal stresses; they appear as variations inside other metres.
:::

The number of feet in a line also has a name: **monometer** (one), **dimeter** (two), **trimeter** (three), **tetrameter** (four), **pentameter** (five), **hexameter** (six; an iambic hexameter is also called an **alexandrine**). The name of a metre combines the foot and the number: Gray's line is **iambic pentameter**; Blake's *Tyger* is **trochaic tetrameter**.

## Iambic pentameter

Iambic pentameter — five iambs, ten syllables — is the line of Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales* (in its Middle English pronunciation), of Shakespeare's plays and sonnets, of Milton's *Paradise Lost*, of Pope's couplets, and of much of Wordsworth and Keats. If you learn to hear one metre, learn this one.

::: widget graph
title: The beat of an iambic pentameter line
nodes: Shall@0,0; I@1,1; com@2,0; pare@3,1; thee@4,0; to@5,1; a@6,0; sum@7,1; mer's@8,0; day@9,1
edges: Shall-I; I-com; com-pare; pare-thee; thee-to; to-a; a-sum; sum-mer's; mer's-day
caption: The ten syllables of the first line of Shakespeare's Sonnet 18 (published 1609), placed high on a metrical beat and low on an offbeat: the zig-zag is the iambic pentameter pattern × / × / × / × / × /. The metre puts a beat on *to*, a function word that ordinary speech would leave unstressed; a natural reading gives it only a light beat, and the line still sounds regular because of the clear pattern around it. A dedicated scansion figure, in which the reader marks the stresses and the metre is named, is planned for this atlas but does not exist yet.
:::

::: theorem How to scan a line {#rule-scan}
1. **Read the line aloud** naturally, as a sentence, more than once.
2. **Count the syllables.** Check words whose syllable count can vary (*heaven* one or two syllables, *flower* one or two, *-ion*, *-ious*, *-ience*).
3. **Mark the fixed stresses**: the stressed syllable of every word of two or more syllables, from a dictionary if necessary (*com***PARE**, **SUM***mer*).
4. **Mark the likely stresses** of one-syllable words: content words usually stressed, function words usually unstressed.
5. **Find the pattern** that fits this line and most of the other lines of the poem; divide into feet.
6. **Note the variations**: places where the natural stress and the metre do not match, and lines with an extra or missing syllable.
7. **Ask what the variations do**: a departure from the pattern is often where the meaning is emphasised.
:::

::: proof
Steps 1–4 come from the language: metre is made of the stresses that speech already has, so a scansion that ignores the dictionary stress of a word (stressing *com*- in *compare*) is wrong whatever the metre. Step 5 recognises that the metre is a property of the whole poem, not a single line; one line may be ambiguous, but twenty lines rarely are. Steps 6 and 7 are where scansion becomes interpretation: poets in English almost never write long stretches of perfectly regular lines, because that sounds mechanical, and readers hear the variations against the expected pattern. This is the "tension" between metre and rhythm described in most guides to prosody (Paul Fussell, *Poetic Meter and Poetic Form*, revised edition 1979; Attridge 1995).
:::

::: example Scanning Gray's line {#ex-gray}
Scan *The curfew tolls the knell of parting day* using [[#rule-scan]].
::: solution
**Syllables:** *the* · *cur* · *few* · *tolls* · *the* · *knell* · *of* · *part* · *ing* · *day* — 10.

**Fixed stresses:** **CUR**-*few*, **PART**-*ing*.

**One-syllable words:** *tolls*, *knell*, *day* are content words — stressed; *the*, *the*, *of* are function words — unstressed.

**Pattern:** *the* **CUR** | *few* **TOLLS** | *the* **KNELL** | *of* **PART** | *ing* **DAY** — `× / × / × / × / × /`, five iambs: **iambic pentameter**, perfectly regular.

**What it does.** Every beat falls on a syllable that speech would stress, and every offbeat on a syllable speech would leave weak, so the line has no tension between metre and natural rhythm. The regularity, with heavy, long-vowelled stressed words (*tolls*, *knell*), imitates the slow, even strokes of a bell — the sound the line describes.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which word is an iamb?
- [ ] *garden*
- [x] *believe*
- [ ] *merrily*
- [ ] *heartbreak*
::: solution
*Be*-**LIEVE** is × /, an iamb. **GAR**-*den* is a trochee, **MER**-*ri*-*ly* a dactyl, and **HEART**-**BREAK** is often given as an example of a spondee ([[#rule-feet]]).
:::
:::

## Variations

A poem in iambic pentameter is not a poem in which every line is `× / × / × / × / × /`. Poets vary the pattern, and the variations are part of the music.

::: definition Common variations {#def-variations}
- **Inversion** (trochaic substitution): a trochee in place of an iamb, most often in the first foot of a line or after a pause: **NEV**-*er did* (/ ×) at the start of a line instead of × /.
- **Spondaic** and **pyrrhic** substitution: two stresses (/ /) or two weak syllables (× ×) in place of an iamb; they often occur together.
- **Feminine ending**: an extra unstressed syllable at the end of the line (eleven syllables in a pentameter). A line ending on a stress has a **masculine ending**.
- **Elision**: two syllables run together and counted as one (*th' expense*, *heav'n*, *disobedience* with *-ience* as one syllable).
- **Caesura**: a strong pause within the line, often marked by punctuation.
- **End-stopped line** and **enjambment**: a line whose sense and grammar pause at its end, or run on into the next line without a pause.
:::

::: example A feminine ending and a pause {#ex-hamlet}
Scan Hamlet's line (Shakespeare, *Hamlet*, c. 1600, Act 3 Scene 1): *To be, or not to be, that is the question*.
::: solution
**Syllables:** *to* · *be* · *or* · *not* · *to* · *be* · *that* · *is* · *the* · *ques* · *tion* — 11. One more than a regular pentameter.

**Stresses:** **QUES**-*tion* (fixed). *Be*, *not*, *be* are naturally stressed in this contrast. *That* and *is* are uncertain.

**A common scansion:** *to* **BE** | *or* **NOT** | *to* **BE** | *that* **IS** | *the* **QUES** | *tion* — five iambs plus an extra unstressed syllable: iambic pentameter with a **feminine ending** ([[#def-variations]]).

**An alternative:** many actors say **THAT** *is* — a trochaic inversion after the strong pause (**caesura**) at the comma, which puts weight on *that* (‘that is the question I must answer’). Both are possible; the metre allows either, and the choice is an interpretation.

**What it does.** The line is broken in the middle by a comma, as Hamlet's mind is divided between two possibilities, and it ends weakly, on the unstressed *-tion*: a falling, unresolved close that suits a question with no answer yet. Feminine endings are much more frequent in Shakespeare's later plays, where they give the verse a looser, more conversational movement.
:::
:::

::: example Inversion and spondees {#ex-rough-winds}
Scan the third line of Sonnet 18: *Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May*. Where does it depart from the iambic pattern, and why might that matter?
::: solution
**Syllables:** *rough* · *winds* · *do* · *shake* · *the* · *dar* · *ling* · *buds* · *of* · *May* — 10.

**Stresses:** *rough* and *winds* are both content words and both naturally stressed; **DAR**-*ling* is fixed; *shake*, *buds*, *May* are content words; *do*, *the*, *of* are function words.

**Scansion:** **ROUGH** **WINDS** | *do* **SHAKE** | *the* **DAR** | *ling* **BUDS** | *of* **MAY** — a **spondee** in the first foot, then four iambs.

**What it does.** The two heavy stresses at the start make the line begin roughly, as the words say: the metre itself is shaken. The rest of the line returns to the smooth iambic movement, appropriate to *darling buds*. The variation enacts the meaning: summer's beauty is disturbed by the wind. (*Do shake* is an older emphatic form; here it also supplies an unstressed syllable for the metre.)
:::
:::

::: example Elision and enjambment in Milton {#ex-milton}
Here are the first lines of John Milton's *Paradise Lost* (1667), in modern spelling (the first edition has *Mans First Disobedience* and *mortal tast*):

> *Of man's first disobedience, and the fruit*
> *Of that forbidden tree, whose mortal taste*
> *Brought death into the world, and all our woe,*

Scan the first line. What happens at the ends of the lines?
::: solution
**Syllables.** *Disobedience* has five syllables in careful speech (*dis*-*o*-*be*-*di*-*ence*), which would give the line eleven. In Milton's verse *-dience* is run together into one syllable (/djəns/), an **elision** of a kind Milton uses very consistently. So the line has ten.

**Scansion:** *of* **MAN'S** | *first* **DIS** | *o*-**BE** | *dience* **AND** | *the* **FRUIT**. *First* and *dis-* may both carry some stress (a spondaic movement in the second foot, *man's first dis-*), and *and* receives a beat that speech would not give it — a **promoted** stress, carried by the pattern. The heavy stresses on *man's first* give weight to the subject of the whole epic: humanity's first sin.

**Line ends.** *The fruit / Of that forbidden tree*: the sentence runs on without a pause — **enjambment**. *Whose mortal taste / Brought death*: again. The opening sentence of *Paradise Lost* runs for sixteen lines — its main verb, *Sing*, arrives only in line 6 — and line after line breaks inside a phrase. Milton defended his unrhymed verse, in a note added in 1668, by saying that musical delight lies in "the sense variously drawn out from one Verse into another". Enjambment is how blank verse avoids monotony ([[reading-literature/poetic-forms]]).
:::
:::

::: warning Scanning against the language
✗ *Shall I* **COM**-*pare thee*… (putting the beat on the first syllable of *compare* because the metre seems to need it)

✓ *Shall* **I** *com*-**PARE** *thee*…

The metre never changes the dictionary stress of a word of more than one syllable in normal modern pronunciation. If a scansion makes you mispronounce a word, it is wrong. Two exceptions need care: (1) some words were stressed differently in earlier English (Shakespeare often has *re*-**VENUE**, *com*-**PLETE** before a stressed noun, or *ex*-**TREME**), which a good edition will note; (2) a one-syllable word can be promoted or demoted by the pattern, as *to* and *and* are in the lines above. But never move the stress inside a word to fit your expectation.
:::

## Metre and rhythm

::: definition Metre and rhythm {#def-metre-rhythm}
The **metre** of a poem is its abstract pattern, the same for every line (iambic pentameter). The **rhythm** of a line is its actual movement when read: the varying strength of its stresses, its pauses, its speed. Rhythm realises the metre but is never identical to it.
:::

A line of perfect metrical regularity read with mechanical equal stress (*the* **CUR** *few* **TOLLS** *the* **KNELL** …, like a metronome) is called **sing-song**, and it is a common mistake of learners reading verse aloud. Good reading keeps the natural stress of the sentence, with its nucleus ([[connected-speech/sentence-stress]]) and its weak forms, and lets the metre be felt underneath. In Sonnet 18, *to* in *thee to a summer's day* takes a metrical beat but a very light stress in reading.

::: theorem Reading verse aloud {#rule-read-aloud}
1. **Read for the sentence first**: follow the punctuation and the grammar, not the line endings. Do not stop at the end of an enjambed line; pause only slightly, or not at all.
2. **Keep the natural stress of words and phrases**, including weak forms of function words ([[connected-speech/weak-forms]]).
3. **Let the metre guide uncertain stresses**: where a word could be stressed or not, the pattern usually decides.
4. **Do not exaggerate the beats**: a light beat on a function word is enough.
5. **Notice where reading feels difficult** — two stresses together, an extra syllable, a pause in mid-line: these are often the most expressive places.
:::

::: proof
The rule follows from the definition of [[#def-metre-rhythm]]. Metre is perceived because English speech already alternates strong and weak syllables ([[connected-speech/rhythm#rule-rhythm]]); a reader who distorts speech to fit the metre destroys the tension that makes verse expressive, and one who ignores the metre entirely loses the pattern. Actors trained in Shakespeare's verse are usually taught a version of these principles: honour the line, but speak the sense.
:::

## Other metres and other systems

Not all English verse is iambic. **Trochaic** metre gives a falling, insistent movement:

> *Tyger Tyger, burning bright,*
> *In the forests of the night;*

(William Blake, "The Tyger", *Songs of Experience*, 1794). **TY**-*ger* | **TY**-*ger* | **BURN**-*ing* | **BRIGHT** — four trochees, the last missing its unstressed syllable (a **catalectic** line, very common in trochaic verse, so that the line ends strongly). Seven syllables, four beats.

**Anapaestic** metre gallops:

> *The Assyrian came down like the wolf on the fold,*
> *And his cohorts were gleaming in purple and gold;*

(Lord Byron, "The Destruction of Sennacherib", 1815). *The as*-**SYR** | *ian came* **DOWN** | *like the* **WOLF** | *on the* **FOLD** — with *Assyrian* counted as three syllables (*as*-**SYR**-*yan*). Two unstressed syllables between each beat give speed: the line rides like the army it describes.

**Dactylic** metre is rarer in English. Alfred Tennyson's "The Charge of the Light Brigade" (1854) begins: *Half a league, half a league, / Half a league onward* — **HALF** *a league* | **HALF** *a league* — the rhythm of horses.

::: example Naming a metre {#ex-name-metre}
Scan the two Byron lines and name the metre. Why might Byron have chosen it?
::: solution
**Line 1:** *the as* **SYR** | *ian came* **DOWN** | *like the* **WOLF** | *on the* **FOLD** — four feet of × × /.

**Line 2:** *and his* **CO** | *horts were* **GLEAM** | *ing in* **PUR** | *ple and* **GOLD** — four feet of × × /.

**Metre: anapaestic tetrameter.** (The first foot of line 1 may be read as × /, *the As-*, if *Assyrian* is pronounced with four syllables and *the* elided; either way the pattern is anapaestic.)

**Why.** The poem tells the biblical story of the Assyrian army destroyed in a night (2 Kings 19). The rushing anapaests, two light syllables hurrying to each beat, give the sense of a cavalry charge, and the rhyme on *fold* / *gold* rings like a march. Anapaests are often used for comic verse too, so the metre is chosen for speed, not for seriousness; the effect depends on the subject.
:::
:::

**Four-beat verse.** Beneath many popular forms — nursery rhymes, ballads, hymns, songs — is a simpler principle: lines of four beats, with a variable number of unstressed syllables between them, often grouped in stanzas of four lines. *Mary had a little lamb* (Sarah Josepha Hale, 1830) has four beats in its first line: **MA**-*ry* **HAD** *a* **LIT**-*tle* **LAMB**. Attridge (1995) argues that this four-beat rhythm is the most natural rhythm of English verse, felt even by children who have never heard of feet. The ballad stanza of [[reading-literature/poetic-forms]] alternates four-beat and three-beat lines.

**Accentual verse** counts only the stresses, not the syllables. Old English poetry (*Beowulf*) uses lines of four main stresses linked by alliteration, divided by a caesura. Gerard Manley Hopkins (1844–89), whose poems were first published in 1918, called his own version **sprung rhythm**: each foot has one stress and any number of unstressed syllables, so stresses may come together. *Glory be to God for dappled things* ("Pied Beauty") has five stresses; the next line, *For skies of couple-colour as a brinded cow*, is longer but is counted the same way.

**Syllabic verse** counts only syllables, as French and Japanese verse do; in English it is a modern experiment. **Free verse** has no regular metre at all, though it has rhythm ([[reading-literature/poetic-forms]]).

::: quiz
How many beats does a line of iambic tetrameter have?
- [ ] 2
- [ ] 3
- [x] 4
- [ ] 5
::: solution
*Tetra-* means ‘four’: four feet, and in iambic verse one beat per foot, so four beats and normally eight syllables. Pentameter has five, trimeter three.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Stress against tone.** Classical Chinese regulated verse (近体诗) is built on syllable count (five or seven characters to a line) and on the alternation of **level and oblique tones** (平仄), with fixed rhyme. English metre is built on **stress**, which Mandarin uses much less; it counts syllables only in combination with stress. The two systems share an idea — a regular alternation of two kinds of syllable — but the kinds are different. For the Chinese system, see the Chinese Atlas lessons on [level and oblique tones](/learn/chinese/lesson.php?c=poetry-metrics&l=level-oblique) and [regulated verse](/learn/chinese/lesson.php?c=poetry-metrics&l=regulated-verse).
- **Caesura in both.** A five-character Chinese line usually divides 2 + 3, a seven-character line 4 + 3 (or 2 + 2 + 3). English pentameter has no fixed caesura, but a pause often falls near the middle; its position varies freely, which is one source of variety in blank verse.
- **Learners' rhythm.** Reading English verse with equal syllables, as Mandarin rhythm tends to suggest ([[connected-speech/rhythm]]), hides the metre entirely: the poem sounds like prose. Practise by tapping the beats, then by reading with light, quick offbeats.
- **Syllable counting.** Words such as *fire*, *hour*, *flower*, *heaven*, and words in *-ion* or *-ious*, may count as one syllable or two in verse. Chinese characters are always one syllable, so this variation can be surprising; check the metre of the surrounding lines.
:::

::: history
The first English treatise on versification is usually said to be George Gascoigne's *Certayne Notes of Instruction concerning the Making of Verse or Ryme in English* (1575), which advised poets to keep the natural stress of words. Chaucer had already used a ten-syllable line in the late fourteenth century; the Earl of Surrey's translations of Virgil, printed in the 1550s, introduced unrhymed iambic pentameter — **blank verse** — which Marlowe and Shakespeare made the verse of the English stage. In the 1580s and 1590s some poets, Sidney among them, experimented with classical quantitative metres in English, and Thomas Campion argued for them in his *Observations in the Art of English Poesie* (1602), to which Samuel Daniel replied with *A Defence of Ryme* (1603); accentual-syllabic verse won. In the nineteenth century, Hopkins developed sprung rhythm, and George Saintsbury's *A History of English Prosody* (1906–10) surveyed the whole tradition in terms of feet. Twentieth-century metrists, among them Paul Fussell and Derek Attridge, moved the emphasis from counting feet to hearing beats and the interplay of metre and speech.
:::

::: summary
- **Metre** is a regular pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables; **scansion** marks the beats and divides the line into **feet** ([[#def-metre]]).
- The main feet are the **iamb** (× /), **trochee** (/ ×), **anapaest** (× × /) and **dactyl** (/ × ×); spondees and pyrrhics occur as variations ([[#rule-feet]]). Line lengths run from monometer to hexameter.
- **Iambic pentameter**, five iambs, is the central line of English verse, from Chaucer to Wordsworth.
- Scan by reading aloud, counting syllables, marking dictionary stresses, finding the pattern and asking what the variations do ([[#rule-scan]]); never move the stress inside a word.
- Common variations: **inversion**, spondaic and pyrrhic substitution, **feminine ending**, **elision**, **caesura**, **enjambment** ([[#def-variations]]).
- **Metre** is the abstract pattern; **rhythm** is the actual movement of the line; read for the sense and let the metre be felt ([[#def-metre-rhythm]], [[#rule-read-aloud]]).
- English also has four-beat verse, accentual verse (Old English, Hopkins's sprung rhythm), syllabic verse and free verse.
:::

::: exercise Find the variation {level=2}
Each line is iambic pentameter with one variation. Name it. (a) *Look in thy glass and tell the face thou viewest* (Sonnet 3) (b) *Never did sun more beautifully steep* (Wordsworth, 1807) (c) *Of man's first disobedience, and the fruit* (Milton, 1667)
::: solution
(a) **Feminine ending**: *view*-*est* gives an extra unstressed syllable (11 syllables); the opening **LOOK** *in* may also be read as an inversion. (b) **Initial trochaic inversion**: **NEV**-*er* | *did* **SUN** … — the line begins with a stress, which suits the emphatic negative. (c) **Elision**: *-dience* counted as one syllable to give ten.

::: exercise End-stopped or enjambed? {level=2}
In the Milton lines of [[#ex-milton]], label each line end as end-stopped or enjambed, and say what the pattern contributes.
::: solution
Line 1 (*the fruit*): enjambed — *the fruit / Of that forbidden tree* runs on. Line 2 (*mortal taste*): enjambed — *taste / Brought death*. Line 3 (*all our woe,*): a slight pause at the comma, but the sentence continues (*With loss of Eden …*), so it is lightly end-stopped. The run-on lines carry the reader forward through the long opening sentence, delaying the main verb and giving the sense of a vast subject unfolding; the sense, as Milton said, is "variously drawn out from one Verse into another".
:::

::: exercise Read and justify {level=3}
Scan the first four lines of Shakespeare's Sonnet 18 (*Shall I compare thee to a summer's day? / Thou art more lovely and more temperate: / Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May, / And summer's lease hath all too short a date*). Mark at least one variation and say what it contributes. Then read the lines aloud, following [[#rule-read-aloud]].
::: hint
Line 2 needs care: how many syllables has *temperate* here? Which syllable of *temperate* takes the last beat?
:::
::: solution
Line 1: *shall* **I** | *com*-**PARE** | *thee* **TO** | *a* **SUM** | *mer's* **DAY** — regular; *to* takes a light, promoted beat. Line 2: *thou* **ART** | *more* **LOVE** | *ly* **AND** | *more* **TEM** | *per*-**ATE** — *temperate* has three syllables, the last carrying a light promoted beat; *and* is also promoted. Several pyrrhic-like feet make the line light and gentle, as its meaning (*more temperate*, ‘milder’) suggests. Line 3: initial spondee (**ROUGH** **WINDS**), see [[#ex-rough-winds]]. Line 4: *and* **SUM** | *mer's* **LEASE** | *hath* **ALL** | *too* **SHORT** | *a* **DATE** — regular, though *all too short* has three strong-ish syllables in a row, slowing the line on the idea of brevity. Other defensible scansions are acceptable if they keep dictionary stress.
:::

::: exercise Write in metre {level=3}
Write four lines of iambic pentameter on a subject of your choice (a city at night, a train journey, a season). Scan them, mark any variations, and say in one or two sentences why you used each variation where you did.
::: solution
Answers will differ. A model line: *the* **TRAIN** | *moves* **SLOW** | *ly* **THROUGH** | *the* **SLEEP** | *ing* **TOWN** — check that each line has ten syllables (or eleven with a feminine ending), that every polysyllable keeps its dictionary stress, and that the beats fall on syllables that can naturally carry them. A good answer explains a variation by its effect, for example an initial inversion on a verb of sudden movement (**STOP**-*ping*).
:::
