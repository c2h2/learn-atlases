In 1924, Virginia Woolf began an argument about the modern novel with a deliberately outrageous sentence: "on or about December 1910 human character changed." She did not mean it literally, and she said so; she meant that the relations between people — masters and servants, husbands and wives, parents and children — had shifted, and that literature had to change with them. But her joke points at something every student of literature meets: the habit of dividing the past into **periods**, each with a name, dates and a character. Chaucer is "medieval", Shakespeare "Renaissance", Wordsworth "Romantic", Dickens "Victorian", Woolf herself "modernist".

Periods are useful. They group writers who shared a language, a society, a set of problems and often a set of ideas about what literature should do, and they let a reader place a new text quickly: a poem in heroic couplets that mocks fashionable society is probably from the early eighteenth century; a long poem in blank verse about the poet's childhood among mountains is probably Romantic. But periods are also conventions made by later readers, and they can mislead. This chapter gives a map of English literature from the Anglo-Saxons to the modernists, with the main writers and features of each period, and a warning about how to use the map.

## What a period is

::: definition Literary period {#def-period}
A **literary period** is a span of time, conventionally bounded by dates, during which literature is taken to share characteristic features — of language, form, subject and belief — that distinguish it from what came before and after. Period names come from different sources: from **language** (Old English, Middle English), from **rulers** (Elizabethan, Jacobean, Victorian), from **cultural movements** (Renaissance, Enlightenment, Romanticism, Modernism) and from **dates** (eighteenth-century, post-war).
:::

::: theorem Using periods with care {#rule-periods}
1. **Period dates are conventions.** The Romantic period is often dated 1798–1832 (from *Lyrical Ballads* to the Reform Act and the death of Walter Scott), but writers did not change on 1 January 1798.
2. **Periods overlap.** Blake was writing "Romantic" poems in the 1790s; Milton published *Paradise Lost* (1667) after the Restoration, though his formation belongs to an earlier age; Hardy's novels are Victorian and his poems are partly modern.
3. **Not every writer fits.** Many important writers are untypical of their period, and the features of a period are usually drawn from a few major figures.
4. **Names were often given later.** The Romantic poets did not call themselves Romantics; the term was applied to them later in the nineteenth century.
5. **Periods describe tendencies**, so use them to raise questions about a text (*how is this poem Romantic? where does it depart?*), not to replace reading it.
:::

::: proof
These cautions are standard in literary history (see the entries on periods in Abrams and Harpham's *A Glossary of Literary Terms*; M. H. Abrams, *The Mirror and the Lamp*, 1953, on the Romantic change in ideas of poetry). The difficulty is built into the idea of a period: it groups texts by what they share, so it is strongest where texts are most alike and weakest at the edges. Periods named after monarchs (Victorian, 1837–1901) are especially arbitrary, since a queen's accession or death has no direct effect on literature, but they are convenient and established. Movement names (Romantic, Modernist) are more meaningful, because they name shared ideas, but they also bring arguments about who belongs. Point 5 is the practical conclusion: a period label is a hypothesis about a text, to be tested against the text.
:::

## Old and Middle English

**Old English** (Anglo-Saxon) literature was written between about 650 and the twelfth century in the language of the Anglo-Saxons, which modern readers cannot understand without study ([[history-of-english/old-english]]). Its most famous poem, *Beowulf*, survives in a single manuscript copied around the year 1000. It begins:

> *Hwæt! We Gardena in geardagum,*
> *þeodcyninga, þrym gefrunon,*

‘Listen! We have heard of the glory of the kings of the Spear-Danes in days of old.’ Each line has four main stresses, two on each side of a pause, linked by alliteration (*Gardena* / *geardagum*; *þeodcyninga* / *þrym*), the accentual metre described in [[reading-literature/metre-rhythm]]. Most readers meet *Beowulf* in translation; remember that a modern translation has its own author and copyright.

**Middle English** (about 1100–1500) is the language after the Norman Conquest, enriched by French ([[history-of-english/norman-french]]). Its greatest writer is Geoffrey Chaucer (c. 1343–1400), whose *Canterbury Tales* (c. 1387–1400) begins:

> *Whan that Aprill with his shoures soote*
> *The droghte of March hath perced to the roote,*
> *And bathed every veyne in swich licour*
> *Of which vertu engendred is the flour;*

::: example Reading Middle English {#ex-chaucer}
Translate the four lines into modern English and list three features that show they are Middle English.
::: solution
**Translation.** *When April with its sweet showers has pierced the drought of March to the root, and bathed every vein (of the plants) in such liquid, by whose power the flower is produced …* (The sentence continues for fourteen more lines before it reaches its main clause: *Thanne longen folk to goon on pilgrimages* — ‘then people long to go on pilgrimages’.)

**Features.**
1. **Spelling and words**: *Whan* ‘when’, *shoures* ‘showers’, *soote* ‘sweet’, *swich* ‘such’, *licour* ‘liquid, sap’ — spellings reflect a pronunciation before the Great Vowel Shift ([[history-of-english/great-vowel-shift]]).
2. **Grammar**: *his* as the possessive of a thing (*Aprill with his shoures*), since *its* did not yet exist; *Whan that* (‘when’) with a redundant *that*; word order with the adjective after the noun (*shoures soote*).
3. **French vocabulary** beside native English: *licour*, *vertu*, *engendred*, *flour*, *perced* are French loans; *shoures*, *droghte*, *roote* (Norse), *bathed* are native or Norse.
4. **Metre**: iambic pentameter, rhyming couplets — but only if the final *-e* of words like *soote* and *roote* is pronounced, as it was in Chaucer's English.

**Period.** The mixture of French and English words, the rhyming pentameter couplets learned from French and Italian poetry, and a pilgrimage frame that brings together characters from every level of society are all characteristic of Chaucer and of his age.
:::
:::

Other major works of the period are *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight* (late fourteenth century), William Langland's *Piers Plowman* and Thomas Malory's *Le Morte Darthur*, printed by William Caxton in 1485.

## The Renaissance (about 1500–1660)

The **Renaissance** — the ‘rebirth’ of classical learning that began in Italy — reached England in the sixteenth century, together with the Reformation and, from 1476, printing. Its literature is often subdivided by monarch: **Elizabethan** (1558–1603) and **Jacobean** (1603–25). It includes the sonnet sequences of Sidney, Spenser and Shakespeare ([[reading-literature/poetic-forms]]), Spenser's *The Faerie Queene* (1590–96), the drama of Marlowe, Shakespeare and Jonson ([[reading-literature/drama-shakespeare]]), the metaphysical poetry of John Donne and George Herbert ([[reading-literature/figurative-language#def-conceit]]), and the prose of the **King James Bible** (1611), whose rhythms entered English literature for three centuries:

> *In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth. And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters. And God said, Let there be light: and there was light.*

(Genesis 1:1–3.) Short clauses joined by *and*, concrete nouns, few adjectives, a rhythm of balanced phrases (*without form, and void*): this style, a translation shaped by the Hebrew original and by earlier English versions, especially William Tyndale's, influenced writers as different as Bunyan, Blake, Whitman and Hemingway.

The period ends, conventionally, with the Civil War (1642–51) and the Restoration of the monarchy (1660). John Milton (1608–74) bridges it: a poet of the Renaissance in learning, who wrote *Paradise Lost* (1667) after the Restoration.

## The Restoration and the eighteenth century (1660–1798)

After 1660, the theatres reopened, and a literature of wit, reason and social life developed. The years around 1700–45 are often called the **Augustan** age, after the age of the Roman emperor Augustus, whose poets (Virgil, Horace) the English writers admired. John Dryden and Alexander Pope wrote in heroic couplets ([[reading-literature/poetic-forms#def-stanza]]); satire flourished; and the novel was born, with Defoe, Richardson and Fielding ([[reading-literature/the-novel]]). The period is also called the **Enlightenment**, for its trust in reason and observation, and Samuel Johnson's *Dictionary* (1755) belongs to it ([[history-of-english/dictionaries-grammars]]).

::: example Recognising Augustan satire {#ex-swift}
Jonathan Swift's *A Modest Proposal* (1729) proposes a solution to the poverty of Ireland. Read this sentence and say what makes it satire.

> *I have been assured by a very knowing American of my acquaintance in London, that a young healthy child well nursed is at a year old a most delicious, nourishing, and wholesome food, whether stewed, roasted, baked, or boiled;*
::: solution
**The proposal.** The speaker suggests that the children of the poor should be sold as food to the rich. No one could mean this.

**The voice.** The speaker is calm, reasonable and polite: he cites an authority (*a very knowing American*), uses the vocabulary of economics and cookery (*nourishing*, *wholesome*, *stewed, roasted, baked*), and lists the methods of cooking with the care of a recipe. The horror is in the contrast between the monstrous proposal and the measured tone.

**Satire.** The speaker is a persona, not Swift ([[reading-literature/narrative#def-narrator]]). His reasoning imitates the way some economists and landlords of the time discussed the Irish poor — as numbers and resources — and pushes it to its logical extreme, so that the reader sees the inhumanity of the original attitude. This is **satire** by **irony** ([[reading-skills/inference]]): the text says one thing and means its opposite.

**Period.** The reliance on reason, the mock-scientific manner, the persona and the political target are typical of Augustan satire; Swift's *Gulliver's Travels* (1726) uses the same methods.
:::
:::

## Romanticism (1798–1832)

In the 1790s, writers in Britain and Germany began to put feeling, imagination, nature, childhood and the individual at the centre of literature. In the Preface to the second edition of *Lyrical Ballads* (1800), William Wordsworth declared that "all good poetry is the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings", and argued that poems should be written in the real language of ordinary people about the incidents of common life. The major Romantic poets were William Blake, Wordsworth and Samuel Taylor Coleridge, and in the next generation Lord Byron, Percy Bysshe Shelley and John Keats. The period also includes the novels of Jane Austen and Walter Scott and Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein* (1818).

::: example Two ideas of poetry {#ex-pope-wordsworth}
Compare Pope's couplet from *An Essay on Criticism* (1711), *True ease in writing comes from art, not chance, / As those move easiest who have learn'd to dance*, with Wordsworth's phrase *the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings* (1800). How do they differ, and what does each suggest about its period?
::: solution
**Pope.** Good writing is *art*, learned like dancing; its ease is the result of training and practice, not *chance*. The model is craft and social grace: the poet as a skilled performer who has mastered rules handed down from the classics.

**Wordsworth.** Good poetry is an *overflow* of *feeling*, *spontaneous* — the image is of water rising and spilling naturally. The model is nature and the individual heart. (Wordsworth adds in the same Preface that such poems come only from a man who has "thought long and deeply", so he does not reject thought; but feeling comes first in his definition.)

**Period.** The contrast is a classic illustration of the change from Augustan to Romantic ideas, and M. H. Abrams's *The Mirror and the Lamp* (1953) made it central to literary history: poetry as an imitation of the world, shaped by art (a mirror), gives way to poetry as an expression of the poet's mind (a lamp). Even the forms differ: Pope's closed, balanced couplet enacts control; Wordsworth's metaphor of overflowing does the opposite. Both are generalisations about each period, and both poets are more complex than the phrases suggest ([[#rule-periods]], point 3).
:::
:::

## The Victorian period (1837–1901)

Named after Queen Victoria's reign, the Victorian period was an age of industry, empire, cities, science and doubt. Its dominant form was the novel — Dickens, Thackeray, the Brontës, Elizabeth Gaskell, George Eliot, Trollope, Hardy — often published in parts ([[reading-literature/the-novel]]). Its poets included Alfred Tennyson, Robert and Elizabeth Barrett Browning, Matthew Arnold, Christina Rossetti and, published only later, Gerard Manley Hopkins. Many Victorian works struggle with the conflict between religious faith and new science; in *In Memoriam* (1850), written before Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859) but after the geology that unsettled the biblical age of the earth, Tennyson describes Nature as "red in tooth and claw". The age ended with the drama and wit of Oscar Wilde.

::: warning Treating a period as a stereotype
✗ *This is a Victorian novel, so it must be prudish and moralistic.*

✓ *This novel was published in 1891, in the late Victorian period; I will look at how it handles sexuality and morality, which many Victorian novels treated with more complexity than the stereotype suggests.*

Period labels attract clichés: Victorians were prudish, Romantics wandered in nature, Augustans were cold. Hardy's *Tess of the d'Urbervilles* (1891), subtitled "A Pure Woman", attacked exactly the moral judgements the stereotype attributes to its age. Use a period label as a question, not as an answer ([[#rule-periods]], point 5).
:::

## Modernism (about 1910–1940)

**Modernism** names the experimental literature of the early twentieth century, written in response to a world changed by cities, technology, new psychology and, above all, the First World War (1914–18). Its writers broke with the conventions of Victorian narration and verse: stream of consciousness and free indirect style in the novel ([[reading-literature/narrative#def-fis]]), free verse and fragmented form in poetry, allusion to many cultures and languages. 1922 is often called its high point: James Joyce's *Ulysses* and T. S. Eliot's *The Waste Land* were both published that year. Other key writers are Joseph Conrad, Virginia Woolf, Katherine Mansfield, W. B. Yeats, Ezra Pound and D. H. Lawrence; the poets of the First World War, such as Wilfred Owen ([[reading-literature/figurative-language]]), stand at its threshold.

W. B. Yeats's "The Second Coming" (published 1920) expresses the sense of a world falling apart after the war:

> *Turning and turning in the widening gyre*
> *The falcon cannot hear the falconer;*
> *Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold;*
> *Mere anarchy is loosed upon the world,*

(*Gyre* ‘spiral’.)

::: example A poem at a turning point {#ex-yeats}
Which features of these four lines connect them with Modernism, and which with older traditions? What later life did the third line have?
::: solution
**Image.** The falcon has flown so far in its widening circles that it can no longer hear the falconer who trained it: an image of a civilisation that has lost contact with its centre. The image is presented without explanation; the reader must make the connection with the post-war world ([[reading-literature/figurative-language#def-symbol]]).

**Syntax.** Line 3 is two short, flat statements separated by a semicolon, *Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold* — general, abstract and absolute, like news headlines of disaster. *Mere anarchy* uses *mere* in its older sense ‘complete, pure’ as well as the modern ‘only’.

**Older traditions.** The lines are in a loose iambic pentameter, and the title alludes to the Christian expectation of Christ's Second Coming. Yeats is not a free-verse poet; his modernity lies in the image, the compression and the vision of collapse, not in a break with metre.

**Later life.** Thirty-eight years later the Nigerian novelist Chinua Achebe took *Things Fall Apart* as the title of his novel (1958) about the destruction of an Igbo community under colonial rule — turning a European poet's image of European crisis into a title for a story about Africa. A line from one period becomes, in another, a reply to it ([[#rule-periods]]).
:::
:::

## After 1945

Literature in English since the Second World War is too varied, and too recent, for a single label, and it is outside the public domain, so it is only mentioned here. Its most important development is that English literature is no longer English, or even British and American: writers from Africa, the Caribbean, South Asia, Australia, Canada, Ireland and elsewhere — Achebe, Wole Soyinka, Derek Walcott, V. S. Naipaul, R. K. Narayan, Salman Rushdie, Alice Munro, among many others — have made literature in English a world literature ([[world-englishes/models]]). **Postcolonial** criticism studies this writing and rereads older texts in its light ([[reading-literature/writing-about-literature]]).

::: theorem Placing a text in its period {#rule-placing}
To place an unfamiliar text, look at:

1. **Language**: spelling, grammar and vocabulary (Middle English forms, *thou* and *-eth*, archaic or modern words) ([[history-of-english/early-modern-english]]).
2. **Form**: alliterative lines, sonnets, heroic couplets, blank verse meditations, the realist novel, free verse, fragmentation.
3. **Subject and attitude**: religion and courtly love, wit and society, nature and the self, industry and doubt, alienation and the city.
4. **Publication**: manuscript, printed book, serial parts, magazine, little review.

Then check the date, and ask how far the text is typical of its period and where it departs.
:::

::: proof
Each of these features changes over time in documented ways. Language changes most reliably, which is why the earliest periods are named after stages of English. Forms rise and fall in popularity — the heroic couplet dominated poetry from about 1660 to 1790 and almost disappeared afterwards; free verse became common only after about 1910. Subjects and attitudes are the least reliable indicators, because ideas recur, and the clichés of [[#rule-periods]] are strongest here. The last instruction, to check the date and look for departures, keeps the procedure from becoming a guessing game: the aim is not to label a text but to understand how it relates to the writing around it.
:::

::: quiz
A poem in heroic couplets mocks a fashionable lady's dressing table and compares a card game to an epic battle. Which period does it most probably belong to?
- [ ] Middle English
- [x] the early eighteenth century (Augustan)
- [ ] Romantic
- [ ] Modernist
::: solution
Heroic couplets, mock-epic and social satire are typical of the Augustan age; the description fits Pope's *The Rape of the Lock* (1712–17). Checking the date confirms it.
:::

::: quiz
Which pair of works was published in 1922?
- [ ] *Lyrical Ballads* and *Frankenstein*
- [ ] *Jane Eyre* and *Wuthering Heights*
- [x] *Ulysses* and *The Waste Land*
- [ ] *Paradise Lost* and *The Pilgrim's Progress*

Joyce's *Ulysses* and Eliot's *The Waste Land* both appeared in 1922. *Jane Eyre* and *Wuthering Heights* were both published in 1847; *Lyrical Ballads* in 1798 and *Frankenstein* in 1818; *Paradise Lost* in 1667 and *The Pilgrim's Progress* in 1678.

::: note English and Chinese
- **Dynasties and movements.** Chinese literary history is usually periodised by dynasty — Tang poetry, Song *ci*, Yuan drama, Ming and Qing fiction — and Chinese readers often expect each period to have a dominant genre (唐诗、宋词、元曲、明清小说). English periods are named more often after stages of the language or cultural movements, and their dominant genres are less clear-cut, though the pattern partly holds (Elizabethan drama, the Victorian novel).
- **Parallel dates.** Chaucer (d. 1400) wrote in the last years of the Yuan and the first of the Ming dynasty, around the time *Water Margin* (《水浒传》) was taking shape; Shakespeare's contemporary was Tang Xianzu; *Hongloumeng* first circulated in print in 1791, seven years before *Lyrical Ballads*; and the modernist years of Joyce and Woolf are the years of Lu Xun's first stories and the New Culture Movement. Such parallels are useful for orientation, not as evidence of influence.
- **World literature.** For the European periods from the Renaissance to Modernism, with Continental writers in context, see the Chinese Atlas lessons on [the Renaissance](/learn/chinese/lesson.php?c=world-literature&l=renaissance), [Romanticism](/learn/chinese/lesson.php?c=world-literature&l=romanticism) and [Modernism](/learn/chinese/lesson.php?c=world-literature&l=modernism).
- **Language of older texts.** Middle English is harder for Chinese learners than Early Modern English, and Old English is a foreign language. Read Chaucer in an edition with facing glosses, and Shakespeare in an annotated edition ([[reading-literature/drama-shakespeare#rule-emode]]).
:::

::: history
Writing the history of English literature as a sequence of periods is itself a historical practice. Thomas Warton's *The History of English Poetry* (1774–81) was the first large attempt. In the nineteenth century, Hippolyte Taine's *History of English Literature* (in French, 1863–64) explained literature by "race, milieu and moment", and university study of English literature began, with chairs and examinations from the later nineteenth century. *The Cambridge History of English Literature* (14 volumes, 1907–16, with an index in 1927) set out the periods that are still taught. F. R. Leavis's *The Great Tradition* (1948) argued for a narrow canon of novelists; from the 1970s, feminist critics recovered many women writers whom the standard histories had neglected, and postcolonial critics questioned whether "English literature" should be defined by England at all. *The Norton Anthology of English Literature* (first edition 1962) has, edition by edition, recorded these changes in what is read.
:::

::: summary
- A **literary period** groups texts of a time span that share features of language, form, subject and belief; its name may come from the language, a ruler, a movement or a date ([[#def-period]]).
- Periods are conventions: their dates are approximate, they overlap, not every writer fits, names were often given later, and they describe tendencies ([[#rule-periods]]).
- The usual map: **Old English** (*Beowulf*), **Middle English** (Chaucer), **Renaissance** (Shakespeare, Donne, the King James Bible), **Restoration and eighteenth century** (Pope, Swift, the first novels), **Romantic** (Wordsworth, Keats, Austen), **Victorian** (Dickens, Eliot, Tennyson, Hardy), **Modernist** (Joyce, Woolf, Eliot, Yeats) and the world literature in English after 1945.
- Place a text by its language, form, subject and publication, then check the date and look for departures ([[#rule-placing]]).
- Use a period label to ask questions about a text, never to replace reading it.
:::

Use [[#rule-placing]] to suggest a period for each description, and give your reasons. (a) A long narrative in rhyming pentameter couplets with spellings like *whan* and *swich*. ::: exercise A label that misleads {level=2}
A student writes: *Since Blake lived in the eighteenth century, he was an Augustan poet who wrote in heroic couplets.* What is wrong, and how should the claim be corrected?
::: solution
Blake (1757–1827) lived partly in the eighteenth century, but his *Songs of Innocence* (1789) and *Songs of Experience* (1794) use short lyric stanzas, not heroic couplets, and their concern with imagination, childhood and protest is usually called **Romantic** (or pre-Romantic). The error is to place a writer by date alone ([[#rule-periods]], points 1–3). A better claim: *Although Blake wrote in the 1790s, his lyrics share the concerns that later critics called Romantic.*
:::

::: exercise Bridges between periods {level=2}
Explain why each of these writers is hard to place in one period: (a) Milton (b) Hardy (c) Hopkins.
::: solution
(a) **Milton** (1608–74) was educated and wrote his early poems in the Renaissance, but published *Paradise Lost* in 1667, after the Restoration. (b) **Hardy** (1840–1928) wrote Victorian novels (1871–95) and then turned to poetry, publishing most of it after 1898 and continuing into the 1920s, alongside the modernists. (c) **Hopkins** (1844–89) was a Victorian who wrote in the 1870s and 1880s, but his poems were first published in 1918, and their sprung rhythm and compression were read as modern. Dates of writing, publication and reception can place one writer in several periods.
:::

::: exercise Compare two openings {level=3}
Compare the first sentence of Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales* (lines 1–4 above) with the first verses of Genesis in the King James Bible. Write 150–200 words on how each is constructed — syntax, vocabulary, rhythm — and what each suggests about the language and literature of its time.
::: solution

A strong answer notes that Chaucer's sentence is long and subordinated (*Whan that …*, with the main clause delayed for many lines), in rhyming couplets, with a mixture of French and English words and Middle English spellings; and that the King James sentences are short, parallel and paratactic (*And … And … And*), with plain, mostly native vocabulary and the rhythms of a translation from Hebrew. It connects these to the period: Chaucer's courtly, French-influenced poetry for an educated audience; the Bible's prose meant to be read aloud in churches, which shaped English style for centuries.
