Here are two opening sentences for an essay on Kate Chopin's "The Story of an Hour" (1894):

- *In "The Story of an Hour", Mrs Mallard hears that her husband has died, goes to her room, feels free, and then dies when he comes home.*
- *"The Story of an Hour" divides its readers from its characters: only we follow Mrs Mallard into her room, so only we can see that the "joy that kills" in the last line is not the joy the doctors imagine.*

The first sentence is accurate, and it tells a reader nothing they could not learn by reading the story. The second makes a claim that a reader could dispute, that needs evidence, and that explains *how* the story produces its effect. The first is a summary; the second is the beginning of an argument. A literary essay is an argument of the second kind.

This chapter turns the reading skills of this course into writing. It explains how to build a thesis about a literary text, how to write paragraphs that join quotation and analysis, the conventions of quoting and citing literature in English, and the main critical approaches — formalist, reader-response, psychoanalytic, Marxist, feminist, postcolonial, historicist — that give essays their questions. The general skills of essay writing are taught in the course *Essay Writing* ([[essay-writing/thesis-introduction]], [[essay-writing/arguing-a-point]]); this chapter is about what is special in writing about literature.

## The thesis

::: definition Literary essay and thesis {#def-thesis}
A **literary essay** is an argued interpretation of one or more literary texts, supported by evidence from the texts. Its **thesis** is the central claim it argues: a statement about what a text means, how it achieves its effects, or how it relates to other texts or contexts, which a reasonable reader could dispute and which the essay supports with evidence.
:::

::: theorem What makes a good thesis about literature {#rule-thesis}
A good thesis about a literary text:

1. **is arguable** — a reasonable reader could disagree, so it needs support (not *Sonnet 130 has fourteen lines*);
2. **is interpretive** — it says something about meaning, effect or significance, not only about plot or facts;
3. **is specific** to this text and question — it could not be written about any poem or any novel;
4. **is about how as well as what** — it points to the features (form, language, narration) through which the text works;
5. **can be supported** within the length of the essay, from the text itself.
:::

::: proof
These criteria follow from what an essay is for. A fact (point 1) needs no essay; a plot summary (point 2) repeats the text; a general statement (point 3) — *Shakespeare uses imagery to convey his themes* — is true of every work and so says nothing about this one. Point 4 reflects the central lesson of close reading ([[reading-literature/close-reading#rule-three-questions]]): meaning is produced by form, so an interpretation that ignores form is incomplete. Point 5 is practical: a thesis about the whole of Victorian literature cannot be supported in 1,500 words. Guides to literary writing make the same points (for example, Sylvan Barnet and William Cain, *A Short Guide to Writing about Literature*, many editions; the guidance on thesis statements in *The MLA Handbook*, 9th ed., 2021).
:::

::: example Improving a thesis {#ex-thesis}
Improve each thesis with [[#rule-thesis]]. (a) *Sonnet 130 is a love poem by Shakespeare.* (b) *In "Dulce et Decorum Est", Owen uses many similes and metaphors.* (c) *Jane Eyre is a great novel about a woman's life.*
::: solution
**(a)** A fact, not arguable (point 1). Better: *Sonnet 130 mocks the conventional comparisons of love poetry for twelve lines in order to make its final couplet's declaration more believable: by refusing to flatter, the speaker earns the right to be trusted.*

**(b)** Description, not interpretation (point 2), and true of many poems (point 3). Better: *The similes of the first stanza of "Dulce et Decorum Est" turn young soldiers into "old beggars" and "hags", so that before the poem names "the old Lie" its imagery has already contradicted the heroic language of its Latin title.*

**(c)** Vague and evaluative (*great*), not specific, not about how (points 3–4). Better: *Jane Eyre's first-person narration makes a poor and "plain" governess's claims to equality the authority of the novel itself, so that the reader is placed on her side against every character who judges her by rank or appearance.*

Each improved thesis names the feature through which the text works (a structure, a set of similes, a narrative method), makes a claim that could be disputed, and could be supported from the text.
:::
:::

## Paragraphs: claim, evidence, analysis

::: theorem Building an analytical paragraph {#rule-paragraph}
Each body paragraph of a literary essay should usually contain:

1. **A claim** (topic sentence) that advances the thesis — an interpretive statement, not a fact or a quotation;
2. **Evidence**: short, precise quotations or exact references to the text;
3. **Analysis**: an explanation of how the evidence supports the claim — what the words, form or narration do — usually longer than the quotations themselves;
4. **A link** back to the thesis or forward to the next point: why this matters for the argument as a whole.
:::

::: proof
This is a version of the paragraph structure taught for argument in general ([[essay-writing/arguing-a-point]]), adapted to literary evidence. School versions are often called PEE or PEEL (Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link). The essential difference in literary writing is that the evidence is language, so the analysis must say *how* the language works — its connotations, syntax, sound, imagery, place in the structure. A paragraph in which quotations are longer than the commentary is usually under-analysed: the quotation shows, but only the analysis argues. Point 4 stops paragraphs from becoming separate observations; an essay is one argument.
:::

::: example Building a paragraph {#ex-paragraph}
Write a body paragraph supporting the thesis about "The Story of an Hour" given at the start of this chapter, using the story's last sentence (*When the doctors came they said she had died of heart disease—of joy that kills*) and one earlier detail.
::: solution
A model paragraph, with its parts labelled afterwards:

*(1) The story's final sentence depends on a knowledge that only the reader possesses. (2) When the doctors pronounce that Mrs Mallard "had died of heart disease—of joy that kills", (3) their diagnosis is given in their own reported words ("they said"), and the narrator, who has followed her thoughts throughout, offers no correction. The reader must supply it. We alone have been in the locked room where she whispered "free, free, free!", so we know that her joy came from her husband's death, not from his return; the phrase "joy that kills" is true in a sense the doctors cannot imagine. (4) The dramatic irony is not a twist added at the end, then, but the culmination of the story's structure, which has separated the public world of careful, protective people from the private room in which Mrs Mallard discovers a self that they never see.*

**(1) Claim**: interpretive and arguable. **(2) Evidence**: the final sentence, quoted briefly and integrated into the sentence. **(3) Analysis**: the reported speech, the narrator's silence, the earlier detail (*free, free, free!*) and the double meaning of *joy*; the analysis is much longer than the quotations. **(4) Link**: back to the thesis about the division between readers and characters.
:::
:::

## Quoting and citing literature

::: definition Conventions of literary quotation {#def-quotation}
In English-language literary essays (following the widely used MLA style, *MLA Handbook*, 9th edition, 2021):

- **Short quotations** are integrated into your own sentences, in double quotation marks (single quotation marks in many British publications): *Blake's speaker hears "mind-forg'd manacles" in every voice.*
- **Line breaks in verse** within a short quotation are shown with a slash, with a space on each side: *"Bare ruin'd choirs, where late the sweet birds sang. / In me thou see'st the twilight of such day"*; a stanza break with a double slash (//).
- **Long quotations** — in MLA style, more than four lines of prose or more than three lines of verse — are set off as indented blocks without quotation marks, keeping the original line breaks of verse.
- **Omissions** are shown with an ellipsis (*…*, often in square brackets *[…]* to show that it is yours); **changes or additions** to make a quotation fit your grammar are in square brackets: *Jane insists that she has "as much soul as [Rochester]".*
- **References** give the location: line numbers for poems (*l.* 4, *ll.* 13–14), act, scene and line for plays (*Hamlet* 3.1.56), and chapter or page for prose, with the edition listed in the works cited.
:::

::: theorem The literary present and other conventions {#rule-conventions}
1. **Use the present tense** to describe what happens in a text and what a text does: *Mrs Mallard sits by the window*; *Shakespeare's speaker turns in the couplet*. Use past tenses for historical facts about authors and publication: *Chopin published the story in 1894.*
2. **Name the speaker or narrator**, not the author, for the voice of the text ([[reading-literature/narrative#def-narrator]]); name the author for the choices that shape it.
3. **Italicise** titles of books, plays and long poems (*Middlemarch*, *Hamlet*, *Paradise Lost*); put titles of short stories and short poems in quotation marks ("Araby", "London").
4. **Quote exactly**, including old spellings and punctuation, from a named edition; never quote a translation or a simplified version as the author's words without saying so.
5. **Keep quotations short**: a word or phrase is often enough; a long quotation needs more analysis, not less.
:::

::: proof
These conventions are those of the major style guides for literary study (the *MLA Handbook*; the *MHRA Style Guide* in Britain) and are expected in English-medium universities. The literary present (point 1) reflects the idea that a text exists for each reading: *Hamlet hesitates* every time the play is read or performed. It also avoids a common confusion between the time of the story and the time of writing. Point 2 prevents the error described in [[reading-literature/narrative]]; point 4 protects the evidence, because an argument about a word is only as good as the accuracy of the word. Citation of critics and other secondary sources follows the general rules of [[academic-writing/citation-referencing]].
:::

::: example Correcting quotations {#ex-quotation}
Correct each sentence. (a) *In Sonnet 73 the speaker said: "That time of year thou mayst in me behold When yellow leaves, or none, or few, do hang".* (b) *Jane says that "I have as much soul as you".* (c) *In the poem The Sick Rose, Blake tells the rose that it is sick.*
::: solution
**(a)** Use the present tense and mark the line break: *In Sonnet 73 the speaker tells the listener, "That time of year thou mayst in me behold / When yellow leaves, or none, or few, do hang".* Better still, quote less and analyse more: *The speaker asks to be seen as "That time of year" when only "yellow leaves, or none, or few" remain.*

**(b)** The grammar of the quotation clashes with the sentence (*Jane says that … I*). Either quote directly — *Jane tells Rochester: "I have as much soul as you"* — or adapt with brackets: *Jane insists that she has "as much soul as [Rochester]".*

**(c)** Title in quotation marks (a short poem), and the speaker, not Blake: *In "The Sick Rose", the speaker addresses the rose directly — "O Rose thou art sick" — as if delivering a diagnosis.*
:::
:::

::: warning Retelling the story
✗ *In chapter 1, Mrs Bennet tells her husband about Mr Bingley. Then the Bennet girls go to a ball, where Mr Darcy refuses to dance with Elizabeth. Later he proposes …*

✓ *Austen introduces the Bennets' marriage through a contrast of forms: Mrs Bennet's eager direct speech against her husband's reported, six-word reply.*

Plot summary is the most common weakness of student essays about literature. Your reader knows the text. Mention events only as far as needed to place your evidence, and make every paragraph begin with a claim, not with *Then* or *Next*. A useful test: if a paragraph could be moved to a summary of the book without change, it is not analysis.
:::

## Critical approaches

Every interpretation starts from some question. Critical approaches — often called "schools" or "theories" — are families of questions that readers bring to texts. The descriptions below are deliberately brief and neutral; each approach has many variants, and good essays often combine them. Jonathan Culler's *Literary Theory: A Very Short Introduction* (2nd ed., 2011), Terry Eagleton's *Literary Theory: An Introduction* (1983; anniversary ed., 2008) and Peter Barry's *Beginning Theory* (4th ed., 2017) give fuller accounts.

::: definition Major critical approaches {#def-approaches}
- **Formalism and New Criticism** ask how the text's form — language, structure, imagery, ambiguity — produces its meaning, and treat the text as a unified whole to be read closely (Russian formalists, 1910s–20s; American New Critics such as Cleanth Brooks, 1930s–50s; [[reading-literature/close-reading]]).
- **Structuralism and narratology** look for the underlying systems and codes that make texts possible — narrative structures, genres, oppositions (Roland Barthes; Gérard Genette; [[reading-literature/narrative]]).
- **Reader-response criticism** asks how readers make meaning: how a text guides and surprises them, what it leaves for them to fill in, how different readers or communities read it (Wolfgang Iser, *The Act of Reading*, English translation 1978; Stanley Fish, *Is There a Text in This Class?*, 1980).
- **Psychoanalytic criticism** reads texts for desire, repression and the unconscious, in characters, authors or readers (after Sigmund Freud and, later, Jacques Lacan).
- **Marxist criticism** relates texts to social class, economic relations and ideology (Raymond Williams, *Marxism and Literature*, 1977; Eagleton).
- **Feminist criticism** examines how texts represent women and gender, recovers neglected women writers, and asks how gender shapes writing and reading (Elaine Showalter, *A Literature of Their Own*, 1977; Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar, *The Madwoman in the Attic*, 1979). **Gender** and **queer** criticism extend these questions to masculinity and sexuality.
- **Postcolonial criticism** studies literature in the context of empire and its aftermath: how colonial texts represent colonised peoples, and how writers from formerly colonised countries write back (Edward Said, *Orientalism*, 1978; Chinua Achebe's lecture "An Image of Africa", 1975, on Conrad's *Heart of Darkness*).
- **New historicism and cultural materialism** read literary texts alongside other documents of their time, as part of the circulation of power and ideas in a culture (Stephen Greenblatt, *Renaissance Self-Fashioning*, 1980).
- **Ecocriticism** studies the representation of nature and the environment.
:::

Each approach makes some features of a text visible and leaves others in shadow. Formalism can explain how Blake's "London" works but says little about the London of 1794; historicism can tell us about chartered companies and child chimney-sweeps but may lose the poem's music. Critics of each approach have criticised the others, and the debates are real. For a student essay, the useful question is not *which theory is true?* but *which questions open up this text, for this essay?*

::: example One text, several approaches {#ex-approaches}
Suggest a question that each of four approaches might ask about *Jane Eyre* (1847): formalist, feminist, postcolonial and Marxist.
::: solution
**Formalist.** *How do the novel's recurring images of fire and ice, and of enclosed rooms (the red-room, the attic), organise its account of Jane's development?* The question concerns patterns of imagery and structure ([[reading-literature/figurative-language#rule-imagery]]).

**Feminist.** *How does the novel represent a woman's demand for independence, and what does it mean that Rochester's first wife, Bertha, is confined in the attic as a "madwoman"?* Gilbert and Gubar (1979) read Bertha as Jane's dark double, the expression of the anger a Victorian woman could not show.

**Postcolonial.** *Bertha comes from Jamaica, and Rochester's wealth is connected with the colonies: how does the novel depend on empire, and how does it represent a Creole woman?* Jean Rhys's novel *Wide Sargasso Sea* (1966) retells Bertha's story from her side, and critics such as Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak (in an essay of 1985) read *Jane Eyre* in this light.

**Marxist.** *How does the novel treat class — Jane's position as a governess, neither servant nor lady — and how is her final independence made possible by an inheritance?* The plot resolves a social problem by a fortunate legacy, which raises questions about what the novel can imagine.

Each question leads to different evidence. An essay may use one approach or combine two, but it should say what it is asking and support its claims from the text.
:::
:::

## Introductions and conclusions

An introduction to a literary essay usually does three things briefly: it places the text (author, title, date, and only as much context as the argument needs), it identifies the issue or question, and it states the thesis. It does not begin with a biography of the author, a definition from a dictionary, or a sentence that would fit any essay (*Since the beginning of time, people have written poems about love*).

A conclusion does not merely repeat the thesis. It can draw the claims together, show what the argument adds to an understanding of the whole text, acknowledge what it leaves open, or connect the text to a wider question — briefly ([[essay-writing/conclusions]]).

::: note English and Chinese
- **Essay or 读后感?** A Chinese 读后感 (response after reading) often centres on what the reader learned or felt; an English literary essay centres on an argument about the text. Personal response is welcome as a starting point, but the essay must move from *I felt* to *the text does*, with evidence.
- **Structure.** The Chinese school pattern 总—分—总 (general—specific—general) resembles the English introduction—body—conclusion, but English literary essays expect the thesis to be a disputable claim, stated early, and each paragraph to advance it, not to illustrate a theme already agreed.
- **Quotation marks and titles.** Chinese marks titles with 《》; English uses italics for books and quotation marks for short works ([[#rule-conventions]], point 3). Chinese 引号 “ ” are the same shape as English double quotation marks, but English places commas and full stops inside closing quotation marks in American style and often outside in British style when they are not part of the quotation; follow one style consistently.
- **Criticism in translation and academic integrity.** If you use Chinese criticism of an English text, cite it as you would any source, and translate quotations yourself with a note. Copying a critic's interpretation without citation, in either language, is plagiarism ([[academic-writing/academic-integrity]]).
- **Formulas.** As in earlier chapters, avoid stock phrases that would fit any text (*运用了……的手法，表达了作者……的思想感情*); the English equivalent (*The author uses imagery to convey his feelings*) is equally empty.
:::

::: history
The study of English literature as a university subject is younger than the study of classical literature. English literature was taught at University College London from the 1820s and at Scottish universities earlier; Oxford established its Honour School of English Language and Literature in 1894, and Cambridge its English Tripos in 1917. At Cambridge, I. A. Richards's *Practical Criticism* (1929) made the close analysis of unseen passages central to examination ([[reading-literature/close-reading]]). The American textbook *Understanding Poetry* (1938) by Brooks and Warren made the analytical essay the standard form of student writing about literature. The Modern Language Association's style sheet (1951) became the *MLA Handbook*, now in its ninth edition (2021). From the late 1960s, structuralist, Marxist, feminist, psychoanalytic and later postcolonial theories transformed literary study; Terry Eagleton's *Literary Theory: An Introduction* (1983) became the most widely read account of these debates.
:::

Which of these are arguable theses ([[#rule-thesis]])? (a) *"Araby" was published in Dubliners in 1914.* (b) *The ending of "Araby" turns the boy's romantic quest into self-knowledge by making the dark bazaar the place where he finally sees himself.* (c) *"Araby" is a short story by James Joyce about a boy.* (d) *In "Araby", light and darkness imagery shows that the boy's love was always an illusion of his own making.*

::: exercise Integrate the quotation {level=2}
Rewrite each sentence so that the quotation fits grammatically and follows [[#def-quotation]]. (a) *Wordsworth's city, "This City now doth like a garment wear The beauty of the morning", is beautiful.* (b) *Lady Bracknell thinks losing both parents "looks like carelessness, Mr. Worthing".* (c) *Macbeth says life "it is a tale told by an idiot".*
::: solution
(a) *Wordsworth's speaker sees the city "like a garment wear / The beauty of the morning", as if its beauty were clothing that could be taken off later.* (Slash for the line break; analysis added.)
(b) *Lady Bracknell suggests that to lose both parents "looks like carelessness".* (The quotation is cut to fit; *Mr. Worthing* comes earlier in the original sentence and is not needed.)
(c) *Macbeth calls life "a tale / Told by an idiot" (5.5.26–27).* (Remove the extra *it is*; mark the line break; give the reference. Line numbers differ slightly between editions; give the edition in the works cited.)

::: exercise From summary to claim {level=2}
Rewrite this summary paragraph as the opening of an analytical paragraph: *In "The Garden Party", Laura wants to stop the party because a man has died. Her family say no. After the party she takes food to the dead man's house and sees him. Then she meets Laurie and says "Isn't life—".*
::: solution
A model: *Laura's unfinished sentence at the end of "The Garden Party" — "Isn't life—" — marks the limit of what her world gives her words for. Her family's refusal to stop the party has already shown that their language cannot admit death into the garden; when she has seen the dead man herself, the language fails her too, and Laurie's tender, empty reply ("Isn't it, darling?") returns her to the family's idiom.* The paragraph now begins with a claim, uses events only as evidence, and analyses short quotations ([[#rule-paragraph]]).

::: exercise Plan an essay with two approaches {level=3}
Plan an essay of about 1,500 words on the question *What does "The Story of an Hour" suggest about marriage?* Write a thesis, three paragraph claims, the evidence for each, and one counter-reading. Your plan should draw on at least two critical approaches.
::: solution
A model plan:

**Thesis.** *Chopin's story suggests that the oppression of marriage lies less in any individual husband's cruelty than in the institution itself, which allows a wife's self to exist only in secret — a claim the story makes through its contrast between public care and private discovery.*

**Claim 1 (formalist).** The story's structure separates public and private space — the parlour of careful people, the locked room with the open window — and the reader alone crosses between them. Evidence: the opening passive sentence; *She would have no one follow her*; the window and *new spring life*.

**Claim 2 (feminist).** What Mrs Mallard discovers is not relief from a bad man but a self: *There would be no one to live for during those coming years; she would live for herself.* Evidence: the narrator's statement that her husband had loved her; the idea that *men and women believe they have a right to impose a private will upon a fellow-creature*.

**Claim 3 (historicist).** In the 1890s, debates about the "New Woman" and married women's rights made the story's subject topical; its first appearance in *Vogue* placed it before a fashionable female readership.

**Counter-reading.** The story could be read as a psychological study of shock rather than a statement about marriage. Response: the narrator's general reflection on *a private will* extends the meaning beyond one woman.

A good plan has an arguable thesis, claims that each advance it, specific evidence, and a fairly stated counter-reading.
:::
