Wilfred Owen, an officer in the British army, wrote "Dulce et Decorum Est" in 1917, during the First World War; he was killed in November 1918, a week before the war ended, and the poem was published in his *Poems* in 1920. It begins with soldiers walking back from the front line:

> *Bent double, like old beggars under sacks,*
> *Knock-kneed, coughing like hags, we cursed through sludge,*
> *Till on the haunting flares we turned our backs*
> *And towards our distant rest began to trudge.*

The poem's title comes from a line by the Roman poet Horace, *Dulce et decorum est pro patria mori* — ‘it is sweet and fitting to die for one's country’ — that British schoolboys of Owen's generation learned by heart. Soldiers in patriotic verse were young, upright and heroic. Owen's two similes overturn that picture in the first two lines: the soldiers are *like old beggars* and *like hags* (ugly old women). Young men have become old; men have become women in the misogynist language of the time; fighters have become the poorest members of society, carrying sacks. Two comparisons do the work of an argument, before the poem has said anything about war directly.

That is the power of **figurative language**: saying one thing in terms of another, so that the reader sees the first thing differently. The figures themselves — metaphor, simile, personification, metonymy — are described as tools of persuasion and style in [[style-and-rhetoric/figures-of-speech]]. This chapter is about how to read them in literature: how to analyse a metaphor, how images build patterns, and how a concrete thing becomes a symbol.

## Metaphor and simile

::: definition Literal and figurative {#def-figurative}
Language is **literal** when its words are used in their ordinary senses (*The soldiers walked slowly*). It is **figurative** when words are used in a sense other than the ordinary one, to suggest a comparison, an association or a transfer of qualities (*The soldiers trudged like beggars*; *the haunting flares*).
:::

::: definition Metaphor, simile, tenor and vehicle {#def-metaphor}
A **simile** states a comparison with *like*, *as*, *as if* or a similar word: *like old beggars under sacks*. A **metaphor** presents one thing as if it were another, without a word of comparison: *Juliet is the sun* (*Romeo and Juliet*). Following I. A. Richards (*The Philosophy of Rhetoric*, 1936), critics call the subject that is being described the **tenor** (Juliet), the thing it is described in terms of the **vehicle** (the sun), and the qualities they are taken to share the **ground** (brightness, warmth, the centre of the speaker's world, the morning).
:::

The tenor–vehicle distinction is the key to analysing any metaphor or simile. Once you have named the tenor and the vehicle, you can ask what is carried across from the vehicle to the tenor — and, often just as important, what is *not*.

::: theorem Analysing a metaphor or simile {#rule-metaphor}
1. **Identify the tenor and the vehicle.** In a metaphor the tenor may be unstated: you must infer it from context.
2. **List the possible grounds**: the qualities of the vehicle that could apply to the tenor (appearance, behaviour, feeling, value, associations).
3. **Ask which grounds the context activates** — and which it excludes or turns against the tenor.
4. **Notice the vehicle's own associations**: its register, the world it comes from (religion, money, war, the body), its connotations ([[collocation-and-register/connotation]]).
5. **See whether the figure is extended**, developed over several lines, or linked with other figures from the same field.
6. **Say what the figure contributes** to the passage: an attitude, an argument, a feeling, a way of seeing.
:::

::: proof
The procedure follows from the structure of a figure. Because a metaphor asserts an identity that is literally false (Juliet is not a star), the reader must work out which features of the vehicle are relevant, and that choice depends on context — the same vehicle can carry opposite meanings in different poems (the sun as life-giving or as burning). Step 4 matters because a vehicle brings its whole field of associations: a metaphor from the field of commerce makes love sound like a transaction, whatever else it says. Richards argued that meaning arises from the interaction of tenor and vehicle, not from a simple list of shared features, and Max Black developed this "interaction" view in *Models and Metaphors* (1962). The procedure here is a practical version of that view.
:::

::: example Owen's similes {#ex-owen}
Analyse the similes in the first two lines of "Dulce et Decorum Est" with [[#rule-metaphor]].
::: solution
**Simile 1.** Tenor: the soldiers (*we*). Vehicle: *old beggars under sacks*. Grounds: they are bent over (*Bent double*), carrying heavy loads (packs like sacks), ragged, exhausted, and — implied — miserable and without dignity.

**Simile 2.** Tenor: the soldiers. Vehicle: *hags* — old, ugly women. Grounds: coughing, weakness, ugliness, age.

**Which grounds matter.** Both vehicles reverse the qualities the patriotic image of soldiers required: youth (→ *old*), strength (→ *bent*, *knock-kneed*), honour (→ *beggars*), manliness (→ *hags*, a word from the insults of the time). The coughing anticipates the gas attack that follows in the next stanza.

**Associations.** *Beggars* and *hags* come from the lowest levels of society and from folk tales; there is nothing military about them. *Sludge* and *trudge*, rhyming, continue the heaviness.

**Contribution.** Before the poem names the patriotic lie it attacks (*The old Lie*, near its end), the similes have already done so. The reader sees the soldiers as ruined, old before their time — the opposite of the image in the poem's Latin title. The figures carry the poem's argument.
:::
:::

::: warning Naming without explaining
✗ *In line 1 Owen uses a simile, "like old beggars under sacks". In line 2 he uses another simile, "coughing like hags". These similes are effective.*

✓ *Owen compares the soldiers to "old beggars" and "hags", vehicles that reverse every quality of the heroic soldier — youth, strength, honour, manhood — so that the opening lines already contradict the patriotic Latin of the title.*

Saying *this is a simile* and *it is effective* adds nothing: every reader can see the word *like*. The analysis begins with tenor, vehicle and ground, and ends with what the comparison does ([[#rule-metaphor]]). The same warning applies to Chinese school formulas (*运用比喻，生动形象地写出了……*): the point is what is compared to what, and why.
:::

## Extended metaphor and the conceit

A metaphor may be developed over several lines or a whole poem. Shakespeare's Sonnet 73 (1609) builds three metaphors for the same tenor — the speaker's age — one in each quatrain:

> *That time of year thou mayst in me behold*
> *When yellow leaves, or none, or few, do hang*
> *Upon those boughs which shake against the cold,*
> *Bare ruin'd choirs, where late the sweet birds sang.*
> *In me thou see'st the twilight of such day*
> *As after sunset fadeth in the west,*
> *Which by and by black night doth take away,*
> *Death's second self, that seals up all in rest.*
> *In me thou see'st the glowing of such fire*
> *That on the ashes of his youth doth lie,*
> *As the death-bed whereon it must expire,*
> *Consum'd with that which it was nourish'd by.*
> *This thou perceiv'st, which makes thy love more strong,*
> *To love that well which thou must leave ere long.*

(*Choirs* ‘the part of a church where the choir sings’; *late* ‘lately’; *by and by* ‘soon’; *Death's second self* ‘sleep, or night, which is like death’.)

:::

::: example An extended metaphor in three stages {#ex-73}
Analyse how the three metaphors of Sonnet 73 develop. Why might Shakespeare have ordered them as he did?
::: solution
**Quatrain 1 — autumn.** Vehicle: late autumn, *yellow leaves, or none, or few*. The hesitation *or none, or few* imitates someone looking more closely and correcting himself, which makes the image precise. *Bare ruin'd choirs*: the bare branches are compared to the ruined choirs of churches — a metaphor inside the metaphor, perhaps recalling the monasteries ruined after the Reformation — where *sweet birds* (singers) once sang. Time span: a season.

**Quatrain 2 — twilight.** Vehicle: the last light after sunset, which *black night* soon takes away. Night is *Death's second self*, so death enters the poem openly. Time span: a few hours.

**Quatrain 3 — a dying fire.** Vehicle: a fire glowing on its own ashes, about to go out. Time span: minutes. And a paradox: the fire is *Consum'd with that which it was nourish'd by* — the fuel that fed it has become the ash that smothers it. Applied to the tenor: the life that sustained the speaker is the life that has used him up.

**Order.** The vehicles shrink in time (year, day, fire) — so death comes closer with each quatrain — and move from the external (a tree) to the internal (a fire *in me*, with its *youth* and *death-bed*). The extended metaphor is also an argument: each quatrain says *you see in me*, and the couplet turns to the listener: *This thou perceiv'st*. The form of the Shakespearean sonnet ([[reading-literature/poetic-forms#def-sonnet]]) supplies one quatrain per vehicle.
:::
:::

::: definition Conceit {#def-conceit}
A **conceit** is an elaborate, surprising or far-fetched metaphor, developed at length, that compares things that seem very unlike. The **metaphysical conceit** is the kind associated with John Donne and other seventeenth-century poets whom Samuel Johnson, in his *Life of Cowley* (1779), called "metaphysical poets"; Johnson complained that in their poetry "the most heterogeneous ideas are yoked by violence together".
:::

The most famous metaphysical conceit closes Donne's "A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning" (printed in his *Poems*, 1633, two years after his death). The speaker is leaving on a journey and tells his wife not to mourn: their two souls are one, or if they are two,

> *If they be two, they are two so*
> *As stiff twin compasses are two;*
> *Thy soul, the fixed foot, makes no show*
> *To move, but doth, if th' other do.*
>
> *And though it in the centre sit,*
> *Yet when the other far doth roam,*
> *It leans, and hearkens after it,*
> *And grows erect, as that comes home.*
>
> *Such wilt thou be to me, who must,*
> *Like th' other foot, obliquely run;*
> *Thy firmness makes my circle just,*
> *And makes me end where I begun.*

(*Compasses* here are the instrument for drawing circles, with two legs joined at the top; *hearkens* ‘listens’; *just* ‘exact, perfect’.)

::: example A metaphysical conceit {#ex-donne}
Work out the conceit of the compasses: what corresponds to what, and why might a reader find it surprising and apt at once?
::: solution
**Mapping.** The two legs of the compasses = the two lovers' souls. The *fixed foot*, at the centre, = the wife, who stays at home. The moving foot, which draws the circle, = the husband, who travels (*far doth roam*, *obliquely run*). The joint at the top = the union of their souls, which keeps them one thing even when apart.

**Development.** Each detail of the instrument is used: the fixed foot *leans* when the other moves away (she inclines towards him, listens for him) and *grows erect* when it returns (she stands up joyfully when he comes home). Her *firmness* keeps his circle *just* — exact — so that he *end[s] where I begun*: the circle closes, and the journey ends at home.

**Surprising.** A drawing instrument, technical and unromantic, is a strange vehicle for married love; this is what Johnson meant by ideas *yoked by violence together*.

**Apt.** But the comparison is precise in a way that a conventional image (two doves, two flowers) could not be: it explains *how* two can be one, how separation can preserve union, and why the traveller will return. The circle was a traditional image of perfection and eternity, so the geometry ends in a value. The pleasure of the conceit is intellectual and emotional together.
:::
:::

## Other figures in brief

Several figures that you will meet in literature are defined in [[style-and-rhetoric/figures-of-speech]]; here is what each typically does in a poem or a story.

- **Personification** gives human qualities to things or ideas: in Wordsworth's sonnet, *the very houses seem asleep* ([[reading-literature/close-reading]]). It can make a scene intimate, or make an abstract idea (Death, Time, Patience in Milton's sonnet) into an actor.
- **Apostrophe** addresses someone absent or something that cannot answer: Keats's *Thou still unravish'd bride of quietness* (to a Grecian urn, published 1820); Donne's *Death, be not proud* (Holy Sonnet, 1633). It creates an intense, dramatic voice.
- **Metonymy** names a thing by something associated with it (*the Crown* for the monarchy); **synecdoche** names a whole by a part (*hands* for workers). In Owen's "Anthem for Doomed Youth" (1920), *What passing-bells for these who die as cattle?* — the church bells rung for the dead stand for the whole ritual of a funeral that these soldiers will not receive.
- **Hyperbole** exaggerates; **understatement** says less than is meant ([[pragmatics/irony-understatement]]).
- **Paradox** states something apparently contradictory that turns out to make sense: the fire *Consum'd with that which it was nourish'd by*.

::: quiz
In *Juliet is the sun*, what is the tenor?
- [x] Juliet
- [ ] the sun
- [ ] the brightness they share
- [ ] the speaker, Romeo
::: solution
The tenor is the subject being described: Juliet. The sun is the vehicle; brightness, warmth and centrality are possible grounds ([[#def-metaphor]]).
:::
:::

## Imagery

::: definition Imagery {#def-imagery}
**Imagery** is language that evokes sensory experience — sight (visual), sound (auditory), smell (olfactory), taste (gustatory), touch and temperature (tactile), movement and bodily tension (kinaesthetic). An **image** may be literal (*yellow leaves*) or figurative (*bare ruin'd choirs*). The images of a work, taken together, often form patterns that carry its themes.
:::

In "Ode to a Nightingale" (1819), Keats wishes for wine that will help him escape:

> *O, for a draught of vintage! that hath been*
> *Cool'd a long age in the deep-delved earth,*
> *Tasting of Flora and the country green,*
> *Dance, and Provençal song, and sunburnt mirth!*

The stanza moves through the senses: temperature (*Cool'd*), depth and earth (*deep-delved*), taste (*Tasting of*), and then — impossibly — the wine tastes of flowers (*Flora*, the Roman goddess of flowers), of *the country green*, of *Dance*, *song* and *sunburnt mirth*. A drink that tastes of song and sunshine mixes the senses (**synaesthesia**), so the image carries the whole southern summer that produced the wine. The imagery is not description for its own sake: the speaker longs for a fullness of sensation that will take him out of his own sorrow, and the stanza gives it to the reader as he imagines it.

::: theorem Tracing patterns of imagery {#rule-imagery}
1. **Collect the images**: list the sensory words and figures, line by line.
2. **Group them** by sense and by field (light and darkness, heat and cold, growth and decay, enclosure and openness, sickness and health).
3. **Look for development**: does a field change across the work (light darkening, open spaces closing)?
4. **Look for contrasts**: two fields set against each other (the garden and the cottages below in Mansfield's "The Garden Party"; fog and clear air in *Bleak House*).
5. **Connect the pattern to the theme**, with evidence from more than one place.
:::

::: proof
Patterns of imagery are evidence of a different kind from single figures: a single image may be chance, but a field of images that recurs and develops is unlikely to be. This is the method by which critics such as Caroline Spurgeon (*Shakespeare's Imagery and What It Tells Us*, 1935) counted and grouped the images of Shakespeare's plays, finding, for example, recurrent images of disease in *Hamlet*. Her conclusions about Shakespeare's personality from his images were contested, but the method of tracing image patterns became standard in the reading of poetry, drama and the novel ([[reading-literature/the-novel#rule-reading-novel]]).
:::

## Symbol

::: definition Symbol and allegory {#def-symbol}
A **symbol** is a concrete thing, person or action in a text that also stands for something beyond itself — an idea, a feeling, a quality — while remaining itself in the story. **Conventional** (public) symbols have meanings shared within a culture (the cross, the dove for peace, the rose for love); **contextual** (literary) symbols acquire their meaning within a particular work (the fog in *Bleak House*, the dust in "Eveline"). An **allegory** is a narrative in which characters and events stand, systematically and one to one, for ideas or moral qualities (John Bunyan's *The Pilgrim's Progress*, 1678, where Christian travels through the Slough of Despond to the Celestial City).
:::

The difference between a metaphor and a symbol is that a metaphor's vehicle is not literally present (Juliet is not really a sun), while a symbol is: the window in "The Story of an Hour" is a real window ([[reading-literature/short-story]]). The difference between a symbol and an allegory is one of system and openness: allegory can be decoded; symbols usually cannot be reduced to one meaning.

William Blake's "The Sick Rose" (*Songs of Experience*, 1794) is eight lines long:

> *O Rose thou art sick.*
> *The invisible worm,*
> *That flies in the night*
> *In the howling storm:*
>
> *Has found out thy bed*
> *Of crimson joy:*
> *And his dark secret love*
> *Does thy life destroy.*

::: example Reading a symbol {#ex-rose}
What might the rose and the worm stand for? Why is it hard — and perhaps wrong — to fix a single meaning?
::: solution
**Literal level.** A rose is attacked by a worm (a grub or caterpillar) that has got into the flower; the rose is dying. Gardeners know this happens.

**Why the reader looks beyond it.** The speaker addresses the rose (apostrophe, *O Rose*); the worm is *invisible*, *flies* in a *howling storm*, and has a *dark secret love*; the rose has a *bed / Of crimson joy*. These words — *bed*, *joy*, *love*, *secret* — belong to human, specifically sexual, experience, so the poem asks to be read as more than gardening.

**Possible meanings.** The rose is a conventional symbol of love and beauty (and of a woman); the worm is a phallic shape and, from Genesis, associated with the serpent. Readings include: love corrupted by secrecy, jealousy or shame; sexual experience destroying innocence; disease (including venereal disease) in a city; a love that destroys what it loves. All of these draw on the evidence.

**Why not one meaning.** The poem gives no key. Its words support several readings at once, and its power comes from that concentration: eight lines that hold love, sex, secrecy, disease and death in one image. Reducing it to *the rose = innocence, the worm = corruption* turns a symbol into an allegory and loses the poem's ambiguity ([[reading-literature/close-reading#def-interpretation]]). A good interpretation proposes a reading, supports it, and acknowledges what it leaves out.
:::
:::

::: theorem Recognising and reading symbols {#rule-symbol}
A concrete detail is likely to be symbolic when it is:

1. **emphasised** — placed in the title, at the beginning or end, or described at unusual length;
2. **repeated**, or returned to at a turning point;
3. **charged with language from another field** (an object described in the words of feeling, morality or religion);
4. **conventional** in the culture of the text (a rose, a journey, a storm, the seasons).

Read it by asking what it stands for **in this text**, with evidence, allowing more than one meaning; do not import a meaning from a dictionary of symbols without checking it against the text.
:::

::: quiz
Which of these is the best evidence that the window in "The Story of an Hour" is symbolic?
- [ ] Most houses have windows.
- [x] The window is *open*, Mrs Mallard faces it alone in her locked room, and through it she sees *new spring life* as her feeling of freedom comes.
- [ ] Windows symbolise freedom in all literature.
- [ ] Chopin was interested in architecture.
::: solution
The second option meets the tests of [[#rule-symbol]]: emphasis (she sits facing it at the turning point), language from another field (*new spring life*, linked to her new feeling) and context. The third imports a general meaning without evidence; the first and fourth are irrelevant.
:::
:::

## Metaphor everywhere

Figurative language is not confined to literature. George Lakoff and Mark Johnson's *Metaphors We Live By* (1980) argued that ordinary thought is structured by **conceptual metaphors** — mappings from one domain to another that appear in hundreds of everyday expressions. ARGUMENT IS WAR: *defend a position*, *attack a claim*, *win an argument*. TIME IS MONEY: *spend time*, *save time*, *waste time*. LIFE IS A JOURNEY: *at a crossroads*, *going nowhere*, *a dead end*. Many such expressions are **dead metaphors**, no longer felt as figures (*the leg of a table*, *a bottleneck*).

Poets often revive these everyday metaphors by extending or twisting them. Sonnet 73 rests on the everyday mapping of a life onto a day and a year (*the autumn of his life*, *the twilight of her career*), but develops it so fully that the familiar idea becomes new. For a learner, recognising the everyday metaphor beneath a literary one is a good first step in analysis.

::: note English and Chinese
- **比 and 兴.** The classical Chinese categories *bi* (比, comparison) and *xing* (兴, an opening image that evokes a feeling or theme, as in many poems of the *Book of Songs*, 《诗经》) are close to simile/metaphor and to the image that sets the mood of a poem. *Xing* has no exact English equivalent: an English poem's opening image is often expected to connect logically with what follows, while *xing* may connect only by mood or sound.
- **Conventional symbols differ.** Willows in classical Chinese poetry suggest parting (折柳 at farewells); in English tradition willows are associated with grief and forsaken love (Desdemona's "Willow Song" in *Othello*). White is the colour of mourning in Chinese tradition and of brides and innocence in Western tradition; the moon carries homesickness in Chinese poetry, and changeability and madness among its English associations. Do not assume that a symbol means in English what it means in Chinese ([[#rule-symbol]], point 4). For Chinese imagery and allusion, see the Chinese Atlas lesson on [imagery and allusion](/learn/chinese/lesson.php?c=poetry-metrics&l=imagery-allusion).
- **Conceptual metaphors are often shared**, but not always: both languages map time onto space, for example, but Chinese uses vertical terms (上个月 ‘last month’, literally ‘the upper month’) where English uses horizontal ones (*the week ahead*). Such differences make some English metaphors hard to translate ([[translation/idioms-culture]]).
- **Formulaic answers.** As the warning above says, an English essay rewards tenor, vehicle and ground, not a formula naming the device.
:::

::: history
Aristotle's *Poetics* (fourth century BCE) defined metaphor as the transfer of a name from one thing to another and called a command of metaphor the greatest gift of a poet, since it showed an eye for resemblances; his *Rhetoric* discussed its uses in speech. Renaissance rhetoric manuals, such as George Puttenham's *The Arte of English Poesie* (1589), listed and named hundreds of figures. Samuel Johnson's *Life of Cowley* (1779) named and criticised the metaphysical poets; T. S. Eliot's essay "The Metaphysical Poets" (1921) defended them and restored their reputation. The Imagists of 1912–17 argued for precise, concrete images instead of decoration. I. A. Richards's *The Philosophy of Rhetoric* (1936) introduced *tenor* and *vehicle*; Max Black's *Models and Metaphors* (1962) proposed the interaction theory; and Lakoff and Johnson's *Metaphors We Live By* (1980) moved the study of metaphor into linguistics and cognitive science.
:::

::: summary
- **Figurative** language uses words beyond their ordinary senses to suggest comparison or transfer ([[#def-figurative]]).
- A **simile** compares with *like* or *as*; a **metaphor** presents one thing as another; analyse both by **tenor**, **vehicle** and **ground** ([[#def-metaphor]], [[#rule-metaphor]]) — and say what the figure does.
- A metaphor may be **extended** (Sonnet 73) or form a **conceit**, an elaborate comparison of unlike things (Donne's compasses) ([[#def-conceit]]).
- **Imagery** evokes the senses; trace images into patterns, developments and contrasts across a work ([[#def-imagery]], [[#rule-imagery]]).
- A **symbol** is a concrete thing that stands for more than itself while remaining itself; an **allegory** is systematic; read symbols in context and allow more than one meaning ([[#def-symbol]], [[#rule-symbol]]).
- Everyday language is full of **conceptual metaphors** (LIFE IS A JOURNEY); poets revive them.
- Symbols and images differ between English and Chinese traditions: do not assume a shared meaning.
:::

::: exercise Analyse a metaphor {level=2}
Analyse Romeo's metaphor *It is the east, and Juliet is the sun* (*Romeo and Juliet*, Act 2 Scene 2) using [[#rule-metaphor]]. Romeo is in the garden at night and sees Juliet appear at a window.
::: solution
**Tenor:** Juliet (and the window where she appears). **Vehicle:** the sun (and the east, where the sun rises). **Grounds:** light in darkness (it is night); warmth; the source of life; the centre around which everything moves; the beginning of a new day. **Context:** Romeo is in the dark garden; Juliet's appearance is like sunrise, turning night into day — an image of new love. **Associations:** the sun is supreme in the sky, so Juliet is placed above everything; it is also distant and cannot be touched. **Contribution:** the metaphor expresses wonder and worship, and introduces the play's pattern of light-and-dark imagery, in which the lovers meet at night and their love is bright but brief.
:::
:::

::: exercise Symbol or not? {level=2}
Is the dust in Joyce's "Eveline" ([[reading-literature/narrative#ex-eveline]]) a symbol? Apply the tests of [[#rule-symbol]].
::: solution
**Emphasis and repetition:** the dust appears in the opening paragraphs (*dusty cretonne*, *all its familiar objects which she had dusted once a week for so many years, wondering where on earth all the dust came from*). **Language from another field:** *so many years* links the dust to the length of her life. **Context:** a story about a woman who cannot leave a stagnant home. The dust is a real detail of housekeeping and also suggests stagnation, repetition, decay and the endless, unrewarded labour of her life. It meets the tests, so it is reasonable to call it symbolic, as long as the reading stays tied to the text and does not claim one fixed meaning.
:::

::: exercise Everyday metaphors {level=2}
Find the conceptual metaphor behind each group of expressions, and add one more expression of your own. (a) *She's at a crossroads in her career; he's going nowhere; we've come a long way.* (b) *He attacked every point I made; she defended her thesis; I couldn't win.* (c) *I've wasted the whole afternoon; this will save you an hour; is it worth your time?*
::: solution
(a) LIFE IS A JOURNEY (*she's taken a wrong turn*); (b) ARGUMENT IS WAR (*her criticism was right on target*); (c) TIME IS MONEY (*I can't afford to lose a day*).
:::

::: exercise A paragraph on a figure {level=3}
Write a paragraph of 150–200 words on one figure in a public-domain poem not analysed in this chapter (for example Burns's *My love is like a red, red rose*, Wordsworth's *I wandered lonely as a cloud*, or a sonnet by Shakespeare). Use all six steps of [[#rule-metaphor]], and quote the poem precisely.
::: hint
Begin with the tenor and the vehicle, but make your first sentence a claim about what the figure does.
:::
::: solution
Answers will differ. A strong paragraph opens with a claim (*Wordsworth's simile makes the speaker's solitude light and free rather than painful*), identifies tenor and vehicle, considers several possible grounds and says which the context activates, notes the associations of the vehicle, says whether the figure is extended or linked to others, and ends with its contribution to the whole poem. It quotes briefly and accurately, and never stops at *this is a simile*.
:::

::: exercise Write a conceit {level=3}
Write four to eight lines (verse or prose) that develop a conceit: compare a relationship, an emotion or a stage of life to an unlikely object (a bicycle, a map, a lift, a kettle, a mobile phone). Develop at least three points of correspondence, as Donne does. Then list the correspondences.
::: solution
Answers will differ. A model (prose): *Our friendship is a pair of chopsticks: two separate sticks, but useless apart; one stays still, held steady at the base, while the other moves to pick things up, and the stillness of the first is what makes the second's movement work; when one breaks, the other is set aside.* Correspondences: two that work only together; the still and the moving partner; dependence of one on the other; the end of one as the end of both. A good answer develops several points, not just one, and keeps the comparison surprising but exact.
