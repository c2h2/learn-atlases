In 1842 Edgar Allan Poe reviewed a collection of stories by Nathaniel Hawthorne, *Twice-Told Tales*, and used the occasion to say what a short story should be. A skilful writer, he argued, does not invent incidents and then look for a point; he first conceives "a certain unique or single effect" and then invents the incidents that will best produce it. Every word of the story should serve that effect, and if even the first sentence does not, the writer has already failed. Because a short story can be read "at one sitting" — Poe suggested something between half an hour and two hours — the reader stays under the writer's control from beginning to end, as no novel can keep a reader.

Not every later writer agreed with Poe, but his question has shaped the reading of short stories ever since: what is everything in this story *for*? A novel can afford digressions, subplots and minor characters who exist mainly to fill a world. A story of a few thousand words cannot. In a good one, the title, the first sentence, a piece of furniture and the last word all do work, and the pleasure of reading closely — the method of [[reading-literature/close-reading]] — is greatest here, because a whole work can be held in mind at once.

This chapter shows how short stories work, through three stories in the public domain that are often read in English classes: Kate Chopin's "The Story of an Hour" (1894), James Joyce's "Araby" (in *Dubliners*, 1914) and Katherine Mansfield's "The Garden Party" (1922). All three are short enough to read in full before you continue; they are easy to find in free transcriptions of their early editions.

## What makes a short story

::: definition Short story {#def-short-story}
A **short story** is a work of prose fiction short enough to be read at one sitting — typically from about 1,000 to 10,000 words, though there is no fixed limit — that is organised around a single situation, action or moment of change, with few characters and a concentrated effect. Works between the short story and the novel, roughly 15,000–40,000 words, are often called **novellas**; very short stories, under about 1,000 words, are called **flash fiction** or short-short stories.
:::

The word counts are conventions, not rules: magazines and prizes set their own limits. What matters is the consequence of shortness. Because a short story has little room, it tends to begin late (close to the moment of change), to give the past only in glimpses, to rely on a few significant details instead of full description, and to end soon after its central event — often before the reader has been told what that event means.

::: theorem How a short story is built {#rule-story-features}
Most short stories share these features, in varying degrees:

1. **Compression.** One main situation or action, a short span of time, a few characters, often one setting.
2. **A late beginning.** The story opens near the moment of change; earlier events are supplied in flashback or by implication ([[reading-literature/narrative#rule-order]]).
3. **Significant detail.** Objects, gestures and settings carry meaning (a window, a fur, a dusty room), often becoming symbols ([[reading-literature/figurative-language]]).
4. **A turn.** Something changes — an event, a realisation, a reversal — usually late in the story.
5. **An ending that asks the reader to complete the meaning.** The story may end with a surprise, a moment of insight, or an open question, and the reader must infer what the change amounts to.
:::

::: proof
These features follow from length. Compression is a necessity: a story of 3,000 words cannot develop many characters or a long sequence of events. A late beginning saves space and creates interest at once. Significant detail is a way of saying much in little, which is why short-story writers are often compared with poets. The turn gives the story its shape. The ending that leaves work for the reader is a tendency, not a rule — Poe's own stories end decisively — but it became characteristic of the modern short story under the influence of Anton Chekhov in Russia and of Joyce and Mansfield in English, and it is what readers of twentieth-century stories most often have to deal with. Handbooks such as Abrams and Harpham's *A Glossary of Literary Terms* (under "short story") describe the form in similar terms.
:::

::: warning Reading only for the plot
✗ *"The Story of an Hour" is about a woman who hears that her husband is dead, then he comes home and she dies. The end.*

✓ *"The Story of an Hour" builds its whole effect on a gap between what the other characters believe Mrs Mallard feels and what the reader has seen her feel, so that its last sentence is an irony the doctors do not understand.*

A plot summary of a short story is often only a few sentences long, and it misses almost everything that matters. Read short stories at least twice: once for what happens, and once for how the details prepare the turn and the ending.
:::

## Openings

Poe's demand that the first sentence serve the effect is a good guide to reading, whether or not writers followed it. Here is Kate Chopin's first sentence:

> *Knowing that Mrs. Mallard was afflicted with a heart trouble, great care was taken to break to her as gently as possible the news of her husband's death.*

::: example What a first sentence sets up {#ex-chopin-opening}
What does this sentence tell the reader, and what does its grammar suggest?
::: solution
**Information.** Mrs Mallard has a heart condition; her husband is (apparently) dead; other people are breaking the news to her carefully. In one sentence we have the central event, the main character's weakness and a cast of careful people around her.

**Grammar.** The sentence begins with a participle, *Knowing*, whose subject should be the people who know; but the main clause is passive, *great care was taken*, with no agent. (Strict grammar would call *Knowing* a dangling participle.) The people who act are present but unnamed — a vague, protective *they*. And Mrs Mallard is the object of their knowledge and care, not a subject who acts.

**Diction.** *Afflicted* and *heart trouble* are words of illness; *break … gently* is the language of protection. Everything centres on her fragility.

**What it prepares.** The story will overturn this frame. The heart that everyone thinks is weak will turn out to have desires that no one has imagined, and the last sentence will return to the *heart* and to what the others *know* — wrongly. Even the passive voice is significant: a woman whose life is arranged by others, in a story about the discovery of freedom.
:::
:::

Joyce's "Araby" begins quite differently:

> *North Richmond Street, being blind, was a quiet street except at the hour when the Christian Brothers' School set the boys free.*

*Blind* here means ‘closed at one end’, a dead-end street — but the ordinary sense of the word is there too. The first sentence of a story about a boy who will finally see himself clearly begins with blindness. And the boys are *set … free* only at a certain hour. Joyce's opening sentences seldom announce their themes; they plant them in the setting.

::: quiz
In the first sentence of "Araby", *blind* most literally means:
- [ ] unable to see
- [x] closed at one end (a dead end)
- [ ] dark, without lights
- [ ] hidden from view
::: solution
A *blind street* or *blind alley* is one that is closed at one end. The literal meaning fits the description of a quiet street; the other meaning, ‘unable to see’, is present as a suggestion, which the story's ending will develop. Recognising both senses is part of close reading ([[reading-literature/close-reading#rule-checklist]]).
:::
:::

## The turn and the ending: "The Story of an Hour"

"The Story of an Hour" is about a thousand words long. It first appeared in *Vogue* in 1894 under the title "The Dream of an Hour". Its plot is quickly told. Mrs Mallard is told that her husband has been killed in a railway accident. She weeps, then goes alone to her room and sits by an open window. Slowly, against her own will, a feeling comes over her, and she whispers that she is *free*. She imagines the years ahead belonging to herself. She comes downstairs; the front door opens; her husband walks in, unhurt — he had never been near the accident — and she falls dead.

The story turns twice. The first turn happens in the room, and Chopin makes the reader feel it before Mrs Mallard can name it:

> *There was something coming to her and she was waiting for it, fearfully. What was it? She did not know; it was too subtle and elusive to name.*

The thing that comes is announced in a single word, repeated: *She said it over and over under her breath: "free, free, free!"* The second turn is the husband's return:

> *Some one was opening the front door with a latchkey. It was Brently Mallard who entered, a little travel-stained, composedly carrying his grip-sack and umbrella.*

And then the last sentence:

> *When the doctors came they said she had died of heart disease—of joy that kills.*

::: definition Irony of situation and dramatic irony {#def-irony}
**Situational irony** (irony of situation) is a contrast between what is expected and what happens: an outcome that reverses the expectations of the characters or the reader. **Dramatic irony** is a gap between what a character believes and what the reader (or audience) knows. Both are distinct from **verbal irony**, in which a speaker says something other than what is meant ([[reading-skills/inference]], [[pragmatics/irony-understatement]]).
:::

::: example Reading the last sentence {#ex-joy}
Explain the irony of *When the doctors came they said she had died of heart disease—of joy that kills.* Use [[#def-irony]].
::: solution
**What the doctors believe.** They interpret her death in the only way their world allows: a loving wife with a weak heart, overwhelmed by joy at seeing her husband alive.

**What the reader knows.** We have spent the story in her room and heard her whisper *free*. We know that her joy came from his death, not his return, and we infer that what killed her was the sudden loss of the freedom she had just discovered — the opposite of joy at his return. This gap between the characters' belief and the reader's knowledge is **dramatic irony**.

**Situational irony** works at the level of the plot: the news that was supposed to be broken gently, to protect her heart, does not kill her; the good news does.

**The phrase itself.** *Joy that kills* is ambiguous. In the doctors' sense, it means joy at his return. In the reader's sense, it may refer to her joy in freedom, which has made his return unbearable. The final sentence is told in the doctors' words (*they said*), so the narrator does not correct them; the reader must. The story's whole structure — a closed room the others cannot enter, a feeling no one else sees — has prepared this moment.

**A caution.** The story does not say that Mr Mallard was a cruel husband; it says, in fact, that he had loved her and that she had sometimes loved him. What she discovers is not escape from a bad man but the value of a life of her own. A strong interpretation keeps this distinction, which makes the story more unsettling, not less.
:::

## Epiphany: "Araby"

The modern short story often turns not on an event but on a realisation.

::: definition Epiphany {#def-epiphany}
An **epiphany** (顿悟) is a sudden moment of insight in which a character — and through the character, the reader — sees the meaning of a situation, often of their own life. The word originally means a manifestation of the divine (the Christian feast of the Epiphany). James Joyce used it, in early writings published after his death, for a sudden spiritual manifestation of the essence of a thing, a gesture or a moment; critics have since used it for the moment of revelation that ends many of the stories in *Dubliners* and many later stories.
:::

In "Araby", a boy in a poor Dublin street falls in love with the older sister of his friend, Mangan. He has hardly ever spoken to her — *her name was like a summons to all my foolish blood*, says the narrator, looking back. When she mentions a bazaar called Araby, he promises to bring her something from it. His uncle comes home late; the boy arrives at the bazaar as it is closing; the stalls are dark; he hears a shop girl flirting with two young men, and buys nothing. The story ends:

> *Gazing up into the darkness I saw myself as a creature driven and derided by vanity; and my eyes burned with anguish and anger.*

::: example An epiphany ending {#ex-araby}
Analyse the last sentence of "Araby". What does the boy see, and how does the sentence's language connect with the rest of the story?
::: solution
**What he sees.** Not the bazaar or the girl, but *himself*: the sentence is about self-knowledge. He sees his devotion to Mangan's sister as *vanity* — a word that means both ‘pride, self-importance’ and, in its older, biblical sense, ‘emptiness, futility’ (*vanity of vanities*, in the King James Bible, Ecclesiastes 1:2). His romantic quest was about his own feelings and was in vain.

**Passives.** *Driven and derided*: he is the object, pushed and mocked by vanity, as if it were a force outside him. The alliteration on /d/ gives the phrase a harsh, stinging quality.

**Light and darkness.** He gazes *up into the darkness*: the bazaar, which he had imagined as a place of Eastern magic (its name, *Araby*, means Arabia), is dark and closing. The story began in a *blind* street; it ends with the boy seeing clearly in the dark — an ironic reversal of the opening.

**Eyes burning.** *My eyes burned with anguish and anger*: the literal physical sensation of holding back tears joins the metaphor of burning. *Anguish* is pain; *anger* is turned partly against himself.

**Narration.** The narrator is an adult looking back ([[reading-literature/narrative#rule-narration]]). The vocabulary (*creature*, *derided*, *anguish*) is the adult's, not a boy's, so the epiphany is felt by the child and named by the man. That double perspective gives the ending its seriousness without making it melodramatic.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which statement best describes an epiphany in the sense used about Joyce's stories?
- [ ] a surprise event in the plot, such as an unexpected return
- [x] a sudden moment of insight in which a character sees the meaning of a situation
- [ ] a religious vision described in the story
- [ ] the final summary in which the narrator explains the moral
::: solution
An epiphany is a moment of revelation or insight ([[#def-epiphany]]). It may be prompted by a small event, but it is the realisation, not the event, that matters. It is not an explicit moral: Joyce's endings rarely explain themselves.
:::
:::

## Open endings: "The Garden Party"

Katherine Mansfield, born in New Zealand in 1888, wrote stories in which very little seems to happen and a great deal changes. In "The Garden Party", a rich family prepares a party on a perfect summer day. Laura, the youngest daughter, learns that a poor man who lived in the cottages below their house has been killed in an accident, and wants to stop the party; her mother and sister refuse. After the party, Laura is sent down with a basket of leftover food for the widow, and sees the dead man laid out in his house. Walking home, she meets her brother Laurie and tries to tell him what she has felt:

> *"Isn't life," she stammered, "isn't life—" But what life was she couldn't explain. No matter. He quite understood.*
>
> *"Isn't it, darling?" said Laurie.*

::: definition Open and closed endings {#def-endings}
A **closed ending** resolves the story's main questions: we learn what happened and what it meant (a solved crime, a marriage, a death explained). An **open ending** leaves important questions unanswered and invites the reader to complete the meaning. A **twist ending** (surprise ending) reverses the reader's understanding with a final fact or event, as in many stories by O. Henry.
:::

::: example An ending that does not finish {#ex-garden-party}
Why does Mansfield end with an unfinished sentence? What is the effect of *No matter. He quite understood* and of Laurie's reply?
::: solution
**The unfinished sentence.** *Isn't life—* breaks off: Laura cannot find a word for what she has felt — that life is beautiful, terrible, strange, all at once. The dash leaves the predicate empty, and the reader must supply it. The story's meaning is exactly what cannot be said.

**Free indirect style.** *But what life was she couldn't explain. No matter. He quite understood.* These sentences are in Laura's voice, in free indirect style ([[reading-literature/narrative#def-fis]]): *No matter* and *quite* are her words. She believes her brother understands.

**Laurie's reply.** *Isn't it, darling?* is a perfect reply in form — a question tag that agrees with an unfinished question — but it has no content either. Does he understand? The story does not say. He may share her feeling, or he may simply be kind. The repetition of *Isn't* makes the exchange tender and empty at the same time.

**Effect.** The ending is **open** ([[#def-endings]]). Laura has had something like an epiphany — her encounter with death has shown her a world beyond the garden party — but the story does not tell us whether it will change her, or whether her class and family will absorb it as easily as Laurie's *darling*. Readers have argued both ways, which is a sign that the ending is doing its work.
:::
:::

::: theorem Reading an ending {#rule-endings}
When you reach the end of a short story, ask:

1. **What has changed** between the first scene and the last — in the situation, in a character, in what the reader knows?
2. **Who understands the change?** The character, the other characters, only the reader? (A gap is often dramatic irony.)
3. **How is the ending phrased?** Whose words are the last ones — the narrator's, a character's, a quoted opinion? Is a sentence left unfinished?
4. **What does the title mean now?** Titles often change meaning by the end ("The Story of an Hour": one hour of freedom; "Araby": a dream of the East that turns out to be a dark hall).
5. **What is left open**, and does the text give you enough evidence to decide, or is the openness the point?
:::

::: proof
The ending of a short story carries more weight than that of a novel, because the story is built towards it ([[#rule-story-features]], point 5). The five questions direct attention to the means by which modern stories make their endings mean: change (the turn), the distribution of knowledge (irony), voice (free indirect style or quoted speech), the frame (the title) and deliberate openness. Question 5 matters because an open ending is not the same as an ending with no meaning: Mansfield's and Joyce's endings are open about *what will happen* but quite precise about *what has been seen*. A reader who says "the ending is open, so it can mean anything" has not yet read it closely.
:::

## Other ways of telling it

The three stories above represent one powerful tradition, but short stories in English take many forms.

- **The tale of effect.** Poe's own stories — "The Tell-Tale Heart" (1843, discussed in [[reading-literature/narrative#ex-poe]]), "The Fall of the House of Usher" (1839) — aim at a single overwhelming mood, often horror, with a decisive ending.
- **The detective story.** Arthur Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes stories (from 1887 in novels, from 1891 in short stories in *The Strand Magazine*) are closed by definition: the ending explains everything. Their pleasure lies in the order in which information is given and withheld ([[reading-literature/narrative#rule-order]]).
- **The twist ending.** O. Henry's "The Gift of the Magi" (1905) tells of a poor young couple who each secretly sell their most precious possession to buy a Christmas present for the other — she her hair, to buy a chain for his watch; he his watch, to buy combs for her hair. The final reversal is both a joke and the point.
- **The story of omission.** Later writers took compression further. Ernest Hemingway, in *Death in the Afternoon* (1932), compared good writing to an iceberg, of which only a small part shows above the water: a writer who knows what he is leaving out can omit it, and the reader will still feel it. Many of his stories consist largely of dialogue in which the main subject is never named.

::: note English and Chinese
- **A different tradition of short fiction.** Chinese has a long history of short narrative — the *zhiguai* (志怪) tales of the strange, the Tang *chuanqi* (传奇), and Pu Songling's *Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio* (《聊斋志异》, compiled in the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries). The modern Chinese short story took shape in the early twentieth century, partly through translations of Western stories; Lu Xun's stories, such as "Kong Yiji" (《孔乙己》, 1919), share with *Dubliners* a narrator who looks back on a humiliated figure, and a quiet, open ending.
- **The 中心思想 habit.** School reading in Chinese and English often asks for the central idea or moral of a story. Modern short stories in English frequently resist a single moral, and the best answer may describe a tension ("the story shows both Laura's new awareness and the ease with which her world absorbs it") rather than a lesson.
- **Small words at the end.** Many endings turn on tiny words that are easy to pass over in a second language: *still*, *quite*, *No matter*, a question tag, a dash. Read the last paragraph of a story slowly and aloud.
- **Translations.** If you read Chekhov or Maupassant in English, remember that the translation has its own author and, if recent, its own copyright; quote it with the translator's name. Constance Garnett's translations of Chekhov (1916–22) are now in the public domain; many later ones are not.
:::

::: history
Short tales are as old as storytelling, but the modern short story as a literary form developed in the nineteenth century with the growth of magazines, which needed fiction that could be read in one issue. In the United States, Washington Irving, Nathaniel Hawthorne and Edgar Allan Poe established the form; Poe's review of Hawthorne's *Twice-Told Tales* (1842) is often treated as its first theory. Brander Matthews's essay *The Philosophy of the Short-Story* (first published in a magazine in 1885, as a book in 1901) argued that it was a genre in its own right. In Russia, Anton Chekhov (1860–1904) wrote stories of everyday life with quiet, open endings that strongly influenced writers in English, among them Katherine Mansfield. James Joyce's *Dubliners* (1914) and Mansfield's *Bliss* (1920) and *The Garden Party* (1922) made the open, epiphanic story central to modern fiction in English. Frank O'Connor's *The Lonely Voice* (1963) is a classic study of the form.
:::

::: summary
- A **short story** can be read at one sitting and is organised around a single situation or moment of change ([[#def-short-story]]).
- Its shortness produces **compression**, a **late beginning**, **significant detail**, a **turn** and an ending that asks the reader to complete the meaning ([[#rule-story-features]]); Poe called for a "single effect".
- First sentences set up situations and themes, often through setting and grammar (Chopin's passive; Joyce's *blind* street).
- **Situational** and **dramatic irony** depend on gaps between expectation and outcome, and between what characters believe and what the reader knows ([[#def-irony]]).
- An **epiphany** is a sudden moment of insight, characteristic of Joyce's *Dubliners* ([[#def-epiphany]]).
- Endings may be **closed**, **open** or **twist** endings ([[#def-endings]]); ask what has changed, who understands, whose words end the story, what the title now means and what is left open ([[#rule-endings]]).
:::

::: exercise The window {level=2}
In "The Story of an Hour", Mrs Mallard sits facing an open window, through which she can see *the tops of trees that were all aquiver with the new spring life*. What might the window and the spring signify in the story? Give two meanings and the evidence for each.
::: solution
**Freedom and the outside world.** The window is *open*, while her room is closed to the others; it connects her to a world outside the house and its roles, and she looks out of it as the feeling of freedom comes.

**New life.** *New spring life*, *aquiver*: spring is the season of rebirth, and the story is about a new life that begins, ironically, with a death. The trees tremble with life as she will tremble with her new feeling.

A good answer ties each meaning to words in the text and notes the irony: the spring of her new life lasts only an hour.
:::

::: exercise Titles {level=2}
Explain how the meaning of each title changes or deepens by the end of its story. (a) "The Story of an Hour" (b) "Araby"
::: solution
(a) At first, the title seems to describe the short time span. By the end, *an hour* is the whole of Mrs Mallard's life of freedom: the story of an hour is the story of a life she lived only in that hour. (Under its first title, "The Dream of an Hour", the freedom is even more explicitly a dream.)

(b) *Araby* is the name of the bazaar, with its promise of Eastern romance. By the end, the word names the boy's illusion: the exotic Araby turns out to be a dark hall with ordinary commerce and flirtation, and the title becomes ironic.
:::
:::

::: exercise Twist or epiphany? {level=2}
Compare the ending of O. Henry's "The Gift of the Magi" (as summarised in this chapter) with the ending of "Araby". How does each ending work on the reader?
::: solution
**"The Gift of the Magi"** ends with a twist: the final fact (each has sold the thing the other's gift was for) reverses the situation and surprises the reader. The meaning is then stated: their gifts are useless, but their love is the real gift. The ending is closed and the effect is a mixture of amusement and sentiment.

**"Araby"** ends with an epiphany: there is no surprising event — the bazaar is simply closing — but the boy sees himself differently. The meaning is not stated as a moral; the reader must interpret *vanity* and the darkness. The effect is quieter and more painful. One works through plot, the other through perception.
:::
:::

::: exercise Write about an ending {level=3}
Choose one story from this chapter, read it in full, and write a paragraph of 150–200 words answering the five questions of [[#rule-endings]] about its ending. Quote at least two short phrases.

Start with what has changed, then move to who understands it; end with what is left open.

::: solution
Answers will differ. A strong paragraph (1) states the change in one sentence; (2) identifies the gap in knowledge, if there is one (the doctors in Chopin; the adult and the boy in Joyce; Laura and Laurie in Mansfield); (3) comments on the phrasing of the final sentence with short quotations; (4) says what the title means by the end; (5) distinguishes what is open from what is clear. It does not retell the plot.

::: exercise Write a very short story {level=3}
Write a story of 300–400 words that begins late, uses one significant object, turns on a single moment of change and ends without explaining what the change means. Then write two or three sentences explaining which features of [[#rule-story-features]] you used and where.
::: solution
Answers will differ.
