Here are the first words of two famous American and British novels:

> *You don't know about me without you have read a book by the name of The Adventures of Tom Sawyer; but that ain't no matter.* (Mark Twain, *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn*, 1884–85)

> *It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife.* (Jane Austen, *Pride and Prejudice*, 1813)

Before anything has happened in either book, you already know a great deal — not about the events, but about who is telling them. The first voice is a boy's, speaking directly to *you*, in a regional, non-standard English (*without you have read* ‘unless you have read’, *ain't no matter*), cheerful and unconcerned with grammar or with books. The second voice is nobody in particular: it speaks with the authority of a philosopher, never says *I*, and, as [[reading-literature/close-reading#ex-austen]] showed, is quietly ironic. The same events told by these two voices would be two different books.

Every story is told by someone, from somewhere, in some order. This chapter gives you the terms that literary critics use to describe how: **story** and **discourse**, **plot**, **character** and **setting**, the **narrator** and **point of view**, and the technique that makes the English novel distinctive, **free indirect style**. These are the tools you will need for [[reading-literature/short-story]] and [[reading-literature/the-novel]].

## Story and plot

::: definition Story, discourse and plot {#def-story-discourse}
A **narrative** is the representation of a sequence of events. Narratologists distinguish:

- the **story** (Russian formalist *fabula*): the events themselves, in the order in which they happened, with the characters who take part in them;
- the **discourse** (*sjuzhet*): the way the story is presented in the text — in what order, at what length, from whose point of view, in whose words.

The **plot** is the arrangement of the events in the discourse, usually with their causes: what the reader is told, in what order, and how one event leads to another.
:::

The distinction comes from the Russian formalist critics of the 1920s and was developed by structuralist critics in France in the 1960s and 1970s, above all Gérard Genette. E. M. Forster, in *Aspects of the Novel* (1927), made a related point about causation in a famous pair of sentences: "'The king died and then the queen died' is a story. 'The king died, and then the queen died of grief' is a plot." For Forster, a story asks *and then?*; a plot asks *why?*

::: theorem Order, duration and frequency {#rule-order}
The discourse can differ from the story in three main ways (Genette, *Narrative Discourse*, 1972; English translation 1980):

1. **Order.** Events can be told out of sequence: a **flashback** (*analepsis*) narrates an earlier event; a **flash-forward** (*prolepsis*) anticipates a later one; a narrative that begins in the middle of the action starts *in medias res* (Latin ‘into the middle of things’).
2. **Duration.** Events can be told at different speeds: a **scene** gives an event at about the speed it happened, often with dialogue; a **summary** compresses a long period into a few sentences (*Three years passed*); an **ellipsis** skips time entirely; a **pause** stops the story for description or comment.
3. **Frequency.** An event that happened once can be told once, or several times (from different points of view); events that happened many times can be told once (*Every Sunday they walked by the river*).
:::

::: proof
These categories describe what every narrative must decide, because the story has a time of its own and so does the text. A reader spends perhaps ten minutes on a chapter that covers a single conversation and ten seconds on a sentence that covers a decade. Where a writer slows down tells you what the narrative considers important; where it jumps or reorders, it creates suspense, surprise or irony. A flash-forward (*She did not know then that she would never see him again*) gives the reader knowledge the character lacks, which is a form of dramatic irony ([[reading-literature/drama-shakespeare]]). Genette's terms are now standard in narratology; English-language textbooks such as H. Porter Abbott's *The Cambridge Introduction to Narrative* (2nd ed., 2008) use simplified versions of them.
:::

::: example Story and discourse in a short narrative {#ex-story-order}
Read this paragraph, written for this lesson. Put the events in story order, then describe how the discourse arranges them.

> *The letter lay unopened on the table for three days. Chen Lin had recognised the handwriting at once: her brother's, whom she had not spoken to since their father's funeral, eleven years before. On the fourth morning she made tea, sat down, and slit the envelope with a kitchen knife. Years later she would say that she had known, even then, what it contained.*
::: solution
**Story order.** (1) The father's funeral; Chen Lin and her brother stop speaking. (2) Eleven years pass. (3) A letter arrives; she recognises the handwriting. (4) It lies unopened for three days. (5) On the fourth morning she opens it. (6) Years later she says she had known what it contained.

**Discourse.** The paragraph begins with event (4), *in medias res*. It then gives a **flashback** to (3) and, inside it, to (1), told in the past perfect (*had recognised*, *had not spoken*). Eleven years are covered by an **ellipsis** — a single phrase, *eleven years before*. The opening of the letter (5) is told as a short **scene**, with small physical details (*made tea*, *a kitchen knife*), which slows the moment down. The last sentence is a **flash-forward** (*Years later she would say*), which tells us that the letter mattered without telling us what it said.

**Effect.** The order creates suspense (what is in the letter?) and shows Chen Lin's reluctance (three days). The flash-forward makes the reader expect bad news, or at least important news, and makes the moment of opening heavier.
:::
:::

## Character and setting

::: definition Character, characterisation and setting {#def-character}
A **character** is a person (or a creature treated as a person) in a narrative. **Characterisation** is the means by which a text presents character: **directly**, by telling the reader what someone is like (*She was proud and impatient*), or **indirectly**, by showing what they say, do, think and how others react to them. The **setting** is the time, place and social world in which the story takes place.
:::

Forster's *Aspects of the Novel* also introduced a pair of terms that are still used: **flat** characters, built around a single idea or quality, who can be summed up in a sentence, and **round** characters, who are complex and capable, in Forster's phrase, of "surprising in a convincing way". Flat is not a criticism: Dickens filled his novels with memorable flat characters, defined by a habit of speech or a single obsession, and comedy depends on them.

Indirect characterisation is the commoner and usually the more interesting kind, because it asks the reader to infer ([[reading-skills/inference]]). A character who tells everyone how honest he is, and then keeps the change from a shop, has been characterised more sharply than any adjective could manage. Setting, too, is rarely mere background. Dickens's fog in *Bleak House* ([[reading-literature/the-novel]]) and the dusty, airless house of Joyce's "Eveline" (below) are part of the meaning of those works.

## The narrator

::: definition Narrator and point of view {#def-narrator}
The **narrator** is the voice that tells the story. The narrator is not the author: Mark Twain is the author of *Huckleberry Finn*; Huck is its narrator. **Point of view** is the position from which the events are perceived and told. Two questions describe it:

1. **Who speaks?** Is the narrator a character in the story (a **first-person narrator**, who says *I*), or outside it (a **third-person narrator**, who refers to all the characters as *he*, *she*, *they*)?
2. **Who sees?** Through whose perception are the events presented? Genette called this **focalisation**: a narrative may move freely among the minds of many characters, stay with one character, or present only what could be observed from outside.
:::

The two questions are independent, and keeping them apart is the most useful single step in describing a narrative. A third-person narrator can tell the story through the eyes of one character, almost as if that character were narrating. A first-person narrator looking back on childhood can present events as the child saw them or as the adult now understands them.

::: theorem Common types of narration {#rule-narration}
1. **First-person narration.** The narrator is a character, often the main one (*Jane Eyre*, *Great Expectations*, *Huckleberry Finn*), sometimes an observer of someone else's story (Dr Watson in the Sherlock Holmes stories). A **retrospective** first-person narrator tells events long after they happened, so there is an older narrating *I* and a younger experiencing *I*.
2. **Third-person omniscient narration.** The narrator stands outside the story, knows the thoughts of any character, can comment, generalise and judge, and may address the reader (George Eliot's *Middlemarch*, Austen's novels, much Victorian fiction).
3. **Third-person limited narration.** The narrator stays with the perceptions of one character (or one at a time), telling only what that character knows or could know (much of Henry James's later fiction, many modern short stories).
4. **Objective (external) narration.** The narrator reports only what could be seen and heard, without entering any mind, like a camera.
5. **Second-person narration** (*You walk into the room*) is rare in fiction, though common in instructions and some modern experiments.
:::

::: proof
These types are standard in literary handbooks (Abrams and Harpham, *A Glossary of Literary Terms*, under "point of view"), and the terms are used in almost every course on fiction. They are ideal types: real narratives mix them. Austen's narrator is omniscient but often limits herself to the heroine's mind; *Wuthering Heights* (1847) is told in the first person by a visitor, Lockwood, who passes most of the story to the housekeeper, Nelly Dean, who narrates in turn — a **frame narrative**, with a story inside a story. The value of the types is that they let a reader ask what each choice makes possible: a first-person narrator offers intimacy and limitation; an omniscient narrator offers range and judgement; a limited narrator offers suspense, because the reader knows no more than the character.
:::

::: example Identifying the narrator {#ex-openings}
Describe the narration of each opening as precisely as you can from the evidence given.

(a) *There was no possibility of taking a walk that day.* (Charlotte Brontë, *Jane Eyre*, 1847)

(b) *1801.—I have just returned from a visit to my landlord—the solitary neighbour that I shall be troubled with.* (Emily Brontë, *Wuthering Heights*, 1847)

(c) *My father's family name being Pirrip, and my Christian name Philip, my infant tongue could make of both names nothing longer or more explicit than Pip.* (Charles Dickens, *Great Expectations*, 1860–61)
::: solution
**(a)** The sentence alone does not show the person: it could open a third-person novel. The rest of the paragraph uses *we* and *I*, and the book is narrated by Jane herself, looking back on her childhood — a **retrospective first-person** narrator. The impersonal opening (*There was no possibility*) has a child's sense of a rule imposed from outside.

**(b)** **First person**, dated like a diary (*1801.—*) and in the present perfect (*I have just returned*), so the narration is close in time to the events — the opposite of retrospective. The narrator, Lockwood, is a newcomer and an outsider, and his confident judgement (*the solitary neighbour that I shall be troubled with*) will prove to be wrong: he understands little of what he sees. He is the outer frame of the story.

**(c)** **Retrospective first person**: an adult narrator (*my infant tongue*) explains how he got his name as a small child. The formal, Latinate diction (*nothing longer or more explicit*) is the older narrator's, not the child's; the gap between the two creates the gentle comedy of the opening and prepares a novel in which the adult judges his younger self.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A novel says: *Mrs Huang looked at her son and wondered whether he had eaten. Across the table, her son was thinking about the exam he had failed, and about how to tell her.* What kind of narration is this?
- [ ] first person
- [ ] third person limited to Mrs Huang
- [x] third person omniscient
- [ ] objective narration
::: solution
The narrator uses *her* and *he* (third person) and enters the minds of two characters — Mrs Huang *wondered*, her son *was thinking* — so it is **omniscient**. A limited narrator would stay with one of them; objective narration would enter neither.
:::
:::

## Reliable and unreliable narrators

A first-person narrator can be wrong, foolish, biased or dishonest, and the reader may see this when the narrator does not.

::: definition Unreliable narrator {#def-unreliable}
An **unreliable narrator** is a narrator whose account of events, or whose judgement of them, the reader has reason to doubt. The term was introduced by Wayne C. Booth in *The Rhetoric of Fiction* (1961), who called a narrator unreliable when he or she does not speak or act in accordance with the norms of the work — the values that the work as a whole implies.
:::

Unreliability is inferred, never announced. The clues are those of [[reading-skills/inference#rule-good-inference]]: contradictions between what the narrator says and what the narrator reports; other characters' reactions; a narrator's insistence on what no one has questioned; and a gap between the narrator's values and those the work implies. Huck Finn is unreliable in an interesting way: he believes that he is doing wrong when he helps Jim, an enslaved man, to escape, and the reader judges that he is doing right. The narrator is morally better than his own judgement — a gap that the novel uses for irony and for moral force.

::: example A narrator who protests too much {#ex-poe}
Read the opening of Edgar Allan Poe's story "The Tell-Tale Heart" (1843). What reasons does the reader have to doubt the narrator?

> *True!—nervous—very, very dreadfully nervous I had been and am; but why will you say that I am mad? The disease had sharpened my senses—not destroyed—not dulled them.*
::: solution
**He answers an accusation that the reader has not made.** *Why will you say that I am mad?* imagines a listener who already thinks him mad; he is defending himself before anything has been told.

**He admits the symptoms he denies.** *Very, very dreadfully nervous*, and *The disease* — he calls his condition a disease while arguing that it has improved him.

**The syntax enacts the disorder.** Dashes break the sentences into fragments; words are repeated (*very, very*); he corrects himself (*not destroyed—not dulled*). The form contradicts the content: a man who claims calm, clear senses cannot finish a sentence calmly.

**Conclusion.** The narrator is **unreliable** ([[#def-unreliable]]): his account will be the story of a murder that he presents as proof of his sanity. The pleasure and horror of the story come from the gap between what he believes he is showing and what the reader sees.
:::
:::

::: warning Confusing the narrator with the author
✗ *In "The Tell-Tale Heart", Poe tells us that he is not mad.*

✓ *In "The Tell-Tale Heart", the narrator insists that he is not mad; Poe's story invites us to disbelieve him.*

The *I* of a story is a character created by the author, even when the author uses details of his or her own life. Write *the narrator*, *the speaker* (in a poem) or the character's name, not the author's name, when you discuss what the narrating voice says or believes. Say *Poe* or *Dickens* only when you discuss the author's choices: *Poe makes his narrator answer an accusation that no one has made.*
:::

## Free indirect style

Novels in English have three main ways of presenting what characters say or think. Compare:

- **Direct speech or thought**: *She thought, "I can't stay here any longer."*
- **Indirect (reported) speech or thought**: *She thought that she could not stay there any longer.*
- **Free indirect style**: *She could not stay here any longer.*

The third sentence is in the third person and the past tense, like the narrator's voice, but its words — and its *here*, which belongs to her position, not the narrator's — are hers. There is no reporting verb (*she thought*), so the sentence floats between narrator and character.

::: definition Free indirect style {#def-fis}
**Free indirect style** (also free indirect discourse or speech; French *style indirect libre*) presents a character's words or thoughts in the narrator's grammatical frame — the third person and the narrative tense — but without a reporting clause, and with features of the character's own language: their vocabulary, exclamations, questions, and expressions of time and place relative to the character (*now*, *here*, *tomorrow*).
:::

::: theorem Recognising free indirect style {#rule-fis}
A sentence in a third-person narrative is probably free indirect style if it is in the third person and the narrative past tense, has no *she thought* or *he said*, and contains one or more of these signs of the character's voice:

1. **exclamations or questions** that the character, not the narrator, would ask (*How could he have forgotten?*);
2. **deictic words** that place the speaker in the character's time and place: *now*, *today*, *tomorrow*, *here*, *this*, combined with a past tense (*Tomorrow was Monday*);
3. **the character's vocabulary**: colloquial or emotional words, a child's words, the character's habitual phrases;
4. **modals and evaluations** that express the character's attitude (*Surely she must have known*, *It was too awful*);
5. **contrast with the surrounding narration**, which has a different register or knowledge.
:::

::: proof
These signs follow from the definition. In indirect speech, English shifts tense and deixis to the reporter's position (*She said she would come the next day*, [[complex-sentences/reported-speech]]). Free indirect style keeps the tense shift but not the deictic shift (*She would come tomorrow*), and keeps the expressive features — exclamations, questions, emotive words — that indirect speech normally loses. That mixture is its mark. Because it lacks a reporting verb, free indirect style can be ambiguous: the reader cannot always tell whether a sentence is the character's thought or the narrator's comment, and novelists use that uncertainty for irony (when the narrator seems to adopt a character's foolish view) and for sympathy (when the narrator seems to think with the character). The term was introduced by the Swiss linguist Charles Bally in 1912; the technique is much older and is especially associated, in English, with Jane Austen.
:::

Here is the opening of James Joyce's story "Eveline", from *Dubliners* (1914). Eveline, a young woman in Dublin, is deciding whether to leave home with a sailor and go abroad:

> *She sat at the window watching the evening invade the avenue. Her head was leaned against the window curtains and in her nostrils was the odour of dusty cretonne. She was tired.*

A little later:

> *Home! She looked round the room, reviewing all its familiar objects which she had dusted once a week for so many years, wondering where on earth all the dust came from.*

::: example Finding the character's voice {#ex-eveline}
Which parts of the two passages from "Eveline" belong to the narrator, and which to Eveline's own thoughts? Use [[#rule-fis]].
::: solution
**Narrator's description.** *She sat at the window watching the evening invade the avenue.* The verb *invade* is a writer's word — a metaphor of an army or a disease spreading — which already suggests that the evening is something hostile. *The odour of dusty cretonne* (a cotton fabric used for curtains) is precise and a little formal.

**A shift.** *She was tired.* Three short words. This could be the narrator's report, but its plainness is closer to how Eveline would put it: it is the first sentence that sounds like her.

**Free indirect style.** *Home!* is an exclamation — sign 1 — and it is Eveline's: the narrator has no reason to exclaim. The phrase *wondering where on earth all the dust came from* is colloquial (*where on earth*, sign 3), the half-exasperated, half-resigned thought of the woman who has done the dusting for years. The narration has moved into her mind without a *she thought*.

**Effect.** The movement from the narrator's description to Eveline's voice brings the reader close to her and makes her small, repeated domestic life felt from inside. The detail of the dust, which keeps returning however often it is removed, becomes an image of her life itself: the same tasks, the same room, for *so many years*.
:::
:::

Virginia Woolf's *Mrs Dalloway* (1925) carries the method further, moving freely through the minds of many characters in a single London day. Its opening shows how quickly narration can enter a mind:

> *Mrs. Dalloway said she would buy the flowers herself.*
> *For Lucy had her work cut out for her. The doors would be taken off their hinges; Rumpelmayer's men were coming. And then, thought Clarissa Dalloway, what a morning—fresh as if issued to children on a beach.*

The first sentence is indirect speech (*said she would*). The second — *For Lucy had her work cut out for her* — is already Clarissa's thought, in free indirect style: the explanatory *For* and the idiom are hers, and so is the next sentence, with its future-in-the-past (*would be taken off*, *were coming*) seen from her morning. Then *thought Clarissa Dalloway* appears, but in the middle of the sentence, after the thought has begun, and is followed by an exclamation. The boundary between narrator and character hardly exists.

::: example Three ways of presenting thought {#ex-convert}
Rewrite this direct thought in indirect thought and in free indirect style, and say what changes: *He looked at the clock and thought, "Oh no, I'm late again. What will she say this time?"*
::: solution
**Indirect thought.** *He looked at the clock and thought that he was late again, and wondered what she would say that time.* The reporting verbs (*thought that*, *wondered*) are explicit; the tenses shift back (*am* → *was*, *will* → *would*); *this time* becomes *that time*; the exclamation *Oh no* disappears, because indirect speech cannot easily report it; the question becomes an indirect question with statement word order.

**Free indirect style.** *He looked at the clock. Oh no — he was late again. What would she say this time?* The tense shifts (*was*, *would*), the person shifts (*he*), but the exclamation, the direct question word order and the deictic *this time* stay, because they are his ([[#rule-fis]]). There is no reporting verb.

**What changes.** Indirect thought keeps the narrator in control and at a distance. Free indirect style gives the character's feelings their own rhythm while keeping the narrator's grammar, so the reader is inside his panic without the interruption of quotation marks.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is in free indirect style?
- [ ] *She said, "I'll leave tomorrow."*
- [ ] *She said that she would leave the next day.*
- [x] *She would leave tomorrow. Nothing could stop her now.*
- [ ] *I will leave tomorrow, she said.*
::: solution
The third option is in the third person and the past (*would*), with no reporting verb, but keeps the character's deictic words (*tomorrow*, *now*) and her emphatic tone. The first and fourth are direct speech; the second is indirect speech, with *the next day* replacing *tomorrow*.
:::

- [x] Is the narrator a character in the story, and through whose perception are events presented?

- [ ] Is the narrator male or female?
::: solution
Genette's distinction between narration (who speaks) and focalisation (who sees) is exactly this pair of questions ([[#def-narrator]]). Tense, realism and the narrator's gender may matter, but they do not separate the two.

::: note English and Chinese
- **Tense and free indirect style.** English free indirect style is recognisable partly because of the tense shift (*was*, *would*) combined with deictic words of the character's present (*now*, *tomorrow*). Mandarin does not mark tense on the verb, so the boundary between narrator and character is drawn by other means — pronouns, sentence-final particles, the choice of words — and is often even more fluid. When you read English fiction, the mixture of past tense and *now* or *here* is a reliable signal; when you translate it into Chinese, the effect usually has to be recreated with word choice ([[translation/literary-translation]]).
- **Frame narratives.** Chinese readers may know a famous frame: Lu Xun's "A Madman's Diary" (《狂人日记》, 1918) opens with a short preface in classical Chinese by a narrator who presents the madman's diary, written in the vernacular. The difference of language marks the two narrative levels, as Lockwood's and Nelly Dean's voices do in *Wuthering Heights*.
- **The narrator is not the author.** Chinese school analysis sometimes treats a first-person *我* as the author. In English literary study, always distinguish the narrator or speaker from the author ([[#def-narrator]]).
- For the theory of narrative in the Chinese tradition and its comparison with Western narratology, see the Chinese Atlas lesson on [narrative](/learn/chinese/lesson.php?c=literary-theory&l=narrative).
:::

::: history
Reflection on how stories are told is as old as Plato and Aristotle, who distinguished telling in the poet's own voice from imitating the voices of characters. The modern study of point of view in English began with Henry James, whose prefaces to the New York Edition of his novels (1907–09) discussed the "centre of consciousness" through which a story is seen; Percy Lubbock's *The Craft of Fiction* (1921) made James's practice into a theory. E. M. Forster's lectures *Aspects of the Novel* (1927) introduced flat and round characters and the distinction between story and plot. Charles Bally named *style indirect libre* in 1912. Wayne C. Booth's *The Rhetoric of Fiction* (1961) introduced the unreliable narrator and the "implied author". Gérard Genette's "Discours du récit" (in *Figures III*, 1972; *Narrative Discourse*, 1980) gave narratology its terms for order, duration, frequency, voice and focalisation, and Mieke Bal's *Narratology* (1985) and Gerald Prince's *A Dictionary of Narratology* (1987) made them widely available.
:::

::: exercise Who speaks, who sees? {level=2}
Describe the narration of each passage, written for this lesson, using the terms of [[#rule-narration]].

(a) *The room was empty except for a table and two chairs. A man came in, sat down and put a key on the table. A woman followed him. Neither spoke.*

(b) *Liu Yang hated these dinners. Her aunt would ask about marriage again; her cousin would talk about his car. She smiled and passed the dumplings.*

(c) *I was nine that summer, and I believed everything my brother told me, which was a mistake I would go on making for twenty years.*
::: solution
(a) **Objective (external) third-person narration**: only what can be seen; no mind is entered; *Neither spoke* is an observation, not a thought.

(b) **Third-person limited**, focalised through Liu Yang, with **free indirect style**: *Her aunt would ask about marriage again* is her expectation in the narrator's grammar; *hated* and *again* give her attitude.

(c) **Retrospective first person**: the narrating *I* is adult (*twenty years*) and judges the experiencing *I* of nine (*which was a mistake*). The flash-forward shows the gap between them.
:::
:::

::: exercise Make it free indirect {level=2}
Rewrite each direct thought in free indirect style. (a) *She thought, "Why didn't I say something? Now it's too late."* (b) *He thought, "This is the best day of my life!"* (c) *They wondered, "Will the train ever come?"*
::: solution
(a) *Why hadn't she said something? Now it was too late.* (b) *This was the best day of his life!* (c) *Would the train ever come?* (In context, the sentence would follow a description of the waiting people.) In each, the tense moves back and the person changes, but the question or exclamation, *now* and *this* stay ([[#rule-fis]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Is the narrator reliable? {level=2}
What clues suggest that this first-person narrator, written for this lesson, is unreliable? *I am the most patient man in the office, everyone agrees. Only yesterday I waited almost a full minute for the lift before I began to bang on the doors, and when the new trainee asked me a question for the second time, I explained it very calmly, though I did have to raise my voice so that the whole floor could hear how simple it was.*
::: solution
The narrator's claim (*the most patient man*) is contradicted by what he reports: banging on the lift doors after less than a minute, raising his voice at a trainee in front of everyone. *Everyone agrees* is an unsupported generalisation. *Very calmly* and *though I did have to raise my voice* contradict each other. The reader infers impatience and a wish to humiliate; the gap between what he says and what he shows makes him **unreliable** ([[#def-unreliable]]) and comic.
:::
:::

::: exercise Analyse a passage {level=3}
In Joyce's "Eveline", the heroine remembers her childhood and her father: *Still they seemed to have been rather happy then. Her father was not so bad then; and besides, her mother was alive.* Write a paragraph of 120–150 words analysing the narration of these sentences. Consider free indirect style, the repeated *then*, and what the sentences suggest about Eveline's state of mind.
::: hint
Who is making the judgement *not so bad*? What does *Still* (‘nevertheless’) concede? What does the order of the reasons suggest?
:::
::: solution
A model: *These sentences are in free indirect style: there is no "she thought", but the judgements are Eveline's, in her own plain, hesitant words. "Still" concedes that the memory is not simply happy, and "seemed" and "rather" weaken the claim further, as if she is trying to persuade herself. The repeated "then" fixes happiness in the past and implies a contrast with now, when her father is, by implication, worse. "Not so bad" is a remarkably low standard of praise for a father, and it hints at violence that the story later confirms. The final reason, added with "and besides", is the most important and the most painful: her mother was alive. By presenting the reasons in Eveline's own uncertain voice, Joyce lets the reader see what she cannot quite say — that her home has long been unhappy — and prepares her final inability to leave it.*
A good answer identifies free indirect style, comments on specific words (*Still*, *seemed*, *then*, *not so bad*, *besides*) and connects them to her state of mind.
:::
:::

::: exercise Retell from another point of view {level=3}
Write a short narrative (150–200 words) of a single event — a missed train, an argument, a meeting — twice: once in retrospective first person, and once in third-person limited narration with at least two sentences of free indirect style. Then write two sentences on what each version allows that the other does not.
::: solution
Answers will differ. Check that (1) the first version shows a gap between the narrating and the experiencing *I* (*I didn't know then that …*); (2) the second version uses *he*/*she*, the past tense and no reporting verb in its free indirect sentences, with the character's exclamations, questions or *now*/*here*; (3) your comment says, for example, that first person allows hindsight and judgement of the younger self, while limited third person allows the reader to be inside a mind and outside it at once, with irony when the narrator's and the character's views differ.
:::
