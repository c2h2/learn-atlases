Here are two versions of a paragraph from a literature review on homework in primary schools. The studies are fictional.

> **Version A.** *Bauer (2012) studied 400 pupils in Germany and found that homework had no effect on maths scores. Kim and Lee (2015) surveyed 1,200 pupils in South Korea and found that pupils who did more homework had higher scores. Okafor (2018) carried out an experiment in Nigeria and found a small positive effect for pupils aged 10–11 but not for younger pupils. Singh (2021) reviewed 25 studies and found that the effect of homework increases with age.*
>
> **Version B.** *Evidence on the value of homework for primary pupils is mixed, but much of the apparent disagreement may reflect pupils' age. Studies that include the youngest pupils tend to find little or no effect (Bauer, 2012), whereas positive effects have been reported for older primary pupils, both in a large survey (Kim & Lee, 2015) and in an experiment (Okafor, 2018), which found a benefit at ages 10–11 but not below. Singh's (2021) review of 25 studies supports this pattern, concluding that the effect of homework increases with age. Most of this research, however, has measured achievement in mathematics only; whether the same pattern holds for reading is largely unknown.*

Version A is accurate, and every study is cited. But it is a list: four summaries in the order the writer happened to read them, each beginning with a name. The reader must do the work of seeing that the studies conflict and why. Version B does that work. It opens with a claim about the research as a whole, groups the studies by what they show, explains the apparent conflict, uses the review as support, evaluates the evidence (*a large survey*, *an experiment*), and ends with a gap. The same four sources have become an argument.

That difference — between **summary** and **synthesis** — is the subject of this chapter. It covers what a literature review is for, how to organise one, how to write paragraphs that synthesise and evaluate, and how to lead the review to the gap that the study fills. Summarising single sources and preparing a synthesis table are taught in [[academic-reading/notes-and-summaries]]; citing is in [[academic-writing/citation-referencing]].

## What a literature review does

::: definition Literature review {#def-literature-review}
A **literature review** (文献综述) is an account of what is known about a question from published research, organised and evaluated so as to show the state of knowledge and, usually, what remains to be found out. It appears as the opening moves of a research article's introduction, as a chapter of a thesis or dissertation, or as a **review article** published in its own right. A **narrative review** selects and discusses studies according to the author's judgement; a **systematic review** finds, selects and appraises studies by an explicit, reproducible method, often with a statistical combination of results (a **meta-analysis**).
:::

::: theorem The jobs of a literature review {#rule-review-jobs}
A literature review in a thesis or research article should:

1. **map** the relevant research: the main findings, approaches and debates that bear on the question;
2. **synthesise** it: show how studies relate — where they agree, conflict, build on each other or differ in method;
3. **evaluate** it: judge how strong the evidence is and where it is weak;
4. **position** the present study: show the gap, problem or open question it addresses, and justify its approach;
5. **define** the key concepts and the theoretical framework the study uses.
:::

::: proof
These jobs follow from the review's place in the research argument. An introduction establishes a territory and a niche before presenting the study ([[academic-writing/research-questions#rule-introduction]]); a literature review is that same argument at greater length. Jobs 1 and 2 establish the territory; jobs 3 and 4 establish the niche, because a gap is visible only to a reader who knows what has been done and how well; job 5 prepares for the method. Boote and Beile (2005), examining dissertations in education, argued that weak literature reviews are among the commonest weaknesses of doctoral research, and that a good review requires the researcher to understand and evaluate a field, not merely to report it. A review that only maps (job 1) leaves the reader asking why this study is needed.
:::

Kwan (2006), studying the literature review chapters of doctoral theses in applied linguistics, found that most had an introduction, a body and a conclusion, and that the body was divided into thematic sections each of which tended to repeat a cycle much like the moves of an introduction: establishing what is known about one theme, then pointing to a gap or a problem in it, and sometimes relating that gap to the present study. A literature review chapter is thus a series of small arguments, each leading towards the study.

## Summary and synthesis

::: definition Synthesis {#def-synthesis}
**Synthesis** (综合) is combining information and ideas from several sources into a new whole organised by the writer's question: grouping sources by what they show, relating them to one another, and drawing conclusions that no single source states. It contrasts with **serial summary**, in which sources are described one after another.
:::

::: theorem Writing a synthesis paragraph {#rule-synthesis}
1. **Open with your own claim** about the research, not with a source: *Evidence on X is mixed*, *Most studies of X have used Y*, *Two explanations of X have been proposed*.
2. **Group sources** that support the same point in one citation, and contrast groups with each other: *Studies of A have found …, whereas studies of B …*.
3. **Relate sources explicitly**, with words that say how: *similarly*, *in contrast*, *building on*, *extending this to*, *however*, *this may explain why*.
4. **Give detail only where it matters**: the design, sample or setting of a study when that explains its result or its weight.
5. **Evaluate** as you go: say which evidence is stronger and why.
6. **End with the point** of the paragraph — often a conclusion about the state of knowledge or a gap — rather than with the last source.
:::

::: proof
Point 1 makes the paragraph the writer's: the topic sentence states a synthesis that the citations then support, as in any argumentative paragraph ([[essay-writing/arguing-a-point#rule-paragraph-shape]]). Points 2 and 3 are what turn summaries into relations, and they are the part that serial summary leaves to the reader. Point 4 prevents the opposite fault, a paragraph so compressed that the reader cannot see why two studies disagree; the details that matter are those that explain differences. Point 5 is job 3 of [[#rule-review-jobs]] done locally, and point 6 keeps each paragraph moving towards the gap. A quick test: if you could reorder the sentences of a paragraph without loss, it is probably a list; in a synthesis, each sentence depends on the one before.
:::

::: example From serial summary to synthesis {#ex-synthesis}
Rewrite this fictional serial summary as a synthesis paragraph. *Patel (2016) interviewed 20 teachers and found that they used English for instructions but Chinese for explanations. Ruiz (2018) observed 12 lessons and found the same pattern. Huang (2020) surveyed 300 teachers; 70% said they switched to Chinese to explain grammar. Morgan (2022) argued that this switching is a useful scaffolding strategy, not a failure of English-only policy.*
::: solution
**Find the claim.** All three empirical studies point to one pattern — English for instructions, Chinese for explanations — found by different methods. Morgan offers an interpretation of it.

**A synthesis:** *Teachers in English-medium classrooms appear to divide their languages by function, using English for instructions and switching to Chinese for explanations. This pattern has been found in interviews (Patel, 2016), in classroom observation (Ruiz, 2018) and in a large survey, in which 70% of 300 teachers reported switching to explain grammar (Huang, 2020). The consistency across methods strengthens the finding, although all three studies relied on small or self-selected samples. Morgan (2022) interprets such switching as scaffolding rather than as a failure to follow English-only policy, but how far it helps students' learning has not yet been tested.*

**What changed.** The paragraph opens with the writer's claim (hedged with *appear*, since the evidence is consistent but limited); the three studies are grouped and distinguished by method, which is the detail that matters; there is evaluation (*consistency across methods strengthens*, *small or self-selected samples*); Morgan is cited integrally because the interpretation is hers; and the paragraph ends with a gap. (The criticism of the samples assumes something not stated in the original — in a real review it would need to be true of the studies.)
:::
:::

## Organising a review

::: theorem Choosing an organisation {#rule-organisation}
Organise a literature review by the question, using one of these patterns or a combination:

1. **thematic**: sections on the main themes, variables or sub-questions (the most common and usually the best);
2. **methodological**: sections on studies using different approaches, when the method explains the findings;
3. **chronological**: the development of thinking over time, when the history of the question matters to the argument (a theory and its revisions);
4. **by debate**: sections on competing positions, when the field is divided;
5. **from general to specific**: from the broad field to the studies closest to yours, ending at the gap.

Avoid organising by **source** (one paragraph per study) and by **country** (*research abroad*, *research in China*) unless the country is itself a variable in the question.
:::

::: proof
Each pattern suits a different question, and the review's purpose is to answer the question. A thematic organisation (point 1) mirrors the sub-questions of the study, so the review prepares directly for the method and the discussion. A methodological organisation is the right choice when, as in the homework example, different designs give different answers. A chronological one is easily overused: it tends to become serial summary in date order, and it is justified only when the development of ideas is part of the argument. Point 5 is less a pattern than a direction that works within any of the others, and it matches the funnel shape of the introduction. The warning about countries is discussed in the note on English and Chinese below.
:::

::: example Outlining a review {#ex-outline}
A master's student is studying *how Chinese university students use machine translation when writing in English*. Her notes cover 30 sources: studies of machine translation quality; surveys of students' use of it; experiments on whether it helps writing; teachers' policies; and two theoretical papers on translation as a learning strategy. Draft an outline for her literature review chapter (about 3,000 words).
::: solution
**2 Literature review**

2.1 *Introduction* (150 words) — scope of the chapter and its organisation.

2.2 *Translation as a strategy in second-language writing* (500) — the two theoretical papers; key concepts (defining *machine translation* and *use*). Prepares the framework.

2.3 *How students use machine translation* (800) — the surveys, grouped by what they show: how often, for which tasks, at which stages of writing. Evaluation: mostly self-report.

2.4 *Effects on writing* (800) — the experiments, grouped by outcome (accuracy, vocabulary, revision); note that short-term lab tasks dominate.

2.5 *Teachers' and institutions' responses* (400) — policies, briefly, as context.

2.6 *Summary and gap* (350) — what is known; what is not: how students actually use the tools while writing real assignments (observed, not reported) — the gap her study, which uses screen recordings, fills.

**Why this order.** Thematic, moving from concepts to the studies closest to her own (2.3–2.4), ending at the gap. The sources on translation quality are used selectively inside 2.4 rather than given their own section, because her question is about students, not about the tools.
:::
:::

## Evaluating and positioning

A review that only reports leaves the reader without a guide. Evaluation means judging the evidence as a reader would ([[academic-reading/evaluating-sources]]) and saying so in the text: which studies are stronger and why, where methods limit what can be concluded, where studies conflict and which explanation is better supported.

::: theorem The language of evaluation {#rule-evaluation-language}
1. **Evaluate the evidence, not the people**: *this study's sample was small*, not *the author was careless*.
2. **Be specific**: name the limitation (sample, design, measure, setting, analysis) and its consequence (*which makes it difficult to separate the effect of X from Y*).
3. **Hedge your criticism** to match its strength: *may overestimate*, *does not appear to account for*, *it is not clear whether*.
4. **Acknowledge strengths** where they exist, especially of studies you go on to criticise: *Although the study was carefully designed, it …*.
5. **Use evaluative words precisely**: *robust*, *consistent*, *limited*, *preliminary*, *inconclusive*, *well established*, *contested*.
:::

::: proof
Academic criticism is conventionally impersonal and moderated, for the reasons that make academic writers hedge in general ([[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging#rule-why-hedge]]): accuracy, protection and politeness towards a community that includes the authors you criticise. Points 1 and 2 also make criticism more convincing: *the sample was small* can be checked, *the study is weak* cannot. Point 4 is both fair and strategic, since it shows that the criticism is not dismissal. The evaluative vocabulary of point 5 is the shorthand of the genre: *preliminary* says that results are early and need confirmation, *inconclusive* that the evidence points no clear way, *contested* that researchers disagree.
:::

::: example Evaluating a study in a review {#ex-evaluate}
A fictional study reports that students who used a vocabulary app for eight weeks scored 20% higher on a vocabulary test than a comparison class. The app group volunteered; the comparison class was another teacher's class; the test was designed by the app's developers. Write two or three sentences that report and evaluate the study for a literature review.
::: solution
**A model:** *Ito and Barros (2021) reported that students who used a vocabulary app for eight weeks scored 20% higher on a vocabulary test than a comparison class. The result is promising, but the design limits what it shows: the app group was self-selected, the classes had different teachers, and the test was developed by the app's makers, so motivation, teaching and the fit between test and app may each account for part of the difference.*

**What the evaluation does.** It reports the finding accurately first; acknowledges its interest (*promising*); names three specific limitations; and states their consequence — alternative explanations — with a hedge (*may each account for part*). It does not call the study *flawed* or the authors *biased*: the reader can draw conclusions from the facts given.
:::
:::

The review ends — and each of its thematic sections may end — by stating what is not yet known. Gaps can be of several kinds: a **population** or **setting** not yet studied; a **method** not yet applied (no longitudinal study, no experiment, no observation of real behaviour); a **variable** not yet considered; a **conflict** between studies not yet explained; a **theory** not yet tested; or an **application** not yet made. State the gap as precisely as the research question it leads to, and modestly ([[academic-writing/research-questions#rule-introduction]], point 5).

::: example From review to research question {#ex-gap}
A review of research on peer feedback in second-language writing concludes: *studies agree that students revise after peer feedback, mostly in short experimental tasks; almost all have been conducted with undergraduates in English-speaking countries; and few have followed students' revisions across a whole course*. Write the final two sentences of the review, stating the gap and leading to the study's question.
::: solution
**A model:** *Peer feedback, then, appears to prompt revision, but the evidence comes largely from short experimental tasks with undergraduates in English-speaking countries, and little is known about how students use peer comments over the course of a real writing module. The present study therefore follows 45 first-year students in an English-medium university in China through one semester to examine which peer comments they take up in their assignments, and how this changes over time.*

**Why it works.** The first sentence summarises the synthesis (*then* signals a conclusion), keeps the hedge (*appears*), and states the gap precisely: setting (not English-speaking countries), duration (a semester, not a task) and material (real assignments). The second presents a study that addresses each of those points, so the reader sees the research question as the logical next step.
:::
:::

::: warning Reviewing everything
✗ A dissertation on vocabulary learning apps whose literature review begins with the history of computers in education, surveys theories of second language acquisition from the 1960s, and reaches apps on page twelve.

✓ A review that begins with the research on learning vocabulary from apps and similar tools, introduces theory where it explains those findings, and reaches the gap within a few pages.

A literature review is not a demonstration of how much the writer has read. It covers what the reader needs in order to understand the study and accept that it was worth doing. Sources that do not bear on the question, however interesting, belong in the notes, not the review.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence would best end a thematic section of a literature review?
- [ ] *Wang (2020) also studied this topic.*
- [ ] *There are many studies on this topic.*
- [x] *These studies agree that feedback improves accuracy in the short term; whether the gains last beyond a few weeks has rarely been tested.*
- [ ] *In conclusion, feedback is very important for all learners.*
::: solution
The third sentence summarises what the section has shown (a synthesis) and states a precise gap, which leads towards the study. The first ends with one more source; the second says nothing; the fourth is a vague overgeneralisation that the review cannot support.
:::
:::

## Tense in a literature review

The tense of a review sentence signals how the writer relates to the research it reports, following the tendencies set out in [[academic-writing/citation-referencing#rule-reporting]]:

- **present perfect** for an area of research as a whole, up to now: *Several studies **have examined** …*; *Little attention **has been paid** to …* — common in topic sentences and in statements of gaps;
- **past simple** for what a specific study did and found: *Okafor (2018) **found** a small effect*;
- **present simple** for current knowledge, the writer's own synthesis, and what a text argues: *Evidence on homework **is** mixed*; *Singh (2021) **argues** …*.

A change of tense in a paragraph is therefore meaningful. In *Several studies have found that homework helps older pupils. Okafor (2018), for example, found a benefit at ages 10–11. These results suggest that age is important*, the paragraph moves from the field (present perfect) to one study (past) to the writer's interpretation (present).

::: quiz
In *Several studies have found that X. Lee (2019), for example, found Y. These findings suggest that Z*, why does the tense change in the second sentence?
- [ ] because Lee's study is older than the others
- [x] because it reports a specific completed study rather than the field as a whole
- [ ] because Lee's finding is doubtful
- [ ] it is a mistake; all three sentences should be in the same tense
::: solution
The present perfect presents research up to now as a whole; the past simple reports what one particular study did; the present returns to the writer's current interpretation. The tense shifts are meaningful, not inconsistent.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **国内外研究现状.** Chinese theses and proposals often organise the literature as *research abroad* (国外研究现状) and *research in China* (国内研究现状). English-language reviews are organised by themes or questions, and studies from all countries appear together where they bear on the same point. If the country matters — because the setting changes the result — it becomes a theme or a point of comparison, not the framework.
- **XX认为 chains.** A review written as a chain of 某某（年份）认为……, 某某（年份）指出…… becomes a serial summary in English (*X (2015) believes … Y (2017) points out …*). Turning each chain into a claim supported by grouped citations ([[#rule-synthesis]]) is the main change.
- **综述 and 述评.** Chinese distinguishes 综述 (a review that surveys) and 述评 (a review that surveys and comments). English *literature review* expects both: description without evaluation is considered incomplete.
- **Criticising published work.** Some writers feel that criticising published or senior scholars is presumptuous. In English academic writing, specific, hedged and fair evaluation of evidence is expected of every writer, students included; it is not a personal criticism. The language of [[#rule-evaluation-language]] keeps it respectful.
- **The gap claim.** *国内尚无相关研究* ('there is not yet any related research in China') is a common claim; translated, it can sound like *no one has studied this*, which a reviewer may know to be false. State precisely what has not been studied, and hedge (*to our knowledge*, *few studies have …*).
:::

::: history
Reviewing the literature is as old as research, but the methods of reviewing became an object of study only in the late twentieth century. In *Effectiveness and Efficiency* (1972), the epidemiologist Archie Cochrane criticised medicine for not organising the results of its trials systematically; the Cochrane Collaboration, founded in 1993 and named after him, made the systematic review central to evidence-based medicine. Reporting guidelines followed: the PRISMA statement for systematic reviews appeared in 2009 and was updated in 2020. For narrative reviews in theses, Chris Hart's *Doing a Literature Review* (1998) became a standard guide; David Boote and Penny Beile's article "Scholars before researchers" (2005) argued that doctoral students must master the literature of their field before researching it; and Swales and Feak's *Telling a Research Story: Writing a Literature Review* (2009) applied genre analysis to reviews.
:::

## Where this leads

The gap at the end of a review becomes the research question ([[academic-writing/research-questions]]) and justifies the method ([[academic-writing/methods-results]]). The studies reviewed return in the discussion, where the results are compared with them ([[academic-writing/discussion-conclusions]]). Synthesis tables and summaries are in [[academic-reading/notes-and-summaries]], and grouping citations in [[academic-writing/citation-referencing#rule-integral]].

::: summary
- A **literature review** maps, synthesises and evaluates research on a question and positions the present study; it may be part of an introduction, a thesis chapter or a review article ([[#def-literature-review]], [[#rule-review-jobs]]).
- **Synthesis** organises sources by the writer's question and relates them; **serial summary** lists them one by one ([[#def-synthesis]]).
- A synthesis paragraph opens with **the writer's claim**, **groups** sources, **relates** them explicitly, gives **detail that matters**, **evaluates**, and ends with **the point** ([[#rule-synthesis]]).
- Organise **thematically** (or by method, debate or chronology when the question needs it), not by source or by country ([[#rule-organisation]]).
- **Evaluate** evidence specifically, impersonally, with hedges and with acknowledgement of strengths ([[#rule-evaluation-language]]).
- Lead to a precise, modestly stated **gap**: a population, setting, method, variable, conflict, theory or application not yet studied.
- **Tense** signals the relation: present perfect for the field, past for specific studies, present for current knowledge and the writer's synthesis.
:::

Possible answers: *Although students say they prefer recorded lectures, there is little evidence so far that recordings change what they learn.* or *Research on recorded lectures suggests a gap between students' preferences and their learning outcomes.* A good topic sentence states a relation between the findings, not just the topic (*There are several studies on recorded lectures*).

Possible answers: (a) *Ahmed's (2020) sample of 15 participants limits how far the findings can be generalised.* (b) *Chen's (2019) analysis does not appear to account for participants' age, which may explain part of the effect.* (c) *Because the study relied on self-report, the results may be affected by social desirability bias.* Each names a specific limitation and its consequence, criticises the evidence rather than the person, and hedges appropriately.

::: exercise Reorganise an outline {level=2}
A student's literature review on *bilingualism and executive function* has these sections: *2.1 Studies in the 1990s; 2.2 Studies in the 2000s; 2.3 Studies in the 2010s; 2.4 Studies in China.* Propose a better organisation, given that the main debate is whether the bilingual advantage is real, and that studies differ in their tasks and in how they define bilingualism.
::: solution
A thematic and debate-based organisation, for example: *2.1 Executive function and how it is measured; 2.2 Evidence for a bilingual advantage; 2.3 Failures to replicate and the current debate; 2.4 Why studies differ: tasks, samples and definitions of bilingualism; 2.5 Summary and gap.* Chronology can survive inside 2.2–2.3, where the debate developed over time. Studies from China are placed wherever they bear on each theme; if Chinese–English bilinguals are the study's population, the gap section can note how far findings for other language pairs apply to them.

::: exercise Write a synthesis paragraph {level=3}
Write a synthesis paragraph of about 150 words from these fictional notes. *Grant (2017): survey, 900 secondary pupils, UK — pupils with more screen time before bed reported later sleep. Mori (2019): experiment, 60 university students, Japan — one week without phones after 10 p.m., bedtime 30 minutes earlier on average. Silva (2020): survey, 2,000 adults, Brazil — association between bedtime phone use and later sleep, stronger for social media than for reading. Zhang (2022): review of 15 studies — consistent association; few experiments; most studies on adolescents.*
::: hint
Decide the claim first. Which studies support it, which is the strongest evidence, and what is the gap?
:::
::: solution
A model: *Evening use of screens is consistently associated with later sleep, in adolescents (Grant, 2017) and adults (Silva, 2020) alike, and a review of fifteen studies found the association across most of them (Zhang, 2022). Whether screens cause later sleep is harder to establish, since almost all of this evidence comes from surveys. The one experiment among these studies, in which university students gave up their phones after 10 p.m. for a week, found bedtimes on average 30 minutes earlier (Mori, 2019), which suggests that at least part of the association may be causal. Silva's (2020) finding that the association was stronger for social media than for reading points to a further question: whether particular kinds of screen activity matter more than screen time as such. As Zhang (2022) notes, experiments remain few, and most research has involved adolescents.* Check for a claim-first opening, grouped citations, evaluation (surveys vs experiment), relations between sources, and an ending that points to gaps.

Choose a research question of your own and five to eight sources you have read. Prepare a synthesis table, draw a map of sources and themes (on paper or with the figure in this chapter), and write a literature review of 600–800 words with at least two thematic sections, ending with a gap that leads to your question.
