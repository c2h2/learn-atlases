Here are two versions of the same paragraph from a student's report on a small study. The study is fictional.

> **Draft.** *Nowadays more and more people are using their phones to read, and this is a really big thing for universities. We wanted to find out if it makes a difference. So we got 40 students to read a text on paper and the same text on a phone, and then we tested them. Basically the paper ones did a lot better, which shows that phones are bad for reading.*
>
> **Revision.** *Undergraduates increasingly read course materials on mobile phones, yet little is known about how screen size affects their comprehension of academic texts. In this study, 40 first-year students read one expository text on paper and a comparable text on a phone, in counterbalanced order, and then answered ten comprehension questions on each. Mean scores were higher after reading on paper (7.4 out of 10) than after reading on a phone (6.1). This difference suggests that, for texts of this length, small screens may make comprehension harder.*

Nothing in the revision is grand. What has changed is that every vague word has been replaced by something a reader can check: *more and more people* by *undergraduates*, *a text* by *one expository text*, *tested them* by *ten comprehension questions*, *a lot better* by two means. The claim at the end has shrunk to match what one study of forty students can show. And the voice has changed: the draft tells the story of what *we* did, in the order it happened, as if speaking; the revision states the problem first, then the procedure, then the result, as the reader of a report expects.

That is what this chapter means by **academic style**: not a special vocabulary, and not complexity for its own sake, but choices that make a text precise, economical and fair to its evidence, in the form readers in a discipline are used to. Vocabulary is treated in [[collocation-and-register/academic-vocabulary]], register in general in [[collocation-and-register/register]], and the hedges that adjust the strength of a claim in [[modality/hedging-boosting]]; here they are brought together in the writer's decisions.

## What academic style is

::: definition Academic style {#def-academic-style}
**Academic style** (学术文体, 学术语体) is the set of linguistic choices that readers of research writing in a discipline expect: precise and consistent terms, claims whose strength matches the evidence, information packed into noun phrases, a formal register, and a conventional way of presenting the writer — as *I*, *we*, *this study* or not at all. It is not one fixed style: the conventions of a paper in physics, a thesis in history and a report in nursing differ, and each is learned by reading the texts of the field.
:::

Swales and Feak (*Academic Writing for Graduate Students*, 3rd edition, 2012) begin their course with six considerations that shape every academic text: **audience**, **purpose and strategy**, **organisation**, **style**, **flow** and **presentation**. Style comes after audience and purpose: a sentence is "academic" because it serves a reader who knows the field, wants the point quickly and will check the claims.

::: theorem Write for an expert reader who will check {#rule-expert-reader}
In academic writing, assume a reader who:

1. **knows the field** in general but not your study, so needs no explanation of common terms but does need every specific detail of what you did;
2. **reads to use** your work — to cite it, build on it, criticise it — and so wants the main point of each paragraph early and in a form that can be quoted;
3. **will test every claim** against your evidence and against other studies, so that a claim stronger than the evidence costs you more than a claim slightly weaker than it;
4. **does not share your situation**: they cannot ask what *this*, *recently* or *a lot* means, so the text must say.
:::

::: proof
Each point corresponds to a feature of academic style. Point 1 explains why technical terms go undefined while procedures are reported in detail; point 2, why paragraphs open with their claim and articles have predictable sections ([[academic-reading/academic-texts#def-imrad]]); point 3, why every claim is hedged to fit its evidence or attributed to a source. Point 4 is the difference between writing and conversation that Biber (1988) called "informational" against "involved" production ([[collocation-and-register/register#rule-mode]]): a writer addressing distant readers must make meaning explicit in the words.
:::

## Precision

The first thing that separates a student draft from a published paper is rarely grammar. It is the number of words that the reader cannot check.

::: theorem Make every claim checkable {#rule-precision}
1. **Replace vague quantities with numbers or defined terms**: *a lot*, *many*, *big*, *a few* → *63%*, *a large effect (d = 0.9)*, *three of the twelve schools*.
2. **Replace vague time words with dates or periods**: *nowadays*, *recently*, *in the past* → *since 2015*, *in the last decade*, *before 1990*.
3. **Name the population, not "people"**: *people* → *adult learners of English in Shanghai*, *patients over 65*, *first-year undergraduates*.
4. **Use one term for one concept, consistently.** Once you have chosen *participants*, do not switch to *subjects*, *students* and *respondents* for the same people.
5. **Define a term when the field uses it in more than one way**, and then keep to your definition.
6. **Prefer the plain precise word to the impressive vague one**: *use* rather than *utilise*, *show* rather than *evidence*, *about* rather than *in the region of*.
:::

::: proof
Points 1–3 follow from [[#rule-expert-reader]]: a reader who will check a claim needs to know exactly what it covers. *Many students preferred the new system* cannot be tested or compared with another study; *41 of the 60 students (68%) preferred the new system* can. Point 4 surprises many writers, who were taught at school to vary their vocabulary to avoid repetition. In academic prose, a change of word signals a change of meaning: a reader who meets *students* in one sentence and *learners* in the next will reasonably wonder whether these are the same group. Fowler's *Modern English Usage* (1926) called the habit of changing words merely for variety "elegant variation" and warned against it, and Swales and Feak (2012) make the same point about technical terms. Point 6 is a tendency, not an absolute: technical vocabulary is often exactly the precise word. A long word is simply not more academic because it is long.
:::

::: warning Elegant variation
✗ *Forty **participants** took part. The **subjects** were aged 18–25, and most of the **students** were women. Each **respondent** completed two tasks.*

✓ *Forty **participants** took part. The **participants** were aged 18–25, and most were women. Each **participant** completed two tasks.*

In the first version a reader cannot tell whether *subjects*, *students* and *respondents* are the same forty people or subsets of them. Where repetition becomes heavy, use a pronoun or leave the noun out (*most were women*), not a synonym.
:::

::: example Making a paragraph precise {#ex-precise}
Revise this fictional paragraph so that every claim is checkable. Invent plausible details where the original is vague, and say what each change does. *These days lots of companies let people work from home. Some research says this is good for them, but other research says it isn't. We did a survey of quite a few workers in a big firm and found that most of them were happier at home.*
::: solution
**A revision:** *Since 2020, many employers have allowed staff to work from home for part of the week. Studies of the effects on employees' well-being have reached conflicting conclusions. We surveyed 312 employees of one logistics company in Guangzhou; 214 of them (69%) reported higher job satisfaction on days worked from home.*

**Changes:**
- *These days* → *Since 2020*: a date the reader can relate to events.
- *lots of companies let people work from home* → *many employers have allowed staff to work from home for part of the week*: *many* is still general, but it is now a background statement that a citation would support; *for part of the week* makes "working from home" precise.
- *quite a few workers in a big firm* → *312 employees of one logistics company in Guangzhou*: number, kind of firm, place.
- *most of them were happier* → *214 of them (69%) reported higher job satisfaction*: the number, and *reported* makes clear that this is what people said, not a measurement of happiness.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is best suited to the results section of a report on a survey of 85 nurses in two hospitals?
- [ ] *Nurses nowadays are under a lot of stress.*
- [ ] *Nurses are extremely stressed by their working conditions.*
- [x] *Of the 85 nurses surveyed, 52 (61%) reported high levels of work-related stress.*
- [ ] *Many of the nurses we spoke to said that they were stressed out by work.*
::: solution
The third sentence gives the number, the percentage and the population, and *reported* says what was measured. The first is vague (*nowadays*, *a lot*) and generalises to all nurses; the second is an emotional overgeneralisation; the fourth is vague (*many*) and informal (*stressed out*).
:::
:::

## Nominalisation: when to pack, when to unpack

::: definition Nominalisation and grammatical metaphor {#def-nominalisation}
A **nominalisation** (名词化) expresses a process or a quality as a noun: *analyse* → *analysis*, *increase* → *an increase*, *rapid* → *rapidity*, *the samples were analysed* → *the analysis of the samples*. Halliday called this kind of shift **grammatical metaphor**: meanings that are "congruently" expressed by verbs and adjectives are expressed by nouns instead (Halliday and Martin, *Writing Science*, 1993). Reading nominalisations is taught in [[academic-reading/dense-sentences#def-nominalisation]]; this section is about using them.
:::

Biber and Gray (*Grammatical Complexity in Academic English*, 2016) showed from corpora that academic writing is not, as often assumed, a style of long subordinate clauses: its complexity lies in **phrases** — nouns modified by nouns (*language policy reform*) and chains of prepositional phrases (*an increase in the number of students from rural areas*). Compression is efficient for an expert reader, but it has a cost: a compressed noun phrase leaves out the relations that a clause would state. *Student language attitudes* could be students' attitudes to languages or attitudes to the language of students.

::: theorem Use nominalisation for a purpose {#rule-nominalise}
**Nominalise** when it helps the reader:

1. to **refer back** to an action or finding as a single idea, so that it can become the subject of the next sentence: *Prices rose by 12%. **This rise** was larger than expected.*
2. to make a process the **topic** of a sentence, so that you can say something about it: ***The translation of technical terms** remains the main difficulty.*
3. when the noun is the field's **technical term**: *evaporation*, *inflation*, *code-switching*.

**Unpack** (use a verb with a subject) when:

4. **who did it matters**: ✗ *There was a failure to consider the effects.* ✓ *The committee did not consider the effects.*
5. a **weak verb** carries a nominalisation that could itself be the verb: *carry out an analysis of* → *analyse*; *make a comparison between* → *compare*; *give consideration to* → *consider*;
6. **several nominalisations are chained** with *of* and the reader must reconstruct who did what: ✗ *the evaluation of the effectiveness of the implementation of the policy* ✓ *how well the policy worked once it was introduced*.
:::

::: proof
Points 1 and 2 are the main reason nominalisation exists in academic prose. A clause states an event; a noun phrase turns it into a thing that can be talked about, compared, measured and cited. *This rise* in point 1 packs the whole previous sentence into two words and links the two sentences, a pattern Halliday and Martin (1993) showed to be central to scientific argument: a finding is stated as a clause, then named as a noun, then used as the starting point for the next claim. Points 4–6 are the costs. A nominalisation can hide the actor (point 4), which is sometimes useful but often evasive; Williams and Bizup (*Style*) build their whole approach to clear writing on restoring characters as subjects and actions as verbs ([[style-and-rhetoric/clarity]]). Point 5 is the commonest fault in student writing: the weak verb adds nothing, and the sentence becomes longer without becoming more precise. Point 6 is the limit of compression: past two or three nouns, the reader starts to lose the relations.
:::

::: example Packing for cohesion {#ex-pack}
Join these two fictional sentences so that the second refers back to the first with a nominalisation, and explain the gain. *(1) Between 2010 and 2020 the number of international students at the university doubled. (2) Because the number doubled, the university needed more teachers of academic English.*
::: solution
**Revision:** *Between 2010 and 2020 the number of international students at the university doubled. **This growth** created a need for more teachers of academic English.*

**The gain.** The second sentence no longer repeats the first; *This growth* names the whole event of sentence 1 and makes it the subject of sentence 2, so that the new information (*a need for more teachers*) comes at the end, where readers expect it ([[style-and-rhetoric/cohesion-emphasis]]). The causal link, which the draft stated with *Because*, is now carried by the verb *created*. Choose the noun with care: *This problem* would add a judgement that the original sentence did not make.
:::
:::

## The passive and the active

The passive is a feature of academic writing, especially in the methods sections of science, but it is not a rule of it. Corpus studies such as the *Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English* (Biber et al. 1999) found passives far more common in academic prose than in conversation or fiction; even so, most finite verbs in academic prose are active. The passive is a tool for a purpose.

::: theorem Choose the voice by the topic of the sentence {#rule-voice}
1. Use the **passive** when the actor is unknown, obvious or unimportant, and the thing acted on is the topic: *The samples were stored at −20 °C.* (Who stored them does not matter.)
2. Use the passive to keep the **topic** of a paragraph in subject position: *The questionnaire contained 20 items. It **was piloted** with 12 students and **revised** before use.*
3. Use the **active with *we* or *I*** where your field accepts it and the actor's choice matters: *We excluded responses completed in under two minutes.* Many style guides now prefer this to the passive (APA style, 7th edition, encourages the first person for describing one's own procedures).
4. Use the **active with a non-human subject** to state findings and relations: *The results show …*, *Table 2 compares …*, *This model predicts …*.
5. **Do not** use the passive to avoid responsibility for a claim or a decision: ✗ *It was decided to exclude the outliers.* ✓ *We excluded the outliers because …*.
:::

::: proof
English puts the topic of a sentence — what it is about — first, and the passive is mainly a way of making the thing acted on the topic. In a method section the topic is usually the materials and procedures, not the researchers, which is why passives cluster there; in a discussion the topic is the findings and the writer's interpretation, which is why active sentences with *we* or with *the results* as subject are more common. Point 5 is about honesty as well as style: *it was decided* hides a decision that a reader may want to question. The active also avoids dangling participles such as *Using this method, the samples were analysed* ([[complex-sentences/participle-infinitive-clauses]]).
:::

## *I*, *we* and the impersonal voice

Many students arrive at university having been told never to use *I* in academic writing. That instruction is a simplification. Hyland (2001), counting self-mention in research articles from eight disciplines, found it in all of them, and far more often in the humanities and social sciences — philosophy, sociology, marketing, applied linguistics — than in physics, biology and engineering. Tang and John (1999) showed that *I* in student essays can play quite different roles, from the modest *guide* (*In this essay I first describe …*) to the *opinion-holder* and the *originator* of an idea (*I argue that …*), and that the last two are the ones students most often avoid.

::: theorem Using the first person {#rule-first-person}
1. **Follow your field.** Read several recent articles in the journal or discipline you write for, and note whether authors write *I*, *we*, or neither. Your tutor's or the journal's guidelines override any general rule.
2. **Use *I* or *we* where you take responsibility**: for your own choices (*We chose a mixed design because …*), your own claims (*I argue that …*), and your own contribution (*We propose a new measure …*).
3. **Avoid *I* where it adds nothing**: ✗ *I think that the results show …* ✓ *The results show …*; ✗ *I found it interesting that …* ✓ *An unexpected finding was …*.
4. **Use *we* for co-authors, *I* for a single author** in most fields today. The "authorial *we*" for a single author is still found, especially in mathematics, but many guides now advise against it (APA style does).
5. **Use inclusive *we* (writer and reader together) sparingly**: *As we have seen …*, *If we consider …*. It builds solidarity in some fields and sounds informal or presumptuous in others.
6. **Impersonal alternatives** — *this paper*, *this study*, *the present analysis*, *it is argued here that* — are standard in many fields; use them consistently, not mixed with *I* in the same function.
:::

::: proof
The disciplinary differences in point 1 have a reason. In experimental sciences the claim to knowledge rests on procedures that anyone could repeat, so the identity of the researcher is played down; in the humanities a claim rests on the writer's interpretation and argument, so the writer's voice is part of the evidence. Hyland (2001, 2002) argued that self-mention is a way of claiming credit and taking responsibility, and found that student writers often avoid it exactly where experts use it — when stating their own claims. On the other side, *I think* weakens a claim as spoken hedges do ([[modality/hedging-boosting]]).
:::

::: quiz
A student writing a sociology dissertation drafts: *In this dissertation, it will be argued by the present author that the survey underestimates rural poverty.* Which revision is most appropriate for a field that accepts the first person?
- [ ] *It is thought that the survey may perhaps underestimate rural poverty.*
- [x] *In this dissertation I argue that the survey underestimates rural poverty.*
- [ ] *I personally feel that the survey kind of underestimates rural poverty.*
- [ ] *We all know that the survey underestimates rural poverty.*
::: solution
The second option states the writer's own claim directly and takes responsibility for it, as the original tried to do through the passive and *the present author*. The first adds hedges that change the claim, the third is informal (*personally feel*, *kind of*), and the fourth uses an inclusive *we* that presents a disputed claim as common knowledge.
:::
:::

::: example Choosing the voice of a paragraph {#ex-voice}
Revise this fictional paragraph from a dissertation in applied linguistics, where the first person is usual. Make the writer's responsibility clear where it matters and keep the passive where it is useful. *It was decided by the researcher that interviews would be used. Twelve teachers were interviewed. The interviews were recorded and transcribed. It is felt that the teachers' answers show that they lack training in assessment.*
::: solution
**A revision:** *I chose semi-structured interviews because they allow teachers to explain their practice in their own terms. Twelve teachers were interviewed; the interviews were recorded and transcribed in full. I argue that their answers point to a lack of training in assessment.*

**Reasons.** *It was decided by the researcher* hides a methodological choice that an examiner will want justified, so it becomes *I chose … because*. The procedural sentences (*were interviewed*, *recorded and transcribed*) keep the passive: the interviews are the topic, and who pressed the record button does not matter. *It is felt that … show* is a claim disguised as a feeling; *I argue that … point to* states it as the writer's interpretation, with a moderate verb (*point to* rather than *show*), since twelve interviews support but do not prove the claim.
:::
:::

## Formality and the zombie rules

Academic prose is formal, but formality is a matter of degree and of consistency ([[collocation-and-register/register#rule-consistent]]). Some features are avoided in nearly all academic writing; others are matters of taste that have been passed off as rules.

::: theorem Features to avoid in academic prose {#rule-formality}
In most academic writing, avoid:

1. **contractions**: *don't*, *it's*, *can't* → *do not*, *it is*, *cannot*;
2. **informal vocabulary and phrasal verbs that have a neutral equivalent**: *a lot of*, *get*, *kids*, *find out*, *look into* → *many*, *obtain* or *become*, *children*, *discover* or *determine*, *investigate* (many phrasal verbs, such as *carry out* and *set up*, are perfectly normal in academic prose);
3. **spoken discourse markers and vague endings**: *well*, *basically*, *you know*, *and so on*, *etc.* at the end of an open list;
4. **direct address to the reader with *you***, except in instructions and some teaching texts;
5. **rhetorical questions** as a substitute for a claim (*So is homework useful?*), and **exclamation marks**;
6. **emotional intensifiers**: *extremely*, *totally*, *amazing*, *huge*.
:::

Some "rules" that students meet are not rules of English at all. Academic writers do begin sentences with *But* and *And*, though *However* and *In addition* are more frequent; they split infinitives when the alternative is awkward or ambiguous (*to more than double*); they end sentences with prepositions (*the method we relied on*); and they use singular *they* for a person whose gender is not known or not relevant, as APA style recommends. These are matters of style for a particular journal or tutor, not of correctness.

::: warning Overformality
✗ *It is the intention of the present writer to endeavour to ascertain whether the utilisation of mobile devices is prejudicial to the comprehension of textual material.*

✓ *This study asks whether reading on mobile phones reduces comprehension.*

Trying to sound academic, many writers pile up long Latinate words (*endeavour*, *ascertain*, *utilisation*), weak verbs with nominalisations, and roundabout phrases. The result is harder to read and no more precise. The second sentence is still formal — no contractions, no informal words — and it says the same thing in nine words instead of twenty-three. Formality and plainness are compatible; formality and vagueness are not.
:::

## How style varies between disciplines

All of the rules above are stated as tendencies because the disciplines differ. Corpus studies give a consistent picture, summarised in the table below; it describes frequencies in published research articles, not rules for any one text.

| feature | more frequent in … | less frequent in … | evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| self-mention (*I*, *we*) | philosophy, sociology, marketing, applied linguistics | physics, biology, engineering | Hyland 2001, 2005 |
| hedges | humanities and social sciences | natural sciences and engineering | Hyland 1998, 2005 |
| passive verbs | natural sciences, medicine, engineering (method sections above all) | humanities | Biber et al. 1999 |
| direct quotation | humanities and social sciences | natural sciences | Hyland 1999 |
| compressed noun phrases | science and medicine | humanities, which use more clauses | Biber and Gray 2016 |
| reporting verbs in past tense (*found*) | experimental fields | humanities (present: *argues*) | Swales and Feak 2012 |

The practical consequence is a method rather than a rule: before writing for an unfamiliar field, study three to five recent articles from your target journals as models — do the authors write *we*, which tense do they use for previous studies, how do they state findings? Swales and Feak (2012) recommend this kind of small genre analysis.

::: example A small genre analysis {#ex-genre-analysis}
A student of public health has three short extracts from the openings of results sections in a target journal (written for this lesson, but typical of the field). What conventions can she infer? *(A) Of the 1,204 adults invited, 862 (71.6%) completed the questionnaire. (B) Table 2 presents the characteristics of participants by smoking status. (C) Mean systolic blood pressure was higher in the intervention group at baseline (see Table 3).*
::: solution
**Tense.** Past simple for what happened and what was measured (*completed*, *was higher*); present for what the tables do (*Table 2 presents*).

**Voice and person.** No *we* in these extracts; non-human subjects (*862*, *Table 2*, *Mean systolic blood pressure*).

**Numbers.** Counts with percentages in parentheses; a sentence does not begin with a numeral (*Of the 1,204 adults …*).

**References to tables.** Either as a subject (*Table 2 presents*) or in parentheses (*see Table 3*). There is no interpretation: results sections report without explaining ([[academic-writing/methods-results]]).
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **本文, 笔者, 我们 and *I*, *we*.** Chinese academic articles often refer to the writer as 笔者 or to the text as 本文 or 本研究, and 我们 is also common, including in single-authored papers. Translated directly, these give *the author*, *this paper* and *we*. *This paper* and *this study* are normal in English; *the author* for oneself now sounds old-fashioned in most fields; and *we* for a single author is discouraged by many guides. Choose by the conventions of the English-language field, not by the Chinese habit.
- **进行 and 开展.** Chinese academic prose often uses a light verb with a verbal noun: 进行分析, 开展调查, 加以讨论. Translated word for word, these produce English weak-verb constructions — *carry out an analysis of*, *conduct an investigation into*, *make a discussion of* (the last is not idiomatic). English usually prefers the plain verb: *analyse*, *investigate*, *discuss* ([[#rule-nominalise]], point 5).
- **Openings.** Chinese essays and papers often open with a broad statement about the age or the development of society (随着社会的发展…, 近年来…), which translates as *With the development of society …* or *In recent years …*. English research writing usually opens closer to the specific topic and with a claim that can be supported ([[academic-writing/research-questions]]). The Chinese opening is not wrong in its own tradition; it is simply not what English readers expect.
- **Four-character expressions and set phrases.** Expressions such as 众所周知 and 不言而喻 become boosters in English (*as is well known*, *it goes without saying*) that claim more agreement than the writer may have. Use them, if at all, only for what really is common knowledge in the field.
:::

::: history
The plainness of scientific English was a programme before it was a habit. In his *History of the Royal Society* (1667), Thomas Sprat wrote that the Society's members had resolved to reject "amplifications, digressions, and swellings of style" and to prefer "a close, naked, natural way of speaking; positive expressions, clear senses; a native easiness", delivering "so many things, almost in an equal number of words". M. A. K. Halliday and J. R. Martin described how scientific English learned to turn processes into nouns in *Writing Science* (1993). Douglas Biber and Bethany Gray (2016) used corpora covering three centuries to show that the compressed noun-phrase style of modern science spread mainly in the twentieth century. At the same time, the impersonal ideal weakened: Ken Hyland's corpus studies from the late 1990s showed how much academic writers in fact present themselves, and many style guides now recommend the first person for authors' own actions.
:::

Academic style serves the planning and argument of a research text, the subject of the next chapter, [[academic-writing/research-questions]]. The tenses and voice of method and results sections are set out in [[academic-writing/methods-results]], and the hedging of interpretations in [[academic-writing/discussion-conclusions]]. For clarity and concision beyond academic writing — characters as subjects, cutting redundancy, putting new information last — see [[style-and-rhetoric/clarity]], [[style-and-rhetoric/concision]] and [[style-and-rhetoric/cohesion-emphasis]]. Academic vocabulary and its collocations are in [[collocation-and-register/academic-vocabulary]].

::: summary
- **Academic style** is a set of choices for an expert reader who will check every claim: precision, claims matched to evidence, compressed noun phrases, formal register and a conventional authorial voice ([[#def-academic-style]], [[#rule-expert-reader]]).
- **Precision** comes from numbers, dates, named populations and one term per concept; varying a key term for elegance confuses the reader ([[#rule-precision]]).
- **Nominalise** to refer back, to make a process the topic, or for technical terms; **unpack** when the actor matters, when a weak verb carries the noun, or when nouns pile up ([[#rule-nominalise]]).
- Choose the **passive** when the thing acted on is the topic, and the **active** with *we*, *I* or a non-human subject when responsibility or findings matter ([[#rule-voice]]).
- Use ***I* and *we*** as your field does, above all for your own choices and claims; avoid *I think* and inconsistent self-reference ([[#rule-first-person]]).
- Avoid contractions, informal words, spoken markers, *you*, rhetorical questions and emotional intensifiers — but beginning with *But* or splitting an infinitive is not an error ([[#rule-formality]]).
- Styles **differ by discipline**; learn yours by analysing a few recent articles from your target journals.
:::

::: exercise Formal register {level=1}
Rewrite each sentence in a formal academic register without changing its meaning. (a) *The results weren't what we expected.* (b) *We looked into why kids drop out of school.* (c) *There are loads of reasons for this, like money, family and so on.*
::: solution
(a) *The results were not as expected.* or *The results did not match our predictions.* (b) *We investigated why children leave school early.* (c) *There are several reasons for this, including financial and family circumstances.* (*and so on* is replaced by *including*, which marks the list as incomplete.)
:::

::: exercise Passive or active? {level=2}
For each sentence from a fictional method section, say whether the passive or the active with *we* is better, and why. (a) *The participants were randomly assigned to two groups.* (b) *It was decided to exclude three participants who did not complete the task.* (c) *The recordings were transcribed using standard conventions.* (d) *A new coding scheme was developed because existing schemes did not distinguish between the two kinds of error.*
::: solution
(a) Passive: the participants are the topic and the actor does not matter. (b) Active: *We excluded three participants who did not complete the task.* The exclusion is a decision the reader may question; *it was decided* hides it. (c) Passive: a routine procedure. (d) Either; the active (*We developed a new coding scheme because …*) makes the authors' contribution and responsibility clear, which matters because the scheme is new. In a field that avoids *we*, the passive is acceptable here.
:::

::: exercise Refer back with a noun {level=2}
Join each pair of fictional sentences by turning the first into a noun phrase that becomes the subject of the second. (a) *Unemployment rose sharply in 2009. It led to a fall in tax revenue.* (b) *The researchers changed the questionnaire after the pilot. This made the results difficult to compare with earlier surveys.*
::: solution
(a) *Unemployment rose sharply in 2009. This rise led to a fall in tax revenue.* (or *The sharp rise in unemployment in 2009 led to a fall in tax revenue.*) (b) *The researchers changed the questionnaire after the pilot. These changes made the results difficult to compare with earlier surveys.* Other suitable nouns: *This increase*; *This revision*. Avoid nouns that add a judgement not in the original (*This mistake*).
:::

::: exercise Consistent terms {level=2}
Find the elegant variation in this fictional paragraph and fix it. *The study examined code-switching among bilingual teenagers. The adolescents were recorded in conversation with friends. Language alternation occurred in 64% of the conversations, and the young people switched most often when discussing school. Mixing was rare in conversations with parents.*
::: solution
Two key terms vary. The people: *bilingual teenagers*, *adolescents*, *young people*. The phenomenon: *code-switching*, *language alternation*, *switched*, *mixing* — and in sociolinguistics *code-mixing* is sometimes defined differently from *code-switching*, so the variation may suggest a distinction the writer does not intend. A revision: *The study examined code-switching among bilingual teenagers. The teenagers were recorded in conversation with friends. Code-switching occurred in 64% of the conversations, most often when the participants discussed school. It was rare in conversations with parents.*
:::

::: exercise Revise a paragraph {level=3}
Revise this fictional paragraph from an undergraduate report in economics into academic style. Keep its content, make it precise (invent details where needed), and choose the voice suitably. *Nowadays loads of young people in China are buying stuff online. We wanted to find out why. So we did a questionnaire and got answers from quite a lot of students at our uni. It turns out that the main thing is that it's cheaper, which is pretty obvious really. But convenience was also really important!*
::: hint
List what a reader cannot check, then decide what the findings are and how strongly the data support them. The writer's own findings need no hedge; any explanation does.
:::
::: solution
**A model:** *Online shopping has grown rapidly among young adults in China. This study examined the reasons that university students give for buying online. A questionnaire was completed by 236 undergraduates at one university in Wuhan. The reason most often rated as "very important" was lower prices (selected by 71% of respondents), followed by convenience (58%).*

**Points to check:** *Nowadays*, *loads of*, *stuff*, *uni*, *quite a lot*, *pretty*, *really* and the exclamation mark are gone; the population (*undergraduates at one university in Wuhan*) and the numbers are given; the purpose is stated as the study's, not as *we wanted to find out*; *it turns out* and *which is pretty obvious* (an emotional comment that also dismisses the finding) are removed; the finding is reported as what respondents said (*the reason most often rated*), not as the truth about why young people shop online. In a real report the opening sentence would cite a source for the growth of online shopping.
:::

::: exercise Your field's conventions {level=3}
Choose three recent research articles from journals in your own field (or a field you hope to study). For each, find (a) whether the authors use *I* or *we*, and where; (b) the tense of the verbs used to report previous studies; (c) the first sentence of the results section; (d) one sentence with a compressed noun phrase of three or more nouns. Write a paragraph of about 150 words describing the conventions you found and one thing you will change in your own writing.
::: solution
Answers depend on the field. A good answer gives evidence from all three articles for each point rather than generalising from one, notices differences between sections (for example, *we* in the introduction and discussion but passives in the method), and states the conventions as tendencies (*two of the three articles …*). The paragraph should end with a specific decision — for example, *I will use* we *for our own procedures instead of the passive with* it was decided — rather than a general resolution to "write more academically".
