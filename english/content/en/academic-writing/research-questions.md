Zhou Min, a second-year student of education, tells her supervisor that her dissertation will be about *social media and young people's mental health*. Her supervisor asks three questions. *Which young people? What do you want to find out about them? And how would you know when you had found it?* Zhou Min goes away and comes back a week later with this:

> *How does the amount of time that first-year undergraduates at one university spend on short-video apps in the evening relate to their self-reported sleep quality and mood during the first semester?*

The new version is less exciting, and that is its strength. It names a population she can reach, two things she can measure, a relation she can test with a questionnaire and a sleep diary, and a period short enough for a dissertation. It also implies what she will **not** claim: nothing about all young people, about mental illness, or about whether short-video apps *cause* poor sleep. A topic tells a reader what a text is about; a research question tells the reader what it will find out, and so what kind of evidence it needs.

This chapter is about the step between the two: turning a topic into a question that can be researched, stating it as aims, questions or hypotheses, building the introduction of a paper around it, and planning the paper or dissertation that answers it. Narrowing the topic of an essay is taught in [[essay-writing/understanding-the-question#rule-narrow]]; a research question goes further, because the writer must also gather the evidence.

## From topic to question

::: definition Research question {#def-research-question}
A **research question** (研究问题) is the question a study is designed to answer. It states what will be investigated, in which population or material, and in what respect, precisely enough to decide what evidence would answer it. A study may have one main question and two or three subsidiary ones. Related terms are the **aim** (研究目的: what the study sets out to do, stated as a purpose), **objectives** (the concrete steps by which the aim is met) and a **hypothesis** (研究假设: a predicted answer that the study tests).
:::

Booth, Colomb and Williams (*The Craft of Research*, 4th edition, with Bizup and FitzGerald, 2016) describe the movement from topic to question to significance with a three-part sentence for writers to complete, roughly: *I am studying [a topic] because I want to find out [a question] in order to help my reader understand [why it matters].* The first slot is easy; the second forces a question; the third forces the writer to say why anyone else should care, which is what the introduction of the paper will have to argue.

::: theorem What makes a good research question {#rule-good-question}
A good research question is:

1. **focused**: it names the population or material, the phenomenon and the aspect studied (*first-year undergraduates*, *evening short-video use*, *sleep quality*);
2. **answerable with evidence you can get**: the data exist or can be collected, by methods you can use, within the time and word limit;
3. **open**: the answer is not already known or trivially yes; a yes/no question is acceptable only when either answer would matter;
4. **neutral**: it does not assume its own answer (✗ *Why does social media damage students' sleep?* assumes that it does);
5. **empirical or clearly argued**: questions of fact can be answered with data; questions of value (*Should universities ban phones in lectures?*) need an argument from stated criteria, and the question should say which;
6. **significant**: an answer would fill a gap, settle a disagreement, test a theory or inform a decision, for readers in a field.
:::

::: proof
Each criterion prevents a typical failure. A broad question (point 1) cannot be answered in a dissertation, so the writer ends up summarising what is known instead of finding something out. A question without accessible data (point 2) leads to a project that collapses half-way. Point 3 rules out questions whose answers are in any textbook; point 4 rules out studies designed to confirm what the writer already believes, which readers and examiners detect quickly. Point 5 is the distinction between *is* and *ought*: data can show what happens when phones are banned, but whether they should be banned also depends on values, which must be argued. Point 6 links the question to the literature: a question is significant in relation to what others have asked, which is why the question and the literature review are usually refined together ([[academic-writing/literature-review]]).
:::

### Kinds of question

Research questions fall into a few broad kinds, and the kind decides the design:

| kind | typical wording | needs |
|---|---|---|
| descriptive | *What …? How often …? How do teachers describe …?* | a survey, observation, interviews, a corpus |
| comparative | *Do A and B differ in …?* | two groups or conditions measured the same way |
| relational | *How is X related to Y?* | measures of both in one sample; shows association, not cause |
| causal | *Does X affect Y? What is the effect of X on Y?* | an experiment, or strong designs that rule out alternatives |
| explanatory or interpretive | *Why …? How do participants make sense of …?* | case studies, interviews, historical or textual analysis |
| evaluative | *How effective is …? To what extent does … achieve its aims?* | criteria stated in advance, and evidence against each |

The commonest mismatch is a causal question with a relational design: *Does short-video use reduce sleep quality?* asked of a one-off questionnaire. The data can show only that the two go together ([[academic-reading/evaluating-sources#def-correlation]]). Zhou Min's question says *relate to* for exactly this reason.

::: example Narrowing a topic into a question {#ex-narrow}
Turn the topic *English-medium instruction in Chinese universities* into a research question for a master's dissertation in applied linguistics that will take six months. Show the steps.
::: solution
**Step 1 — choose an aspect.** The topic contains many questions: policy, students' learning of content, students' English, teachers' English, attitudes. Choose one: how students cope with lectures in English.

**Step 2 — choose a population you can reach.** *Students at Chinese universities* is too broad. Choose *second-year engineering students on English-medium programmes at one university*, where the writer has access.

**Step 3 — choose what to find out.** *How students cope* is vague. Narrow to *the strategies students report using to understand English-medium lectures* — something interviews and a questionnaire can capture.

**Step 4 — decide the kind of question.** Descriptive, with a possible comparative part (stronger and weaker listeners).

**Step 5 — write it and check it against [[#rule-good-question]].** *What strategies do second-year engineering students on an English-medium programme at one Chinese university report using to understand lectures, and do these strategies differ between students with higher and lower listening proficiency?* Focused (one programme, one skill); answerable (questionnaire, listening test, interviews in six months); open; neutral (it does not assume that weaker students use *worse* strategies); empirical; significant if the literature has studied attitudes to English-medium instruction more than students' strategies — which the literature review must show.
:::
:::

::: warning Questions that assume their answer
✗ *Why is task-based teaching better than grammar teaching?*

✓ *How do the speaking scores of students taught with tasks and with grammar-focused lessons compare after one term?*

The first question presupposes the result, so the study can only look for reasons to agree. The second leaves the result open and says what will be measured. The same problem hides in words such as *problem*, *failure* and *impact*: *What is the impact of …?* assumes there is one. Write the question so that a reader who expects the opposite answer would accept it as fair.
:::

::: quiz
Which is the best research question for a one-semester undergraduate project?
- [ ] *What is the effect of globalisation on language?*
- [ ] *Why are online dictionaries better than paper dictionaries?*
- [x] *How often do first-year students of English at our university use online dictionaries when writing essays, and for what kinds of lookup?*
- [ ] *Should schools stop teaching handwriting?*
::: solution
The third question is focused (one group, one activity), answerable with a questionnaire or logs in a semester, open and neutral. The first is far too broad; the second assumes its answer; the fourth is a question of value that a one-semester project could not answer without first defining its criteria and evidence.
:::
:::

## Aims, questions and hypotheses

Research writing states its purpose in one of three main forms, often more than one at once. Fields differ: experimental psychology and medicine favour hypotheses; much education and social research uses questions; humanities research often states an aim or an argument (*This article argues that …*).

::: theorem Stating the purpose of a study {#rule-purpose}
1. **Aim**, as a purpose: *This study aims to identify / examine / compare …*; *The purpose of this study was to …* (past in a report of finished work, present in a proposal or in the paper's own terms: *This paper examines …*).
2. **Objectives**, as steps, usually numbered and beginning with verbs: *(1) to measure …; (2) to compare …; (3) to interview …*.
3. **Questions**, as direct questions, numbered if there are several: *RQ1. How …? RQ2. To what extent …?* In running text, use an indirect question: *This study asks whether …* (not ✗ *This study asks whether does …*).
4. **Hypotheses**, as statements of the expected result, with the direction if it is predicted: *H1. Students who receive feedback within a week will revise more extensively than those who receive it later.*
5. **Keep them consistent**: every question should be answered in the results or discussion, and nothing should be answered that was not asked.
:::

::: proof
The forms differ in commitment. An aim commits the writer to doing something; a question, to answering it; a hypothesis, to a prediction that the data may contradict. A hypothesis is the strongest form, appropriate when theory or earlier studies justify a prediction; using one without that basis looks like guessing. Point 3 deals with a common grammatical slip: indirect questions keep statement word order ([[complex-sentences/noun-clauses]]). Point 5 is what examiners check first: a dissertation that asks three questions and answers two, or whose conclusion answers a different question, signals that the question was never really the organising principle of the work.
:::

## Building the introduction around the question

The introduction of a research paper is an argument whose conclusion is the research question. Swales (1990) described its typical structure as three moves, the **CARS** model — establishing a territory, establishing a niche, occupying the niche — which [[academic-reading/academic-texts#def-cars]] teaches you to recognise as a reader. As a writer you use the same moves in reverse: you know your question, and the introduction has to make a reader see why it needed asking.

In *Research Genres* (2004), Swales revised the model in ways that matter for writers. Move 1 is now made through generalisations of increasing specificity, supported by citations throughout. Move 2 has two main forms: **indicating a gap** in earlier research and **adding to what is known** (extending earlier work to a new population or setting), with an optional justification of why the gap matters. Move 3, renamed **presenting the present work**, must announce the research descriptively or by its purpose and may add research questions or hypotheses, definitions, a summary of methods, the main findings, the value of the work, and an outline of the paper.

You can drag the boxes.
:::

::: theorem Writing an introduction {#rule-introduction}
1. **Start close to the topic.** Open with a claim about the specific area of research, supported by citations, not with a statement about the world in general.
2. **Narrow in steps**, each sentence more specific than the last, towards the point where earlier work stops.
3. **State the niche precisely**: name what is missing (a population, a method, a variable, a setting, a contradiction between studies) and, if it is not obvious, why it matters.
4. **Announce the study as the answer to the niche**, with the aim and the research questions or hypotheses, using a link such as *therefore* or *to address this gap*.
5. **Claim the gap honestly**: *little research has examined* or *few studies have* is usually safer than *no study has ever*, which a single counter-example refutes.
6. **Write or revise the introduction last**, when you know what the study found and claims.
:::

::: proof
Points 1–4 follow from the purpose of the introduction: each sentence moves the reader from what they know towards what the study will add, so that the research question arrives as the natural next step. Openings that start with the world in general (*Since the dawn of time …*, *In today's globalised world …*) waste the reader's attention and claim nothing that a citation could support. Point 5 is a matter of accuracy: a writer rarely knows the whole literature, and reviewers often know a study the writer missed. *To our knowledge, no study has …* is the conventional hedge when the claim is important. Point 6 is common advice in guides to research writing: the introduction promises what the paper delivers, and what the paper delivers is known only at the end. Many writers therefore draft the method section first, because it is the most concrete.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is the best opening for the introduction of a paper on the vocabulary of Chinese secondary-school English textbooks?
- [ ] *Since ancient times, language has been the most important tool of human communication.*
- [ ] *In today's globalised world, English is more and more important.*
- [x] *The vocabulary that a textbook introduces largely determines the words that learners meet in the classroom (Author, year).*
- [ ] *No one has ever studied textbook vocabulary before.*
::: solution
The third sentence starts close to the topic with a claim a citation could support, ready to be narrowed in the next sentences. The first two start from the world in general; the fourth is a gap claim that is almost certainly false and comes before any territory has been established.
:::
:::

Here is an introduction written for this lesson, for a fictional study based on Zhou Min's question.

> *(1) Sleep plays a central role in the academic performance and well-being of university students (Author A, year; Author B, year). (2) Students in their first year are particularly vulnerable to irregular sleep, as they adjust to new routines and greater independence (Author C, year). (3) A growing body of research has linked evening use of smartphones to later bedtimes and poorer sleep quality in adolescents and young adults (Author D, year; Author E, year). (4) Most of this research, however, has measured screen time in general, without distinguishing between activities. (5) Short-video apps, which present an endless sequence of brief clips, may be especially difficult to stop using, but their relation to students' sleep has received little attention. (6) The present study therefore examines how evening use of short-video apps relates to self-reported sleep quality and mood among first-year undergraduates at one university. (7) Specifically, it asks: (RQ1) How much time do students spend on short-video apps after 9 p.m.? (RQ2) How is this time related to their sleep quality and mood over the first semester?*

The citations are placeholders, because the sources would be the student's own; in a real paper every sentence of Move 1 that makes a claim about earlier research needs a real citation.

::: example Analysing your own introduction {#ex-intro-moves}
Label the moves and steps of the model introduction, and find the words that do the work of each.
::: solution
**Move 1 (sentences 1–3).** Sentence 1 claims the importance of the territory (*plays a central role*), with citations. Sentence 2 narrows to first-year students (*particularly vulnerable*). Sentence 3 reviews research nearest the study (*A growing body of research has linked …*); the present perfect presents earlier research as relevant now.

**Move 2 (sentences 4–5), indicating a gap.** *however* marks the turn; *has measured screen time in general, without distinguishing between activities* names a limitation of method. Sentence 5 gives a reason the gap matters (*may be especially difficult to stop using* — hedged, since it is a conjecture) and states the gap modestly (*has received little attention*).

**Move 3 (sentences 6–7).** *The present study therefore examines …* announces the work as the answer to the gap; *therefore* makes the link explicit. Sentence 7 gives the research questions. There is no outline of the paper, which in a short article in this field is optional.

**What makes it work.** Each sentence is narrower than the last: sleep and students → first-year students → smartphones and sleep → activities on smartphones → short-video apps → this study. Words from the question (*evening*, *short-video apps*, *sleep quality*, *first-year*) appear before the question does, so it arrives as expected.
:::
:::

::: example Repairing an introduction {#ex-repair}
Revise this opening of a student's introduction (fictional). *Since ancient times, people have always wanted to learn foreign languages. Nowadays, with the development of globalisation, English has become more and more important. Therefore, this paper will study the vocabulary learning strategies of Chinese college students. No one has ever studied this topic before.*
::: solution
**Problems.** Sentences 1–2 begin with the world in general and make claims no citation could support (*always*, *more and more important*). There is no review of research, so the niche cannot be established. *Therefore* links the study to globalisation, not to a gap. The final sentence is false: vocabulary learning strategies of Chinese students have been widely studied.

**A revision** (with placeholder citations): *Vocabulary knowledge is one of the strongest predictors of reading comprehension in a second language (Author, year). Learners' strategies for acquiring vocabulary have therefore been studied extensively, including among Chinese university students (Author, year; Author, year). Most of these studies, however, have relied on questionnaires administered at a single point, which show what strategies students report but not how their use changes over time. The present study follows 40 first-year students over one academic year to examine whether and how their vocabulary learning strategies change.*

**What changed.** The opening is about the specific field and can be cited; earlier research is acknowledged rather than denied; the niche is a limitation of method (single-point questionnaires), which the study addresses with a different design; *therefore* now connects the study to that niche.
:::
:::

## Planning the paper or dissertation

A research question decides the evidence; the plan decides how the evidence will be presented. Most empirical papers follow the IMRaD pattern ([[academic-reading/academic-texts#def-imrad]]), and most empirical dissertations extend it into chapters. Paltridge and Starfield (*Thesis and Dissertation Writing in a Second Language*, 2007) describe the main variants: the "traditional" thesis with chapters for introduction, literature review, methodology, results, discussion and conclusion; the thesis made of a series of related studies or published papers, each with its own IMRaD structure, framed by an introduction and a conclusion; and the topic-based thesis common in the humanities, whose chapters follow the argument rather than the stages of research.

::: theorem Planning a research text {#rule-plan}
1. **Start from the question.** Write the research questions at the top of the plan, and give each section a job in answering them.
2. **Choose the structure your field and institution expect**: IMRaD or its chapter equivalent for empirical work; an argument-based structure for theoretical or textual work. Check the regulations and look at recent successful dissertations in your department.
3. **Give each section a word budget** that reflects its importance — in many empirical dissertations, the results and discussion together take the largest share — and check it against the total limit.
4. **Plan the evidence for each question**: which data, which analysis, which table or figure.
5. **Plan the time backwards** from the deadline, leaving time for ethical approval (if you work with people), data collection, analysis, drafting, feedback and revision, and for things going wrong.
6. **Revise the plan as you go.** A reverse outline — a list of what each paragraph of the draft actually does — shows where the text has drifted from the plan.
:::

::: proof
Points 1 and 4 keep the text tied to the question: sections with no job in answering it are the first candidates for cutting. Point 2 matters because examiners judge a dissertation against the expectations of their department, which vary even within one university. Point 3 prevents the commonest imbalance in student dissertations, a long literature review and a thin discussion; word budgets are a planning tool, not a rule, and the right proportions differ by field. Point 5 reflects the fact that studies with human participants usually need approval from an ethics committee before data collection can begin, which can take weeks ([[academic-writing/academic-integrity]]). Point 6 is the planning side of revision ([[essay-writing/revising-editing]]): a plan is a hypothesis about the text, and drafting tests it.
:::

::: application A one-page proposal
Many programmes ask for a short research proposal before a dissertation begins. A typical one-page proposal has: a **working title**; the **background and gap** in a paragraph (a compressed Moves 1–2); the **aim and research questions**; the **method** — participants or materials, data, analysis; **ethical considerations**; a **timeline**; and **key references**. Writing it is the best test of whether a question is ready: if the method paragraph cannot be written, the question is not yet answerable.
:::

::: example A word budget {#ex-budget}
Zhou Min's dissertation has a limit of 10,000 words, excluding references and appendices. Her department expects the traditional structure. Draft a word budget and justify it.
::: solution
**A budget:**

| chapter | words | job in answering the questions |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Introduction | 1,000 | territory, niche, RQ1 and RQ2, outline |
| 2 Literature review | 2,200 | what is known about screen use and sleep; why short-video apps may differ |
| 3 Method | 1,500 | participants, questionnaire, sleep diary, analysis, ethics |
| 4 Results | 2,000 | answers RQ1 (time spent), then RQ2 (relations), with tables |
| 5 Discussion | 2,500 | interprets both answers, compares with earlier studies, limitations |
| 6 Conclusion | 800 | answers the questions directly; implications; further research |

**Justification.** The results and discussion together get 4,500 words, the largest share, because they contain her contribution. The literature review is limited to what the questions need, so it does not grow into a general survey of social media research. The method is detailed enough to be repeated. The figures are a plan, not a rule; the proportions in her department's recent dissertations are the best guide.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **开题报告 and a proposal.** Chinese universities usually require a 开题报告 (a proposal presented and defended before the thesis is written), often with separate sections for 选题背景, 研究意义 (theoretical and practical significance), 国内外研究现状, 研究内容, 研究方法 and 进度安排. English proposals contain much the same information, but the significance is usually argued inside the background and gap rather than listed separately, and "the current state of research at home and abroad" is a literature review organised by themes, not by country ([[academic-writing/literature-review]]).
- **研究问题 and 研究内容.** Chinese proposals often state 研究内容 (the content to be studied) as a list of topics. An English proposal expects questions or hypotheses that say what will be found out. Turning each item of 研究内容 into a question is a useful exercise.
- **Openings.** The general opening discussed in [[academic-writing/academic-style]] (随着…的发展) is especially common in introductions. English introductions start at Move 1 with a specific, citable claim.
- **Indirect questions.** Chinese questions keep the same word order as statements, adding 吗 or a question word in place. English direct questions invert (*How do students …?*) but indirect questions do not (*This study asks how students …*). Learners often carry the inversion into indirect questions: ✗ *This study examines how do students cope.*
:::

::: history
The idea that research begins with a problem rather than with the collection of facts has a long history in philosophy: John Dewey's *How We Think* (1910) described inquiry as starting from a "felt difficulty", and Karl Popper's *Logik der Forschung* (1934; English translation *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*, 1959) argued that science proceeds by proposing hypotheses that could be shown false. The rhetoric of research questions was studied much later. In 1981 John Swales published *Aspects of Article Introductions*, a study of introductions to research articles, which he developed into the CARS model of *Genre Analysis* (1990) and revised in *Research Genres* (2004). In 1995 Wayne Booth, Gregory Colomb and Joseph Williams published *The Craft of Research*, which taught students to move from a topic to a question and from a question to a problem that matters to readers.
:::

## Where this leads

A question is only as good as the sources that show it is open: [[academic-writing/finding-sources]] covers searching for and judging them, and [[academic-writing/literature-review]] turns them into the argument of Moves 1 and 2. The method that answers the question is written up as in [[academic-writing/methods-results]], and the introduction's promise is kept in [[academic-writing/discussion-conclusions]]. The shorter introductions of essays are in [[essay-writing/thesis-introduction]].

::: summary
- A **research question** states what a study will find out, in which population or material and in what respect; aims, objectives and hypotheses are related forms ([[#def-research-question]]).
- A good question is **focused, answerable, open, neutral**, clear about fact or value, and **significant** ([[#rule-good-question]]).
- The **kind** of question — descriptive, comparative, relational, causal, interpretive, evaluative — decides the design; a relational design cannot answer a causal question.
- State purpose as **aims, objectives, questions or hypotheses**, keep indirect questions in statement order, and answer every question you ask ([[#rule-purpose]]).
- Build the **introduction** with the CARS moves: start close to the topic, narrow in steps, state the niche precisely and honestly, and present the study as its answer ([[#rule-introduction]]).
- **Plan** from the question, in the structure your field expects, with word budgets, evidence for each question and a timeline that allows for ethics approval and revision ([[#rule-plan]]).
:::

::: exercise Write Move 2 {level=2}
For each situation write one or two sentences that establish a niche, in the form indicated. (a) Indicating a gap: studies of online learning have focused on universities, not on vocational colleges. (b) Adding to what is known: a method for measuring reading fluency has been tested in English but not in Chinese. (c) Counter-claiming: earlier studies concluded that class size has no effect on learning, but they measured only test scores.
::: solution
Possible answers: (a) *However, almost all of this research has been conducted in universities; little is known about online learning in vocational colleges, whose students and courses differ considerably.* (b) *This method has proved reliable for readers of English; whether it can be applied to Chinese, with its different writing system, has not yet been tested.* (c) *These conclusions, however, rest on test scores alone, and may underestimate effects of class size on outcomes such as participation and motivation.*

Write a four- to six-sentence introduction for a fictional study of your choice, with all three CARS moves, using placeholder citations (*Author, year*). Then label each sentence with its move and underline the words that signal the turn from Move 1 to Move 2 and from Move 2 to Move 3.

::: solution
Answers will vary. Check that: the first sentence is about the specific area and could carry a citation; each sentence narrows; the turn to Move 2 is marked (*however*, *but*, *yet*, *few studies*, *remains unclear*); the gap is specific and stated modestly; Move 3 presents the study as the answer (*therefore*, *to address this gap*) and states an aim or question that matches the gap; nothing in the introduction claims findings the study could not produce.

::: exercise A one-page proposal {level=3}
Write a one-page proposal (about 400 words) for a small research project you could actually carry out, following the headings in the application box above. Then exchange it with a classmate and check each other's research question against [[#rule-good-question]].
::: solution
Answers will vary. A strong proposal has one main question (with at most two or three sub-questions) that passes all six criteria; a background paragraph that ends in a precise gap; a method paragraph that says who or what will be studied, how many, what data will be collected and how they will be analysed; a realistic timeline that includes ethics approval if people are involved; and three to six references that the writer has actually read. The peer check should give at least one specific suggestion, such as narrowing the population or rewording a question that assumes its answer.
