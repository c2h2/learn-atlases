Three students at an English-medium university (all fictional) each receive an email asking them to meet the module leader about their essays.

- **Liu Yang** had written an essay on the same topic for another course the year before. He reused four paragraphs of it without changing or mentioning them.
- **Sofia** found a useful article in Spanish, her first language, and translated two of its paragraphs into English for her essay. She cited the article in her reference list but not in the text, and used no quotation marks, because the words, she reasoned, were now her own.
- **Kwame** wrote his essay himself, then pasted it into an AI chatbot and asked it to "make it sound more academic". The rewritten version, which he submitted, contained two references he had never seen; one of them does not exist. The module's guidance said that AI tools could be used for checking grammar but not for rewriting.

None of the three thought of themselves as cheating, and none of them bought an essay or copied a classmate's. Yet each has probably broken the university's rules: Liu Yang by submitting the same work twice, Sofia by presenting a translation of someone else's text as her own writing, and Kwame by using a tool beyond what the module allowed and by citing sources he had not read. Most breaches of academic integrity look like these — not dramatic frauds but misunderstandings of where the lines are. This chapter sets out the lines: what academic integrity is, what counts as plagiarism and other misconduct, how to use sources and tools within the rules, and what text-matching software does and does not show. The technique of paraphrase, and the difference between paraphrase and patchwriting, is taught in [[academic-reading/paraphrase-and-quotation]]; it is assumed here.

## What academic integrity is

::: definition Academic integrity {#def-integrity}
**Academic integrity** (学术诚信) is acting honestly and responsibly in study and research: presenting your own work as your own and others' work as theirs, reporting what you actually did and found, and following the rules of your institution and field. The International Center for Academic Integrity describes it through six fundamental values — honesty, trust, fairness, respect, responsibility and courage. **Academic misconduct** (学术不端) is a breach of these standards.
:::

The rules exist because academic work depends on trust. A tutor marking an essay trusts that it shows what the student can do; a researcher citing a study trusts that its data are real; a reader trusts that the words in quotation marks are the source's and the rest the writer's. Each kind of misconduct below breaks one of those trusts. That is why universities treat even unintentional breaches seriously, and also why most of them distinguish between poor practice, which is dealt with by teaching, and deliberate deception, which can lead to failing a course, a degree being withheld, or, for researchers, retraction of papers and loss of a career.

## Kinds of misconduct

::: definition Plagiarism {#def-plagiarism}
**Plagiarism** (抄袭, 剽窃) is presenting another person's words, ideas, data or other work as your own, without adequate acknowledgement. It includes copying without quotation marks and citation, paraphrasing too closely (patchwriting, [[academic-reading/paraphrase-and-quotation#def-patchwriting]]), using another's ideas or structure without citation, and translating another's text without acknowledgement. In most institutional policies, plagiarism does not require intent: what matters is what the submitted text does, although intent affects how a case is treated.
:::

| kind | what it is | example |
|---|---|---|
| plagiarism | others' words or ideas as your own | copying a paragraph from a website; patchwriting; translating a source without citation |
| self-plagiarism or duplicate submission | reusing your own earlier work as if new, without permission or acknowledgement | submitting part of an old essay again; publishing the same results twice |
| collusion | unauthorised collaboration on work meant to be individual | two students writing one answer and submitting it as two |
| contract cheating | getting another person or service to do work you submit as yours | buying an essay; paying someone to sit an online exam |
| fabrication | inventing data, results or sources | making up survey responses; citing a reference that does not exist |
| falsification | changing data, procedures or results so that the record misrepresents the research | removing inconvenient data points without saying so; altering an image |
| unauthorised use of tools | using a tool (including AI) beyond what the rules of the task allow | generating text where the assignment forbids it |

::: theorem When you must acknowledge {#rule-acknowledge}
1. **Words** from any source, in any language: quotation marks (or a block quotation) and a citation.
2. **Ideas, findings, arguments, structures and data** from any source: a citation, whether you quote, paraphrase, summarise or translate.
3. **A translation** of a source is still the source's text: cite it, and quote it as your translation if the wording is close (*my translation*).
4. **Your own earlier work**: cite it, and reuse substantial parts of it only if the rules of the task allow it.
5. **Help from other people** beyond ordinary feedback — a classmate's idea that shaped your argument, a statistician's analysis — in the way your field expects (an acknowledgement, co-authorship, or a note).
6. **Tools**, including AI tools, in the way the rules of the task require.
:::

::: proof
The common principle is that a reader should be able to tell, for every part of a text, whose work it is. Points 1 and 2 are the familiar ones. Point 3 catches many multilingual writers: translating a paragraph changes every word but keeps the author's content and structure, so it is the source's text in a new language, and an unacknowledged translation is plagiarism in the same way as unacknowledged copying. Point 4 follows from the purpose of assessment and publication: a mark or a publication certifies new work, so reusing old work without saying so misleads the marker or the editor. Point 5 marks a boundary that varies: in most courses, a friend's comments on a draft need no acknowledgement, but in research, people whose intellectual contribution shaped the work are acknowledged or become authors. Point 6 is the newest, and the one where rules differ most (see below).
:::

::: warning "I cited it, so it isn't plagiarism"
✗ A paragraph copied word for word from a source, with *(Okonkwo, 2021)* at the end but no quotation marks.

✓ The same wording in quotation marks with a citation and page number — or, better, a paraphrase in your own words and structure with a citation.

A citation tells the reader where the **ideas** came from. Quotation marks tell the reader where the **words** came from. A citation without quotation marks therefore claims that the words are yours, which is false. The same applies to a listing in the reference list without any citation in the text: it does not show which parts of the text came from the source. Both are among the commonest forms of plagiarism in student work, and both usually come from notes in which copied text was not marked as copied.
:::

::: example Reusing your own work {#ex-own-work}
Liu Yang, in the introduction, reused four paragraphs from an essay he wrote for another course. What could he have done instead, if he wanted to build on that earlier work?
::: solution
**Check the rules.** Some courses allow students to develop earlier work; many forbid submitting work that has already been assessed. The module handbook or the module leader decides.

**If reuse is allowed:** say so, in the form required — often a note to the marker, such as *Sections 2.1 and 2.2 develop material from my essay for [course], submitted in [term]* — and revise the material for the new question rather than pasting it in unchanged.

**If it is not allowed:** use the earlier essay as notes, not as text. Its sources can be cited again (they are other people's work, and citing them is not reuse), and his own earlier ideas can be developed in new writing. What he cannot do is present the old paragraphs as work done for this course.

**In research**, the same principle applies to publications: reusing one's published text or data in a new paper requires citation of the earlier paper and, for substantial overlap, the editor's agreement.
:::
:::

## Using sources responsibly

Most plagiarism in student writing is not deliberate copying but the result of a working method: notes copied from sources without quotation marks, a draft written with the source open, a paraphrase that changed the words and kept the structure. The cure is the method of [[academic-reading/paraphrase-and-quotation#rule-paraphrase]] and the habits of [[academic-reading/notes-and-summaries#rule-good-notes]]: mark every copied phrase in your notes, write from notes rather than from the source, and record the source and page of every point.

Writers in a second language face a particular question: if I must not reuse the wording of my sources, how can I learn the language of my field, which I learn from those sources? The answer lies in the difference between the shared language of a genre and the distinctive language of a particular text.

::: theorem What wording can be reused {#rule-reuse}
1. **Free to reuse**: technical terms (*working memory*, *regression analysis*), names of methods and instruments, and short **formulaic phrases** that belong to the genre rather than to any author: *little is known about*, *the aim of this study was to*, *these findings suggest that*, *in line with previous research*.
2. **Free to reuse**: common collocations and grammatical patterns (*play a role in*, *be associated with*).
3. **Must be quoted**: a source's distinctive phrases, metaphors, definitions and formulations — anything a reader would recognise as that author's way of putting it.
4. **Must be rewritten or quoted**: any sequence of a source's sentences, or a sentence whose structure and most of whose content words are the source's, even if some words are changed.
:::

::: proof
Academic writing is full of recurrent phrases, and learning them is part of learning the genre: corpus studies of academic English list hundreds of such formulas, and nobody owns *the results indicate that*. Learner writers who avoid all phrases they have seen in their reading end up with unidiomatic English; those who borrow whole sentences end up plagiarising. Points 1–2 and 3–4 draw the line between the two. A useful test is whether the phrase could appear in thousands of papers on different subjects (reusable) or whether it carries this author's particular content or style (quote or rewrite). Text-matching software often flags short formulaic matches; they are not plagiarism, and tutors reading a report know the difference.
:::

The figure sets out the logic of acknowledging wording. Let *c* stand for *the source is cited at this point*, *q* for *the wording is in quotation marks*, and *o* for *the wording and structure are your own*.

::: widget truthtable
title: When is use of a source acceptable
formula: c & (q | o)
compare: c
caption: The first column is the condition for acceptable use of a source's content: it must be cited (*c*), **and** its wording must either be marked as a quotation (*q*) or be genuinely your own (*o*). The second column, *c* alone, is what many students believe is enough. The two differ in the rows where *c* is true but *q* and *o* are both false — cited but copied, or cited but patchwritten. Those rows are plagiarism. (A row in which *q* and *o* are both true cannot really occur; the table shows it because logic does not know that.)
:::

::: example Is it plagiarism? {#ex-cases}
Decide whether each case (all fictional) is acceptable, and if not, what the writer should have done. (a) A student writes *Little is known about how teachers in rural schools use technology*, a sentence frame she has seen in several articles, followed by her own review of the few studies that exist. (b) A student's literature review follows the order, headings and selection of studies of a published review article, with all the studies correctly cited but the review itself cited only once. (c) A student translates a paragraph from a Chinese journal article into English and cites the article at the end of the paragraph. (d) A student includes in her dissertation a table of survey results from a government report, with the report cited under the table.
::: solution
(a) **Acceptable.** *Little is known about* is a formula of the genre; the content of her sentence is her own claim, and she supports it with her own review.

(b) **Not acceptable.** The structure and selection of the review — the review author's intellectual work — have been taken over without acknowledgement, even though each study is cited. She should build her own structure around her question, or acknowledge clearly that she follows the published review (*This section draws on the organisation of X's review*), and add her own reading and synthesis.

(c) **Not acceptable as it stands.** The citation acknowledges the ideas, but the translated wording is the source's text. She should either paraphrase it — writing from notes in her own structure — with the citation, or quote the key part as her translation (*"…" (Wang, 2019, p. 33, my translation)*).

(d) **Acceptable**, provided the table is reproduced accurately and the citation says so (*Source: …* or *Adapted from …* if changed). Reproducing tables or figures in a published thesis or article may also need permission from the copyright holder, depending on the licence.
:::
:::

## Text-matching software

Many universities and publishers check submissions with **text-matching software** (often called "plagiarism checkers"): Turnitin and its iThenticate service are widely used in English-medium institutions, and Chinese universities commonly use the checking services of CNKI and others. The software compares a text with a database of web pages, publications and earlier submissions and produces a **similarity report**: the matching passages, their sources, and a percentage of the text that matches.

::: theorem Reading a similarity report {#rule-similarity}
1. **A similarity percentage is not a plagiarism score.** Correctly quoted and cited passages, reference lists, technical terms and common phrases all match, and are acceptable.
2. **No percentage is safe or unsafe in itself.** A short text with one copied paragraph can have a low percentage; a well-cited literature review with many quotations can have a high one.
3. **What matters is each match**: is it quoted and cited, is it a formula of the genre, or is it someone else's wording presented as yours?
4. **Software misses some plagiarism**: translated text, closely paraphrased text, ideas and structures taken without citation, and sources not in its database.
5. **Do not write to the software.** Changing words to reduce the percentage without changing the method produces patchwriting, which is still plagiarism and is often obvious to a reader.
:::

::: proof
Points 1–3 reflect how the reports are meant to be used: as evidence for a human reader to interpret, not as a verdict. Institutions that use them generally say so in their own guidance, and many let students see their reports before final submission so that they can find and fix problems. Point 4 is the reason that a low percentage proves nothing: the software matches strings of text, so anything that changes the strings — translation, synonym substitution — escapes it, while remaining plagiarism by [[#def-plagiarism]]. Point 5 describes a practice that some students learn under pressure to meet a percentage limit: rewording flagged sentences one word at a time. The result keeps the source's structure and ideas, and a marker who compares it with the source sees it at once.
:::

## AI writing tools

Since late 2022, tools that generate text from prompts have become widely available, and institutions have been working out how they fit academic integrity. As of 2026, **policies vary greatly** — between countries, between universities, between departments, and between assignments on the same course. Some assignments forbid all use; some allow particular uses, such as brainstorming, checking grammar or translating instructions, but not generating submitted text; some allow or require AI use and ask for it to be described; and policies are revised often. There is no general rule a student can safely assume. What follows is a description of the issues, not a policy.

For research publication, the Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) and many publishers stated in 2023 that AI tools cannot be listed as authors, because authorship carries responsibility for the work that a tool cannot take, and that authors who use such tools should disclose how. Journals' requirements for disclosure differ in detail and continue to change.

::: theorem Using AI tools within the rules {#rule-ai}
1. **Find the rule for this task**: the assignment brief, the module handbook, the university's policy, the journal's instructions. If they are unclear, ask the tutor or editor before you use a tool, and keep their answer.
2. **Use the tool only as permitted**, and only for the purposes permitted (for example, grammar checking but not rewriting).
3. **Disclose** use in the form required: which tool, for what purpose, and where the output appears in the work.
4. **Check everything** a tool gives you: facts, interpretations, quotations and especially references, which such tools have been widely reported to invent. You are responsible for every word you submit.
5. **Keep your drafts, notes and search records**, which show how the work was produced if questions arise.
6. **Protect others' material**: do not paste confidential data, participants' information, or work you are reviewing for a journal into an online tool, unless the rules explicitly allow it.
:::

::: proof
Point 1 comes first because the same use can be permitted on one course and forbidden on the next; the rules of the task, not general opinion, decide. Points 3 and 4 follow from [[#def-integrity]]: disclosure lets the reader know whose work the text is, and checking follows from the writer's responsibility for its accuracy. Generative tools produce fluent text by predicting likely words, not by consulting sources, so they can state errors confidently and produce references that do not exist ([[academic-writing/finding-sources]]). Point 5 is practical: if a submission's authorship is questioned, drafts and notes are the best evidence of your own work. Software that claims to detect AI-generated text is also used by some institutions; its reliability has been questioned, and one study found that several such detectors more often misclassified essays by non-native writers of English as machine-generated (Liang et al. 2023). Point 6 matters because text entered into some online tools may be stored or used by their providers, and confidentiality is an obligation to other people.
:::

::: example A disclosure statement {#ex-disclosure}
A fictional module allows students to use an AI tool to check grammar and spelling, provided they disclose it. Liu Yang used one to correct the grammar of his final draft and accepted about twenty of its corrections. Write a suitable disclosure, and say what he should not have done.
::: solution
**A disclosure:** *I used [name of tool, version] to check the grammar and spelling of my final draft. I reviewed each suggested correction and accepted those that did not change my meaning. All ideas, structure, sources and wording are otherwise my own.*

**What it does.** It names the tool, states the purpose, describes how the output was used, and makes clear what was not generated. Many institutions provide a form or a template for this; use it if there is one.

**What he should not have done** under this rule: asked the tool to rewrite paragraphs, to generate content or arguments, to summarise sources he had not read, or to supply references — none of which grammar checking covers. If he was unsure whether, say, rephrasing a single awkward sentence counted as grammar checking, the rule of [[#rule-ai]] (point 1) applies: ask first.
:::
:::

## Research integrity

When study becomes research, integrity covers more than the use of sources. Research with human participants usually needs approval from an **ethics committee** (伦理委员会) before data are collected, and participants' **informed consent**, confidentiality and right to withdraw must be respected. Data must be reported honestly: a study that drops participants, changes its measures or tries many analyses must say so, because otherwise the record is falsified even if no number is invented. And **authorship** must reflect contribution. The International Committee of Medical Journal Editors, whose recommendations many journals outside medicine also follow, lists four criteria for authorship: a substantial contribution to the conception, design, data or analysis; drafting the work or revising it critically; approval of the final version; and agreement to be accountable for the work. Adding an author who did not contribute (**gift authorship**) or leaving out one who did (**ghost authorship**) misrepresents the work.

::: example Who should be an author? {#ex-authorship}
A fictional study was carried out by a team. Using the four criteria above, decide who should be listed as an author. (a) Dr Osei designed the study, analysed the data, wrote the draft and approved the final version. (b) Ma Lin collected the data in three schools, commented in detail on two drafts, approved the final version and agreed to answer for the work. (c) Professor Brandt is head of the department and secured the funding, but did not otherwise take part. (d) A research assistant entered questionnaire data into a spreadsheet. (e) A colleague read the final draft and corrected the English.
::: solution
(a) **Author**: meets all four criteria. (b) **Author**: data collection is a substantial contribution, detailed comments are critical revision, and she approved the version and accepts accountability. (c) **Not an author** by these criteria: obtaining funding or heading a department alone does not qualify; listing him would be gift authorship. He can be named in the acknowledgements, and the funding stated in the funding section. (d) **Acknowledgement**, not authorship, unless the role went beyond data entry. (e) **Acknowledgement**: language editing is help, not intellectual contribution. Fields outside medicine apply their own conventions, but the principle — authorship follows contribution and responsibility — is widely shared.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which of these is falsification rather than plagiarism or fabrication?
- [ ] citing an article that does not exist
- [ ] copying a methods paragraph from another paper
- [x] removing five participants whose results weakened the effect, without reporting the removal
- [ ] submitting the same essay to two courses
::: solution
Removing data without reporting it changes the research record so that it misrepresents what was found: falsification. Citing a non-existent source is fabrication; copying a methods paragraph is plagiarism (although some fields accept reuse of standard method descriptions with citation); submitting the same essay twice is duplicate submission.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A tutor's brief says that AI tools may be used for brainstorming but that all submitted text must be the student's own. Which use is within this rule?
- [x] asking a tool for a list of possible angles on the topic, then choosing one and writing the essay yourself
- [ ] asking a tool to write a first draft, then editing it
- [ ] asking a tool to paraphrase your sources
- [ ] asking a tool to write the conclusion because you are short of time
::: solution
Brainstorming — generating ideas to choose from — is the permitted use, and the essay's text remains the student's. The other three put text produced by the tool into the submission, which the brief forbids. Whether the use must be disclosed depends on the brief; when unsure, disclose.
:::
:::

## When something goes wrong

If you realise before submission that a draft contains unmarked copying, unchecked references or a use of tools beyond the rules, fix it: find the sources, mark the quotations, rewrite from notes, remove what you cannot verify. If you realise after submission, contact your tutor; an honest correction is usually treated very differently from a breach that is discovered later. If you are asked to a meeting about a possible breach, read your institution's procedure, bring your notes and drafts, and use the support services — student advisers, writing centres, students' unions — that most universities provide. Integrity procedures generally distinguish between poor academic practice, which leads to teaching and resubmission, and serious misconduct.

::: note English and Chinese
- **Terms.** 抄袭 and 剽窃 correspond to *plagiarism*; 学术不端 to *academic misconduct*; 代写 to *contract cheating* or *ghost-writing*; 查重 to *text-matching* or *a similarity check*; 重复率 to the *similarity percentage*.
- **Rules in China.** Chinese universities have their own rules against academic misconduct. The Ministry of Education's regulation on preventing and handling academic misconduct in higher education institutions (《高等学校预防与处理学术不端行为办法》, in force from 2016) lists plagiarism, fabrication, falsification, ghost-writing and improper authorship, among others; and the Degree Law of 2024, in force from 2025, provides for degrees to be withheld or revoked for misconduct such as ghost-writing, plagiarism and fabrication in a thesis. Many universities set a maximum 重复率 for theses.
- **降重 and patchwriting.** Under a percentage limit, students sometimes learn to "reduce the repetition rate" (降重) by replacing words and reordering clauses in flagged sentences. In English-medium institutions this produces patchwriting, which is treated as plagiarism whatever the percentage ([[#rule-similarity]], point 5).
- **Translation.** Translating a Chinese source into English (or the reverse) without acknowledgement is plagiarism in both systems. Software comparing English texts will not detect it; markers who read both languages often do.
- **Collaboration and collusion.** Studying together, discussing readings and sharing notes are encouraged at most universities. The line is crossed when work that is assessed individually is written jointly or shared. If a group project is assessed partly individually, ask which parts must be written alone.
- **Culture is not the explanation.** As [[academic-reading/paraphrase-and-quotation]] notes, research does not support the idea that Chinese students plagiarise because of their culture. What differs is the exact position of the lines, and students who know where they are rarely cross them.
:::

::: history
The word *plagiarism* comes from the Latin *plagiarius*, 'kidnapper', which the Roman poet Martial used in the first century AD for a rival who recited Martial's poems as his own; *plagiary* and then *plagiarism* entered English in the early seventeenth century. Modern rules took shape with modern research and teaching. In 1997 a group of medical editors founded the Committee on Publication Ethics; in 1999 the Center for Academic Integrity (now the International Center for Academic Integrity) published *The Fundamental Values of Academic Integrity*, adding courage to the values in a later edition; and in 2000 the United States government defined research misconduct as fabrication, falsification or plagiarism. Writing research changed the understanding of student plagiarism: Rebecca Moore Howard (1993) named patchwriting as a stage of learning, and in 2006 Thomas Lancaster and Robert Clarke gave the name *contract cheating* to the buying of assignments. Since 2023 universities, publishers and bodies such as COPE have issued policies on AI tools, which continue to change.
:::

## Where this leads

The method of paraphrase that prevents most plagiarism is in [[academic-reading/paraphrase-and-quotation]], and the mechanics of citation in [[academic-writing/citation-referencing]]. Integrity in reporting data — what was done and found, including what went wrong — continues in [[academic-writing/methods-results]], and honest statements of limitations in [[academic-writing/discussion-conclusions]]. Confidentiality in peer review is part of [[academic-writing/peer-review]].

::: summary
- **Academic integrity** means presenting your own work as yours and others' as theirs, reporting truthfully and following the rules of the task and field ([[#def-integrity]]).
- **Plagiarism** includes copying, patchwriting, taking ideas or structures without citation, and unacknowledged translation; intent is not required ([[#def-plagiarism]]).
- Other misconduct: **duplicate submission**, **collusion**, **contract cheating**, **fabrication**, **falsification**, **misused authorship** and **unauthorised use of tools**.
- Acknowledge **words** with quotation marks and citation, **ideas** with citation, **translations**, **your own earlier work**, substantial **help** and **tools** as required ([[#rule-acknowledge]]); a citation alone does not license copied wording.
- Genre **formulas** and technical terms are free to reuse; distinctive wording must be quoted or rewritten ([[#rule-reuse]]).
- A **similarity percentage** is not a verdict; read each match, and never reword to beat the software ([[#rule-similarity]]).
- **AI tool** policies vary by institution and task and are changing (as of 2026): find the rule, use only what it permits, disclose, verify everything, keep drafts, and protect confidential material ([[#rule-ai]]).
:::

::: exercise Read a similarity report {level=2}
A student's report shows a similarity of 31%. The matches are: 12% from her reference list; 8% from three correctly quoted and cited definitions; 4% from short phrases such as *the purpose of this study was to*; and 7% from one paragraph that matches a website almost exactly, with a citation at the end but no quotation marks. Which matches are a problem, and what should she do?
::: solution
Only the last, 7% match is a problem: copied wording with a citation but no quotation marks is plagiarism ([[#rule-acknowledge]]). She should rewrite the paragraph from notes in her own words and structure, keeping the citation, or quote the one part whose wording matters. The reference list, quoted definitions and formulaic phrases are acceptable matches. The total percentage tells her nothing by itself.
:::

::: exercise A translated source {level=2}
You want to use this point from a fictional Chinese article (Zhao 2020, p. 45): 农村学校的英语教师普遍缺乏专业培训的机会，这在一定程度上影响了教学质量。 Write (a) a paraphrase in English with a citation, and (b) a quotation of it as your translation.
::: solution
(a) *Opportunities for professional training are generally scarce for English teachers in rural schools, which, Zhao (2020) suggests, partly explains differences in the quality of teaching.* (A paraphrase restructured in English, keeping the hedge 在一定程度上 as *partly*. Check that *explains differences* does not go beyond the source; *affects the quality of teaching* is closer.) (b) *Zhao (2020) observes that "English teachers in rural schools generally lack opportunities for professional training, which to some extent affects the quality of teaching" (p. 45, my translation).*
:::

::: exercise Within the rules? {level=2}
A module's AI policy says: *You may use AI tools to help you understand readings and to check grammar. You may not use them to produce any text that you submit. Any use must be declared.* For each use, say whether it is allowed. (a) asking a tool to explain a difficult paragraph in a set reading; (b) asking it to write a summary of the reading, which you paste into your essay with a citation; (c) asking it to check the grammar of your draft, and declaring this; (d) asking it for five references on your topic, which you cite without reading.

::: exercise Advise a student {level=3}
Write an email of about 150 words to Sofia, the student in the introduction, explaining what was wrong with her use of the Spanish article and how she could use it correctly. Be clear and kind; she did not mean to deceive.
::: hint
Explain the principle (whose words are these?) before the rule, and give her two concrete ways to use the source.
:::
::: solution
A model: *Dear Sofia, thank you for coming to see me. I understand that you didn't intend to present someone else's work as your own, so let me explain why your two paragraphs count as plagiarism under our rules. When you translate a passage, the words change, but the content and structure are still the author's, so a translation is still the source's text. Because the article was in your reference list but not cited in those paragraphs, and the translation was not marked, a reader would assume the paragraphs were your own writing. You can use the article in two ways. You can paraphrase it: take notes on its main points, close it, and write the ideas in your own words and order, with a citation. Or, where the exact wording matters, you can quote a short passage as your own translation, with the page number and "my translation". I'm happy to look at a revised version. Best wishes, …* Check that an answer explains the principle, names the two correct methods, and keeps a respectful tone.
:::
:::

::: exercise Your institution's rules {level=3}
Find your institution's (or a university you might attend's) policy on academic integrity and its current guidance on AI tools. Write about 200 words summarising, in your own words and with citations: what counts as plagiarism, how cases are handled, what uses of AI tools are allowed and how they must be declared, and one point you found surprising. Date your summary, since the policy may change.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A good answer paraphrases the policy rather than copying it (with short quotations only where the wording matters, such as a definition), cites the documents with their dates, distinguishes clearly between general university rules and any rules that depend on the course or assignment, and notes when the guidance was last updated.
:::
