Three months after submitting her first article, Dr Ana Ruiz (a fictional researcher) receives an email from the journal. The decision is *major revision*. Reviewer 2's report begins:

> *The topic is important and the data set is valuable, but the manuscript in its present form overstates what a cross-sectional survey can show. The discussion repeatedly describes evening phone use as "causing" poor sleep. The authors should also explain why participants who reported shift work were included. Finally, the literature review omits the recent experimental work on this question.*

Her first draft of a reply begins: *We strongly disagree with Reviewer 2, who seems not to have read our paper carefully.* Her second draft, written a week later, begins: *We thank Reviewer 2 for these comments, which have helped us to clarify the scope of our claims. We have revised the discussion throughout to describe the relationship as an association (pp. 14–16) …*

The first draft is understandable; the second is the one that gets the paper published. It treats the report not as an attack but as information about how an expert reader read the paper, accepts what is right in it, changes the text, and says exactly where. Where Ana still disagrees — perhaps about the shift workers — she can say so, politely and with reasons. This chapter is about that process: how peer review works, how to read feedback from reviewers, supervisors and tutors, how to revise in response to it, how to write a response letter, and how to give useful feedback yourself. How peer review shapes the reliability of published work, from a reader's point of view, is discussed in [[academic-reading/evaluating-sources#def-peer-review]]; revising an essay is taught in [[essay-writing/revising-editing]].

## How peer review works

::: definition Peer review {#def-peer-review}
**Peer review** (同行评审) is the evaluation of a manuscript by experts in its field, usually two or three, chosen by a journal's editor, who advise whether it should be published and how it should be improved. The editor makes the decision. In **single-anonymous** review the reviewers know the authors' names but not the reverse; in **double-anonymous** review neither knows the other; in **open** review identities are known, and in some journals the reports are published with the article. The same word is used for review of grant applications, conference papers and, in teaching, for students' comments on one another's drafts.
:::

::: widget graph
title: The path of a manuscript
nodes: submission@0,8; editor screens@4,8; desk rejection@4,11; sent to reviewers@8,8; reviewers' reports@12,8; editor's decision@12,4; accept@16,4; minor or major revision@12,0; reject@16,0; revised manuscript and response@6,0
edges: submission>editor screens; editor screens>desk rejection; editor screens>sent to reviewers; sent to reviewers>reviewers' reports; reviewers' reports>editor's decision; editor's decision>accept; editor's decision>reject; editor's decision>minor or major revision; minor or major revision>revised manuscript and response; revised manuscript and response>editor screens
caption: The usual path of a journal article. The editor may reject a manuscript without review (a desk rejection) if it does not fit the journal or is clearly not ready. Otherwise reviewers report, and the editor decides: acceptance as submitted is rare; most published papers pass through at least one round of revision, with a response to the reviewers, and the revised version may go back to the same reviewers. You can drag the boxes.
:::

The common decisions are **accept**; **minor revision** (small changes, usually checked by the editor); **major revision** (substantial changes, usually sent back to the reviewers); **reject and resubmit** (a new submission is invited); and **reject**. *Major revision* is a positive outcome: the editor thinks the paper can become publishable. Rejection is common in every field, and most researchers' papers have been rejected at least once; a rejected paper is often revised in the light of the reports and published elsewhere.

Students meet the same process in smaller forms: a supervisor's comments on a dissertation chapter, a tutor's feedback on an essay draft, a classmate's comments in a peer-review session, and — in many systems, including China's — anonymous external review of a master's or doctoral thesis before the defence.

## Reading feedback

::: theorem Reading a review or feedback {#rule-reading-feedback}
1. **Read it, then wait.** A first reading of criticism produces a reaction; a second reading, a day or more later, produces a plan.
2. **List every comment** separately and number them, including those embedded in general paragraphs.
3. **Sort them**: changes you will make; points you need to think about or check; points of misunderstanding; points you disagree with.
4. **Look for the concern behind the comment.** *Why were shift workers included?* may mean *I suspect they distort your results*; answer the concern, not only the question.
5. **Treat a misunderstanding as information**: if an expert reader misread your text, others will; clarify the text rather than only explaining in the response.
6. **Notice where reviewers agree or conflict.** Comments made by two reviewers carry extra weight; where they conflict, the editor's letter often says which to follow, and if not, choose and explain.
7. **Separate tone from content.** A curt comment may be correct; a polite one may be wrong.
:::

::: proof
Most of these steps exist because criticism of one's work is hard to read objectively, and the first reaction — that the reviewer is unfair or careless — is rarely the most useful one. Points 2 and 3 turn a report into a task list, which is what the response letter will follow. Point 4 reflects how reviewers write: they often phrase a concern as a question or a brief instruction, and an answer that addresses only the literal words leaves the concern in place. Point 5 is the most valuable habit: a writer cannot see their own text as a newcomer does, and a reviewer's misreading shows exactly where the text failed. Point 6 matters because editors decide; their summary of the reports is the best guide to what must change.
:::

::: example Sorting a review {#ex-sort}
Sort the four comments in Reviewer 2's report (at the start of the chapter) and decide what Ana should do with each.
::: solution
**1. "Overstates what a cross-sectional survey can show … 'causing'."** *Will change.* The criticism is correct: a cross-sectional survey shows association ([[academic-writing/discussion-conclusions#rule-interpret]]). Revise every causal claim in the abstract, discussion and conclusion, and add a sentence on the direction of the relationship to the limitations.

**2. "The topic is important and the data set is valuable."** *No action*, but worth noting: it is what the reviewer wants to keep, and it can be acknowledged in the response.

**3. "Explain why participants who reported shift work were included."** *Think and check.* The concern behind the question is probably that shift workers sleep at unusual times and may distort the results. Ana can check whether the results change when they are excluded. If they do not, she can report this (a *sensitivity analysis*) and keep them; if they do, she must reconsider. Either way, the method section should explain the decision.

**4. "Omits the recent experimental work."** *Will change*, after checking: find the studies, read them, add them to the literature review and compare them with her results in the discussion. If she knows of the work but judged it irrelevant, she should explain why in the response — and perhaps in the paper.
:::
:::

::: example Conflicting comments {#ex-conflict}
Zhou Min's dissertation chapter (fictional) receives comments from her two supervisors. Supervisor A writes: *The literature review is too long; cut section 2.3 on smartphone use in general.* Supervisor B writes: *Section 2.3 is useful context, but needs more on how earlier studies measured screen time.* What should she do?
::: solution
**Find the concerns.** A's concern is length and focus: section 2.3 seems to stray from the question. B's concern is relevance of a different kind: the section would be useful if it explained the measurement problem that her study addresses.

**Look for a revision that answers both.** Shorten 2.3 by cutting the general material about smartphones, and refocus what remains on how earlier studies measured screen time — which is the gap her question depends on ([[academic-writing/research-questions]]). The section becomes shorter (A) and more useful (B).

**Say what she did.** In a short note to both supervisors: *I have cut section 2.3 from 900 to 450 words, removing the general background (A's comment), and refocused it on how earlier studies measured screen time (B's comment), since this leads to my research question.* If no revision could satisfy both, she should ask them, or follow the one whose concern bears most directly on her question and explain why.
:::
:::

## Revising

::: theorem Revising in response to feedback {#rule-revising}
1. **Revise the paper, not only the response.** Every change you describe in the response must be made in the text, and anything you explain to the reviewer that a reader would also need should go into the paper.
2. **Deal with major issues first**: the argument, the claims, the analysis, the structure — then the paragraphs, then the sentences.
3. **Check the knock-on effects**: a change to a claim in the discussion may require changes in the abstract, the introduction and the conclusion.
4. **Keep a record**: a table of comments, actions and locations, and a version of the manuscript with changes marked if the journal asks for one.
5. **Do not make unrequested changes silently.** If you revise something no reviewer mentioned, say so in the response.
6. **Keep to the limits**: if revision adds words, cut elsewhere.
:::

::: proof
Point 1 is the commonest failure in revision: a long, persuasive response letter attached to a manuscript that has barely changed. Editors and reviewers check the text, and an explanation that stays in the letter does not help future readers, who will have the same question. Point 2 applies the general order of revision ([[essay-writing/revising-editing]]) for the same reason: polishing sentences that will be cut wastes time. Point 3 matters because the claims of a paper are repeated in several places, and a paper that is cautious in the discussion but still overclaims in the abstract shows that the revision was not thorough. Points 4 and 5 make the reviewers' work easier, which makes a positive second report more likely.
:::

## The response letter

::: definition Response letter {#def-response}
A **response to reviewers** (修改说明, 审稿意见回复) is a document sent with a revised manuscript. It thanks the editor and reviewers, then answers every comment in order: usually by quoting or summarising the comment, stating what has been changed and where (page and line numbers), or explaining why no change was made.
:::

::: theorem Writing a response {#rule-response}
1. **Open briefly**: thank the editor and reviewers, and summarise the main changes in a few sentences.
2. **Answer every comment**, in order, quoting or numbering each so that the reader can follow.
3. **Say what you changed and where**: *We have revised the discussion to … (p. 14, lines 8–20)*; quote the new text if it is short.
4. **Agree plainly** where the reviewer is right; do not over-apologise.
5. **Disagree politely and with reasons** where you think the reviewer is wrong: acknowledge the point, give your reasons and evidence, and, where possible, make a change that addresses the concern behind it.
6. **Answer the content, not the tone**, and never criticise the reviewer.
7. **Keep it factual and concise**: a reviewer should be able to check each response against the manuscript quickly.
:::

::: proof
The response letter is read by the people who will decide whether the revision succeeded, so its job is to make checking easy (points 2, 3, 7) and to show that every comment was taken seriously (point 2). Point 4 reflects the register of the genre: *We agree and have revised …* is enough; repeated apologies (*We are deeply sorry for our careless mistakes*) waste space and can make a writer seem less confident in the rest of the work. Point 5 is where the letter is most difficult and most important. Reviewers are not always right, and editors expect authors to disagree when they have good reasons; what persuades them is evidence and a concession that shows the concern was understood. Point 6 follows from the purpose: an angry response does not change the editor's mind about the science, and it may change it about the authors.
:::

The language of responses is a small, formal set of phrases:

| function | typical phrases |
|---|---|
| thanking | *We thank the reviewer for this helpful comment.* / *We are grateful for the careful reading of our manuscript.* (once or twice, not before every point) |
| agreeing and acting | *We agree, and have revised …* / *As suggested, we have added …* / *This has now been corrected (p. 7).* |
| clarifying | *We apologise for the lack of clarity. We have rewritten the paragraph to make clear that …* |
| disagreeing | *We appreciate this point, but we would argue that …* / *We have considered this carefully; however, …* / *While we agree that …, we believe that … because …* |
| partly accepting | *We have not removed …, for the reasons below, but we have added a sentence acknowledging …* |
| pointing to evidence | *As shown in the new Table 4, …* / *This approach follows Author (year), who …* |

::: example Responding to two comments {#ex-response}
Write Ana's responses to comments 1 and 3 of Reviewer 2. Assume that for comment 3 she found that excluding the 41 shift workers did not change the results.
::: solution
***Comment 2.1.*** *"The manuscript in its present form overstates what a cross-sectional survey can show. The discussion repeatedly describes evening phone use as 'causing' poor sleep."*

***Response.*** *We agree, and thank the reviewer for pointing this out. We have revised the abstract, discussion and conclusion so that the relationship is described throughout as an association (abstract; p. 14, lines 3–25; p. 17, lines 2–6). We have also added to the limitations a paragraph explaining that the cross-sectional design cannot establish the direction of the relationship, and that poor sleepers may use their phones more because they cannot sleep (p. 16, lines 10–18).*

***Comment 2.3.*** *"The authors should also explain why participants who reported shift work were included."*

***Response.*** *We included shift workers because they form a substantial part of the population of interest (41 of 612 participants, 6.7%), and excluding them would limit the generalisability of the findings. We recognise, however, that their sleep patterns may differ. We therefore repeated the main analyses without them; the results were very similar (new supplementary Table S2). We now explain the decision in the method section (p. 8, lines 12–17) and report the additional analysis in the results (p. 11, lines 20–24).*

**What the responses do.** The first agrees without fuss and lists every location changed, including the abstract, where the same overclaim appeared ([[#rule-revising]], point 3). The second disagrees with an implied suggestion (exclude them), gives a reason, concedes the concern (*We recognise …*), answers it with evidence (the new analysis), and puts the explanation into the paper as well as the letter.
:::
:::

::: warning Two ways to spoil a response
✗ *The reviewer has clearly misunderstood our paper. Shift workers are obviously part of the population. We see no need to change anything.*

✗ *We are terribly sorry for this serious mistake, and for all the other mistakes in our poor manuscript. We have done everything the reviewer said.*

✓ *We included shift workers because … We recognise, however, that … We therefore … (p. 8, lines 12–17).*

The first response is defensive: it attacks the reviewer, gives no reason and offers no evidence. The second is self-abasing, and *We have done everything the reviewer said* without saying what or where makes it impossible to check. Both put the writer's feelings in place of information. A good response is courteous, specific and confident.
:::

::: quiz
A reviewer writes: *The sample of 64 students is too small for any conclusions to be drawn.* The authors believe the sample was adequate for the main comparison but accept that it limits the delayed-test analysis. Which response is best?
- [ ] *We disagree. Our sample is fine.*
- [ ] *We apologise for the small sample, which is a serious weakness of our study.*
- [x] *We agree that the sample limits some conclusions. A power analysis before the study indicated that 64 participants were sufficient to detect an effect of the size expected for the main comparison, which was confirmed (d = 0.98). We accept that the study had less power to detect the smaller difference at the delayed test, and have added this to the limitations (p. 16, lines 4–9).*
- [ ] *Many published studies have smaller samples than ours.*
::: solution
The third response concedes what is right, explains with evidence why the main conclusion stands, and changes the paper to reflect the valid part of the criticism. The first gives no reason; the second concedes more than the authors believe and undermines the paper; the fourth is an irrelevant comparison.
:::
:::

## Giving feedback

Most researchers are reviewers as well as authors, and most students are asked to comment on classmates' drafts. Writing useful feedback is a skill of its own.

::: theorem Writing a useful review {#rule-giving}
1. **Begin with a short summary** of the paper's aim, method and main claim, as you understood it: this shows the author what came across.
2. **Name the strengths** honestly and specifically.
3. **Separate major from minor points**: major ones concern the question, design, analysis and whether the conclusions follow; minor ones concern clarity, presentation and language.
4. **Be specific**: say where (section, page, paragraph), what the problem is, and why it matters.
5. **Suggest how to fix it** where you can, without rewriting the paper as you would have written it.
6. **Comment on the work, not the writer**, and write as you would wish to be written to.
7. **Respect confidentiality**: do not share, use or upload a manuscript you are reviewing, including to online tools, unless the journal or course explicitly allows it.
:::

::: proof
Points 1–3 give the author and the editor what they need: the summary checks understanding, the strengths tell the author what not to lose, and the division into major and minor points shows what is essential. Point 4 distinguishes useful from useless comments: *The discussion is weak* gives the author nothing to do, while *The discussion does not consider whether the effect could be due to differences between the classes (p. 15)* does. Point 5 has a limit, because a reviewer's job is to judge whether the paper achieves its own aims, not to impose different ones. Point 7 is an obligation to the authors, whose unpublished work is in the reviewer's hands; journals' policies on review increasingly mention AI tools explicitly ([[academic-writing/academic-integrity#rule-ai]]).
:::

::: example Turning vague comments into useful ones {#ex-feedback}
A student has written these comments on a classmate's draft literature review. Rewrite them as useful feedback. (a) *Not critical enough.* (b) *Good!* (c) *Grammar mistakes.* (d) *I don't agree with your conclusion.*
::: solution
(a) *In section 2.2 the four studies are summarised one by one, but the review does not say which is stronger or why they disagree. Could you group them by method and comment on the difference between the surveys and the experiment?* (specific location, problem, suggestion)

(b) *The opening paragraph works well: it starts close to the topic and leads clearly to the gap in section 2.4.* (a specific strength the writer should keep)

(c) *There are several tense shifts in the method summaries (for example, p. 3, "Lee finds … and reported"); it may help to check every reporting verb against the pattern you chose.* (a pattern, an example and a way to check, rather than a list of corrections)

(d) *Your conclusion says that recordings improve learning, but the studies you review mostly measured students' preferences. Is there evidence on learning that you could add, or could the conclusion be limited to preferences?* (the concern behind the disagreement, linked to evidence)
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Terms.** 审稿 (reviewing), 审稿人 (reviewer), 审稿意见 (reviewers' comments), 大修 and 小修 (major and minor revision), 退稿 (rejection), 修改说明 or 回复信 (response letter); 盲审 (anonymous review), as in the external review of master's and doctoral theses that Chinese universities commonly use before a defence.
- **Disagreeing with a reviewer or a supervisor.** Some writers feel that disagreeing with a senior or anonymous expert is disrespectful. In English-language review, reasoned disagreement is a normal part of the process, and editors expect it when authors have good grounds. What matters is the form: acknowledge, explain, offer evidence, and change what can be changed.
- **Politeness formulas.** Chinese responses often open each answer with a phrase of thanks (感谢审稿专家的宝贵意见). In English, thanking once at the beginning, and occasionally for a particularly helpful comment, is usual; repeating it before every point adds length without adding information.
- **意见 and *opinion*.** 审稿意见 is *reviewers' comments* or *the reviews*, not *the reviewers' opinions*, which in English sounds as if the comments were merely personal views.
- **Indirect criticism.** Reviewers writing in English often phrase criticism indirectly: *The authors may wish to consider …*, *It is not entirely clear whether …*, *I wonder whether …*. These are usually requests for change, not optional suggestions ([[pragmatics/meaning-beyond-words]]). Treat them as comments that need an answer.
:::

::: history
Editorial review is as old as the learned journal: in 1752 the Royal Society of London took responsibility for the *Philosophical Transactions* and set up a committee to decide which papers to print. Review by outside experts spread unevenly; many journals relied mainly on their editors' judgement well into the twentieth century, and systematic external review became general only after the Second World War, as the number of submissions grew. From the late 1980s peer review itself became a subject of research: the first International Congress on Peer Review in Biomedical Publication was held in 1989, and later studies compared anonymous and open review, and examined reviewers' agreement and bias. Since then journals have experimented with new forms, including open review with named reviewers, the publication of reports alongside articles, and *registered reports*, in which a study's question and method are reviewed before the data are collected.
:::

::: summary
- **Peer review** is the evaluation of a manuscript by experts, who advise and an editor who decides; it may be single-anonymous, double-anonymous or open ([[#def-peer-review]]).
- *Major revision* is a positive decision; most published papers are revised at least once, and rejection is common.
- **Read feedback twice**, list and sort every comment, look for the **concern behind it**, and treat misunderstandings as signs that the text needs clarifying ([[#rule-reading-feedback]]).
- **Revise the paper**, not only the response: major issues first, with knock-on changes in the abstract, introduction and conclusion ([[#rule-revising]]).

For each reviewer comment, state the concern that probably lies behind it. (a) *Why were the interviews conducted in English?* (b) *How were the four schools chosen?* (c) *The authors should report effect sizes.*

(b) The schools may not be representative, or may have been chosen in a way that favours the result. ::: exercise Write a response letter {level=3}
Write a response letter (about 300 words) to these three fictional comments on a paper reporting a survey of 200 students' use of online dictionaries. *(1) The title suggests the study covers all students, but the sample is from one university. (2) The paper should compare online and paper dictionaries experimentally. (3) Table 2 is hard to read.* Accept 1 and 3; disagree, politely and with reasons, with 2.
::: hint
For comment 2, consider what question the paper actually asked and what a survey can and cannot do, and whether a change to the text could address the concern.
:::
::: solution
Answers will vary. A strong letter opens with brief thanks and a summary of changes; for (1) revises the title and states the new title and any related changes in the abstract; for (2) acknowledges that an experiment would answer a different and valuable question, explains that the paper's question was how students use dictionaries, not which kind is more effective, and adds a sentence to the limitations or further research suggesting such an experiment; for (3) describes how the table has been changed and where it is. Every response says where the change was made; none apologises excessively or criticises the reviewer.
