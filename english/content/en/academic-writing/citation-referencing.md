Here is the same sentence as it might appear in three journals. The source is fictional.

> **A.** *Students who received feedback within a week revised their essays more extensively than those who received it later (Okafor & Lin, 2021, p. 214).*
>
> **B.** *Students who received feedback within a week revised their essays more extensively than those who received it later (Okafor and Lin 214).*
>
> **C.** *Students who received feedback within a week revised their essays more extensively than those who received it later [7].*

Version A follows APA style, used across psychology, education and much of the social sciences: author and year, with a page number because the point is specific. Version B follows MLA style, used in literature and languages: author and page, no year. Version C uses a numbered style, common in medicine and engineering: the number points to the seventh entry in a numbered list. The conventions differ in almost every detail, but all three do the same three things. They tell the reader **whose** finding this is, **where** to find it, and — by contrast with the sentences around them that carry no citation — which claims are the writer's own.

This chapter is about citing from the writer's side: what citation is for, the choice between putting the author in the sentence or in brackets, choosing and using reporting verbs, the main citation systems as of 2026, and reference managers. It describes the styles rather than reproducing them; for any submission, follow the current edition of the style your department or journal requires. Reading the stance that reporting verbs carry is taught in [[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging#rule-reporting-verbs]], and paraphrase and quotation in [[academic-reading/paraphrase-and-quotation]].

## What citation is for

::: definition Citation and reference {#def-citation}
A **citation** (引用, 文内引注) is the mark in the text that attributes an idea, finding or wording to a source: *(Okafor & Lin, 2021)*, *Okafor and Lin (2021) found …*, a superscript or bracketed number. A **reference** (参考文献条目) is the full description of the source in the **reference list** at the end of the text, which allows a reader to find it. A **bibliography** sometimes means the same as a reference list and sometimes a wider list of works consulted; most author–date styles ask for a reference list of cited works only.
:::

::: theorem What must be cited {#rule-what-to-cite}
Cite a source for:

1. **any idea, argument, finding, theory or model** you take from someone else, whether you quote, paraphrase or summarise it;
2. **specific facts and figures** that are not common knowledge in your field: statistics, dates of particular events, the results of a survey;
3. **wording** you reproduce, which also needs quotation marks ([[academic-reading/paraphrase-and-quotation#rule-when-quote]]);
4. **data, images, tables, figures, code and instruments** (questionnaires, tests) that others created;
5. **your own earlier published work** when you use it again ([[academic-writing/academic-integrity]]).

Common knowledge — facts that a reader in the field would know without looking them up and that are not in dispute — needs no citation. When in doubt, cite.
:::

::: proof
Citation serves several readers at once. For the **reader**, it provides the evidence and a way to check it: a claim with a citation can be traced, a claim without one rests on the writer's authority alone. For the **cited author**, it gives credit, which in research is how reputations, careers and funding are built. For the **writer**, it shows knowledge of the field and places the new study in a conversation, which is what Moves 1 and 2 of an introduction do ([[academic-writing/research-questions#rule-introduction]]). And by marking what came from others, citation also marks what did not: the uncited claims are the writer's contribution. "Common knowledge" depends on the reader: *Water boils at 100 °C at sea level* needs no citation anywhere, but a fact well known to linguists may need one in an article for engineers. Point 5 surprises many students, but reusing one's own published text without citation misleads readers about what is new.
:::

## Integral and non-integral citation

::: definition Integral and non-integral citation {#def-integral}
In an **integral citation** the cited author's name is part of the sentence, usually as its subject: *Okafor and Lin (2021) found that …*; *According to Okafor and Lin (2021), …*. In a **non-integral citation** the name (or number) stands outside the grammar of the sentence, in brackets or as a number: *… revised more extensively (Okafor & Lin, 2021).* The distinction was introduced by Swales (1990).
:::

The two forms put the emphasis in different places. An integral citation makes the **researcher** the topic: it is about what Okafor and Lin did, found or argued. A non-integral citation makes the **finding** the topic and the researchers its support. Hyland (1999), comparing citations in research articles from eight disciplines, found that non-integral citation was the more common form overall and especially dominant in the sciences and engineering, where findings are presented as the shared knowledge of the field; integral citation, with reporting verbs, was relatively more frequent in the humanities and social sciences, where who argued what is often part of the point.

::: theorem Choosing the form of a citation {#rule-integral}
1. Use a **non-integral** citation when the **finding or idea** matters and who produced it is secondary, and when several studies support one claim: *Delayed feedback is associated with less revision (Okafor & Lin, 2021; Park, 2019).*
2. Use an **integral** citation when the **author** matters: to contrast authors' positions, to introduce a distinctive theory or argument, to discuss one study in detail, or to show your stance towards a source with a reporting verb: *Park (2019) argues that …, whereas Okafor and Lin (2021) …*.
3. **Vary the form for a reason, not for variety**: a paragraph of integral citations, each beginning *X (year) found …*, reads as a list of summaries, not as an argument ([[academic-writing/literature-review]]).
4. **Place a non-integral citation next to the claim it supports**: at the end of the sentence if it supports the whole sentence, after the relevant clause if it supports only part.
:::

::: proof
Point 1 explains why the sciences favour non-integral citation: the knowledge claim is meant to stand independently of its authors, and the citation is evidence for it. Point 2 explains why integral citation is the form of debate: when the question is which view is right, the holders of the views become the topic. Point 3 is the most useful in practice. Student writing often over-uses the integral pattern, because it is the easiest way to report one source at a time; experienced writers organise paragraphs by claims and use non-integral citations to support them, keeping integral ones for the places where an author's own position is the issue. Point 4 is a matter of accuracy: *Bilingual children perform better on switching tasks (Abara, 2008), probably because they practise suppressing one language* attributes only the finding to Abara; the explanation, after the citation, is the writer's.
:::

::: example Integral or non-integral? {#ex-integral}
Rewrite this fictional paragraph so that the citation forms suit their purpose. *Wang (2017) found that students used online dictionaries more than paper ones. Lee (2019) also found that students used online dictionaries more. Garcia (2020) found the same. Ahmed (2022) argues, however, that this preference reduces the depth of word learning, because students look up only the first meaning listed.*
::: solution
**Diagnosis.** Sentences 1–3 report the same finding three times, each with an integral citation and *found*. The authors are not the point: the finding is. Sentence 4 is different — it presents one author's argument, which the paragraph will presumably discuss — so the integral form is right there.

**Revision.** *Students have consistently been found to prefer online dictionaries to paper ones (Garcia, 2020; Lee, 2019; Wang, 2017). Ahmed (2022) argues, however, that this preference may reduce the depth of word learning, since students tend to look up only the first meaning listed.*

**Note.** The three studies are grouped in one non-integral citation (in APA style, in alphabetical order, separated by semicolons; other styles order them by date). *Consistently been found* states the writer's synthesis of the three. In sentence 2, *may* and *tend to* are not in the original; add them only if they report Ahmed's own stance accurately.
:::
:::

## Reporting verbs: the writer's choices

An integral citation needs a verb, and the verb carries meaning beyond *said*. Reading it is a matter of stance ([[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging#rule-reporting-verbs]]); writing it is a matter of choosing deliberately among the possibilities.

::: theorem Choosing a reporting verb {#rule-reporting}
1. **Match the verb to the kind of act** being reported (Hyland 2002): a **research act** — what the source did or found (*find*, *observe*, *show*, *measure*, *demonstrate*); a **cognition act** — what the source thinks (*believe*, *assume*, *suspect*, *view*); a **discourse act** — what the source says in the text (*argue*, *claim*, *state*, *suggest*, *propose*, *describe*, *note*).
2. **Match the verb to your stance**: factive verbs (*show*, *demonstrate*, *establish*) commit you to the claim; neutral verbs (*argue*, *propose*, *report*) report it; distancing verbs (*claim*, *assert*) suggest doubt ([[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging#rule-reporting-verbs]]).
3. **Match the verb to the source's own strength**: if the source said *may*, report it with *suggests* or *proposes*, not *shows*.
4. **Use the grammar of each verb correctly**: *argue that*, *suggest that* (✗ *suggest to*), *describe X as Y*, *attribute X to Y*, *distinguish between X and Y*, *point out that*.
5. **Choose the tense** by what you are reporting (below), and keep it consistent within a passage.
:::

::: proof
Hyland's (2002) classification explains why some verbs fit some sources and not others: a survey *finds*, a theorist *argues*, but ✗ *Chomsky found that language is innate* misreports an argument as a discovery. Point 2 matters because the verb is often the only signal of the writer's view of a source. A writer who reports every source with *claims* sounds suspicious of the whole field; one who reports every source with *shows* has no critical position at all. Point 3 is honesty towards the source: a citation that makes a cautious finding sound certain misrepresents it, and is one of the commonest faults in summaries ([[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging#rule-read-hedge]]). The grammar in point 4 is where many second-language writers slip; learner's dictionaries give each verb's patterns.
:::

The tense of reporting verbs varies between fields and between writers, but Swales and Feak (2012) describe three common tendencies:

| tense | example | typical use |
|---|---|---|
| present simple | *Park (2019) argues that …* | the source's ideas as still current; the usual choice in the humanities, and for theories and arguments |
| past simple | *Park (2019) found that …* | a specific study as a completed event; common in the sciences and social sciences for findings |
| present perfect | *Several studies have found …* | research up to now, usually with non-integral citations; common in introductions |

A citation also affects the tense of the reported claim: *Okafor and Lin (2021) found that delayed feedback **led** to less revision* reports a specific result; *… found that delayed feedback **leads** to less revision* presents it as a general truth that the writer accepts. The tenses of texts are treated in [[tense-and-aspect/tenses-in-texts]].

::: warning Mixing two ways of citing
✗ *According to Park (2019), he argues that feedback should be immediate.*
✓ *According to Park (2019), feedback should be immediate.* or ✓ *Park (2019) argues that feedback should be immediate.*

✗ *As Park (2019) mentioned, feedback should be immediate.*
✓ *As Park (2019) argues, feedback should be immediate.*

*According to X* already does the reporting, so the sentence needs no second reporting verb. *Mention* means to refer to something briefly, in passing; it suits a side remark, not a source's main argument, and over-using it makes every source sound casual. *As X argues* also commits you to agreement: use it only when you accept the claim.
:::

::: quiz
A source reports, as the result of a small pilot study, that *peer review may help some students revise*. Which report of it is most accurate?
- [ ] *Haddad (2023) proved that peer review helps students revise.*
- [ ] *Haddad (2023) claims that peer review helps students revise.*
- [x] *Haddad's (2023) pilot study suggests that peer review may help some students revise.*
- [ ] *Haddad (2023) mentioned peer review.*
::: solution
The third option keeps the source's caution (*suggests*, *may*, *some*) and signals the kind of study. *Proved* overstates the finding; *claims* adds a doubt that is the writer's, not the source's, and still drops the hedges; *mentioned* misdescribes a finding as a passing reference.
:::
:::

::: example What needs a citation? {#ex-what-cite}
Mark the places in this fictional paragraph from a student's paper in education where a citation is needed, and say why. *(1) Reading aloud to young children is a common practice in many families. (2) In a survey of 3,000 households in 2019, 62% of parents reported reading to their children at least three times a week. (3) Shared reading has been described as a "conversation around a book", in which the adult asks questions and the child answers. (4) This kind of reading has been linked to larger vocabularies at school entry. (5) In my view, the evidence is strongest for children from homes with few books. (6) The questionnaire used in this study was adapted from an earlier instrument.*
::: solution
(1) **Probably none**: a general statement that readers in the field would accept; a citation would do no harm if a source is at hand. (2) **Citation needed**: a specific survey and figure. (3) **Citation and page number needed**: the definition is quoted, so it needs quotation marks (which it has), the author and the page. (4) **Citation needed**: a research finding, probably from several studies — a non-integral citation of two or three. (5) **No citation for the view itself**, which is the writer's own; but *the evidence is strongest* refers to evidence that the reader will expect to see cited, perhaps in the next sentence. (6) **Citation needed**: the earlier instrument belongs to someone else, and may also need permission to adapt.
:::
:::

## The main citation systems

::: definition Citation systems {#def-systems}
- **Author–date** systems put the author's surname and year of publication in the text and list references alphabetically: APA style, Harvard style, and the author–date system of the *Chicago Manual of Style*.
- **Author–page** citation puts the surname and page number in the text, with an alphabetical list: MLA style.
- **Numbered** systems put a number in the text, in square brackets or as a superscript, pointing to a numbered list in order of first citation: Vancouver style in medicine, IEEE style in engineering, and many science journals' own versions.
- **Notes and bibliography** systems put references in footnotes or endnotes, usually with a bibliography: the notes system of the *Chicago Manual of Style*, common in history and some humanities.
:::

As of 2026, the current editions of the main guides are the seventh edition of the *Publication Manual of the American Psychological Association* (2020, released in late 2019), the ninth edition of the *MLA Handbook* (2021), and the eighteenth edition of *The Chicago Manual of Style* (2024). "Harvard" has no single authority: it is a family of author–date styles, and each university or publisher that asks for it publishes its own version. Vancouver style follows the recommendations of the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors, which refer to the US National Library of Medicine's *Citing Medicine*; IEEE publishes its own reference guide. Styles change between editions — APA's seventh edition, for example, changed how many authors are listed before *et al.* in the text — so check the edition your department or journal asks for, not an old handout.

The table below describes, in outline, how the main styles cite a fictional journal article by two authors in the text. It is a sketch, not a substitute for the guides.

| style | in-text citation | reference list | notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| APA 7th | *(Okafor & Lin, 2021, p. 214)*; *Okafor and Lin (2021)* | "References", alphabetical, hanging indent; author surnames and initials, year in brackets, article title in sentence case, journal title and volume in italics, DOI as a link | *&* inside brackets, *and* in the sentence; *et al.* in the text for three or more authors |
| MLA 9th | *(Okafor and Lin 214)* | "Works Cited", alphabetical; built from "core elements" — author, title of source, title of container, other contributors, version, number, publisher, date, location | no year in the text; page numbers for all specific references |
| Chicago 18th, notes | a superscript number, with a footnote giving the source and page | "Bibliography", alphabetical | shortened notes after the first citation of a source |
| Chicago 18th, author–date | *(Okafor and Lin 2021, 214)* | "References", alphabetical | similar in shape to APA, different in details of punctuation |
| Harvard (varies) | *(Okafor and Lin, 2021, p. 214)* or without the comma | "Reference list", alphabetical | follow your institution's version exactly |
| Vancouver | *[7]* or a superscript *7* | numbered in order of first citation; journal titles abbreviated | a source keeps its number each time it is cited |

::: theorem Rules shared by all systems {#rule-shared}
1. **Every citation in the text has a reference**, and (in reference-list systems) every reference is cited in the text.
2. **The citation must match the reference exactly**: the same spelling of names, the same year.
3. **Give page numbers** (or paragraph or section numbers for unpaginated sources) for quotations, and for paraphrases of specific points where your style asks for them.
4. **Be consistent**: one style, one edition, applied to every reference, including punctuation and capitalisation.
5. **Cite what you read**: if you know a source only through another, cite it as a secondary source in the form your style uses (APA: *Abara, 2008, as cited in Chen, 2022*), and list only the source you read.
6. **Take details from the source**, including the DOI where one exists, not from another reference list.
:::

::: definition DOI {#def-doi}
A **DOI** (Digital Object Identifier, 数字对象唯一标识符) is a permanent identifier given to a published item, such as *10.xxxx/abcd1234*. Unlike a web address, it continues to lead to the item if the publisher's website changes. APA style gives DOIs as links (*https://doi.org/…*); other styles also include them.
:::

::: example One source in two styles {#ex-two-styles}
A fictional article: authors Grace Okafor and Lin Jie; published 2021; title *Timing of teacher feedback and the extent of revision in EFL writing*; journal *Journal of Second Language Writing Studies* (fictional), volume 14, issue 3, pages 205–222; DOI 10.0000/jslws.2021.0143 (an invented number of the right shape). Describe how its reference would be built in APA 7th and MLA 9th, and give the in-text citation of a quotation from page 214 in each.
::: solution
**APA 7th.** Authors as surname plus initials, in the order given on the article, joined by *&*: *Okafor, G., & Lin, J.*; the year in brackets: *(2021)*; the article title in sentence case, with no italics: *Timing of teacher feedback and the extent of revision in EFL writing.*; the journal title in title case and italics, then the volume in italics and the issue in brackets: *Journal of Second Language Writing Studies, 14*(3); the page range: *205–222*; the DOI as a link. In the text: *(Okafor & Lin, 2021, p. 214)*.

Note the second author: Lin Jie writes the family name first, so the surname is *Lin* and the initial *J.* A reference manager that reads *Jie* as the surname would give *Jie, L.* — a common error to check for.

**MLA 9th.** The first author's name inverted, the second in normal order, with full given names as printed in the source: *Okafor, Grace, and …*; for an author whose family name comes first, check the *Handbook*'s guidance on such names rather than inverting by habit. Then the article title in quotation marks and title case; the container (the journal) in italics; *vol. 14, no. 3*; the year; *pp. 205–22*; and the DOI. In the text: *(Okafor and Lin 214)*.

**What to take away.** The information is the same; the order, punctuation, capitalisation and abbreviations differ. That is why the decisions are best left to a reference manager or to the guide itself, and why the writer's job is to record the information completely and check the result.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A reader sees *(Okafor and Lin 214)* in a text. Which system is the writer most probably using?
- [ ] APA 7th
- [x] MLA 9th
- [ ] Vancouver
- [ ] Chicago notes and bibliography
::: solution
Author and page with no year and no comma is the MLA pattern. APA gives the year and uses *&* in brackets; Vancouver uses a number; Chicago's notes system uses a superscript number and a footnote.
:::
:::

## Reference managers

Reference-management software stores references, imports them from databases and publishers' pages, attaches PDFs and notes, and inserts citations and a formatted reference list into a word-processor document in any of thousands of styles. Widely used examples include Zotero (free and open source), EndNote and Mendeley; many universities provide one and offer training. They save time and prevent mismatches between citations and references, but they format what they are given. Imported records are often incomplete or wrong: titles in capital letters, missing issue numbers, a conference paper recorded as a journal article, Chinese names split into the wrong given and family names, the same source imported twice. Check every reference against the source before submission, and check that the style selected is the right edition.

::: example Checking a reference list {#ex-check-list}
Find the problems in this fictional excerpt of an APA-style reference list, and say how to fix each.

> *Jie, L., & Okafor, G. (2021). TIMING OF TEACHER FEEDBACK AND THE EXTENT OF REVISION IN EFL WRITING. Journal of Second Language Writing Studies.*
>
> *Park, S. (2019). Feedback in the writing classroom. Retrieved from a website.*
>
> *Park, S. (2019). Feedback in the writing classroom. Routledge.*
::: solution
**Entry 1.** The authors are in the wrong order (Okafor is first on the article) and Lin Jie's names have been swapped, so the surname appears as *Jie*; the title is in capitals (an import error) and should be in sentence case; the volume, issue, pages and DOI are missing.

**Entry 2 and 3.** The same book has been entered twice, once with a useless "retrieved from a website" — a duplicate import. Delete entry 2. Check that entry 3 is complete for a book (APA 7th gives the publisher but no place of publication).

**And in the text.** Search the text for every citation of *Jie* and *Park*: the first must become *Okafor & Lin* everywhere, and if two different Park (2019) works really did exist, they would be distinguished as *2019a* and *2019b*.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **GB/T 7714.** Chinese journals and theses usually follow the national standard for references, GB/T 7714 (信息与文献 参考文献著录规则; the 2015 edition has been the one in general use). It offers a numbered system (顺序编码制) and an author–date system (著者-出版年制), and marks each reference with a document-type code, such as [M] for a book, [J] for a journal article, [D] for a thesis and [EB/OL] for an online resource. English-language journals do not use these codes. When you write in English, use the style the journal or department requires, even for Chinese sources.
- **Names.** In APA and most English styles, a reference gives the **surname** followed by initials: Wang Fang becomes *Wang, F.*, and Zhang Xiaoming *Zhang, X.* (some styles keep the hyphen in *Zhang, X.-M.* when the given name is hyphenated in the source). Many Chinese authors publishing in English print their names in Western order (*Fang Wang*), so check which part is the surname before entering it. Because many authors share the surnames Wang, Li, Zhang, Liu and Chen, APA style adds initials in the text when two first authors share a surname and a year would not tell them apart.
- **Citing Chinese sources in English.** Most styles give the author's name in Pinyin, the title in Pinyin (or in characters) with an English translation in square brackets, and the journal name in Pinyin; check your style's rule for non-English sources.
- **参考文献 and the reference list.** Some Chinese theses list works consulted but not cited. In author–date styles such as APA, the reference list contains only works cited in the text, and every work cited must appear in it.
- **引用 and plagiarism.** A citation does not make copied wording acceptable: wording also needs quotation marks ([[academic-writing/academic-integrity]]).
:::

::: history
Author–date citation is often called the "Harvard system". Eli Chernin, writing in the *British Medical Journal* in 1988, traced the name to a paper of 1881 by Edward Laurens Mark, a zoologist at Harvard, which cited sources by author and year in brackets and listed them at the end, a habit that spread from American zoology. The great style guides came later. The University of Chicago Press published the first edition of its *Manual of Style* in 1906; a group of psychologists published guidelines for manuscripts in the *Psychological Bulletin* in 1929, the origin of APA style; the Modern Language Association published its *Style Sheet* in 1951; and in 1978 a group of medical editors meeting in Vancouver agreed the common requirements for manuscripts that gave Vancouver style its name. In 1955 Eugene Garfield proposed counting citations to trace the influence of papers, and his *Science Citation Index* (1964) turned citations into data — the origin of citation counts and impact factors.
:::

## Where this leads

Citing is the mechanics of using sources honestly, which is the subject of the next chapter, [[academic-writing/academic-integrity]]. Using many citations to build an argument, rather than a list, is taught in [[academic-writing/literature-review]], and comparing your results with cited studies in [[academic-writing/discussion-conclusions]]. ::: summary
- A **citation** in the text points to a **reference** in a list; together they give credit, allow checking and mark which claims are yours ([[#def-citation]]).
- Cite **ideas, findings, specific facts, wording, data and instruments** from others, and your own earlier work; common knowledge in the field needs no citation ([[#rule-what-to-cite]]).
- Use **non-integral** citations when the finding matters, **integral** ones when the author's position matters, and organise paragraphs by claims rather than by sources ([[#def-integral]], [[#rule-integral]]).
- Choose **reporting verbs** by the kind of act, your stance and the source's own strength, with the right grammar and a consistent tense ([[#rule-reporting]]).
- The main systems are **author–date** (APA 7th, Harvard, Chicago), **author–page** (MLA 9th), **numbered** (Vancouver, IEEE) and **notes** (Chicago); use the edition your department or journal requires ([[#def-systems]]).
- In every system: one reference per citation, exact matches, page numbers for quotations, consistency, secondary citations marked, details taken from the source ([[#rule-shared]]).
- **Reference managers** save time but must be checked, especially for duplicates, capitalisation and the order of Chinese names.
:::

::: exercise From integral to non-integral {level=2}
Rewrite as one sentence with a non-integral citation in APA style. *Kim (2015) found that bilingual children were faster at switching tasks. Abara (2008) found this too. So did Novak (2019).*
::: solution
*Bilingual children have been found to perform switching tasks faster than monolingual children (Abara, 2008; Kim, 2015; Novak, 2019).* (APA orders multiple citations alphabetically; *than monolingual children* states the comparison the original implied — check that the sources really compared with monolinguals.)
:::

::: exercise Secondary citation {level=2}
You have read Chen (2022), which reports on page 19 that Abara (2008) found an effect. You could not obtain Abara (2008). Write the in-text citation in APA style, and say which work goes in the reference list.
::: solution
*Abara (2008, as cited in Chen, 2022, p. 19) found that …* or, non-integrally, *(Abara, 2008, as cited in Chen, 2022, p. 19)*. Only Chen (2022) goes in the reference list, because that is the work you read.
:::

::: exercise Revise a paragraph's citations {level=3}
Revise this fictional paragraph so that the citations support an argument rather than listing sources, choosing integral and non-integral forms and reporting verbs deliberately. *Smith (2010) said that homework improves achievement. Jones (2012) said homework improves achievement in secondary schools. Brown (2015) said homework does not improve achievement in primary schools. Taylor (2018) said the effect depends on the age of the pupils. Green (2020) said the same as Taylor.*
::: hint
What is the claim that all five sources together support? Which source offers the explanation, and which deserves to be named in the sentence?
:::
::: solution
A model: *The effect of homework on achievement appears to depend on pupils' age. Studies in secondary schools have found positive effects (Jones, 2012; Smith, 2010), whereas research in primary schools has found little or no benefit (Brown, 2015). Taylor (2018) proposes that the difference reflects older pupils' greater capacity for independent study, an explanation supported by Green's (2020) later findings.* The paragraph now opens with the writer's synthesis; the studies that agree are grouped non-integrally; Taylor is cited integrally because the explanation is Taylor's; *said* has been replaced by verbs that fit each act (*found*, *proposes*). Check that Smith (2010) did study secondary schools before grouping it with Jones: the original does not say.
:::

::: exercise A reference list {level=3}
Take five sources from your own reading — at least one book, one journal article with a DOI, one chapter in an edited book and one web document — and write a reference list for them in the style your department uses, from the current edition of its guide. Then write one sentence citing each source in the text, using at least two integral and two non-integral citations.
::: solution
Answers will vary. Check against the guide itself: the order and punctuation of elements, capitalisation of titles, italics, the treatment of editors in the book chapter, DOIs as the style requires, alphabetical order (or numbering), and an exact match between each in-text citation and its reference. Check names of Chinese authors especially carefully. A reference manager may be used, but the output must be checked line by line.
:::
