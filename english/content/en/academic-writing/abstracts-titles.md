Two abstracts for the same fictional study, the vocabulary-app study of [[academic-writing/methods-results]]:

> **Version A.** *Vocabulary is very important in learning English. With the development of technology, more and more students use apps. In this paper, the effect of a vocabulary app on students is studied. The design of the study, the results and their implications for teaching will be discussed.*
>
> **Version B.** *Spaced-repetition apps are widely used by learners of English, but there is little evidence of their effect on the vocabulary taught in university courses. This study compared the vocabulary gains of 64 first-year English majors at one Chinese university, two classes of whom used a spaced-repetition app for 15 minutes a day for eight weeks while two did not. App users gained more words on a 60-item test of textbook vocabulary than non-users (9.4 against 6.2; d = 0.98), but four weeks after use ended the advantage had narrowed and was no longer statistically significant. The findings suggest that such apps may support vocabulary learning while they are used, but that their benefit may depend on continued review.*

Version A tells the reader what the paper is **about**; Version B tells the reader what it **found**. Version A could have been written before the study was done, and a reader who finishes it knows nothing they could cite. Version B gives the gap, the design, the sample, the main result with its size, the result that went against the hypothesis and a hedged conclusion, in about 110 words. Most readers of a paper read only its title and abstract, and decide from them whether to read further; databases show the abstract, and other writers cite from it. That is why the abstract and the title are the most-read parts of a paper, and why they are usually written last, with great care.

This chapter is about writing them: informative and indicative abstracts, the moves of an abstract and their tenses, structured abstracts, abstracts in the humanities, titles and keywords. Reading abstracts critically is taught in [[academic-reading/academic-texts#rule-reading-abstract]].

## Informative and indicative abstracts

::: definition Informative and indicative abstracts {#def-informative}
An **informative abstract** (报道性摘要) reports the content of a paper: its purpose, method, main results and conclusion, so that a reader can learn the main findings without reading the paper. An **indicative abstract** (指示性摘要) describes what the paper is about and what it contains (*This paper discusses …*, *The implications are considered*) without stating the findings. Research articles in most fields expect informative abstracts; indicative ones suit some review articles, book chapters and conference programmes. The basic definition of an abstract is in [[academic-reading/academic-texts#def-abstract]].
:::

Hyland (2000), studying abstracts in eight disciplines, described five moves that abstracts contain in various combinations: **Introduction** (context, motivation), **Purpose** (aim, question, thesis), **Method** (design, sample, procedure), **Product** (results, the argument) and **Conclusion** (interpretation, implications). He found that the purpose, method and product moves were the most frequent and that a large share of abstracts in the sciences consisted mainly of them, while abstracts in the humanities and social sciences more often included an introduction move to establish why the topic mattered.

::: theorem Writing an informative abstract {#rule-abstract}
1. **Include all five moves** for an empirical paper, with the **product** (results) as the longest; combine introduction and purpose where space is short.
2. **Give the essentials of the method**: design, sample size and kind, setting, main measures.
3. **State the main results specifically**, with key numbers where the field expects them, including important negative or unexpected results.
4. **End with a conclusion calibrated to the results**, not stronger than the discussion.
5. **Make it self-contained**: no citations (in most fields), no undefined abbreviations, no references to tables or figures, nothing that is not in the paper.
6. **Keep to the word limit** of the journal or institution (often 150–250 words for articles; dissertations may allow more).
7. **Use the conventional tenses**: past for what was done and found (*compared*, *gained*); present for the conclusion and for what the paper itself does (*suggest*, *This paper argues*); present or present perfect for background (*are widely used*, *has received little attention*).
:::

::: proof
Points 1–3 follow from the abstract's readers: those deciding whether to read on need the purpose and method to judge relevance and quality, and those who will never read on need the results. An abstract without results — the indicative pattern — fails both, which is why so many guides warn against sentences such as *The results are discussed*. Point 4 matters because abstracts are where overclaiming is most visible and most often repeated by others ([[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]]): a reader who sees *apps improve vocabulary* in an abstract may cite it so. Point 5 reflects how abstracts are used — separately from the paper, in databases and search results, where a citation or *Table 2* means nothing. The tense pattern of point 7 mirrors the sections the moves come from ([[academic-writing/methods-results]]).
:::

::: example Writing an abstract from a paper {#ex-write-abstract}
Write an abstract of about 150 words for the fictional interview study of [[academic-writing/methods-results#ex-method]]: eight teachers of English at two secondary schools in Chengdu, four with less than five years' experience and four with more than fifteen; semi-structured interviews in Chinese, analysed thematically. Main findings: seven of the eight teachers switched to Chinese to explain grammar, mostly because of time pressure; less experienced teachers expressed more unease about switching; all used English for routine instructions.
::: solution
**A model:** *English-only policies are common in Chinese secondary schools, yet little is known about how teachers themselves account for their use of Chinese in English lessons. This study explored the reasons that teachers give for switching between English and Chinese, and whether these differ with experience. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with eight teachers at two secondary schools in Chengdu, four with less than five years' experience and four with more than fifteen, and analysed thematically. All eight reported using English for routine classroom instructions, and seven described switching to Chinese to explain grammar, most often attributing this to the pressure of covering the syllabus. Less experienced teachers expressed more unease about switching than their more experienced colleagues. The findings suggest that teachers' language choices are shaped as much by time constraints as by beliefs about language learning, and that policy guidance may need to address both.* (about 150 words)

**Check.** Introduction and gap (sentence 1); purpose (2); method with sample and analysis (3); product (4–5), the longest move; conclusion hedged (*suggest*, *may*). Past tense for the study, present for the background and the conclusion. No citations, no abbreviations, nothing not in the study.
:::
:::

::: warning The abstract that promises
✗ *The results of the study are presented and discussed, and some implications for teaching are suggested.*

✓ *Students who received feedback within a week revised more extensively than those who received it later, but the timing of feedback had no effect on final grades. Teachers may therefore be able to improve revision by returning drafts sooner.*

Sentences that describe the paper's contents (*are presented*, *are discussed*, *will be examined*) turn an informative abstract into an indicative one at the point where readers most want information. If you find such a sentence in your draft, ask what the results **are** and write them instead. The future tense (*will be discussed*) is a sign of an abstract written for a proposal or a conference before the study was finished.
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is best suited to the product move of an informative abstract?
- [ ] *The results are analysed in detail.*
- [ ] *Table 3 shows that the intervention worked.*
- [x] *Pupils in the intervention schools read on average 40% more books over the year than pupils in the comparison schools.*
- [ ] *This proves that reading programmes are always effective.*
::: solution
The third sentence reports a specific result with its size. The first is indicative; the second refers to a table, which the abstract reader cannot see, and does not say what *worked* means; the fourth overclaims.
:::
:::

## Structured abstracts

::: definition Structured abstract {#def-structured}
A **structured abstract** (结构式摘要) divides the abstract into labelled parts, such as *Background*, *Objective* (or *Aim*), *Methods*, *Results* and *Conclusions*. It became the norm in clinical medicine after a 1987 proposal in the *Annals of Internal Medicine*, and is now required by many journals in medicine, health sciences, psychology and education. The headings vary by journal: some add *Design*, *Setting*, *Participants* or *Limitations*.
:::

A structured abstract makes the moves explicit, which makes omissions obvious: a writer cannot leave out the results when there is a heading for them. It is also easier to scan. It is usually somewhat longer than an unstructured abstract, and sentences under the headings are often shortened (*Objective: To compare …*, with an infinitive rather than a full sentence), as the journal's examples show.

::: example From unstructured to structured {#ex-structured}
Rewrite Version B of the vocabulary-app abstract (at the start of this chapter) as a structured abstract with the headings *Background*, *Aim*, *Methods*, *Results* and *Conclusions*.
::: solution
***Background.*** *Spaced-repetition apps are widely used by learners of English, but there is little evidence of their effect on the vocabulary taught in university courses.*

***Aim.*** *To compare the vocabulary gains of students who did and did not use a spaced-repetition app, and to test whether any advantage lasted after use ended.*

***Methods.*** *Sixty-four first-year English majors in four intact classes at one Chinese university took part. Two classes (n = 33) used the app for at least 15 minutes a day for eight weeks; two (n = 31) followed the usual course. Vocabulary was measured with a 60-item test of textbook words before, immediately after and four weeks after the eight weeks.*

***Results.*** *App users gained more words than non-users at the immediate post-test (9.4 vs 6.2; d = 0.98). At the delayed test the difference had narrowed (7.1 vs 5.8) and was not statistically significant.*

***Conclusions.*** *Such apps may support vocabulary learning while they are used, but their benefit may depend on continued review. Randomised studies over longer periods are needed.*

**Notes.** The aim is an infinitive phrase, a common convention under *Aim* or *Objective*. The method gains detail the unstructured version left out (the delayed test). The results keep the numbers. *Randomised studies over longer periods are needed* is specific enough to be useful; journals differ on whether further research belongs in the abstract.
:::
:::

## Abstracts in the humanities

Papers in history, literary studies, philosophy and much theoretical work do not report experiments, and their abstracts follow the argument rather than IMRaD. The moves are still there in a different form: a context or debate (introduction), the question or thesis (purpose), the material and approach (method), the argument's main steps and claims (product), and the significance (conclusion). The present tense dominates, because the abstract reports what the paper argues: *This article argues that …*, *Drawing on …, I show that …*. The first person is common.

::: example A humanities abstract {#ex-humanities}
Write an abstract of about 120 words for a fictional article in literary studies, which argues that the letters in a nineteenth-century English novel are used not only to advance the plot but to show how characters misread one another, and that this reflects anxieties about the expanding postal system of the time.
::: solution
**A model:** *Critics have long noted the number of letters in [novel], but have treated them chiefly as devices of plot. This article argues that the novel's letters are better understood as scenes of misreading. Drawing on close analysis of six letters and on contemporary commentary on the postal reforms of the period, I show that each letter is interpreted by its recipient in ways its writer did not intend, and that these misreadings increase as the novel's characters come to rely on correspondence rather than conversation. The novel thus registers a wider anxiety about communication at a distance. Reading its letters in this way, I suggest, offers a model for the study of correspondence in the fiction of the period.*

**Moves.** Introduction (the critical debate and its limitation), purpose and thesis (*This article argues that*), method (*Drawing on close analysis … and on contemporary commentary*), product (*I show that …*; *The novel thus registers …*), conclusion (*offers a model*). The present tense and *I* are the conventions of the field; the claims are interpretive and stated as arguments, not as findings.
:::
:::

## Titles

A title is the shortest summary of a paper and the first thing a reader or a search engine meets. Most research titles belong to a few types:

- **nominal** (noun phrase): *Effects of a spaced-repetition app on vocabulary gains in first-year English majors*;
- **compound** (two parts, usually with a colon): *Learning words or learning the app? Spaced repetition and vocabulary in a university English course*;
- **full-sentence** (declarative): *Spaced-repetition apps increase short-term but not long-term vocabulary gains* — common in some biological and medical journals, rare elsewhere;
- **question**: *Do vocabulary apps help university learners?*

::: theorem Writing a title {#rule-title}
1. **Be specific**: name the main variables or topic, the population or material, and where relevant the design (*a randomised trial*, *a corpus study*).
2. **Put the key terms first** or early, and use the words a researcher would search for.
3. **Cut empty openings**: *A study of*, *An investigation into*, *Some observations on*, *Research on* usually add nothing.
4. **Match the claim to the evidence**: a full-sentence title states a finding as fact and is suitable only for a clear, well-supported result; a question title should be answered by the paper.
5. **Avoid abbreviations** (except very well-known ones) and jargon that readers outside the immediate specialism will not recognise.
6. **Use a colon** to combine a topic with a specification or an engaging phrase with an informative one, but make sure the informative part carries the key terms.
7. **Follow the journal's style** for capitalisation (title case or sentence case) and length.
:::

::: proof
Points 1 and 2 serve the two readers of a title, the human and the database: a specific title tells the human whether to read on, and key terms early make it easy to find and to scan in a list of results. Point 3 follows from the title's job: every paper is a study, so *A study of* conveys nothing. Swales and Feak (2012) give similar advice and note that research titles have become longer and more specific over time. Point 4 applies calibration ([[academic-writing/discussion-conclusions]]) to the most visible sentence of the paper. Point 6 explains the popularity of compound titles in the humanities and social sciences: an evocative first part, perhaps a quotation from the material, followed by an informative second part, so that the title can attract a reader and still be found.
:::

::: example Improving titles {#ex-titles}
Improve each fictional title. (a) *A Study of Some Problems of English Learning* (b) *The Effect of Using an App* (c) *An Investigation into the Relationship between Students' Use of Short-Video Apps in the Evening and Their Sleep Quality and Mood during Their First Semester at University* (d) *Short videos destroy students' sleep*
::: solution
(a) Too vague: which problems, which learners? *Difficulties with English articles among Chinese secondary-school learners: an error analysis*. (b) Which app, effect on what, for whom? *Effects of a spaced-repetition app on vocabulary gains in first-year English majors*. (c) Too long, with an empty opening; the key terms come late: *Evening short-video use, sleep quality and mood in first-year undergraduates*. (d) Overclaims (*destroy*, a causal claim from what was a correlational study) and is too informal: *Evening short-video use and sleep quality in first-year undergraduates* — or, as a question, *Is evening short-video use associated with poorer sleep in first-year undergraduates?*
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which is the best title for a corpus study of hedges in 200 abstracts from English-medium and Chinese-medium applied linguistics journals?
- [ ] *An Investigation of Hedging*
- [ ] *Chinese writers do not hedge enough*
- [x] *Hedging in English-medium and Chinese-medium applied linguistics abstracts: a corpus-based comparison*
- [ ] *Some observations on abstracts*
::: solution
The third title names the feature, the two sets of texts, the field and the method, with the key terms first. The first and fourth are too vague and start with empty phrases; the second states a generalisation about a group of writers as fact, which a comparison of abstracts cannot support.
:::
:::

### Keywords

Many journals ask for four to six **keywords** (关键词) to be printed with the abstract and used for indexing. Choose terms that a researcher would search for, include the main concepts, method and population, and — since words in the title are usually indexed already — many journals advise adding terms that are not in the title, such as a synonym or a broader field. Follow the journal's instructions on whether to use a list of controlled terms.

::: note English and Chinese
- **摘要 and *abstract*.** Chinese journals usually print a Chinese abstract (摘要) with 关键词 and an English abstract with *Keywords*. The English abstract is often a translation of the Chinese, which carries Chinese conventions into English. The Chinese national standard for abstracts, GB/T 6447-1986 (文摘编写规则), distinguishes 报道性文摘 (informative), 指示性文摘 (indicative) and a mixed type; English-language journals in most fields expect the informative kind.
- **本文 and *This paper*.** *本文对……进行了研究* becomes ✗ *This paper makes a study on …* or *In this paper, the author studies …*. Better: *This study examines …*, or simply begin with the gap or the purpose.
- **Tense.** Chinese abstracts need no tense marking, so English versions often use the present throughout (*Sixty students take part …*). Use the past for what was done and found, the present for the conclusion ([[#rule-abstract]], point 7).
- **……的研究 and *A Study of*.** Chinese titles often end with 研究 or 探析, or begin with 浅析, 初探 or 论. Translated, these give *A Study of …*, *A Tentative Analysis of …*, *On …*. English research titles usually leave such words out (*On* survives in some humanities titles), and modesty is expressed by hedging the claims, not by the title.
- **Capitals.** English titles use title case (*Effects of Feedback on Revision*) or sentence case (*Effects of feedback on revision*) according to the journal; Chinese has no equivalent choice, so check the style.
:::

::: history
Abstracts began as a service for readers who could not keep up with the journals: abstracting journals such as *Chemical Abstracts*, founded in 1907, summarised the literature of a field for its researchers. Over the twentieth century authors' own abstracts, printed with their articles, became standard, and with online databases from the 1970s onwards the abstract became the part of a paper that most readers see. In 1987 an ad hoc working group proposed structured abstracts for clinical articles in the *Annals of Internal Medicine*, and many medical journals adopted them within a few years. The genre itself was described by genre analysts: Vijay Bhatia's *Analysing Genre* (1993) and Ken Hyland's *Disciplinary Discourses* (2000) analysed its moves, and Swales and Feak's *Abstracts and the Writing of Abstracts* (2009) made that analysis into a guide for writers.
:::

## Where this leads

The abstract and title are written last but read first, so they pass first through the hands of editors and reviewers, the subject of the last chapter, [[academic-writing/peer-review]]. Each move of the abstract compresses a section of the paper: [[academic-writing/research-questions]], [[academic-writing/methods-results]] and [[academic-writing/discussion-conclusions]]. Reading abstracts critically is part of [[academic-reading/academic-texts]].

::: summary
- An **informative abstract** reports purpose, method, results and conclusion; an **indicative** one only describes contents, and is rarely right for a research article ([[#def-informative]]).
- Its moves are **introduction, purpose, method, product, conclusion** (Hyland 2000); the **product** move deserves the most space.
- An abstract is **self-contained**, within the **word limit**, with **past** tense for what was done and found and **present** for the conclusion; its conclusion is **calibrated** to the results ([[#rule-abstract]]).
- **Structured abstracts** label the moves and are standard in medicine and many related fields ([[#def-structured]]).
- **Humanities abstracts** follow the argument, mostly in the present tense, often with *I*.
- A **title** is specific, puts key terms early, cuts empty openings, matches its claim to the evidence and follows the journal's style ([[#rule-title]]); **keywords** add search terms.
:::
