A student's first draft of a method section (for a fictional study) reads:

> *We gave some students a vocabulary test before and after using the app for a while. The test had a lot of questions. Then we compared the scores using statistics.*

Her supervisor writes one comment in the margin: *Could someone repeat this study from what you have written?* The answer is no. A reader does not know how many students took part or how they were chosen, which app was used, for how long and how often, what the test contained, whether there was a comparison group, or which statistical test was used. The revision reads:

> *Participants were 64 first-year English majors at one university in Nanjing, recruited from four intact classes. Two classes (n = 33) used a spaced-repetition vocabulary app for at least 15 minutes a day for eight weeks; the other two (n = 31) followed the usual course without the app. Vocabulary was measured before and after the eight weeks with a 60-item test of words from the course textbook, in which participants chose the meaning of each word from four options. Gains were compared between the groups with an independent-samples t-test.*

Nothing in the revision is interpretation, and nothing is hedged: it is a plain report of what was done. That is what makes it credible. A reader can now judge the design — intact classes, not random assignment; a test of textbook words, which may favour the app if it used the same list — and could repeat it. The method section exists so that readers can evaluate and reproduce the work, and the results section so that they can see what was found before being told what it means.

This chapter is about those two sections: what each contains, the conventions of tense and voice in each, how to report numbers and statistics, how to write about tables and figures, and where results end and discussion begins. The shape of the research article as a whole is described in [[academic-reading/academic-texts#def-imrad]], and reading numbers and statistics in [[academic-reading/reading-data]].

## The method section

::: definition Method section {#def-method}
The **method section** (研究方法, also called *Methods*, *Methodology*, *Materials and methods* or *Procedure*) describes how a study was carried out, in enough detail for a competent reader to judge its validity and, in principle, to repeat it. In empirical studies it typically covers the **design**, the **participants** or **materials** and how they were selected, the **instruments** or measures, the **procedure**, the **analysis**, and **ethical** approval and consent.
:::

::: theorem What to report in a method section {#rule-method}
1. **Design**: the kind of study (experiment, survey, case study, corpus analysis, interviews) and its main features (groups, conditions, time points).
2. **Participants or materials**: who or what, how many, how chosen, with the characteristics that matter for the question (age, proficiency, setting), and how many dropped out.
3. **Instruments and measures**: what was measured and how — tests, questionnaires, interview guides, coding schemes — with their source, and evidence of reliability where relevant.
4. **Procedure**: what happened, in what order, for how long, under what conditions, administered by whom.
5. **Analysis**: how the data were prepared and analysed — the statistical tests, the software, or the method of qualitative coding.
6. **Ethics**: approval by an ethics committee, informed consent, and protection of participants' identity.
7. **Justify choices** that a reader might question: why this sample, this measure, this analysis.
:::

::: proof
The method section answers the question every critical reader asks first: can these results be trusted? Points 1–5 give the information needed to judge that and to repeat the study; point 6 shows that the research was conducted responsibly ([[academic-writing/academic-integrity]]); point 7 anticipates the reviewer's objections, which is why expert method sections contain short justifications (*because existing scales did not distinguish …*). Many fields publish **reporting guidelines** that list what to include for each kind of study, such as CONSORT for randomised trials, STROBE for observational studies in epidemiology and COREQ for interview studies, and APA style includes Journal Article Reporting Standards for quantitative and qualitative research. A student writer who checks a draft against the relevant guideline finds most omissions at once.
:::

### Tense and voice in methods

::: theorem Tense and voice in a method section {#rule-method-tense}
1. Use the **past simple** for what was done: *Participants **completed** the questionnaire online.*
2. Use the **passive** or the **active with *we*** according to your field ([[academic-writing/academic-style#rule-voice]]): *The interviews **were recorded** …* / *We **recorded** the interviews …*; keep one choice for the actor throughout.
3. Use the **present simple** for things that are still true: the content of an instrument, a standard procedure, a statistical property: *The scale **consists of** 20 items*; *The test **is** widely used in …*.
4. In a **proposal**, use the future or the present: *Participants **will be** recruited …*. Change these to the past in the final report.
5. Give **numbers, durations and sources** exactly, and **do not hedge** the description of your own procedure.
:::

::: proof
The past simple fits because the method reports finished events at a definite time — the time of the study. Point 3 marks a distinction a reader uses: *The scale consisted of 20 items* suggests the version used in this study, perhaps modified; *The scale consists of 20 items* describes the instrument as it exists. Point 4 matters to students who turn a proposal into a dissertation: a method chapter still written in the future (*will be interviewed*) tells the examiner that the text was not revised. Point 5 repeats [[modality/hedging-boosting#rule-what-to-hedge]]: *We may have interviewed 30 teachers* would make a reader doubt the whole study.
:::

::: example Revising a method paragraph {#ex-method}
Revise this fictional method paragraph from a dissertation in education, and say what each change does. *I will interview some teachers about their use of English in class. The interviews were done in Chinese. Then I am going to analyse them with thematic analysis. The teachers maybe had different experience.*
::: solution
**Revision:** *Semi-structured interviews were conducted with eight teachers of English at two secondary schools in Chengdu, selected to include teachers with less than five years' experience (n = 4) and more than fifteen (n = 4). Each interview lasted 40–60 minutes and was conducted in Chinese, the teachers' first language, so that they could express themselves fully. The interviews were recorded, transcribed and translated into English by the researcher, and the transcripts were analysed thematically following Braun and Clarke's (2006) six phases.*

**Changes.** Tenses: the mixture of future (*will interview*, *am going to*) and past becomes consistent past simple. Precision: *some teachers* becomes the number, schools and city; *different experience* becomes the selection criterion, stated as a fact, not hedged (*maybe had*). Justification: the reason for interviewing in Chinese is given. Procedure and analysis: duration, recording, transcription, translation, and a cited method of analysis. Voice: passives keep the interviews as the topic; *by the researcher* records who translated, which matters because translation is an interpretive step.
:::
:::

::: warning The method section as a story
✗ *First we had a meeting with the head teacher, who was very helpful. Then we designed the questionnaire, which took a long time because it was difficult to decide on the questions. After that we went to the school …*

✓ *The questionnaire (Appendix A) was developed from two existing instruments (Author, year; Author, year) and piloted with 15 pupils; three ambiguous items were revised. It was administered in class in March …*

A method section is not a diary of the project. Readers need what was done and why, in an order that makes the design clear (participants, instruments, procedure, analysis), not the history of how the researchers arrived at it, their feelings, or who was helpful (thanks belong in the acknowledgements). Difficulties belong in the method only if they affected the data — and then they must be reported.
:::

## The results section

::: definition Results section {#def-results}
The **results section** (研究结果) reports what the study found: the data, summarised and analysed, in an order that answers the research questions, usually with tables and figures. In the IMRaD pattern it reports findings **without interpreting** them; interpretation belongs in the discussion. Some fields and journals combine the two in a single *Results and discussion* section, and qualitative studies often present *Findings* in which description and interpretation are closely linked.
:::

::: theorem Writing a results section {#rule-results}
1. **Follow the research questions**: report the results for RQ1, then RQ2, with subheadings if there are several.
2. **Lead with the finding**, not with the table: *The app group gained more words than the comparison group (Table 2)*, rather than *Table 2 shows the results*.
3. **Give the numbers** that support each finding: means and standard deviations, counts and percentages, test statistics, *p*-values and effect sizes, in the format your style requires.
4. **Report all the results that bear on the questions**, including non-significant ones and those that go against your hypothesis.
5. **Do not repeat** every number in a table in the text; highlight the main points and refer the reader to the table for the rest.
6. **Use the past simple for findings** (*scored*, *reported*, *differed*) and the **present for what tables and figures show** (*Table 2 presents …*).
7. **Keep interpretation out**: no explanations, no comparison with other studies, no implications — or only brief signals if your field combines results and discussion.
:::

::: proof
Points 1 and 2 make the section usable: a reader looking for the answer to a question finds it in the expected place, stated in words, with the table as evidence. Point 3 follows from the reader who will check ([[academic-writing/academic-style#rule-expert-reader]]): *higher* means little without the size of the difference and its uncertainty. Point 4 is a matter of integrity: reporting only the results that support a hypothesis misrepresents the study, a practice that has been identified as one cause of findings that later fail to replicate. Point 6 reflects the two times involved: the findings happened in the study (past), but the table is in front of the reader now (present). Point 7 keeps the IMRaD division of labour: in the results, the reader can see the data before being told what the writer thinks they mean, and judge both.
:::

### Reporting numbers

Conventions for numbers differ by style; APA style, widely used in the social sciences, gives a typical set. Statistical symbols are in italics (*M*, *SD*, *t*, *p*, *n*); test statistics are followed by degrees of freedom in brackets and the value; exact *p*-values are given to two or three decimal places (*p* = .032), with *p* < .001 for very small values; and APA omits the zero before the decimal point for values that cannot exceed 1, such as *p*-values and correlations. An effect size and, where possible, a confidence interval show how large and how precise an effect is, which a *p*-value alone does not ([[academic-reading/reading-data#rule-significance]]). A result in the text might read: *The app group gained more words (M = 9.4, SD = 3.1) than the comparison group (M = 6.2, SD = 3.4), t(62) = 3.93, p < .001, d = 0.98.*

::: example Data commentary on a table {#ex-commentary}
Write the text that would accompany this fictional table in a results section answering *RQ1: Did students who used the app gain more vocabulary than those who did not?*

| group | n | pre-test M (SD) | post-test M (SD) | gain M (SD) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| app | 33 | 31.2 (6.0) | 40.6 (6.4) | 9.4 (3.1) |
| comparison | 31 | 30.8 (5.7) | 37.0 (6.1) | 6.2 (3.4) |
::: solution
**A model:** *Table 1 presents the pre-test, post-test and gain scores of the two groups. The groups scored similarly on the pre-test (app: M = 31.2, SD = 6.0; comparison: M = 30.8, SD = 5.7). Both groups improved over the eight weeks, but the app group's gain (M = 9.4, SD = 3.1) was larger than the comparison group's (M = 6.2, SD = 3.4), a difference of 3.2 words out of 60.* [The statistical test would follow, as in the example sentence above.]

**What the commentary does.** It locates the table (*Table 1 presents* — present tense); establishes that the groups started at the same level, which the reader needs before comparing gains; states the main finding in words with its numbers (past tense); and gives the size of the difference in terms the reader can picture. It does not explain *why* the app group gained more, does not call the app *effective* and does not compare with other studies: that is for the discussion. Swales and Feak (2012) describe this sequence — a location statement, a summary, and a highlighting of the main points — as the typical structure of data commentary.
:::
:::

::: example Reporting a result that went against the hypothesis {#ex-negative}
The study above also asked *RQ2: Did the app group retain more of their gain at a delayed test four weeks later?* It hypothesised that they would. The delayed-test gains were app *M* = 7.1, *SD* = 3.6; comparison *M* = 5.8, *SD* = 3.5; the difference was not statistically significant (*p* = .15). Write the result.
::: solution
**A model:** *At the delayed test four weeks later, the app group retained a gain of M = 7.1 words (SD = 3.6) and the comparison group a gain of M = 5.8 (SD = 3.5). The difference between the groups was smaller than at the immediate post-test and was not statistically significant, t(62) = 1.46, p = .15. Hypothesis 2 was therefore not supported.*

**Points to note.** The result is reported as fully as the positive one, with the test statistic. *Not statistically significant* is not the same as *no difference*: the app group still gained more on average, but the study cannot rule out chance as the explanation. The sentence *Hypothesis 2 was therefore not supported* states the outcome plainly. Speculation about why (forgetting, the end of the app use) waits for the discussion.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence belongs in a **results** section rather than a discussion?
- [ ] *This suggests that spaced repetition may be particularly effective for low-frequency words.*
- [x] *Gains were larger for low-frequency words (M = 5.2) than for high-frequency words (M = 4.2).*
- [ ] *This finding is consistent with earlier research on spaced practice (Author, year).*
- [ ] *Teachers may therefore wish to recommend vocabulary apps to their students.*
::: solution
The second sentence reports a finding with its numbers. The first interprets it, the third compares it with other studies, and the fourth draws an implication — all moves of the discussion ([[academic-writing/discussion-conclusions]]).
:::
:::

### Qualitative findings

Reports of interview, observation and textual studies present **themes** or **categories** rather than numbers, and support them with **extracts** from the data. Each theme is named and defined, its prevalence indicated where useful (*seven of the eight teachers*), and illustrated with short quotations from participants, identified by pseudonyms or codes. Description and interpretation are more closely linked than in quantitative results, but the same principle holds: the reader should see the evidence for each claim.

::: example Reporting an interview theme {#ex-theme}
Write a short paragraph reporting a theme from the fictional interview study in [[#ex-method]]: most teachers said they switched to Chinese to explain grammar, mainly because of time pressure. Use two invented, translated extracts.
::: solution
**A model:** ***Switching to save time.*** *Seven of the eight teachers described switching to Chinese to explain grammar, and six of them linked this to the pressure of covering the syllabus. As Teacher C (with 18 years' experience) put it, "If I explain the passive in English, it takes twenty minutes and half of them still don't get it. In Chinese it takes five." Less experienced teachers expressed more unease about the practice: "I know I should use English, but there just isn't time" (Teacher F, 3 years).*

**What the paragraph does.** It names the theme, gives its prevalence, links it to a reason the teachers themselves gave, and supports it with two extracts identified by codes and a relevant characteristic. It notes a difference between groups, which the design (selecting by experience) was meant to reveal. The extracts are translations and should be marked as such in the method section; quoting the Chinese originals in an appendix allows readers to check the translation.
:::
:::

## Where results end and discussion begins

The boundary is clearest in experimental fields and least clear in qualitative and humanities research, but writers in every field should know which kind of sentence they are writing. A results sentence says *what was found*; a discussion sentence says *what it means*, *why it happened*, *how it compares with other studies* or *what follows from it*. Verbs are a guide: *scored*, *reported*, *increased*, *differed*, *was associated with* report; *suggests*, *may reflect*, *is consistent with*, *implies* interpret. A results section that contains *suggests* every few lines has started the discussion too early.

::: note English and Chinese
- **Tense in reports.** Chinese marks time with words and context, not with verb forms, so writers often mix past and present in a method or results section without noticing (✗ *Participants complete the test and then they were interviewed*). Check every finite verb in these sections: what was done and found is past; what an instrument or table contains is present.
- **Passive and 被.** Method sections in English use many neutral passives (*The data were analysed*). They should not be avoided because the Chinese 被 passive suggests misfortune ([[academic-reading/paraphrase-and-quotation]]), and they should not be translated back with 被; Chinese method sections usually express the same meaning without it (数据采用……进行分析).
- **本研究 and *we*.** Where a Chinese method section writes 本研究采用问卷调查法, English may write *A questionnaire survey was used* or *We used a questionnaire* — not ✗ *This study adopted the questionnaire survey method*, which is understandable but unidiomatic.
- **显著 and *significant*.** In Chinese academic prose 显著 is used both for statistical significance and, more loosely, for 'marked'. In an English results section, *significant* is understood as *statistically significant*; use *considerable*, *marked* or *large* otherwise.
- **Numbers.** 万 and 亿 must be converted (3.2万 = 32,000; 1.5亿 = 150 million). English uses a comma as the thousands separator and a full stop as the decimal point. And *a rise from 20% to 30%* is a rise of 10 **percentage points** (50 per cent) ([[essay-writing/describing-data#rule-percentage-points]]).
:::

::: history
The detailed method section has its origins in the seventeenth century. The historian of science Steven Shapin (1984) argued that Robert Boyle's long, circumstantial reports of his air-pump experiments were a "literary technology" of *virtual witnessing*: by describing apparatus and procedure so fully, Boyle allowed readers who were not present to witness the experiments in imagination and trust their results. The statistical apparatus of the modern results section came much later. Ronald Fisher's *Statistical Methods for Research Workers* (1925) popularised significance testing and the 5% level, and their use spread through the experimental sciences. Concern about selective and incomplete reporting led to reporting guidelines — CONSORT for clinical trials (1996), APA's Journal Article Reporting Standards (2008) — and in 2016 the American Statistical Association issued a statement warning against reading *p*-values as measures of the size or importance of an effect.
:::

## Where this leads

The results are interpreted, compared with earlier studies and qualified in [[academic-writing/discussion-conclusions]], and summarised in the abstract ([[academic-writing/abstracts-titles]]). Reading statistics is taught in [[academic-reading/reading-data]], describing trends and charts in [[essay-writing/describing-data]], and the general choice between passive and active in [[academic-writing/academic-style]].

::: summary
- The **method** section reports design, participants or materials, instruments, procedure, analysis and ethics in enough detail to evaluate and repeat the study, with justifications for questionable choices ([[#def-method]], [[#rule-method]]).
- Methods use the **past simple** for what was done, the **present** for what remains true, the passive or *we* consistently, and **no hedges** on the procedure ([[#rule-method-tense]]).
- The **results** section answers the research questions in order, **leads with the finding**, gives the **numbers**, reports **all** relevant results, and keeps **interpretation out** ([[#def-results]], [[#rule-results]]).
- Report statistics in the format of your style, with **effect sizes** and spread, not *p*-values alone; *not significant* is not *no difference*.
- **Data commentary** locates the table, summarises it and highlights the main points; qualitative findings present **themes** supported by **extracts**.
- Results sentences say **what was found**; discussion sentences say **what it means**.
:::

Correct the tenses. (a) *Participants complete the questionnaire and then they were interviewed.* (b) *The test consisted of 40 items and is designed by the Ministry.* (c) *Data will be analysed with SPSS.* (in a finished dissertation)

(a) *Participants completed the questionnaire and were then interviewed.* (b) *The test consists of 40 items and was designed by the Ministry.* (present for the instrument as it exists, past for its design — or *consisted* if a modified version was used). (c) *Data were analysed with SPSS.*

Rewrite each sentence so that it leads with the finding. (a) *Table 3 shows the scores of the two groups.* (b) *The results of the survey can be seen in Figure 2.*
::: solution
Possible answers: (a) *The experimental group scored higher than the control group on all three measures (Table 3).* (b) *Most respondents (72%) used the library less than once a month (Figure 2).* Any answer that states the main point and refers to the table or figure in brackets is acceptable; the details of the finding are invented.

::: exercise Complete the method {level=2}
List what is missing from this fictional method paragraph, using [[#rule-method]]. *Students were given a reading test and a questionnaire about their reading habits. The data were analysed to see if reading habits were related to test scores.*
::: solution
Missing: the design (correlational survey); the participants — how many, who, how selected, setting; the test — what it was, its source, number of items, reliability; the questionnaire — what it asked, its source, response format; the procedure — when, where, in what order, how long, administered by whom; the analysis — which statistics (for example, correlation or regression) and software; ethics — approval and consent.

::: exercise Report a result {level=2}
Write a results sentence from this fictional information, in APA style. *Group A (n = 25) mean = 72.4, SD = 8.1; Group B (n = 25) mean = 68.0, SD = 9.3; t(48) = 1.78, p = .081, d = 0.50.*

This fictional results paragraph has slipped into discussion. Rewrite it as results only. *Students who studied with music scored lower (M = 61.2) than those who studied in silence (M = 67.8), which shows that music distracts the brain, as Okafor (2019) also found. Schools should therefore ban music in study rooms.*
::: solution
*Students who studied with music scored lower (M = 61.2) than those who studied in silence (M = 67.8).* (Then the standard deviations and the statistical test.) Removed: the causal explanation (*music distracts the brain*), the comparison with Okafor, and the recommendation — all of which belong in the discussion, where the explanation would also need hedging.
