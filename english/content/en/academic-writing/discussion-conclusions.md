Here are two openings of the discussion section of the fictional vocabulary-app study from [[academic-writing/methods-results]], in which the app group gained more words than the comparison group after eight weeks, but the difference had shrunk and was no longer significant four weeks later.

> **Version A.** *The app group gained 9.4 words and the comparison group 6.2 words. At the delayed test the gains were 7.1 and 5.8. The difference at the delayed test was not significant. This proves that vocabulary apps are effective and should be used in all English courses.*
>
> **Version B.** *This study asked whether daily use of a spaced-repetition app would increase first-year students' vocabulary gains, and whether any advantage would last. The app group gained about three more words than the comparison group over eight weeks, an advantage similar in size to that reported for spaced practice in classroom studies (Author, year). Four weeks after the app use ended, however, the advantage had shrunk and was no longer statistically significant. One explanation is that the app's benefit depends on continued review: once students stopped using it, they forgot at a similar rate to the comparison group. Alternatively, the difference may simply have been too small for a study of this size to detect reliably at the delayed test.*

Version A repeats the results and then leaps to a conclusion that the evidence cannot bear: *proves*, *effective*, *all English courses*. Version B does what a discussion is for. It reminds the reader of the question, restates the main finding in a sentence, compares it with earlier work, faces the result that went against the hypothesis, and offers two explanations, each hedged, without choosing between them before the evidence allows.

This chapter is about writing that section and the conclusion that follows it: the moves of a discussion, how to interpret results and compare them with other studies, how to state limitations, how to make each claim exactly as strong as the evidence, and what a conclusion should contain. The grammar and vocabulary of hedging are taught in [[modality/hedging-boosting]], and reading other writers' hedged claims in [[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]]; here they are applied to the part of the paper where they matter most.

## What a discussion does

::: definition Discussion {#def-discussion}
The **discussion** (讨论) of a research article interprets the results: it says what they mean, why they may have come out as they did, how they relate to earlier research and theory, what their limitations are, and what follows from them. Where the introduction moves from the general field to the specific study, the discussion moves back from the specific findings to the field. The **conclusion** (结论), a separate section in many papers and most dissertations, states the answer to the research questions, the contribution of the study and its implications briefly and directly.
:::

Studies of discussion sections have found that they are built from a small set of recurring moves, often repeated for each main result. Hopkins and Dudley-Evans (1988), analysing discussions in articles and dissertations, described a cycle that typically begins with a statement of a result and goes on to comment on it — whether it was expected, how it compares with previous research, how it might be explained — before moving to the next result; Yang and Allison (2003), studying applied linguistics articles, described similar moves and found that commenting on results was the most frequent. Swales and Feak (2012) summarise the typical content as: background information (the question, or the theory), a statement of the key results, comments on them, limitations, and implications or recommendations.

::: theorem The moves of a discussion {#rule-discussion-moves}
A discussion typically:

1. **reorients** the reader: the aim or research question, briefly;
2. **states the main finding(s)** in a sentence or two, without repeating the numbers;
3. **interprets** each finding: what it means and how it might be explained, with alternatives;
4. **compares** it with earlier studies: agreement, disagreement, and possible reasons for differences;
5. **addresses unexpected or negative results** rather than passing over them;
6. **states limitations** and their consequences;
7. **draws implications** for theory, practice or method, and suggests specific further research.

Moves 2–5 usually cycle, once for each main finding, in order of importance; moves 6 and 7 often come at the end.
:::

::: proof
The moves answer, in a reasonable order, the questions an expert reader has after the results: *So what did you find? What does it mean? Is it new? Why might it have happened? How far can I trust it? What should I do with it?* Move 1 is needed because many readers skip from the introduction to the discussion. Move 2 restates the finding in words rather than numbers, so that the interpretation has a clear object. Moves 3 and 4 are the core: a result gains its meaning from an explanation and from its relation to what was known, which is why the studies reviewed in the literature review return here. Move 5 is the one inexperienced writers most often omit, and the one reviewers most often ask for. The order of moves 6 and 7 varies by field, and some journals ask for a separate *Limitations* subsection.
:::

## Interpreting results

A result is a fact about a sample; an interpretation is a claim about what that fact means. The step between them is where most overclaiming happens, and where hedging belongs ([[modality/hedging-boosting#rule-what-to-hedge]]).

::: theorem Interpreting a finding {#rule-interpret}
1. **Restate the finding** in words, at the level of generality the data support: *students who used the app gained more words over eight weeks*, not *apps improve vocabulary*.
2. **Offer an explanation**, and say what kind of claim it is: a conclusion the design supports, a likely explanation, or a speculation.
3. **Consider alternative explanations** — chance, the sample, the measure, a confounding variable — and say which the design rules out and which it does not.
4. **Use causal language only when the design supports it**: an experiment with random assignment can support *X increased Y*; a survey supports *X was associated with Y*, and a causal reading must be hedged and argued.
5. **Treat unexpected results as findings**, not embarrassments: report them, consider explanations, and say what they imply.
:::

::: proof
Point 1 prevents the commonest leap in student discussions, from a finding about one sample to a claim about a whole population or a whole category (*apps*, *students*, *Chinese learners*). Points 2 and 3 are what reviewers look for: an explanation offered as the only possible one is weaker than an explanation weighed against its rivals, because the reader can usually think of the rivals ([[academic-reading/evaluating-sources#rule-causation]]). Point 4 ties the language to the design. In the vocabulary study, the groups were intact classes, not randomly assigned, so the larger gain might reflect differences between the classes or their teachers; the writer can say the app group *gained more*, and that the app *may have contributed*, but should not write that the app *caused* the gain without addressing this. Point 5 matters for honesty and for science: unexpected results often lead to the most interesting questions.
:::

::: example Interpreting an unexpected result {#ex-unexpected}
A fictional survey of 400 university students found, contrary to the authors' hypothesis, that students who used AI chatbots most often for homework reported *higher* confidence in their writing than students who used them rarely. Write two or three sentences for the discussion that interpret this finding.
::: solution
**A model:** *Contrary to our hypothesis, frequent users of AI chatbots reported greater confidence in their writing than infrequent users. Because the survey measured both variables at one time, the direction of this association cannot be determined: it is possible that confident writers are more willing to experiment with new tools, rather than that the tools increase confidence. It is also possible that frequent users judge their writing partly by the quality of text they have revised with a chatbot, in which case their confidence may not reflect their unaided ability.*

**What the interpretation does.** It names the finding as unexpected (*Contrary to our hypothesis*); points out what the design cannot show (direction); offers two explanations, each hedged (*it is possible that*); and connects one of them to a measurement issue (*confidence may not reflect ability*), which suggests a further study. It does not dismiss the finding or explain it away.
:::
:::

## Comparing with earlier research

The comparison move links the study to the literature review and shows what it adds. Its language is a small set of phrases of agreement and difference, chosen with care because each makes a claim about the relation:

- **agreement**: *This finding is consistent with / in line with / supports / confirms / replicates …* (*confirms* and *replicates* are strong; use them only if the studies were comparable);
- **extension**: *This extends earlier findings to …*; *Unlike earlier studies, which examined …, the present study …*;
- **disagreement**: *This finding differs from / contrasts with / does not support …*; *Contrary to …*;
- **explanation of differences**: *One reason for this difference may be …*; *The discrepancy may reflect differences in …*.

::: example Explaining a difference from earlier studies {#ex-compare}
The fictional vocabulary study found an advantage of about three words at eight weeks. An earlier fictional study (Ruiz 2019) found an advantage of about twelve words for a similar app. Write a comparison that considers why the results differ, given that Ruiz's participants used the app for a whole semester and were tested on the app's own word list.
::: solution
**A model:** *The advantage found here is considerably smaller than the twelve-word gain reported by Ruiz (2019) for a similar app. Two differences between the studies may account for this. Ruiz's participants used the app for a full semester, twice as long as in the present study, and were tested on the app's own word list, whereas our test drew on the course textbook. The larger effect in Ruiz's study may therefore partly reflect the closer match between what was practised and what was tested; our result may be the better estimate of the app's effect on the vocabulary of a course.*

**Points to note.** The comparison is specific (sizes of effects); it names concrete differences in design rather than a vague *different context*; it hedges the explanation (*may account for*, *may partly reflect*); and it ends with a cautious claim about what the present study adds — an interpretation the writer must be prepared to defend.
:::
:::

## Limitations

::: definition Limitation {#def-limitation}
A **limitation** (研究局限) is a feature of a study's design, sample, measures or analysis that restricts what can be concluded from it. Stating limitations is not an admission of failure: every study has them, and a writer who identifies them shows that they understand what their evidence can and cannot support.
:::

::: theorem Writing about limitations {#rule-limitations}
1. **Name the specific limitation**: not *the sample was small* but *the sample was drawn from one university and included only English majors*.
2. **State its consequence** for the conclusions: *so the findings may not apply to students of other subjects, who may be less motivated to learn vocabulary*.
3. **Say what reduces its effect**, where something does: *although the groups did not differ at pre-test*.
4. **Choose the limitations that matter** — those a careful reader would raise — rather than listing every possible weakness.
5. **Turn the main limitations into further research** where possible: *A study with random assignment would allow …*.
6. **Keep the tone factual**: neither defensive (*this was unavoidable*) nor self-destructive (*the results are therefore meaningless*).
:::

::: proof
A limitation without its consequence (point 1 without point 2) tells the reader nothing they can use: every study is limited in sample, and what matters is how this limits the conclusion. Point 3 balances the account and is often what persuades a reviewer that the conclusion can still stand. Point 4 is a matter of judgement: a long list of trivial limitations suggests either that the writer does not know which ones matter or that they are hiding the important one among the rest. Point 6 reflects the genre's view of research as incremental: limitations define what the next study should do, which is why point 5 links them to further research.
:::

::: warning The empty limitation
✗ *This study has some limitations. The sample size was small. More research is needed.*

✓ *The study has two main limitations. First, the participants were 64 English majors at one university, so the results may not generalise to students for whom vocabulary learning is less central. Second, because intact classes rather than randomly assigned groups were compared, differences between the classes may have contributed to the app group's advantage, although the groups did not differ at pre-test. A randomised study with students of several subjects would address both points.*

The first version could end any paper ever written. The second names each limitation, says what it does to the conclusions, gives the mitigating fact, and turns the limitations into a specific design for further research. *More research is needed* is the emptiest sentence of the genre; say what research, and why.
:::

## Calibrating claims

The discussion and conclusion are where claims are strongest and where a reader checks them most closely. Matching each claim to its evidence — **calibration** — is the main skill of the section. The tools are those of [[modality/hedging-boosting]]: reporting verbs, modals, adverbs, quantifiers and limits of scope. What the discussion adds is a set of typical relations between the kind of evidence and the strength of claim it can bear.

| evidence | claim it can usually bear | typical wording |
|---|---|---|
| a descriptive finding in this sample | a statement about the sample | *Most participants reported …* |
| an association in one study | an association; a causal explanation only as a hypothesis | *X was associated with Y*; *X may contribute to Y* |
| a difference in a randomised experiment | an effect under these conditions | *X increased Y in this setting*; *these results indicate that X can improve Y* |
| consistent results across several studies and designs | a general conclusion, still bounded | *There is now consistent evidence that …* |
| one result that conflicts with earlier ones | a question, not a conclusion | *This raises the possibility that …* |
| any result, applied to practice or policy | a cautious implication | *Teachers may wish to consider …*; *These findings may be relevant to …* |

::: example Calibrating a discussion paragraph {#ex-calibrate}
Revise this fictional discussion paragraph so that its claims fit the evidence: a survey of 150 secondary pupils in one city, which found that pupils who read for pleasure at least three times a week had higher English scores. *This study clearly demonstrates that reading for pleasure improves English. Pupils who read more will always do better at school. Therefore, every school in China must make pupils read for pleasure every day.*
::: solution
**A revision:** *In this sample, pupils who read for pleasure at least three times a week had higher English scores than those who read less often. This association is consistent with the view that extensive reading supports language development (Author, year), but because the data come from a single survey, they cannot show whether reading raised pupils' scores or whether pupils who were already better at English chose to read more. If the association is confirmed in longitudinal or experimental studies, schools may wish to consider ways of encouraging reading for pleasure.*

**Changes.** *clearly demonstrates … improves* (booster and causal verb) → *had higher scores* (the finding) and *is consistent with* (a link to theory, not proof). *will always do better at school* (an absolute prediction about a different outcome) → removed. The alternative explanation (reverse direction) is stated. *every school in China must* (a national policy from one city's survey) → a conditional, hedged implication (*If … confirmed …, may wish to consider*).
:::
:::

::: quiz
A cross-sectional survey found that students who slept less reported more stress. Which sentence is best calibrated for the discussion?
- [ ] *Lack of sleep causes stress in students.*
- [x] *Shorter sleep was associated with higher reported stress, although the direction of this relationship cannot be determined from these data.*
- [ ] *It might perhaps be possible that sleep could possibly be related to stress in some way.*
- [ ] *Our data prove that students need more sleep.*
::: solution
The second sentence states the association accurately and names the limit of the design. The first and fourth overclaim (a causal claim and *prove*); the third piles up hedges until it says nothing ([[modality/hedging-boosting#rule-what-to-hedge]], point 4).
:::
:::

## Conclusions

::: theorem What a conclusion contains {#rule-conclusion}
1. **Answer the research questions** directly, in the order they were asked.
2. **State the contribution**: what the study adds to knowledge, method or practice, in a sentence or two.
3. **Give the main implications**, hedged to fit the evidence.
4. **Suggest specific further research**, following from the limitations and the findings.
5. **Add nothing new**: no new results, sources or arguments.
6. **Do not simply repeat the abstract or the discussion**: the conclusion is shorter and more direct than the discussion, and written for a reader who has read the paper.
:::

::: proof
The conclusion is the last thing a reader reads and often the first place an examiner looks after the introduction, to check that the questions asked were the questions answered (point 1). Points 2–4 compress the discussion's final moves into a statement the reader can take away and cite. Point 5 is the rule shared with essay conclusions ([[essay-writing/conclusions]]): a new idea at the end has no support in the text. Point 6 marks the difference from the abstract, which is written for readers who have not read the paper ([[academic-writing/abstracts-titles]]). In many articles the conclusion is a single paragraph at the end of the discussion; in dissertations it is usually a chapter, which may also reflect on the research process.
:::

::: example Writing a conclusion {#ex-conclusion}
Write a conclusion of about 120 words for the fictional vocabulary-app study, which asked (RQ1) whether app users gained more vocabulary over eight weeks and (RQ2) whether any advantage lasted four weeks after use ended.
::: solution
**A model:** *This study examined whether daily use of a spaced-repetition app increased first-year English majors' vocabulary gains, and whether any advantage lasted. In answer to the first question, students who used the app gained about three more words out of sixty over eight weeks than those who did not. In answer to the second, this advantage had narrowed and was no longer statistically significant four weeks after use ended. The findings suggest that such apps may support vocabulary learning while they are used, but that their benefits may depend on continued review. Randomised studies over a full academic year, with students of several subjects and tests independent of the app, would show how far these results generalise.*

**Check.** Questions answered in order; contribution and implication hedged (*suggest*, *may*); further research specific and linked to the limitations; no new information.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **结论与启示 and implications.** Chinese papers in education and applied linguistics often end with a section of 启示 that gives recommendations for teachers in strong terms (教师应该……, 必须……). Translated directly, *teachers should* and *must* overclaim for a single study. English discussions usually frame implications as possibilities (*teachers may wish to*, *these findings suggest that … could*), and reserve *should* for implications supported by a body of evidence.
- **研究不足与展望.** The Chinese section on 不足 (shortcomings) and 展望 (future prospects) corresponds to limitations and further research. Formulaic statements (本研究样本量较小，有待进一步研究) translate as the empty limitation above; make each one specific, with its consequence.
- **证明 and *prove*.** 本研究证明了…… is best translated *This study shows* or *indicates*, not *proves* ([[modality/hedging-boosting]], note on English and Chinese).
- **Comparing with Chinese studies.** In an English-language paper, studies published in Chinese are compared with your results like any others. Explain their context briefly if readers may not know it.
:::

::: history
The modern discussion section took shape with the IMRaD article in the twentieth century, and became an object of study in the 1980s, when genre analysts such as Tony Dudley-Evans and his colleagues described its moves. Ken Hyland's corpus work (1998) showed that hedges are concentrated in discussions, where results become claims. The question of how strongly results should be stated gained wider urgency in the 2000s and 2010s. In 2005 John Ioannidis argued in *PLoS Medicine* that many published research findings are likely to be false, because of small studies, small effects and flexible analyses; and in 2015 the Open Science Collaboration reported in *Science* that, in attempted replications of 100 psychology studies, fewer than half of the original results were reproduced. These debates strengthened the case for the careful calibration of claims and the full reporting of limitations that discussion sections are expected to contain.
:::

## Where this leads

The conclusion is compressed further into the last sentences of the abstract, the subject of [[academic-writing/abstracts-titles]]. Discussions are where reviewers' comments most often fall, and responding to them is the subject of [[academic-writing/peer-review]]. Hedging and boosting are in [[modality/hedging-boosting]], and reading claims critically in [[academic-reading/claims-and-evidence]] and [[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]].

::: summary
- A **discussion** interprets results and moves from the study back to the field; a **conclusion** answers the questions and states the contribution directly ([[#def-discussion]]).
- Its moves: **reorient**, **state** each finding, **interpret** it, **compare** it with earlier work, address **unexpected** results, state **limitations**, draw **implications** ([[#rule-discussion-moves]]).
- Interpret at the level the data support, weigh **alternative explanations**, and use **causal language** only when the design supports it ([[#rule-interpret]]).
- Compare with earlier studies precisely, and explain differences by concrete differences in design.
- State **specific limitations with their consequences**, and turn them into specific further research; *more research is needed* says nothing ([[#def-limitation]], [[#rule-limitations]]).
- **Calibrate** every claim to its evidence: associations as associations, implications as possibilities.
- A **conclusion** answers the questions in order, states the contribution and implications, suggests further research and adds nothing new ([[#rule-conclusion]]).
:::

::: exercise Causal or not? {level=1}
For each design, say whether *X caused Y* is a justified claim. (a) a randomised experiment in which X was manipulated; (b) a survey measuring X and Y at one time; (c) interviews in which participants said X had changed Y.
::: solution
(a) Yes, under the conditions of the experiment (and with appropriate hedging about generalisation). (b) No: an association only; a causal reading must be hedged and argued. (c) No: participants' accounts of causes are evidence of how they understand the relation, not proof of it.

Rewrite each overclaim so that it fits the evidence in brackets. (a) *Online classes make students lonely.* (a survey of 200 students at one university found that those with more online classes reported more loneliness) (b) *The new method works for all learners.* (an experiment with 40 adult learners of Spanish) (c) *Our results confirm that bilingualism improves memory.* (one study found a small, non-significant advantage)

::: exercise Write a discussion paragraph {level=3}
Write a discussion paragraph of about 200 words for this fictional finding: in a study of 80 university students, those who took handwritten notes in a lecture answered more conceptual questions correctly a week later than those who typed notes, but there was no difference on factual questions. An earlier fictional study (Okafor 2019) found an advantage for handwriting on both kinds of question.
::: hint
State the finding, interpret both parts of it, compare with Okafor, and consider at least one alternative explanation and one limitation.
:::
::: solution
Answers will vary. A strong paragraph states the finding in words (an advantage for handwriting on conceptual but not factual questions); offers a hedged interpretation (for example, that handwriting may encourage summarising rather than copying, which helps understanding more than recall); compares with Okafor, explaining the difference by possible concrete differences (questions, delay, participants); considers an alternative (for example, that students who chose to write by hand differ in other ways, if they were not randomly assigned); states a limitation with its consequence; and uses no causal booster such as *proves*. Check the calibration of every claim.
