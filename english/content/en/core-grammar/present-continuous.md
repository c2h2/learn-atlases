Wang Fang's phone rings. It is her friend Amina.

**Amina:** *Hi! What are you doing?*

**Wang Fang:** *I'm cooking dinner. I always cook on Fridays.*

Wang Fang uses two different present forms. *I'm cooking* describes what is happening at this moment; *I always cook* describes a habit. In Chinese the difference can be shown with 在 or 正在 (我在做饭), but it is often left to the context. In English the choice between *I cook* and *I'm cooking* has to be made in every sentence, and choosing wrongly changes the meaning: *What do you do?* asks about someone's job; *What are you doing?* asks about this moment.

This lesson shows how to form the **present continuous** (also called the present progressive), how to spell the *-ing* form, what the present continuous means, and how to choose between it and the present simple.

## Form

::: definition Present continuous {#def-present-continuous}
The **present continuous** is formed with the present of *be* (*am*, *is*, *are*) + the ***-ing* form** of the main verb: *I am working*, *she is working*, *they are working*. The *-ing* form is the base form + *-ing* (*work* → *working*); it never changes for the subject.
:::

| | statement | negative | question |
|---|---|---|---|
| *I* | *I'm working.* | *I'm not working.* | *Am I working?* |
| *he*, *she*, *it* | *She's working.* | *She isn't working.* / *She's not working.* | *Is she working?* |
| *you*, *we*, *they* | *They're working.* | *They aren't working.* / *They're not working.* | *Are they working?* |

*Be* is the operator, so questions and negatives follow the rules of [[core-grammar/questions-and-negatives]] without *do*:

- *Are you listening? — Yes, I am.*
- *Is it raining? — No, it isn't.*
- *What are you reading?*
- *Why is the baby crying?*
- *Who is singing?* (subject question)

::: warning Leaving out *be*, or using *be* with the base form
✗ *She working now.* ✓ *She is working now.*

✗ *I'm go to the shop.* ✓ *I'm going to the shop.*

✗ *Do you waiting for the bus?* ✓ *Are you waiting for the bus?*

The present continuous always has two parts: *be* and the *-ing* form. Leave out either one, and the sentence breaks. Questions use *be*, not *do*.
:::

### Spelling of the *-ing* form

::: theorem Spelling of *-ing* {#rule-ing-spelling}
1. Most verbs just add ***-ing***: *work* → *working*, *read* → *reading*, *play* → *playing*, *go* → *going*.
2. Verbs ending in a silent ***-e*** drop the *e*: *make* → *making*, *write* → *writing*, *come* → *coming*. But *-ee*, *-ye* and *-oe* keep it (*see* → *seeing*, *agree* → *agreeing*, *dye* → *dyeing*), and so does *be* → *being*.
3. Verbs ending in ***-ie*** change it to ***-y***: *lie* → *lying*, *die* → *dying*, *tie* → *tying*.
4. If a verb ends in **one vowel letter + one consonant letter**, and the last syllable is **stressed**, double the consonant: *sit* → *sitting*, *run* → *running*, *stop* → *stopping*, *begin* → *beginning*, *forget* → *forgetting*. Do not double *w*, *x* or *y*: *snow* → *snowing*, *fix* → *fixing*, *stay* → *staying*.
5. If the last syllable is **not stressed**, do not double: *visit* → *visiting*, *open* → *opening*, *listen* → *listening*. Exception: British English doubles final *-l* even when it is unstressed (*travel* → *travelling*, *cancel* → *cancelling*); American English usually does not (*traveling*, *canceling*).
:::

::: proof
The doubling rule protects the pronunciation of the vowel. In English spelling, a single consonant between two vowels usually signals a "long" vowel sound (*hoping* /ˈhəʊpɪŋ/, from *hope*), while a double consonant signals a "short" one (*hopping* /ˈhɒpɪŋ/, from *hop*). Doubling the final consonant of *hop*, *sit* and *run* keeps the short vowel of the base form. In an unstressed final syllable (*visit*, *open*), the vowel is reduced anyway, so no doubling is needed. The same rules apply to the *-ed* ending ([[core-grammar/past-simple]]) and to comparatives such as *bigger* ([[core-grammar/adjectives-adverbs]]).
:::

::: example Spelling *-ing* forms {#ex-ing}
Write the *-ing* form and give the rule: *swim*, *take*, *study*, *die*, *prefer*, *happen*, *fix*, *see*.
::: solution
| verb | *-ing* form | rule |
|---|---|---|
| *swim* | *swimming* | one vowel + one consonant, stressed: double *m* |
| *take* | *taking* | silent *e* dropped |
| *study* | *studying* | add *-ing* (*y* stays; compare *studies*) |
| *die* | *dying* | *-ie* → *-y* |
| *prefer* | *preferring* | stress on the last syllable (*preˈfer*): double *r* |
| *happen* | *happening* | stress on the first syllable (*ˈhappen*): no doubling |
| *fix* | *fixing* | *x* is never doubled |
| *see* | *seeing* | *-ee* keeps both *e*s |
:::
:::

## Meaning and use

::: theorem Uses of the present continuous {#rule-pc-uses}
Use the present continuous for:

1. **actions in progress at the moment of speaking**: *Look — it's snowing!* *Be quiet, the baby is sleeping.*
2. **actions in progress around now**, even if not at this exact moment: *I'm reading a great book at the moment* (but now I am talking to you). *Kenji is studying for his exams this week.*
3. **temporary situations**: *My parents are staying with us for a few days.* *I'm working from home this month.*
4. **changing and developing situations**: *The weather is getting warmer.* *Prices are going up.* *Your English is improving.*
5. **future arrangements** — plans fixed with other people: *I'm meeting Li Wei at six.* (See [[core-grammar/talking-about-the-future]].)
:::

::: proof
The common idea is that the situation is **in progress** and **limited in time**: it has started and has not finished, and it is not permanent. Use 1 is the clearest case. In uses 2 and 3 the time frame is wider (*this week*, *this month*), but the speaker still sees the situation as temporary. In use 4 the change itself is in progress. Use 5 extends the idea: the arrangement exists now, in the speaker's diary, although the action is in the future. This shared meaning — an activity seen from the inside, as unfinished — is what grammars call **progressive aspect**; [[tense-and-aspect/time-tense-aspect]] explains the term.
:::

Typical time expressions: *now*, *right now*, *at the moment*, *today*, *this week*, *this year*, *these days*, *currently*, and imperatives such as *Look!* and *Listen!*

- *Listen! Someone is knocking at the door.*
- *What are you working on these days?*
- *More and more people are learning Chinese.*
- *I'm not drinking coffee this month — I'm trying to sleep better.*

There is also a special use with *always*, *constantly* or *forever*, for a habit that the speaker finds surprising or annoying: *He's always losing his keys!* *You're constantly checking your phone.* Here the continuous makes the habit sound like something that never stops.

::: quiz
Why does the speaker use the present continuous in *I'm living with my aunt until I find a flat*?
- [ ] Because the action is happening at this exact moment.
- [x] Because the situation is temporary.
- [ ] Because *live* is a state verb.
- [ ] Because it describes a habit.
::: solution
*Until I find a flat* shows that the speaker sees the situation as temporary, so the present continuous is natural. Compare *I live with my aunt*, which presents the situation as permanent or long-term. *Live* is not usually counted as a state verb that resists the continuous; it is used freely in both forms.
:::
:::

## Present simple or present continuous?

::: theorem Choosing between the present simple and the present continuous {#rule-simple-continuous}
- Use the **present simple** for habits, permanent situations, facts and states: *I work in a bank.* *She speaks four languages.* *Water boils at 100 °C.*
- Use the **present continuous** for actions in progress now or around now, and for temporary situations: *I'm working at home today.* *She's speaking to a customer.* *The water is boiling — turn off the gas.*
:::

| present simple | present continuous |
|---|---|
| *I live in Qingdao.* (my permanent home) | *I'm living in Qingdao this year.* (temporary) |
| *He plays the guitar.* (an ability or a habit) | *He's playing the guitar.* (now) |
| *It rains a lot here in spring.* (a fact about the climate) | *It's raining.* (now) |
| *What do you do?* (your job) | *What are you doing?* (now) |
| *The bus usually stops here.* (regularly) | *The bus is stopping.* (now) |

::: proof
The two forms present the same action from two angles. The simple form presents it as a whole — a fact, a habit, a permanent truth, with no limits in time. The continuous form presents it as unfolding — in progress, unfinished, limited in time. So the choice depends on how the speaker sees the situation, not only on the clock. *I live in Qingdao* and *I'm living in Qingdao* can describe the same person on the same day: the first says Qingdao is home, the second that the stay is temporary.
:::

### State verbs

Some verbs describe **states** — knowing, liking, owning, being — rather than actions. A state is not "in progress": it simply exists. These verbs are usually used in the simple form, even about the present moment.

::: definition State verb {#def-state-verb}
A **state verb** (or stative verb) describes a state that continues without change, not an action or event. State verbs are usually not used in the continuous. Common ones are verbs of
- **thinking and knowing**: *know*, *believe*, *understand*, *remember*, *forget*, *mean*, *realise*;
- **liking and wanting**: *like*, *love*, *hate*, *prefer*, *want*, *need*, *wish*;
- **having and being**: *have* (possess), *own*, *belong*, *contain*, *seem*, *cost*, *weigh*;
- **the senses** used without effort: *see*, *hear*, *smell*, *taste*.
:::

- ✓ *I know the answer.* — ✗ *I'm knowing the answer.*
- ✓ *Do you understand?* — ✗ *Are you understanding?*
- ✓ *This bag belongs to Amina.* — ✗ *This bag is belonging to Amina.*
- ✓ *I need a break.* — ✗ *I'm needing a break.*

::: remark State verbs in the continuous
"Usually" is important. Several of these verbs have a second meaning that is an action, and then the continuous is normal: *I **have** a car* (possess) but *I'm **having** lunch* (eating); *I **think** she's right* (opinion) but *I'm **thinking** about it* (considering); *I **see** what you mean* (understand) but *I'm **seeing** the doctor tomorrow* (meeting). And speakers sometimes put state verbs in the continuous to stress that a feeling is temporary or growing: *I'm loving this weather!* *I'm liking him more and more.* These uses are informal and are spreading, especially in speech (Leech et al. 2009 report a rise in the use of the progressive in the twentieth century), but in writing and in exams keep state verbs in the simple form.
:::

::: example Choosing the form {#ex-choose}
Choose the present simple or the present continuous and explain. (a) *Shh! I (try) to concentrate.* (b) *My uncle (own) three restaurants.* (c) *Kenji usually (walk) to work, but this week he (take) the bus because his ankle hurts.* (d) *This soup (taste) wonderful.* (e) *The population of the city (grow) fast.*
::: solution
(a) *I'm trying* — in progress now (*Shh!* shows the moment).

(b) *owns* — *own* is a state verb, and the situation is long-term.

(c) *walks* — a habit (*usually*); *is taking* — a temporary situation (*this week*, with a reason).

(d) *tastes* — *taste* here describes a quality of the soup, a state. (Compare *The chef is tasting the soup*, where *taste* is an action: she is trying it.)

(e) *is growing* — a changing, developing situation. (*The population grows every year* would also be possible as a general fact; the continuous presents the growth as going on now.)
:::
:::

::: example Completing a dialogue {#ex-dialogue}
Complete the conversation with the present simple or the present continuous of the verbs in brackets, and explain each choice.

**Tom:** *Hi, Li Wei! What (1) ___ you ___ (do) here?*

**Li Wei:** *I (2) ___ (wait) for my sister. She (3) ___ (have) a piano lesson in that building.*

**Tom:** *Oh, (4) ___ she ___ (play) the piano? I didn't know.*

**Li Wei:** *Yes, she (5) ___ (go) every Saturday. And this month she (6) ___ (prepare) for a concert, so she (7) ___ (practise) for hours every day!*
::: solution
1. *are you doing* — Tom asks about this moment.
2. *'m waiting* — in progress now.
3. *is having* — *have a lesson* is an action, in progress now. (*has* would mean she has lessons regularly, which is also true, but Li Wei is explaining why he is here now.)
4. *Does she play* — Tom asks about an ability or a habit, not about this moment.
5. *goes* — a habit (*every Saturday*).
6. *is preparing* — a temporary situation (*this month*).
7. *practises* (AmE *practices*) or *is practising* — both are possible. The present simple presents the daily practice as her routine at the moment; the continuous stresses that it is temporary, during this month. The British spelling of the verb is *practise*; American English uses *practice* for both noun and verb.
:::
:::

::: warning The present continuous for habits
✗ *I'm getting up at seven every day.* ✓ *I get up at seven every day.*

✗ *My father is working in a bank.* (if this is his permanent job) ✓ *My father works in a bank.*

✗ *How often are you going to the gym?* ✓ *How often do you go to the gym?*

Words such as *every day*, *usually* and *how often* signal habits, which need the present simple. Some learners use the continuous because they think it means ‘the present’ in general; in English, the simple form is the normal present.
:::

::: example Describing a picture {#ex-picture}
You are describing a photo of a park to a friend on the phone. Write five sentences, using the present continuous for what is happening in the photo and the present simple for any facts you add.
::: solution
A possible description:

- *Some children **are playing** football on the grass.*
- *An old man **is sitting** on a bench and **reading** a newspaper.* (one *is* for both *-ing* forms)
- *Two women **are jogging** along the path.*
- *It **isn't raining**, but the sky **looks** grey.* (*look* here means ‘seem’, a state)
- *I **know** this park — my grandparents **live** near it, and they **go** there every evening.* (facts and a habit: present simple)

When we describe pictures, the present continuous is the natural choice for actions, because the picture shows them in progress.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **在 and 正在 are optional; *be* + *-ing* is not.** Chinese can mark an action in progress with 在, 正在 or 呢 (他在打电话, 他打电话呢), but it can also leave it unmarked when the context is clear. In English, if the action is in progress, the continuous is needed: *He's on the phone* or *He's making a phone call*, not ✗ *He makes a phone call* (which sounds like a habit or a stage direction).
- **着 is not always *-ing*.** 着 often describes a state that results from an action: 门开着 means *The door is open* (a state), not ✗ *The door is opening* (it is moving now). 墙上挂着一幅画 is *There is a picture on the wall* or *A picture is hanging on the wall*. When 着 describes clothes, *wear* in the continuous often fits: 他穿着一件红外套 → *He's wearing a red coat.*
- **Chinese does not mark habit.** 我每天跑步 and 我在跑步 differ only by 在 and the time word; English needs two different verb forms: *I run every day* / *I'm running*.
- **Future arrangements.** Chinese can use 在 only for the present; English can use the present continuous for fixed plans (*I'm flying to Chengdu on Monday*), which surprises many learners. See [[core-grammar/talking-about-the-future]].
:::

::: history
The construction *be* + *-ing* has existed since Old and Middle English, but for centuries it was rare. It became much more frequent from the seventeenth century onwards, and it has continued to spread: Leech, Hundt, Mair and Smith, in *Change in Contemporary English* (2009), found that the progressive was used clearly more often in written English of the 1990s than in the 1960s, both in British and in American English. One part of the system is very young. The **progressive passive** (*The house is being built*) appeared only around the end of the eighteenth century, and some nineteenth-century grammarians condemned it as clumsy; they preferred the older *The house is building*, which now sounds strange. Today *is being built* is completely standard — a reminder that usage, not the rules of grammar books, decides what is correct.
:::

## Where this leads

The present continuous returns in [[core-grammar/talking-about-the-future]], where it expresses arrangements, and in *Tense and Aspect*, which explains progressive aspect in all tenses ([[tense-and-aspect/simple-and-progressive]]), including the past continuous (*I was cooking when you called*, [[tense-and-aspect/narrative-tenses]]). The difference between Chinese 在, 着 and English *-ing* is examined more closely in [[tense-and-aspect/tense-and-chinese]].

::: summary
- The present continuous is *am/is/are* + the *-ing* form ([[#def-present-continuous]]); questions and negatives use *be* as the operator, not *do*.
- Spelling: drop silent *e* (*making*), *-ie* → *-y* (*lying*), double a final consonant after a single stressed vowel (*sitting*, *beginning*); BrE *travelling*, AmE *traveling* ([[#rule-ing-spelling]]).
- Use it for actions in progress now or around now, temporary situations, changing situations and future arrangements ([[#rule-pc-uses]]).
- Use the present simple for habits, permanent situations and facts; the choice depends on how the speaker sees the situation ([[#rule-simple-continuous]]).
- State verbs (*know*, *like*, *want*, *own*, *belong* …) are normally not continuous, except when they have an action meaning (*I'm having lunch*) ([[#def-state-verb]]).
- Chinese 在 and 正在 are optional and 着 often describes a resulting state; English *be* + *-ing* is required whenever an action is in progress.
:::
