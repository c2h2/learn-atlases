In Chinese you can say 下雨了 with no subject at all, and 去过北京吗？ without saying who has been to Beijing. The listener knows. In English both sentences need a subject:

- *It's raining.*
- *Have you been to Beijing?*

The *it* in *It's raining* does not refer to anything. It is there only because an English statement must have a subject. Word order matters just as much. *Tom called Anna* and *Anna called Tom* use the same three words, but they describe different events, because English shows who did what mainly by the **order** of the words.

This lesson explains the parts of an English sentence — subject, verb, object, complement and adverbial — and the small number of patterns they make. Once you can see these patterns, you can build correct sentences and also understand long ones, because even a long sentence is built on one of them.

## The parts of a sentence

A simple sentence (one **clause**) is made of a few parts called **clause elements**. Grammar books label them with letters: S, V, O, C and A.

::: definition Subject and verb {#def-subject-verb}
The **subject** (S) is the part of a sentence that the sentence is about; in a statement it usually comes first and tells us who or what does the action or is in the state. The **verb** (V) is the verb phrase that comes after the subject: it says what the subject does or is, and it shows tense (*works*, *worked*). In a statement, the verb agrees with the subject: *she works*, *they work*.
:::

- ***My brother** (S) **works** (V) in a bank.*
- ***The children** (S) **are playing** (V).*
- ***It** (S) **rained** (V) all night.*

The subject is often a noun phrase (*my brother*, *the children*) or a pronoun (*it*, *she*). The verb can be one word (*works*) or several (*are playing*, *has been*, *can swim*); together they are the **verb phrase**.

::: definition Object {#def-object}
An **object** (O) is a noun phrase or pronoun that comes after the verb and completes its meaning, usually the person or thing that the action is done to: *She plays **the piano**.* Some verbs take two objects: an **indirect object** (Oi), usually the person who receives something, and a **direct object** (Od), the thing given: *He gave **me** (Oi) **a book** (Od).*
:::

::: definition Complement {#def-complement}
A **complement** (C) is a noun phrase or adjective that describes the subject or the object, rather than being something the action is done to. A **subject complement** comes after *be* or a similar verb (*become*, *seem*, *look*, *feel*) and describes the subject: *Amina is **a nurse**.* *The soup tastes **good**.* An **object complement** comes after the object and describes it: *The news made **her** (O) **happy** (C).*
:::

::: definition Adverbial {#def-adverbial}
An **adverbial** (A) is a part of the sentence that tells us more about the situation: where, when, how, how often or why. It can be an adverb (*quickly*, *yesterday*), a prepositional phrase (*in the park*, *at six*) or a noun phrase of time (*last week*). Most adverbials can be left out; some verbs, however, need one (*She lives **in Nanjing**.*).
:::

The difference between an object and a complement is easy to test. An object is a different person or thing from the subject; a complement is the same person or thing described again.

- *Li Na met **a doctor**.* — Li Na and the doctor are two people: *a doctor* is an object.
- *Li Na became **a doctor**.* — Li Na *is* the doctor: *a doctor* is a complement.

::: quiz
What is *tired* in *You look tired*?
- [ ] subject
- [ ] object
- [x] complement
- [ ] adverbial
::: solution
*Tired* describes the subject *you*, and comes after *look*, a verb like *be*. It is a subject complement. It is not an object: nothing is done to "tired". It is not an adverbial: it does not say how you look at something (that would be *You look **carefully***).
:::
:::

## The basic word order

::: theorem Word order in statements {#rule-svo}
In an English statement, the subject comes before the verb, and the object or complement comes after the verb:

**S + V (+ O or C)**

Adverbials are more mobile, but most often they stand at the end of the sentence. When there are several adverbials at the end, the usual order is **how – where – when**: *She sang **beautifully** **at the concert** **last night**.*
:::

::: proof
English nouns do not change their form to show whether they are the subject or the object, so word order carries this information. *The dog bit the man* and *The man bit the dog* differ only in order. (Pronouns still keep a trace of the old case endings: *she* / *her*, *they* / *them*, so *She saw them* and *They saw her* are clear even out of context.) Reference grammars (Quirk et al. 1985; Swan 2016) describe the order of end adverbials as a tendency, not a fixed law: a short adverbial often comes before a long one, and a time adverbial can also stand at the start of the sentence, especially when it is the topic: *Last night she sang beautifully at the concert.*
:::

Some typical positions of adverbials:

- At the end (the most common): *I saw her **yesterday**.* *We met **at the station**.*
- At the beginning, to set the scene: ***In the morning**, I go for a run.* ***Unfortunately**, the shop was closed.*
- In the middle, before the main verb: *I **often** read in bed.* *She has **already** left.* (Adverbs of frequency and a few others go here; see [[core-grammar/adjectives-adverbs]].)

But an adverbial does not normally go between the verb and its object:

✗ *I like very much tea.* ✓ *I like tea very much.*

✗ *She speaks very well English.* ✓ *She speaks English very well.*

::: warning Putting the time and place first, as in Chinese
In Chinese, time and place usually come before the verb: 我昨天在图书馆看书. Translating word for word gives an unusual English order:

? *I yesterday in the library read.*

✓ *I read in the library yesterday.* ✓ *Yesterday I read in the library.*

In English, place and time go at the end (place before time) or, for emphasis or to set the scene, at the very beginning — not between the subject and the verb.
:::

## The seven clause patterns

The verb decides what must follow it. *Sleep* needs nothing after it; *like* needs an object; *be* needs a complement or an adverbial; *give* can take two objects. Quirk et al. (1985) showed that almost all English clauses follow one of seven patterns.

::: theorem The seven clause patterns {#rule-seven}
Leaving out optional adverbials, an English clause follows one of these patterns:

| pattern | example |
|---|---|
| **SV** | *The baby (S) is sleeping (V).* |
| **SVO** | *Kenji (S) plays (V) the guitar (O).* |
| **SVC** | *My mother (S) is (V) a teacher (C).* *The soup (S) smells (V) good (C).* |
| **SVA** | *We (S) live (V) in Nanjing (A).* *The meeting (S) is (V) on Friday (A).* |
| **SVOO** | *My aunt (S) sent (V) me (Oi) a card (Od).* |
| **SVOC** | *The news (S) made (V) her (O) happy (C).* *They (S) called (V) the baby (O) Lily (C).* |
| **SVOA** | *She (S) put (V) the keys (O) on the table (A).* |

Any of these can have extra, optional adverbials: *The baby is sleeping **in the next room** (A) **now** (A).*
:::

::: proof
The pattern comes from the verb's meaning. A verb such as *sleep* or *arrive* describes something that involves only the subject, so it needs nothing more (SV). *Like*, *eat* and *see* involve a second participant, the object (SVO). Linking verbs — *be*, *become*, *seem*, *look*, *feel*, *taste* — connect the subject to a description of it (SVC). *Live*, *stay* and *be* in the sense ‘be located’ need a place or time (SVA): *We live* alone is incomplete. *Give*, *send*, *show* and *tell* involve a giver, a receiver and a thing (SVOO). *Make*, *call*, *find* and *keep* can describe the object (SVOC), and *put* needs a place for the object (SVOA). School grammars in China and Japan often teach five patterns (SV, SVC, SVO, SVOO, SVOC); the seven patterns add SVA and SVOA, the patterns of verbs that need an adverbial. The patterns are a description, not a law: some verbs belong to several patterns (*She sings* SV; *She sings a song* SVO; *She sang me a song* SVOO).
:::

::: example Finding the clause elements {#ex-elements}
Label the clause elements and name the pattern: *Last summer my cousin worked in a hotel in Sanya.*
::: solution
1. Find the verb first: *worked*.
2. Ask "who worked?": *my cousin* — the subject.
3. Ask what follows the verb: *in a hotel in Sanya* is a place, not something the action is done to — an adverbial. *Last summer* is a time — another adverbial.
4. Are the adverbials necessary? *My cousin worked* is a complete sentence (it means she had a job), so they are optional.

Labels: *Last summer* (A) *my cousin* (S) *worked* (V) *in a hotel in Sanya* (A). The pattern is **SV**, with two optional adverbials.
:::
:::

::: example Object or complement? {#ex-obj-comp}
Name the pattern of each sentence. (a) *Wang Fang found a wallet.* (b) *Wang Fang found the film boring.* (c) *Wang Fang felt ill.* (d) *Wang Fang felt the cold wind.*
::: solution
(a) *a wallet* is a different thing from Wang Fang, and something she found: an object. **SVO**.

(b) *the film* is the object; *boring* describes the film (the film was boring): an object complement. **SVOC**.

(c) *ill* describes Wang Fang (she was ill): a subject complement. **SVC**. Here *feel* is a linking verb.

(d) *the cold wind* is something different from Wang Fang that she felt with her body: an object. **SVO**.

The same verb (*find*, *feel*) can belong to different patterns, so always ask whether the word after the verb is a new person or thing (object) or a description (complement).
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence follows the pattern **SVOO**?
- [ ] *She became a pilot.*
- [ ] *She put the bag on the seat.*
- [x] *She showed us her photos.*
- [ ] *She keeps the room tidy.*
::: solution
*She* (S) *showed* (V) *us* (Oi) *her photos* (Od): two objects. *She became a pilot* is SVC, *She put the bag on the seat* is SVOA and *She keeps the room tidy* is SVOC.
:::

## Every statement needs a subject

::: theorem An English clause needs a subject {#rule-subject}
In English, every statement and every question has a subject, even when the meaning is clear without one. When there is no real subject — with weather, time, distance and similar situations — English uses **dummy *it***. To say that something exists, English uses ***there*** + *be*. Only **imperatives** (commands and requests) normally have no subject.
:::

::: proof
English verbs carry little information about their subject (only *-s* in *she works*), so the subject cannot be "recovered" from the verb, as it can in Spanish (*llueve* ‘it rains’) or Italian. English therefore always fills the subject position, even with a word that means nothing: *it* in *It's late*. Chinese, by contrast, allows the subject to be left out when the context makes it clear (Li & Thompson 1981 discuss this as "zero pronouns"). The rule has two well-known exceptions. Imperatives have no subject because the subject is always *you*: *Sit down, please.* And in informal speech and messages, English speakers often drop an obvious subject at the start: *Sounds good.* *Hope you're well.* *See you tomorrow.* These are normal in conversation and text messages, but not in formal writing.
:::

Dummy *it*:

- *It's cold today.* (weather)
- *It's half past seven.* (time)
- *It's ten kilometres to the airport.* (distance)
- *It's nice to see you.* (*it* stands for *to see you*, which comes later)

Existential *there* (more in [[core-grammar/be-and-have]]):

- *There is a bank near my flat.*
- *There are thirty students in my class.*

Imperatives:

- *Close the door, please.*
- *Don't worry.*

::: warning Leaving out the subject
✗ *Is very hot today.* ✓ *It is very hot today.*

✗ *Yesterday went to the cinema.* ✓ *Yesterday **I** went to the cinema.*

✗ *Have a lot of people here.* ✓ ***There are** a lot of people here.*

In Chinese, 今天很热, 昨天去看电影了 and 这里有很多人 are complete. In English, put the subject in: a person, *it* or *there*.
:::

## Every clause needs a verb

::: theorem An English clause needs a finite verb {#rule-verb}
Every English statement and question contains a **finite verb**: a verb that shows tense (present or past) and, in the present, agrees with the subject. When the main idea is an adjective, a noun or a place, the verb is usually *be*.
:::

::: proof
A finite verb is what makes a group of words into a statement about a particular time. *She tired* has no such verb, so it says nothing about whether she is tired now or was tired yesterday; *She is tired* and *She was tired* do. This is also why English and Chinese differ with adjectives: in Chinese, 她很累 needs no verb, because the adjective itself can be the predicate (see [[core-grammar/word-classes]]), but in English the adjective must be joined to the subject by *be*.
:::

✗ *My father a doctor.* ✓ *My father **is** a doctor.*

✗ *She very happy.* ✓ *She **is** very happy.*

✗ *The shop near the station.* ✓ *The shop **is** near the station.*

A sentence also has only **one** finite verb in each clause. When you want to add a second verb, you need a conjunction (*and*, *but*, *because*) or another verb form, such as the *to*-infinitive:

✗ *I want go home.* ✓ *I want **to go** home.*

✗ *She went shopping bought a coat.* ✓ *She went shopping **and** bought a coat.*

Which verbs take *to* + verb and which take *-ing* is explained in [[verb-patterns/ing-or-infinitive]]; joining clauses correctly is the topic of [[sentences-and-paragraphs/complete-sentences]].

::: example Repairing sentences {#ex-repair}
Each sentence is missing a subject or a verb. Repair it and say what was missing. (a) *Is raining again.* (b) *This book very interesting.* (c) *Every morning goes to the gym.* (d) *Many people in the park.*
::: solution
(a) Missing subject. The weather needs dummy *it*: *It is raining again.* (or *It's raining again.*)

(b) Missing verb. An adjective after the subject needs *be*: *This book is very interesting.*

(c) Missing subject. We need to say who goes: *Every morning **he** goes to the gym.* (or *she*, *my father*, etc.; *goes* shows the subject is *he*, *she* or *it*).

(d) Missing verb. For existence, use *there* + *be*: *There are many people in the park.* (*Many people are in the park* is also correct, but *there are* is more natural when we introduce something new.)
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is a correct, complete English statement?
- [ ] *Very busy this week.*
- [ ] *My sister a student at Fudan.*
- [x] *It snowed in Harbin last night.*
- [ ] *Is a good idea.*
::: solution
*It snowed in Harbin last night* has a subject (*it*, dummy subject for weather) and a finite verb (*snowed*). The others need a subject (*I'm very busy this week*; *It's a good idea*) or a verb (*My sister is a student at Fudan*). In a quick, informal text message *Very busy this week* would be understood, but it is not a complete sentence.
:::
:::

## Topic first: a Chinese pattern in English

Chinese often begins a sentence with a **topic** — what the sentence is about — and then says something about it: 这本书我已经看过了 (‘this book, I've already read’). English can do this too, but only in speech and for emphasis: *This book, I've already read.* The normal written order is subject–verb–object:

✓ *I've already read this book.*

Chinese also uses topics that are not the subject or the object: 那场火，幸亏消防队来得早 (‘that fire, luckily the fire brigade came early’). English has to express the link with a preposition or a full clause: *Luckily, the fire brigade arrived early **at that fire**.* or *As for that fire, luckily the fire brigade arrived early.* The phrase *as for* is the usual English way to announce a topic.

::: example From topic–comment to subject–verb–object {#ex-topic}
Turn each Chinese-style English sentence into normal English word order. (a) *English grammar, I think it is not so difficult.* (b) *This kind of fruit, my hometown has a lot.* (c) *The homework, I finished yesterday.*
::: solution
(a) The topic *English grammar* is repeated by *it*. Remove the repetition: *I think English grammar is not so difficult.* (or *I don't think English grammar is so difficult.*, which is more natural.)

(b) The topic *this kind of fruit* is really the object of *has*. Put it after the verb: *My hometown has a lot of this kind of fruit.* Even better, use *there*: *There is a lot of this kind of fruit in my hometown.*

(c) *The homework* is the object of *finished*: *I finished the homework yesterday.* (In speech, *The homework I finished yesterday* is possible with strong stress on *homework*, to contrast it with something else.)
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Mandarin Chinese and English both use the order subject–verb–object for simple statements (我喝茶 / *I drink tea*), which makes many English sentences easy for Chinese speakers. The differences lie elsewhere.

- **Subjects.** Chinese can leave out the subject when the context makes it clear; English cannot, and it uses *it* and *there* when there is no real subject.
- **Topics.** Chinese is often described as **topic-prominent**: a sentence starts with what it is about, which need not be the subject (Li & Thompson 1981). English is **subject-prominent**: the subject position is always filled, and topics are marked only in speech or with phrases such as *as for*.
- **Adverbials.** In Chinese, time and place usually come before the verb (我明天在家工作); in English they usually come at the end, place before time (*I'm working at home tomorrow*).
- **Linking verbs.** Chinese adjectives can be predicates without a verb (她很高); English needs *be* (*She is tall*). Because Chinese 是 is used mainly before nouns, learners sometimes leave *be* out before adjectives: ✗ *She very tall.*
- **Words for *be* and *have*.** English *there is* corresponds to Chinese 有 in 桌子上有一本书 (*There is a book on the table*), not to *have*: see [[core-grammar/be-and-have]].
:::

::: history
The idea of sentence patterns has a long history in grammar teaching. C. T. Onions, in *An Advanced English Syntax* (1904), divided predicates into five forms; these are usually seen as the source of the five basic sentence patterns (五种基本句型) still taught in China and Japan. A. S. Hornby, who taught English in Japan before the Second World War, worked out a detailed list of verb patterns for learners and published it in *A Guide to Patterns and Usage in English* (1954). The seven clause types used in this lesson come from the descriptive grammars of Randolph Quirk and his colleagues, set out in *A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language* (1985).
:::

## Where this leads

The patterns of this lesson return in every later lesson. [[core-grammar/be-and-have]] looks at the SVC and SVA patterns with *be* and at *there is*. [[core-grammar/questions-and-negatives]] shows how the order changes in questions (*Is she a teacher?*). ::: summary
- An English clause is built from clause elements: subject (S), verb (V), object (O), complement (C) and adverbial (A).
- The normal order in statements is S + V (+ O or C) ([[#rule-svo]]); adverbials usually go at the end (how – where – when) or at the beginning, not between the verb and its object.
- An object is a different person or thing that the action is done to; a complement describes the subject or the object.
- Almost all clauses follow seven patterns: SV, SVO, SVC, SVA, SVOO, SVOC and SVOA ([[#rule-seven]]); the verb decides the pattern.
- Every statement needs a subject: use dummy *it* for weather, time and distance, and *there* + *be* for existence ([[#rule-subject]]). Only imperatives, and some informal speech, leave it out.
- Every clause needs one finite verb; with adjectives, nouns and places, that verb is usually *be* ([[#rule-verb]]).
:::
