Here are five ordinary Chinese sentences and their English translations:

| Chinese | English |
|---|---|
| 她是老师。 | *She **is** a teacher.* |
| 我很累。 | *I **am** very tired.* |
| 他在家。 | *He **is** at home.* |
| 我有两个哥哥。 | *I **have** two older brothers.* |
| 桌子上有一本书。 | ***There is** a book on the table.* |

Chinese uses three different words here — 是, 在 and 有 — and in 我很累 no verb at all. English uses only two verbs, *be* and *have*, plus the pattern *there is*. And the matching is not one to one: 是, 在 and "no verb" all become *be*, while 有 becomes either *have* or *there is*. This is why *be* and *have* cause so many mistakes, and why they come so early in this course.

## The forms of *be*

*Be* is the most irregular verb in English. It has eight forms: *be*, *am*, *is*, *are*, *was*, *were*, *been* and *being*.

::: definition The present and past of *be* {#def-be-forms}
In the present, *be* has three forms: ***am*** after *I*, ***is*** after *he*, *she*, *it* and singular nouns, and ***are*** after *you*, *we*, *they* and plural nouns. In the past it has two: ***was*** after *I*, *he*, *she*, *it* and singular nouns, and ***were*** after *you*, *we*, *they* and plural nouns.
:::

| subject | present | short form | negative | past |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *I* | *am* | *I'm* | *I'm not* | *was* |
| *you* | *are* | *you're* | *you aren't* / *you're not* | *were* |
| *he*, *she*, *it* | *is* | *he's*, *she's*, *it's* | *isn't* / *'s not* | *was* |
| *we* | *are* | *we're* | *we aren't* / *we're not* | *were* |
| *they* | *are* | *they're* | *they aren't* / *they're not* | *were* |

The short forms (*contractions*) are normal in speech and in informal writing: *I'm tired*, *She's from Chengdu*, *They're late*. In formal writing, such as an essay or a report, the full forms are more usual. There is no short form *amn't* in standard English; the negative of *I am* is *I'm not*, and in questions speakers use *aren't I?*: *I'm right, aren't I?* The past negatives are *wasn't* and *weren't*.

- *I'm a student. I'm not a teacher.*
- *Is your brother at home? — No, he isn't.*
- *We were in Xiamen last week. The weather was beautiful.*

::: quiz
Choose the correct form: *My parents ___ both doctors.*
- [ ] *is*
- [x] *are*
- [ ] *am*
- [ ] *be*
::: solution
The subject *my parents* is plural, like *they*, so the verb is *are*. *Be* itself is used after modal verbs and *to* (*They want to be doctors*), not as the verb of a statement.
:::
:::

::: example Choosing the form of *be* {#ex-be-forms}
Complete the dialogue with the right form of *be*, and explain each choice.

**Tom:** *Where ___ (1) you last night? I called twice.*

**Li Wei:** *Sorry! My cousins ___ (2) here from Dalian, and we ___ (3) at a restaurant. My phone ___ (4) in my bag.*

**Tom:** *___ (5) they still here?*

**Li Wei:** *Yes, they ___ (6). I ___ (7) free tonight, though. Do you want to meet?*
::: solution
1. *were* — past (*last night*), subject *you*.
2. *were* — past, plural subject *my cousins*.
3. *were* — past, subject *we*.
4. *was* — past, singular subject *my phone*.
5. *Are* — present (*still*), plural subject *they*; in a question *be* comes before the subject (see [[core-grammar/questions-and-negatives]]).
6. *are* — a short answer repeats the verb of the question: *Yes, they are.* (Not ✗ *Yes, they're*: the short form is not used at the end of a sentence.)
7. *am* (or *I'm free tonight*) — present, subject *I*.

To choose a form of *be*, ask two questions: present or past? And who is the subject — *I*, *he/she/it* (singular) or *you/we/they* (plural)?
:::
:::

## What *be* does

::: theorem Uses of *be* {#rule-be}
*Be* joins the subject to information about it. Use *be*:

1. before a **noun phrase** that says what or who the subject is: *She is a teacher.* *This is my phone.*
2. before an **adjective** that describes the subject: *I am tired.* *The room is clean.*
3. before a **place** or **time**: *He is at home.* *The party is on Friday.*
4. for **age**, **size**, **price** and **weather**: *I am nineteen.* *It is cold.* *How much is it?*
5. as an **auxiliary**, to make the progressive and the passive: *She is reading.* *The door was locked.* (See [[core-grammar/present-continuous]] and [[verb-patterns/the-passive]].)
:::

::: proof
In uses 1–4, *be* has almost no meaning of its own: it is the finite verb that every English clause needs (see [[core-grammar/sentence-patterns#rule-verb]]), and it carries tense and agreement (*is* / *was*, *am* / *are*). This explains why English needs *be* where Chinese has no verb. In 我很累 the adjective 累 can be the predicate by itself; in English an adjective cannot be the predicate, so *be* is needed to join it to the subject. The same is true for place: Chinese uses the verb 在, while English uses *be* followed by a preposition (*at home*, *in the kitchen*), and the preposition does the work of showing the location.
:::

### *Be* with feelings, states and age

English uses *be* + adjective for many states where Chinese uses a verb or a different structure. Learn these as fixed pairs:

- *I'm hungry.* *I'm thirsty.* *I'm cold.* *I'm hot.* (not ✗ *I have hungry*)
- *I'm afraid of dogs.* (not ✗ *I afraid dogs*)
- *I'm twenty years old.* or *I'm twenty.* (not ✗ *I have twenty years*)
- *She's interested in art.* *He's good at maths.*
- *Are you ready?* *I'm sure.* *We're late.*

French, Spanish and Italian speakers say ‘I have hunger’ and ‘I have twenty years’, so ✗ *I have twenty years* is a common error in many countries; Chinese speakers make it less often, but they sometimes leave *be* out: ✗ *I hungry now.*

::: warning *Be* before an ordinary verb
✗ *I am agree with you.* ✓ *I agree with you.*

✗ *She is like music.* ✓ *She likes music.*

✗ *We are go to school by bus.* ✓ *We go to school by bus.*

*Agree*, *like* and *go* are already verbs, so they do not need *be*. Use *be* + verb only in the progressive, with the *-ing* form: *We are going to school now.* This mistake is common because learners know that "every sentence needs *be*" and then add it everywhere. The rule is: every clause needs **a** verb — sometimes that verb is *be*, sometimes it is another verb.
:::

::: example Translating 是, 在 and "no verb" {#ex-shi-zai}
Translate into English, and say which use of *be* each sentence shows: (a) 我的房间很小。 (b) 她是医生。 (c) 你的钥匙在包里。 (d) 我十八岁。 (e) 今天星期三。
::: solution
(a) *My room is very small.* — *be* + adjective (use 2). Chinese has no verb; English needs *is*. Note that 很 here is often just part of the adjective predicate in Chinese and need not be translated as *very*: *My room is small* is also a good translation.

(b) *She is a doctor.* — *be* + noun phrase (use 1). Chinese 是 → *is*. English also needs the article *a* (see [[nouns-and-articles/a-and-the]]).

(c) *Your keys are in your bag.* — *be* + place (use 3). Chinese 在 → *are* + the preposition *in*. The verb is *are* because *keys* is plural. English also repeats the owner: 包里 here means *your bag*.

(d) *I'm eighteen.* or *I'm eighteen years old.* — age (use 4). Not ✗ *I have eighteen years*.

(e) *It's Wednesday today.* or *Today is Wednesday.* — time (use 4). With days, dates and times English often uses dummy *it*.
:::
:::

## *There is* and *there are*

::: definition Existential *there* {#def-there}
To say that something **exists** or is **present** somewhere, English uses ***there*** + *be* + noun phrase, usually followed by a place or time: *There is a bank near here.* The word *there* here has no meaning of place; it fills the subject position (see [[core-grammar/sentence-patterns#rule-subject]]). The verb agrees with the noun phrase that follows it: ***there is*** + singular or uncountable noun, ***there are*** + plural noun.
:::

- *There is a supermarket at the end of the street.*
- *There are two bedrooms in our flat.*
- *There is some milk in the fridge.* (uncountable: *is*)
- *There was a problem with my ticket.*
- *There were a lot of people at the concert.*

Negatives and questions:

- *There isn't a lift in this building.*
- *There aren't any eggs left.*
- *Is there a toilet on this floor? — Yes, there is.*
- *Are there any questions? — No, there aren't.*

::: remark *There's* with a plural
In informal speech, many speakers say *there's* before a plural noun: *There's two cafés on this street.* This is very common in conversation in both British and American English, and Swan (2016) and Carter & McCarthy (2006) both describe it. In writing and in exams, use *there are*.
:::

::: theorem *There is* or *have*? {#rule-there-have}
Chinese 有 has two meanings, and English uses a different structure for each:

1. **existence** in a place — 桌子上有一本书 — use ***there is/are***: *There is a book on the table.*
2. **possession** — 我有一本书 — use ***have***: *I have a book.*

If the Chinese sentence begins with a place or time (桌子上, 这个城市, 昨天), it usually needs *there*. If it begins with a person or thing that owns something (我, 我们学校), it usually needs *have*.
:::

::: proof
*Have* needs a subject that owns or contains something: *My school has a big library.* A place phrase such as *on the table* cannot be the subject of *have*, so ✗ *On the table has a book* is impossible; the subject position is filled by *there* instead. Both structures are sometimes possible with the same meaning, when the "owner" is a place that can be a noun phrase: *Our town has two cinemas* and *There are two cinemas in our town* are both correct. The second is more neutral; the first presents the cinemas as a feature of the town.
:::

::: warning ✗ *There have …* and ✗ *In the room has …*
✗ *There have many students in the library.* ✓ *There are many students in the library.*

✗ *In my hometown has a famous temple.* ✓ *There is a famous temple in my hometown.* ✓ *My hometown has a famous temple.*

These come from translating 有 word for word. In English, *there* goes with *be*, never with *have*.
:::

::: example *There is* or *have*? {#ex-there-have}
Translate, choosing *there is/are* or *have/has*: (a) 我家附近有一个公园。 (b) 王芳有一只猫。 (c) 这个句子有两个错误。 (d) 冰箱里没有牛奶了。
::: solution
(a) Begins with a place (我家附近) and says that something exists: *There is a park near my home.* (Also possible: *My home is near a park*, with a different focus.)

(b) Begins with an owner (王芳): *Wang Fang has a cat.* (*has* because the subject is *he*/*she*/*it*-like.)

(c) The sentence "contains" the errors. Both structures work: *There are two mistakes in this sentence.* or *This sentence has two mistakes.*

(d) Existence in a place, negative, with an uncountable noun: *There isn't any milk in the fridge.* or *There's no milk in the fridge.* The word 了 shows a change (the milk is finished); English can add *left*: *There's no milk left.*
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *There have a lot of trees in the park.*
- [ ] *In the park has a lot of trees.*
- [x] *There are a lot of trees in the park.*
- [ ] *There is a lot of trees in the park.*
::: solution
To say that something exists in a place, use *there* + *be*, and the verb agrees with the noun that follows: *trees* is plural, so *there are*. *There have* and *In the park has* translate 有 word for word. *There is a lot of trees* is heard in informal speech (see the remark above) but is not accepted in writing.
:::
:::

## The forms of *have*

::: definition The present and past of *have* {#def-have-forms}
In the present, *have* has two forms: ***has*** after *he*, *she*, *it* and singular nouns, and ***have*** after all other subjects. In the past it has one form for every subject: ***had***.
:::

| subject | present | past |
|---|---|---|
| *I*, *you*, *we*, *they*, plural nouns | *have* | *had* |
| *he*, *she*, *it*, singular nouns | *has* | *had* |

- *I have a brother and a sister.*
- *My sister has long hair.*
- *We had a dog when I was a child.*

With *have* as a main verb meaning ‘possess’, questions and negatives use *do*: *Do you have a pen?* *She doesn't have a car.* *Did you have a good weekend?* (These forms are explained in [[core-grammar/questions-and-negatives]].)

### *Have* and *have got*

::: theorem *Have* and *have got* for possession {#rule-have-got}
For possession, relationships, illnesses and features, English can use *have* or ***have got***: *I have a car* = *I've got a car*. *Have got* is common in informal British English, especially in speech. *Have* is the usual form in American English and is correct everywhere; American speakers also use *have got* (especially *I've got*, *you've got*) in informal speech, though less than British speakers. *Have got* is used only in the present; in the past, use *had*.
:::

::: proof
Biber et al. (1999), counting forms in large corpora, found *have got* far more frequent in British than in American conversation, and *have* (with *do* in questions and negatives) dominant in American English. In both varieties *have got* belongs mainly to speech; in formal writing *have* is preferred. The forms of questions and negatives differ:

| | *have* | *have got* |
|---|---|---|
| statement | *She has a cold.* | *She's got a cold.* |
| negative | *She doesn't have a cold.* | *She hasn't got a cold.* |
| question | *Does she have a cold?* | *Has she got a cold?* |
| past | *She had a cold.* | (*She had a cold.*) |

The form *I haven't a car* (without *got* or *do*) is found in older British English but sounds formal or old-fashioned today.
:::

::: warning Mixing *have* and *have got*
✗ *Do you have got a pen?* ✓ *Do you have a pen?* ✓ *Have you got a pen?*

✗ *I don't have got time.* ✓ *I don't have time.* ✓ *I haven't got time.*

✗ *I had got a cold last week.* ✓ *I had a cold last week.*

Choose one pattern and keep it: *do* goes with *have*, and *have got* makes its own questions and negatives.
:::

### *Have* for actions

*Have* is also used in many fixed expressions that describe actions, not possession:

- meals: *have breakfast*, *have lunch*, *have dinner*, *have a coffee*
- activities: *have a shower*, *have a bath*, *have a rest*, *have a swim*
- experiences: *have a good time*, *have a party*, *have a problem*, *have a chat*

In this action meaning, *have* behaves like an ordinary verb. It can be progressive (*I'm having lunch — can I call you back?*), and *have got* is not possible (✗ *I've got breakfast at seven*).

::: example Possession or action? {#ex-have-action}
Which sentences are correct? Correct the others. (a) *I'm having a sister.* (b) *We're having a party on Saturday.* (c) *He's got a shower every morning.* (d) *She's got two cats.*
::: solution
(a) Wrong. *Have* here means possession, which is a state, so it is not used in the progressive: *I have a sister.* or *I've got a sister.*

(b) Correct. *Have a party* is an action, and the present continuous here is used for a future arrangement (see [[core-grammar/talking-about-the-future]]).

(c) Wrong. *Have a shower* is an action, so *have got* is not possible: *He has a shower every morning.* (AmE more often *takes a shower*.)

(d) Correct. Possession; *have got* is natural here, especially in British English.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which question is correct?
- [ ] *Have you a brother?*
- [x] *Have you got a brother?*
- [ ] *Do you have got a brother?*
- [ ] *Are you have a brother?*
::: solution
*Have you got a brother?* is correct (common in British English); so is *Do you have a brother?* (usual in American English, also very common in British English). *Have you a brother?* is old-fashioned, *Do you have got* mixes two patterns, and *Are you have* adds *be* where it is not needed.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **No verb with adjectives in Chinese.** 她很忙 has no verb; *She is busy* needs *is*. The Chinese 很 is often required for the sentence to sound complete and neutral, and does not always mean *very*; English *busy* alone is neutral, so 她很忙 is often best translated *She's busy*.
- **在 is a verb; English *be* + preposition.** 他在家 → *He is at home*. Learners sometimes drop *be* (✗ *He at home*) or the preposition (✗ *He is the office*). With the word *home* alone, *He's home* is also correct in informal English.
- **有 for existence and possession.** Chinese uses the same verb; English uses *there is/are* for existence and *have* for possession ([[#rule-there-have]]).
- **是 before adjectives.** In Chinese, 是 before an adjective expresses emphasis or contrast (她是很聪明 ‘she really is clever’). English does this with stress, not with an extra word: *She **is** clever.* (with *is* stressed).
- **Agreement.** Chinese verbs do not change for person or number. English *am*, *is*, *are*, *was*, *were*, *has* and *have* change with the subject, and choosing the right one takes practice: *My friends are*, not ✗ *My friends is*.
:::

::: history
The many forms of *be* come from several different old verbs, a pattern linguists call **suppletion**. Old English had a verb *wesan*, which gave the past forms *was* and *were*; a verb *bēon*, which gave *be*, *being* and *been*; and present forms built on a third root, which gave *am* and *is* (related to German *ist* and Latin *est*). *Are* came into standard English from the northern dialects of Middle English. Many of the world's languages have irregular forms for *be*, because the most frequent verbs keep old irregular forms longest. *Have got* has a different story: it began as the present perfect of *get* (‘have obtained’) and came to mean simply ‘have’.
:::

## Where this leads

*Be* and *have* return in almost every lesson. *Be* is the auxiliary of the progressive ([[core-grammar/present-continuous]]) and of *be going to* ([[core-grammar/talking-about-the-future]]); *have* is the auxiliary of the perfect, studied in [[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect]]. Questions and negatives with *be* and *have* are in [[core-grammar/questions-and-negatives]]. Agreement after *there is* and *there are* with more difficult nouns (*There is a lot of people*?) is treated in [[nouns-and-articles/agreement]].

::: summary
- *Be* has the present forms *am*, *is*, *are* and the past forms *was*, *were*; the short forms *I'm*, *she's*, *they're* are normal in speech ([[#def-be-forms]]).
- English needs *be* before nouns, adjectives, places, times and ages ([[#rule-be]]), where Chinese uses 是, 在 or no verb at all. Do not add *be* before an ordinary verb (✗ *I am agree*).
- Use *I'm hungry*, *I'm cold*, *I'm twenty*, not *have*.
- *There is* + singular/uncountable and *there are* + plural say that something exists; *have* says that someone owns something ([[#rule-there-have]]). Never ✗ *there have*.
- *Have got* = *have* for possession, mainly in informal British English; *have* is correct everywhere ([[#rule-have-got]]). Do not mix them (✗ *Do you have got*).
- In expressions such as *have lunch* and *have a shower*, *have* is an action verb: it can be progressive, and *have got* is not possible.
:::
