Look at three Chinese phrases: 一个漂亮的姑娘, 她很漂亮, 她唱得很漂亮. The same word, 漂亮, describes a person in the first two and the way someone sings in the third. English uses two different words:

- *a **beautiful** girl* — *She is **beautiful**.* (adjective)
- *She sings **beautifully**.* (adverb)

English also puts them in different places. The adjective goes before the noun or after *be*; the adverb of manner usually goes after the verb, and after the object if there is one: *She speaks English **well***, not ✗ *She speaks well English*. This lesson explains where adjectives and adverbs go, how to form adverbs with *-ly*, how to use adverbs of frequency such as *always* and *never*, and how to compare things with *-er*, *-est*, *more* and *most*.

## Adjectives

An adjective describes a noun (see [[core-grammar/word-classes#def-adjective]]). It can stand in two positions.

::: theorem Positions of adjectives {#rule-adj-position}
1. **Before a noun** (attributive position): *a **cold** day*, *an **interesting** book*, *my **new** phone*.
2. **After *be*** and other linking verbs — *become*, *get*, *seem*, *look*, *feel*, *sound*, *taste*, *smell* (predicative position): *The day is **cold**.* *You look **tired**.* *This soup tastes **good**.*

English adjectives never change for number: *a **long** road*, *two **long** roads*.
:::

::: proof
These are the two jobs of an adjective in the clause patterns of [[core-grammar/sentence-patterns]]: as part of a noun phrase, or as a subject complement (SVC). Most adjectives can do both. A few can only stand after the verb, especially those beginning with *a-*: *asleep*, *awake*, *alive*, *alone*, *afraid*. We say *The baby is asleep* but not ✗ *an asleep baby*; we use another adjective instead: *a sleeping baby*, *a frightened child*. A few others are used only before a noun: *the **main** reason*, *an **only** child*. Linking verbs such as *look* and *taste* take an adjective, not an adverb, because the word describes the subject, not the action: *She looks **happy*** (she is happy), compared with *She looked **happily** at the photos* (the way she looked).
:::

When there are several adjectives before a noun, they usually follow this order:

| opinion | size | age | shape | colour | origin | material | purpose | noun |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *lovely* | *big* | *old* | *round* | *red* | *Chinese* | *wooden* | *dining* | *table* |

- *a **beautiful old** house*
- *a **small black** cat*
- *a **lovely Italian** restaurant*
- *a **big wooden** box*

Two or three adjectives before a noun are normal; more than three sound heavy. The order is a strong tendency, described in all reference grammars, not a law: an opinion adjective (*lovely*, *nice*) comes first, and the adjectives closest to the noun describe the most permanent features.

### *-ed* and *-ing* adjectives

Many adjectives come in pairs: *bored* / *boring*, *interested* / *interesting*, *tired* / *tiring*, *excited* / *exciting*, *surprised* / *surprising*, *confused* / *confusing*.

::: theorem *-ed* and *-ing* adjectives {#rule-ed-ing}
An ***-ed*** adjective describes how a person **feels**: *I'm **bored**.* *She was **surprised**.* An ***-ing*** adjective describes the person or thing that **causes** the feeling: *The film is **boring**.* *The news was **surprising**.*
:::

- *I was **interested** in the talk because it was **interesting**.*
- *The journey was **tiring**, so we were very **tired**.*
- *Are you **confused**? This map is very **confusing**.*

::: warning *I'm boring* and *I'm bored*
✗ *I'm very boring today — let's go out.* ✓ *I'm very bored today — let's go out.*

*I'm boring* means ‘other people find me dull’, which is probably not what you want to say. The same with *I'm confusing* (✓ *I'm confused*) and *I'm exciting* (✓ *I'm excited*). Ask: am I describing my feeling (*-ed*) or the thing that causes it (*-ing*)?
:::

::: example Choosing the adjective {#ex-ed-ing}
Choose the correct adjective. (a) *The lesson was so (bored / boring) that I fell asleep.* (b) *We were (amazed / amazing) by the view.* (c) *Kenji is (interested / interesting) in old cameras.* (d) *It was a (tired / tiring) day.* (e) *Don't be (worried / worrying) — everything is fine.*
::: solution
(a) *boring* — the lesson caused the feeling. (b) *amazed* — how we felt. (c) *interested* — Kenji's feeling. (d) *tiring* — the day caused tiredness. (e) *worried* — how you feel.
:::
:::

## Adverbs

::: theorem Forming adverbs with *-ly* {#rule-ly}
Most adverbs of manner are formed by adding ***-ly*** to an adjective: *quick* → *quickly*, *careful* → *carefully*, *bad* → *badly*. Spelling changes:

1. consonant + ***-y*** → ***-ily***: *happy* → *happily*, *easy* → *easily*, *angry* → *angrily*;
2. ***-le*** → ***-ly***: *gentle* → *gently*, *simple* → *simply*, *possible* → *possibly*;
3. ***-ic*** → ***-ically***: *basic* → *basically*, *automatic* → *automatically* (but *public* → *publicly*);
4. a few short words drop *-e*: *true* → *truly*, *whole* → *wholly*; and *full* → *fully*.
:::

::: theorem Irregular adverbs {#rule-irregular-adverbs}
- The adverb of *good* is ***well***: *She is a good singer.* → *She sings well.*
- Some adverbs have the **same form** as the adjective: *fast*, *hard*, *early*, *late*, *daily*, *monthly*, *straight*, *far*: *a fast car* → *She drives fast.* *a hard job* → *He works hard.* *an early train* → *I got up early.*
- ***Hardly*** and ***lately*** are not the adverbs of *hard* and *late*: *hardly* means ‘almost not’ (*I hardly slept* = I slept very little) and *lately* means ‘recently’.
:::

::: proof
The *-ly* ending comes from an old word meaning ‘having the form of’ (related to *like*), so *quickly* once meant roughly ‘in a quick way’. Adverbs without *-ly*, such as *fast* and *hard*, are older forms that kept the adjective's shape; English once had many of these "flat adverbs", and a few survive in informal use (*Drive slow*, *Come quick*, *Go easy on him*) and in fixed phrases (*Hold tight*, *Sleep tight*). In standard written English the *-ly* form is usual where it exists. Note that *well* is also an adjective meaning ‘healthy’ (*I'm well, thanks*), and that some *-ly* words are adjectives, not adverbs: *friendly*, *lovely*, *lonely*, *ugly*, *silly*. To use them adverbially we need a phrase: *She smiled **in a friendly way***.
:::

::: warning *Hard* and *hardly*
✗ *He works hardly to pass the exam.* ✓ *He works hard to pass the exam.*

*He hardly works* means ‘he does almost no work’ — the opposite of what the learner intends. Similarly: ✓ *I've been busy lately* (recently), but ✓ *I arrived late* (not on time).
:::

::: example Linking verb or action verb? {#ex-linking}
Choose the adjective or the adverb, and explain. (a) *The soup smells (good / well).* (b) *The cook smelled the soup (careful / carefully).* (c) *You look (tired / tiredly).* (d) *She looked (angry / angrily) at the broken window.* (e) *I feel (bad / badly) about what I said.*
::: solution
Ask whether the word describes the **subject** (adjective, after a linking verb) or the **action** (adverb).

(a) *good* — *smell* is a linking verb here: the soup has a good smell. (*The soup smells well* would suggest that the soup has a good nose!)

(b) *carefully* — *smell* is an action verb here, with an object (*the soup*): the adverb says how the cook smelled it.

(c) *tired* — *look* = seem: it describes you.

(d) *angrily* — *look at* is an action (directing the eyes): the adverb says how she looked.

(e) *bad* — *feel* is a linking verb; *I feel bad* means ‘I feel sorry or guilty’. *I feel badly* is also heard, especially in American English, with the same meaning, but *bad* is the safer choice.
:::
:::

### Where adverbs go

::: theorem Positions of adverbs {#rule-adv-position}
1. **Manner** (*how?*), **place** (*where?*) and most **time** adverbs (*when?*) usually go at the **end** of the clause, after the verb and its object: *She speaks English **fluently**.* *I'll meet you **outside**.* *We arrived **yesterday**.* When there are several, the usual order is manner – place – time (see [[core-grammar/sentence-patterns#rule-svo]]).
2. **Frequency** adverbs (*always*, *usually*, *often*, *sometimes*, *rarely*, *never*) and a few others (*already*, *just*, *also*, *still*, *even*) usually go in the **middle**: before the main verb, but after *be* and after the first auxiliary: *I **often** walk to work.* *She is **always** late.* *I have **never** been to Tibet.*
3. **Degree** adverbs (*very*, *quite*, *really*, *too*, *extremely*, *so*) go directly **before the adjective or adverb** they modify: ***very** cold*, ***really** quickly*, ***too** expensive*.
4. An adverb does **not** normally go between a verb and its object: ✗ *She speaks fluently English.*
:::

::: proof
These positions are tendencies described in reference grammars (Quirk et al. 1985; Swan 2016), and there is some freedom. Manner adverbs can come before the verb in writing (*She **quickly** opened the door*), and for emphasis many adverbs can begin the sentence (***Sometimes** I walk to work*; ***Suddenly**, the lights went out*). *Usually*, *often* and *sometimes* are the most mobile frequency adverbs; *always* and *never* rarely move from the middle position (✗ *Always I walk* is unusual). What English avoids is splitting the verb and its object, because the verb and its object form a close unit. A long object can sometimes follow the adverb in careful writing (*She explained **clearly** the reasons for her decision*), but with a short object, the adverb goes after it.
:::

### Adverbs of frequency

Adverbs of frequency say how often something happens. They are normally used with the present simple or past simple ([[core-grammar/present-simple#rule-ps-uses]]).

::: widget graph
title: Adverbs of frequency
nodes: never@0,0; rarely@1.6,0; sometimes@3.6,0; often@5.6,0; usually@7.4,0; always@9,0
edges: never>rarely; rarely>sometimes; sometimes>often; often>usually; usually>always
caption: Adverbs of frequency on a scale from *never* (not at all) to *always* (every time). The positions are approximate: there is no exact percentage for *often* or *usually*, and speakers differ. *Seldom* is close to *rarely* but more formal; *hardly ever* means almost never; *frequently* is close to *often*.
:::

- *I **always** have breakfast.*
- *We **usually** go to bed at eleven.*
- *She **often** forgets her keys.*
- *I **sometimes** take the bus.* / ***Sometimes** I take the bus.*
- *He **rarely** cooks.*
- *They **never** eat meat.*
- *He's **never** late.* (after *be*)
- *Do you **ever** go to the theatre?* (*ever* in questions = ‘at any time’)

::: example Placing adverbs of frequency {#ex-frequency}
Put the adverb in the correct position. (a) *My brother is hungry.* (always) (b) *I drink coffee in the evening.* (never) (c) *Have you been to Japan?* (ever) (d) *We go out on Fridays.* (usually) (e) *She can't sleep.* (sometimes)
::: solution
(a) *My brother is **always** hungry.* — after *be*.

(b) *I **never** drink coffee in the evening.* — before the main verb. (Do not add *don't*: *never* is already negative.)

(c) *Have you **ever** been to Japan?* — after the first auxiliary *have*, before the main verb.

(d) *We **usually** go out on Fridays.* — before the main verb; *Usually we go out on Fridays* is also possible.

(e) *She **sometimes** can't sleep.* or ***Sometimes** she can't sleep.* — with a negative modal, *sometimes* usually comes before it or at the start; ✗ *She can't sometimes sleep* means something different and sounds odd.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence has the adverb in the right place?
- [ ] *I eat always rice for lunch.*
- [ ] *She speaks very well Chinese.*
- [x] *They have already finished.*
- [ ] *He is late often.*
::: solution
*Already* goes after the first auxiliary (*have*) and before the main verb (*finished*). The others should be *I always eat rice for lunch*, *She speaks Chinese very well* (not between the verb and its object) and *He is often late* (after *be*). *He is late quite often* is also possible, with *quite often* at the end.
:::
:::

## Comparatives and superlatives

::: definition Comparative and superlative {#def-comparison}
The **comparative** form of an adjective or adverb compares two people, things or groups: *taller*, *more expensive*, *faster*. It is usually followed by ***than***. The **superlative** form picks out one from a group of three or more as having the most of a quality: *the tallest*, *the most expensive*. It is usually preceded by ***the***.
:::

::: theorem Forming comparatives and superlatives {#rule-er-est}
1. **One-syllable** adjectives add ***-er*** and ***-est***: *tall* → *taller* → *the tallest*, *cheap* → *cheaper* → *the cheapest*. Spelling: *nice* → *nicer* (add only *-r*); *big* → *bigger*, *hot* → *hotter* (double after one vowel + one consonant); *dry* → *drier*.
2. **Two-syllable** adjectives ending in ***-y*** change it to ***-ier***, ***-iest***: *happy* → *happier* → *the happiest*, *easy* → *easier*, *busy* → *busiest*.
3. **Other two-syllable** adjectives and **all longer** ones use ***more*** and ***most***: *careful* → *more careful* → *the most careful*; *expensive* → *more expensive*; *interesting* → *the most interesting*. Some common two-syllable adjectives can take either form: *quiet* (*quieter* / *more quiet*), *simple*, *narrow*, *clever*, *polite*.
4. **Irregular**: *good* → *better* → *the best*; *bad* → *worse* → *the worst*; *far* → *farther*/*further* → *the farthest*/*furthest*; *much*/*many* → *more* → *the most*; *little* → *less* → *the least*.
5. **Adverbs** ending in *-ly* use *more*/*most*: *more slowly*, *most carefully*. Adverbs with the same form as the adjective add *-er*/*-est*: *faster*, *harder*, *earlier*. *Well* → *better* → *best*; *badly* → *worse* → *worst*.
:::

::: proof
The choice is mainly about length. Short words take endings easily (*cheaper*), while long words with endings would be hard to say (✗ *expensiver*), so they use *more* and *most*. Two-syllable adjectives are the border zone, and usage varies; dictionaries such as the *Oxford* and *Cambridge* learner's dictionaries show the forms of each one. For *far*, both *farther* and *further* are used for distance; *further* also means ‘more, additional’ (*further information*), and *further* is more common in British English. *More* and *the most* are sometimes used with one-syllable adjectives for emphasis or contrast (*more true*), but the *-er* forms are the norm.
:::

Comparatives with *than*, and superlatives with *in* or *of*:

- *Shanghai is **bigger than** Hangzhou.*
- *This phone is **more expensive than** that one.*
- *My sister runs **faster than** me.*
- *Mount Everest is **the highest** mountain **in** the world.*
- *That was **the best** day **of** my life.*
- *She is **the most experienced** player **in** the team.*

After *than* and *as*, English uses an object pronoun in everyday speech (*She's taller than **me***) or a subject pronoun + verb (*She's taller than **I am***); *taller than I* without a verb is formal.

To say that two things are equal, use ***as* … *as***: *My room is **as big as** yours.* To say they are not equal, use ***not as* … *as*** (or *not so … as*, less common): *Wuhan is **not as cold as** Harbin.*

::: warning Double comparatives and missing *than*
✗ *My brother is more taller than me.* ✓ *My brother is taller than me.*

✗ *This is the most easiest question.* ✓ *This is the easiest question.*

✗ *Her English is more better than mine.* ✓ *Her English is better than mine.* (or *much better*, for emphasis)

✗ *He is taller me.* ✓ *He is taller than me.*

Use *-er* **or** *more*, never both. To make a comparative stronger, use *much*, *far* or *a lot*: *much taller*, *far more expensive*.
:::

::: example Comparing {#ex-compare}
Write one comparative and one superlative sentence from each set of facts. (a) *Train: 4 hours. Plane: 2 hours. Bus: 9 hours.* (fast/slow) (b) *Hotel A: ¥300. Hotel B: ¥550. Hotel C: ¥900.* (cheap/expensive) (c) *Li Na: 8/10. Tom: 6/10. Carlos: 9/10.* (good)
::: solution
(a) *The plane is faster than the train.* *The bus is the slowest.* (also *The plane is the fastest way to travel.*)

(b) *Hotel B is more expensive than Hotel A.* *Hotel A is the cheapest.* (or *Hotel C is the most expensive.*)

(c) *Li Na's score is better than Tom's.* *Carlos got the best score.* (or *Carlos did the best*; *did better than* uses the adverb *well* → *better*.)
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *This exercise is more easy than the last one.*
- [ ] *He is the most tall boy in the class.*
- [x] *Today is hotter than yesterday.*
- [ ] *My English is worst than yours.*
::: solution
*Hot* has one syllable and doubles its *t*: *hotter*. *Easy* takes *-ier*: *easier*. *Tall* has one syllable: *the tallest*. *Bad* → *worse* (comparative), *worst* (superlative): *My English is worse than yours*.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **比 and *than*.** Chinese puts the comparison before the adjective: 他比我高. English puts the adjective first, in its comparative form, and then *than*: *He is taller than me.* Learners sometimes keep the Chinese order (✗ *He than me tall*) or forget to change the adjective (✗ *He is tall than me*).
- **最 and *the* + superlative.** 最高 → *the highest*. English needs *the* before most superlatives, and an *in* or *of* phrase for the group: *the tallest **in** the class*.
- **越来越.** 越来越冷 → *colder and colder*; 越来越贵 → *more and more expensive*.
- **地 and the position of adverbs.** Chinese manner adverbs come before the verb (慢慢地走, 认真地学习). In English most manner adverbs go after the verb and its object: *walk slowly*, *study English seriously*. Putting them between the verb and object is a common error (✗ *study seriously English*).
- **得 and complements.** 她唱得很好 → *She sings very well*: the Chinese 得 phrase usually corresponds to an English adverb at the end.
- **很.** In 她很漂亮 the 很 often just completes the adjective predicate; *She's beautiful* or *She's very beautiful* are both possible translations, depending on emphasis.
:::

::: history
The rules for comparatives were not always as strict as they are today. Double comparatives and superlatives were normal in earlier English: in Shakespeare's *Julius Caesar*, Mark Antony calls Brutus's blow "the most unkindest cut of all". Eighteenth-century grammarians such as Robert Lowth condemned these forms as illogical, and in standard English they have disappeared, though they live on in some dialects. The same grammarians favoured adverbs in *-ly* over the older "flat" adverbs (*He spoke very wise*), which are still common in many spoken varieties but are no longer standard in writing. The order of adjectives before a noun, by contrast, was never taught as a rule until modern times; native speakers follow it without knowing it, and it was described in detail only by twentieth-century grammars such as Quirk et al. (1985).
:::

## Where this leads

Adjectives inside longer noun phrases, with prepositional phrases and relative clauses after the noun, are covered in [[nouns-and-articles/noun-phrases]] and [[complex-sentences/relative-clauses]]. Word families that link adjectives and adverbs (*careful*, *carefully*, *care*) are explained in [[word-formation/suffixes]], and the choice of the right adjective for a noun (*heavy rain*, *strong coffee*) in [[collocation-and-register/verb-noun-adjective-noun]].

::: summary
- Adjectives go before a noun (*a cold day*) or after *be* and linking verbs (*It's cold*, *You look tired*), and never take a plural ending ([[#rule-adj-position]]).
- *-ed* adjectives describe feelings (*bored*), *-ing* adjectives the cause (*boring*) ([[#rule-ed-ing]]).
- Most adverbs add *-ly* (*quickly*, *happily*, *gently*); *good* → *well*; *fast*, *hard*, *early*, *late* do not change; *hardly* and *lately* have different meanings ([[#rule-ly]], [[#rule-irregular-adverbs]]).
- Manner, place and time adverbs go at the end; frequency adverbs before the main verb but after *be* and the first auxiliary; never between a verb and its object ([[#rule-adv-position]]).
- Comparatives: *-er* for one syllable, *-ier* for two-syllable *-y* words, *more* for longer words; irregular *better*, *worse*; use *than* after a comparative and *the* before a superlative ([[#rule-er-est]]).
- Never combine *more* and *-er*; Chinese 比, 最, 越来越 and 地 correspond to *than*, *the -est*, *-er and -er* and an adverb after the verb.
:::
