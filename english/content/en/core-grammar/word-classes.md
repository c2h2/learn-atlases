Look at the word *water* in these three sentences:

- *I drink a lot of water.*
- *Can you water the plants?*
- *The hotel has a water park.*

The word looks the same each time, but it does a different job. In the first sentence it names a thing. In the second it is an action. In the third it describes the word *park*. How does an English speaker know which job a word is doing? Mostly from its **position** in the sentence and from the small words around it: *a lot of water*, *you water*, *a water park*.

These jobs are called **word classes**, or parts of speech. A dictionary marks them with short labels — *n.*, *v.*, *adj.* — and almost every grammar rule you will learn mentions them: "add *-s* to the verb", "put the adjective before the noun", "use an adverb, not an adjective". This first lesson shows the eight main word classes of English, how to recognise each one, and how they compare with the word classes of Chinese.

## What a word class is

::: definition Word class {#def-word-class}
A **word class** (also called a **part of speech**) is a group of words that behave in the same way in sentences: they appear in the same positions, take the same endings and combine with the same kinds of words. The main word classes of English are **nouns**, **verbs**, **adjectives**, **adverbs**, **pronouns**, **determiners**, **prepositions** and **conjunctions**.
:::

Meaning helps, but it is not enough. People often say "a noun is a thing" and "a verb is an action". That is a good first idea, but many words do not fit it. *Arrival* and *decision* are nouns, but they describe things that happen. *Know* and *seem* are verbs, but they are not actions. So grammar books use **tests**: what can come before the word, what endings it can take, and where it can stand in a sentence.

::: theorem Word class depends on use {#rule-class-by-use}
In English, the class of a word in a sentence is decided by how the word is used there: its position, its endings and the words it combines with. Many English words belong to more than one class, and the same spelling can be a noun in one sentence and a verb or adjective in another.
:::

::: proof
English has very few endings, so it relies on word order to show grammar. A word in the position after *a* or *the* is treated as a noun (*a walk*, *the answer*); a word after a subject such as *I* or *they* is treated as a verb (*they walk*, *I answer*). Dictionaries show this by giving one word several entries: the *Oxford*, *Cambridge* and *Longman* learner's dictionaries all list *water* both as a noun and as a verb. The rule has limits: most words have one class that is much more common than the others, and you cannot turn any word into any class (*She beautifuls the room* is not English).
:::

## The eight main word classes

The table gives the job of each class, some typical members and a quick test.

| class | its job | examples | quick test |
|---|---|---|---|
| noun | names a person, thing, place or idea | *teacher*, *phone*, *Beijing*, *music*, *idea* | can follow *the* or *a*: *the phone* |
| verb | says what someone does or what is the case | *eat*, *go*, *be*, *have*, *know* | can follow *I*, *you*, *they*: *they eat* |
| adjective | describes a noun | *big*, *happy*, *red*, *difficult* | fits *a very ___ thing* and *It is ___*: *It is difficult* |
| adverb | says how, when, where or how often | *quickly*, *today*, *here*, *often*, *very* | answers *how?* *when?* *where?*: *She runs quickly* |
| pronoun | stands for a noun | *I*, *you*, *she*, *it*, *we*, *them*, *this* | can replace a noun phrase: *Tom* → *he* |
| determiner | comes before a noun and limits it | *a*, *an*, *the*, *this*, *my*, *some*, *every* | comes first in a noun phrase: *my new phone* |
| preposition | links a noun to the rest of the sentence | *in*, *on*, *at*, *with*, *from*, *after* | is followed by a noun or pronoun: *with her* |
| conjunction | joins words, phrases or clauses | *and*, *but*, *or*, *because*, *when*, *if* | joins two parts: *tea and coffee* |

Some grammars add an eighth or ninth class, **interjections**: short words such as *oh*, *wow*, *hello* and *ouch* that stand alone and show feeling. They are common in speech but have almost no grammar.

### Nouns

::: definition Noun {#def-noun}
A **noun** is a word that names a person, an animal, a thing, a place, an event or an idea. Most nouns can follow *a*, *an* or *the*, and most have a plural form with *-s*.
:::

Examples: *student*, *cat*, *table*, *London*, *party*, *love*, *information*.

- *The **teacher** gave us a **test**.*
- *My **brother** lives in **Shanghai**.*
- ***Music** makes me happy.*

Names of particular people and places (*Li Wei*, *Shanghai*, *Monday*) are **proper nouns** and begin with a capital letter. All others (*city*, *day*) are **common nouns**. Some nouns, such as *information* and *music*, have no plural; the lesson [[core-grammar/nouns-pronouns]] explains countable and uncountable nouns.

### Verbs

::: definition Verb {#def-verb}
A **verb** is a word that says what someone or something does, or what is the case. Most verbs change their form: *work*, *works*, *worked*, *working*. Every complete English sentence contains at least one verb.
:::

- *They **play** football on Saturdays.* (an action)
- *I **know** the answer.* (a state of mind)
- *Amina **is** a nurse.* (*be* links *Amina* and *a nurse*)

Two special groups of verbs help other verbs. **Auxiliary verbs** (*be*, *have*, *do*) help to make questions, negatives and tenses: *Do you like it?* *She is working.* **Modal verbs** (*can*, *must*, *should*, *will* …) add meanings such as ability or obligation: *I can swim.* You will meet them in [[core-grammar/questions-and-negatives]] and [[core-grammar/first-modals]].

### Adjectives

::: definition Adjective {#def-adjective}
An **adjective** is a word that describes a noun or a pronoun: its size, colour, age, quality, feeling and so on. An adjective usually stands before a noun (*a **cold** day*) or after *be* and similar verbs (*The day is **cold**.*).
:::

- *Kenji has a **new** bike.*
- *This soup is **hot**.*
- *She looks **tired**.*

Most adjectives can follow *very* (*very cold*) and have comparative forms (*colder*, *more difficult*). English adjectives do not change for plural: *a **red** car*, *two **red** cars* — never ✗ *two reds cars*.

### Adverbs

::: definition Adverb {#def-adverb}
An **adverb** is a word that tells us more about a verb, an adjective, another adverb or a whole sentence. Adverbs answer questions such as *how?*, *when?*, *where?*, *how often?* and *how much?*
:::

- *She speaks **slowly**.* (how?)
- *We arrived **yesterday**.* (when?)
- *Please wait **here**.* (where?)
- *I **often** walk to work.* (how often?)
- *The film was **very** good.* (how much? — *very* tells us more about the adjective *good*)

Many adverbs are made from adjectives with *-ly*: *quick* → *quickly*, *careful* → *carefully*. But not every word ending in *-ly* is an adverb: *friendly*, *lovely* and *lonely* are adjectives (*a friendly dog*). The lesson [[core-grammar/adjectives-adverbs]] looks at this more closely.

### Pronouns

::: definition Pronoun {#def-pronoun}
A **pronoun** is a word that stands for a noun or a noun phrase, so that we do not have to repeat it: *I*, *you*, *he*, *she*, *it*, *we*, *they*, *me*, *him*, *her*, *us*, *them*, and words such as *this*, *that*, *someone* and *nothing*.
:::

- *Wang Fang is my classmate. **She** comes from Xi'an.*
- *I bought a cake and ate **it**.*
- ***Someone** called you.*

### Determiners

::: definition Determiner {#def-determiner}
A **determiner** is a word that comes at the beginning of a noun phrase and tells us which thing or how much of it we mean. The most common determiners are the **articles** *a*, *an* and *the*; others are *this*, *that*, *these*, *those*, *my*, *your*, *his*, *her*, *our*, *their*, *some*, *any*, *no*, *every*, *each* and numbers.
:::

- ***The** bus is late.*
- *I need **a** pen.*
- ***My** parents live in Wuhan.*
- *Can I have **some** water?*

Determiners come before any adjectives: *my **new** phone*, not ✗ *new my phone*. Chinese has words with similar meanings (这, 那, 我的, 一些), but it has no articles, so *a* and *the* need special attention. They are a whole course of their own: [[nouns-and-articles/a-and-the]].

### Prepositions

::: definition Preposition {#def-preposition}
A **preposition** is a short word that comes before a noun, a pronoun or a noun phrase and links it to the rest of the sentence, often to show place, time or direction: *in*, *on*, *at*, *to*, *from*, *with*, *for*, *of*, *about*, *after*, *under*.
:::

- *The keys are **on** the table.*
- *I'll see you **after** lunch.*
- *She went **to** the bank **with** her sister.*

A preposition together with its noun phrase is a **prepositional phrase**: *on the table*, *after lunch*. The lesson [[core-grammar/prepositions-time-place]] covers the most common ones.

### Conjunctions

::: definition Conjunction {#def-conjunction}
A **conjunction** is a word that joins two words, phrases or clauses. **Coordinating conjunctions** (*and*, *but*, *or*, *so*) join parts of equal rank; **subordinating conjunctions** (*because*, *when*, *if*, *although*, *before*) begin a clause that depends on another clause.
:::

- *I like tea **and** coffee.*
- *It was cold, **but** we went out.*
- *I stayed at home **because** I was ill.*
- ***When** it rains, I take the bus.*

::: quiz
What is the word class of *happy* in *The children look happy*?
- [ ] noun
- [ ] verb
- [x] adjective
- [ ] adverb
::: solution
*Happy* describes the children (a noun phrase) and comes after the verb *look*, a verb like *be*. It fits the test *a very happy child*. It is an adjective. The adverb would be *happily*, which describes how someone does something: *They played happily*.
:::
:::

## Open and closed classes

The eight classes are not all the same size. English has hundreds of thousands of nouns, but only a small number of prepositions.

::: definition Open and closed classes {#def-open-closed}
**Open classes** — nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs — have very many members and accept new words all the time (*selfie*, *to google*, *podcast*). **Closed classes** — pronouns, determiners, prepositions and conjunctions, together with the auxiliary and modal verbs — are small, and new members are very rarely added.
:::

The words of the open classes carry most of the meaning, so they are often called **content words**. The words of the closed classes are fewer, but each one is used very often; they show how the content words fit together, and are often called **function words**. In a typical English text, the most frequent words — *the*, *of*, *and*, *a*, *to*, *in* — are almost all function words. The course *Core Vocabulary* looks at them in [[core-vocabulary/function-words]].

- [ ] *computer*
- [ ] *quickly*

*Under* is a preposition, a closed class: English has only a small number of prepositions, and new ones are hardly ever made. *Computer* (noun), *quickly* (adverb) and *beautiful* (adjective) belong to open classes.

## How to recognise a word class

When you meet a new word in a sentence, you can usually find its class by asking a few questions in order.

::: theorem Tests for word class {#rule-tests}
To find the class of a word in a sentence, try these tests:

1. **Can it follow *a*, *the* or *my*?** If yes, it is probably a noun: *the **meeting***.
2. **Can it follow a subject such as *I*, *she* or *they*, and change its form for time (*-s*, *-ed*)?** If yes, it is a verb: *she **works***, *she **worked***.
3. **Does it describe a noun, and fit *a very ___ person* or *He is ___*?** If yes, it is an adjective: *a very **kind** person*.
4. **Does it tell us how, when, where or how often something happens?** If yes, it is probably an adverb: *She left **early***.
5. **Is it one of the small, frequent words?** Then check the list: a pronoun replaces a noun phrase (*they*), a determiner starts a noun phrase (*every*), a preposition is followed by a noun phrase (*under the bed*), a conjunction joins two parts (*but*).
:::

::: proof
Each test uses a property that only one class has. Determiners combine with nouns, so a word after *the* is a noun (or an adjective followed by a noun: *the **red** car* — so check what comes next). Only verbs change for time with *-s* and *-ed*. Gradable adjectives combine with *very* and stand after *be*. Adverbs usually answer the circumstance questions. The closed classes are small enough to learn as lists. The tests are not perfect: *very* goes with adverbs too (*very quickly*), and some words pass two tests because they really belong to two classes (*fast* is both an adjective and an adverb: *a fast car*, *she drives fast*).
:::

Endings are another useful clue. They do not prove the class, but they make a good guess.

| ending | usual class | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *-tion*, *-sion*, *-ment*, *-ness*, *-ity*, *-er* (a person) | noun | *information*, *decision*, *payment*, *kindness*, *city*, *teacher* |
| *-ful*, *-less*, *-ous*, *-al*, *-able*, *-ive*, *-y* | adjective | *useful*, *careless*, *famous*, *national*, *comfortable*, *active*, *rainy* |
| *-ly* | adverb (but see *friendly*, *lovely*) | *slowly*, *really*, *happily* |
| *-ise*/*-ize*, *-en*, *-ify* | verb | *organise*, *widen*, *simplify* |

::: example Guessing the class from the ending {#ex-endings}
You do not know these words, but what class is each one probably? *The **committee** found the plan **impractical**, so they **simplified** it and voted **unanimously** for the **amendment**.*
::: solution
- *committee* — after *the*, and it is the subject of *found*: a **noun**.
- *impractical* — the ending *-al* suggests an adjective, and it describes *the plan* (*found the plan impractical* = ‘thought the plan was impractical’): **adjective**.
- *simplified* — the ending *-ify* (here *-ified*, with *-ed* for past time) and the position after *they*: **verb**.
- *unanimously* — the ending *-ly*, and it tells us how they voted: **adverb**.
- *amendment* — the ending *-ment* and the position after *the*: **noun**.

Position and ending agree each time, so the guesses are safe. (The meanings: *impractical* ‘not sensible or not workable’, *unanimously* ‘with everyone agreeing’, *amendment* ‘a change to a text or a law’.)
:::
:::

The spelling *-ise* is common in British English and *-ize* in American English (*organise*, *organize*); both are correct, and some British publishers also use *-ize*. The course *Word Formation* explains these endings in [[word-formation/suffixes]].

::: example Labelling the words of a sentence {#ex-label}
Give the word class of every word in: *My sister bought a red bike in Hangzhou yesterday.*
::: solution
Work from left to right and use the tests.

- *My* — comes first in the noun phrase *my sister* and says whose sister: **determiner**.
- *sister* — follows *my*, names a person: **noun**.
- *bought* — follows the subject *my sister*; it is the past form of *buy*: **verb**.
- *a* — the indefinite article: **determiner**.
- *red* — describes *bike*, fits *a very red bike* (possible, though unusual) and *It is red*: **adjective**.
- *bike* — follows *a red*, names a thing: **noun**.
- *in* — followed by the noun *Hangzhou*, shows place: **preposition**.
- *Hangzhou* — the name of a city: **noun** (a proper noun).
- *yesterday* — tells us when: **adverb**.

So the sentence is: determiner + noun + verb + determiner + adjective + noun + preposition + noun + adverb.
:::
:::

::: example One word, three classes {#ex-light}
What is the class of *light* in each sentence? (a) *Turn on the light.* (b) *This bag is very light.* (c) *We light candles on her birthday.*
::: solution
(a) *the light* — after the determiner *the*, names a thing: **noun**.

(b) *very light*, after *is*, describes the bag: **adjective** (meaning ‘not heavy’).

(c) *We light* — follows the subject *we*; in the past we would say *We lit candles*: **verb**.

The tests work even when the meaning changes. In (b) *light* is the opposite of *heavy*, which is a different meaning from (a), but the class still comes from the position.
:::
:::

## One word, several classes

[[#rule-class-by-use]] says that the same spelling can belong to different classes. This is very common in English, especially between nouns and verbs.

- *a **walk*** (noun) — *we **walk*** (verb)
- *a **book*** (noun) — ***book** a table* (verb, ‘reserve’)
- *an **email*** (noun) — *please **email** me* (verb)
- *a **fast** train* (adjective) — *she runs **fast*** (adverb)
- ***after** dinner* (preposition) — *after we ate* (here *after* is a conjunction, because a clause follows)

Some words change class with a change of stress. As a noun, *record* is stressed on the first syllable, /ˈrekɔːd/ (GB), /ˈrekərd/ (GA); as a verb it is stressed on the second, /rɪˈkɔːd/ (GB), /rɪˈkɔːrd/ (GA). The same happens with *present*, *increase* and *export*. The course *Sounds of English* explains word stress in [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics]].

::: warning Using a noun where an adjective is needed
Many learners use the noun instead of the adjective after *be* or *very*.

✗ *She is very beauty.* ✓ *She is very beautiful.*

✗ *I am very interest in music.* ✓ *I am very interested in music.*

✗ *He is a China student.* ✓ *He is a Chinese student.*

*Beauty*, *interest* and *China* are nouns. After *very* and *be*, or to describe a noun, English needs the adjective: *beautiful*, *interested*, *Chinese*. When you learn a new word, learn its family too: *beauty* (n.) — *beautiful* (adj.) — *beautifully* (adv.).
:::

::: warning Using an adjective where an adverb is needed
✗ *He speaks English very good.* ✓ *He speaks English very well.*

✗ *Drive careful!* ✓ *Drive carefully!*

To describe *how* someone does something, use an adverb: *well*, *carefully*. *Good* and *careful* are adjectives; they describe nouns (*a good speaker*, *a careful driver*). In informal American English you will hear *Drive safe* and *I'm doing good*, but in writing and in exams use the adverb.
:::

::: example Choosing the right form {#ex-choose-form}
Complete each sentence with the right form of the word in brackets, and give its class. (a) *She gave a very ___ talk.* (interest) (b) *Please speak ___.* (clear) (c) *Thank you for your ___.* (kind)
::: solution
(a) The gap comes after *a very* and before the noun *talk*, so we need an adjective: *a very **interesting** talk*. (*Interested* is also an adjective, but it describes how a person feels: *I was interested*. The talk causes the feeling, so *interesting*.)

(b) The gap tells us *how* she should speak, so we need an adverb: *Please speak **clearly**.*

(c) The gap comes after the determiner *your*, so we need a noun: *Thank you for your **kindness**.*
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence uses *well* as an adjective, not an adverb?
- [ ] *She sings well.*
- [x] *I was ill last week, but I'm well now.*
- [ ] *He did well in the test.*
- [ ] *Well done!*
::: solution
In *I'm well now*, *well* comes after *be* and describes the person, meaning ‘healthy’: here it is an adjective. In *She sings well* and *He did well*, it describes how someone does something: an adverb. *Well done!* is a fixed expression in which *well* is also an adverb (it describes *done*).
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese also has word classes. Chinese school grammar uses the names 名词, 动词, 形容词, 副词, 代词, 介词 and 连词, and these match the English classes fairly well. But there are some important differences.

- **English shows class with endings; Chinese mostly does not.** In English, *decide* → *decision*, *happy* → *happiness*, *quick* → *quickly*: the ending often tells you the class. Chinese words rarely change form in this way, so Chinese learners sometimes forget that English needs the right form for the position: ✗ *I am very interest in music*.
- **Chinese adjectives can be the main word of a sentence; English adjectives cannot.** In 她很高 (literally ‘she very tall’) the adjective 高 is the predicate and no verb is needed. English needs the verb *be*: *She is very tall.* This is why Li & Thompson (1981) treat Chinese adjectives as a kind of verb. The lesson [[core-grammar/be-and-have]] returns to this.
- **English has articles; Chinese has classifiers.** English *a*, *an* and *the* have no direct equivalent in Chinese. Chinese classifiers (量词: 个, 本, 张) have no equivalent class in English: we say *a book*, *two books*, with nothing between the number and the noun, and use words such as *piece* only with some nouns (*a piece of paper*).
- **Chinese aspect particles have no English word class.** Particles such as 了, 着 and 过 show aspect. English shows similar meanings with verb forms and auxiliaries (*-ed*, *be … -ing*, *have …*), not with separate particles.
:::

::: history
The idea of word classes is more than two thousand years old. A short Greek grammar called the *Technē grammatikē*, traditionally said to be by Dionysius Thrax (second century BC, though scholars debate how much of it is his), listed eight parts of speech for Greek: noun, verb, participle, article, pronoun, preposition, adverb and conjunction. Roman grammarians such as Donatus (fourth century AD) adapted the list for Latin, which has no article, and added the interjection. The first grammars of English, from William Bullokar's *Pamphlet for Grammar* (1586) onwards, took over this Latin list, and "eight parts of speech" became the standard number in English schools. The adjective was long treated as a kind of noun and became a separate class only later. In China, Ma Jianzhong's 《马氏文通》 (1898) used a similar Latin model for Chinese. Modern grammars revise the list: Huddleston and Pullum (2002), for example, treat words such as *because* and *before* as prepositions, not conjunctions. This course uses the traditional labels, because they are the ones used in dictionaries and school textbooks.
:::

## Where this leads

Every later lesson uses these classes. [[core-grammar/sentence-patterns]] shows how words of different classes make up the parts of a sentence: subject, verb, object and complement. Nouns and pronouns are the topic of [[core-grammar/nouns-pronouns]]; adjectives and adverbs of [[core-grammar/adjectives-adverbs]]; prepositions of [[core-grammar/prepositions-time-place]]. Determiners and articles have their own course, *Nouns and Articles* ([[nouns-and-articles/a-and-the]]), and the course *Word Formation* shows how endings change a word's class ([[word-formation/suffixes]], [[word-formation/conversion]]).

::: summary
- A word class (part of speech) is a group of words that behave alike in sentences. English has eight main classes: noun, verb, adjective, adverb, pronoun, determiner, preposition and conjunction.
- The class of a word depends on how it is used: its position, its endings and the words around it ([[#rule-class-by-use]]). Many words belong to more than one class (*water*, *book*, *fast*).
- Use the tests of [[#rule-tests]]: a noun follows *the* or *a*; a verb follows the subject and changes for time; an adjective fits *a very ___ thing*; an adverb says how, when, where or how often.
- Nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs are open classes that carry most of the meaning; pronouns, determiners, prepositions and conjunctions are small closed classes of very frequent words.
- Endings such as *-tion*, *-ful* and *-ly* are good clues to class, but not proof (*friendly* is an adjective).
- Unlike Chinese, English needs *be* before an adjective (*She is tall*), uses articles, has no classifiers and often changes a word's form when its class changes.
:::

::: exercise The right form {level=2}
Choose the right word. (a) *She is very (beauty / beautiful).* (b) *He answered (quick / quickly).* (c) *We had a (wonderful / wonderfully) time.* (d) *Thank you for your (help / helpful).* (e) *They speak English (good / well).* (f) *I'm (interest / interested) in history.*
::: solution
(a) *beautiful* (adjective after *very*); (b) *quickly* (adverb: how he answered); (c) *wonderful* (adjective before the noun *time*); (d) *help* (noun after *your*); (e) *well* (adverb: how they speak); (f) *interested* (adjective after *be*; *interest* is a noun or a verb).
:::
:::

::: exercise Correct the sentences {level=3}
Each sentence has one word in the wrong class. Correct it and say what was wrong. (a) *My brother is very success.* (b) *She smiled happy at us.* (c) *I can't decision now.* (d) *This is a very danger road.* (e) *He is a Japan teacher.*
::: hint
Ask what class the position needs, then find the member of the word family that belongs to that class.
:::
::: solution
(a) *My brother is very **successful**.* After *very* and *be* we need an adjective, not the noun *success*.

(b) *She smiled **happily** at us.* We need an adverb to say how she smiled.

(c) *I can't **decide** now.* After *can't* we need a verb, not the noun *decision*.

(d) *This is a very **dangerous** road.* Before the noun *road* and after *very* we need an adjective, not the noun *danger*.

(e) *He is a **Japanese** teacher.* Before a noun, to say where someone comes from, use the adjective *Japanese*, not the name of the country. (Note that *a Japanese teacher* can mean a teacher from Japan or a teacher of Japanese; the context decides.)
:::
:::

::: exercise Analyse a short text {level=3}
Read the text and give the class of every word in bold.

*Every **Saturday**, Maria and **her** friends meet **at** a small café **near** the river. They talk **happily** for hours, **and** sometimes they **play** cards. The café is **quiet**, **so** it is **perfect** for a long **conversation**.*
::: solution
*Saturday* — noun (a proper noun, so it has a capital letter); *her* — determiner (it comes before the noun *friends*); *at* — preposition; *near* — preposition (followed by *the river*); *happily* — adverb (how they talk); *and* — conjunction; *play* — verb; *quiet* — adjective (after *is*, describes the café); *so* — conjunction (it joins two clauses and shows a result); *perfect* — adjective; *conversation* — noun (after *a long*).

(Some grammars call *her* in *her friends* a possessive pronoun or possessive adjective; the job is the same — it says whose friends.)
:::
:::
