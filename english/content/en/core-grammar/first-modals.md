*Can*, *must* and *should* are among the most frequent verbs in English, and they behave differently from all the others. You say *she can swim*, not ✗ *she cans swim*; *Can you swim?*, not ✗ *Do you can swim?*; *you must go*, not ✗ *you must to go*. These are **modal verbs**, and they add the speaker's view — about ability, permission, obligation or advice — to the main verb.

Chinese has words for the same meanings — 能, 会, 可以, 必须, 得, 应该 — but the meanings are divided differently. Above all, one pair causes trouble for almost every Chinese learner: *mustn't* and *don't have to*. 你不必去 and 你不能去 are very different, and so are their English equivalents, but learners often say *You mustn't go* when they mean *You don't have to go*. This lesson introduces the form of modal verbs and the meanings of *can*, *could*, *must*, *have to* and *should*. A complete treatment of modality is the subject of the course [[modality/what-modals-do]].

## The form of modal verbs

::: definition Modal verb {#def-modal}
A **modal verb** is an auxiliary verb that expresses the speaker's attitude to an action — whether it is possible, allowed, necessary or advisable. The central modals are *can*, *could*, *may*, *might*, *must*, *shall*, *should*, *will* and *would*.
:::

::: theorem The grammar of modal verbs {#rule-modal-form}
1. A modal is followed by the **base form** of the verb, without *to*: *I can **swim**.* *You must **stop**.* *She should **rest**.*
2. A modal has **no *-s*** in the third person singular: *She **can** drive.* *He **must** go.*
3. A modal makes questions and negatives **by itself**, without *do*: ***Can** you help?* *You **shouldn't** worry.* *I **can't** come.*
4. A modal has **no *-ing*** or ***to*** form and no participle: there is no ✗ *to can*, ✗ *canning*, ✗ *musted*. Other verbs are used instead: *to be able to*, *to have to*.
:::

::: proof
Modals are the clearest examples of the operators described in [[core-grammar/questions-and-negatives#rule-operator]]: they move in front of the subject in questions and take *not* directly. Their lack of *-s* has a historical reason: most of them were once past-tense forms that came to have present meaning (these are called *preterite-present* verbs), and past forms in English never had a third-person *-s*. Negative contractions: *can't* (BrE /kɑːnt/, AmE /kænt/; the full negative is written as one word, *cannot*), *couldn't*, *mustn't* /ˈmʌsnt/ (the first *t* is silent), *shouldn't*. *Have to* looks like a modal in meaning but is grammatically an ordinary verb: *She **has** to go*, ***Does** she **have** to go?*, *She **doesn't have** to go*.
:::

::: warning Adding *to*, *-s* or *do* to modals
✗ *I can to speak French.* ✓ *I can speak French.*

✗ *She cans play the piano.* ✓ *She can play the piano.*

✗ *Do you can help me?* ✓ *Can you help me?*

✗ *He musts finish today.* ✓ *He must finish today.*

✗ *You should to see a doctor.* ✓ *You should see a doctor.*

The exceptions to remember are *have to* and *ought to*, which do include *to*, and *be able to*.
:::

::: example Fixing the form {#ex-form}
Correct the sentences. (a) *My brother can to cook very well.* (b) *Does she can come tomorrow?* (c) *You don't should eat so much sugar.* (d) *He must works on Saturday.* (e) *I will can help you next week.*
::: solution
(a) *My brother can cook very well.* — no *to* after a modal.

(b) *Can she come tomorrow?* — the modal forms the question.

(c) *You shouldn't eat so much sugar.* — the modal takes *not* directly.

(d) *He must work on Saturday.* — base form after a modal.

(e) *I will be able to help you next week.* — two modals cannot be combined in standard English (✗ *will can*), so *can* is replaced by *be able to*. (Some dialects, such as those of Scotland and the southern United States, allow "double modals" like *might could*, but they are not standard.)
:::
:::

## *Can* and *could*

::: theorem Uses of *can* and *could* {#rule-can}
***Can*** expresses:

1. **ability**: *She **can** speak three languages.* *I **can't** swim.*
2. **permission**: *You **can** use my phone.* ***Can** I sit here?* *You **can't** park here.*
3. **possibility** (what is possible in general): *It **can** be very cold in Harbin in January.*
4. **requests and offers**: ***Can** you pass the salt?* ***Can** I help you?*

***Could*** is used:

5. for **past ability**: *I **could** read when I was four.* *She **couldn't** come to the party.*
6. for **more polite requests**: ***Could** you open the window, please?* ***Could** I ask a question?*
:::

::: proof
For a single past achievement in a particular situation, English normally uses *was able to* or *managed to*, not *could*: *The fire spread quickly, but everyone **was able to** escape* (not ✗ *could escape*). *Could* is used for general past ability (*He could run fast when he was young*) and, in the negative, for single occasions too (*I **couldn't** find my keys*). This distinction is subtle and is described in reference grammars such as Swan (2016). In requests, *could* is more polite than *can* because it is more tentative; *may* (*May I come in?*) is more formal still. *Can I…?* for permission was once criticised by school grammars, which insisted on *May I…?*, but *Can I…?* is now normal in everyday English. For future ability, use *will be able to*: *I'll be able to drive next year.*
:::

- *Can you ride a bike?* (ability)
- *Can I borrow your pen?* (permission)
- *Could you speak more slowly, please?* (polite request)
- *When I was a child, I couldn't swim.* (past ability)

::: warning *Can*, *know how to* and 会
✗ *I can play the piano when I was ten.* ✓ *I could play the piano when I was ten.*

✗ *I know swim.* ✓ *I can swim.* / *I know how to swim.*

Chinese 会 covers learned skills (我会游泳) and also predictions (明天会下雨). English uses *can* for the first and *will* for the second, so 我会开车 → *I can drive*, but 他会来的 → *He'll come*.
:::

## *Must*, *have to* and *should*

::: theorem *Must* and *have to* for obligation {#rule-must}
***Must*** and ***have to*** both express **obligation** — something is necessary:

- *You **must** wear a seatbelt.* *I **have to** get up at six tomorrow.*

In British English, there is a tendency to use ***must*** when the obligation comes from the **speaker** (*I must call my mother — I haven't called her for weeks*), and ***have to*** when it comes from **outside** — rules, laws, other people (*I have to wear a uniform at work*). In American English, *have to* is much more common in both cases, and *must* sounds formal or strong. Informal British English also uses ***have got to***: *I've got to go now.*

***Must*** has no past form. For past obligation, use ***had to***: *I **had to** work late yesterday.* For the future, use ***will have to***: *You'**ll have to** wait.*
:::

::: theorem *Mustn't* and *don't have to* {#rule-mustnt}
The negatives have **completely different meanings**:

- ***mustn't*** = it is **not allowed**, don't do it (prohibition; Chinese 不能, 不许, 不要): *You **mustn't** smoke in here.* *You **mustn't** tell anyone.*
- ***don't have to*** = it is **not necessary**, but you can if you want (Chinese 不必, 不用): *You **don't have to** come — it's optional.* *Tomorrow's Sunday, so I **don't have to** get up early.*
:::

::: proof
The difference lies in what the negative applies to. In *You mustn't go*, the necessity is positive and the action is negative: it is necessary that you **not** go. In *You don't have to go*, the necessity itself is negative: it is **not** necessary that you go. Chinese makes the same distinction with different words (不能 / 不必), so the problem is not the idea but the English forms, which look as if they should mean the same thing. *Needn't* (*You needn't come*) is a British, slightly formal equivalent of *don't have to*. In American English *mustn't* is rare; prohibition is usually expressed with *can't*, *don't* or *are not allowed to*: *You can't smoke in here.*
:::

The two negatives look similar but are opposite in meaning.
:::

::: example *Mustn't* or *don't have to*? {#ex-mustnt}
Complete with *mustn't* or *don't have to*, and give the Chinese meaning. (a) *You ___ bring food — we'll provide lunch.* (b) *You ___ use your phone during the exam.* (c) *We ___ hurry. The train doesn't leave until ten.* (d) *You ___ touch that wire — it's dangerous!* (e) *Children under six ___ pay on the bus.*
::: solution
(a) *don't have to* — 不必带 (lunch is provided; you can bring food if you want).

(b) *mustn't* — 不能用 (it is forbidden).

(c) *don't have to* — 不用急 (there is time).

(d) *mustn't* — 不要碰 (it is dangerous).

(e) *don't have to* — 不用付钱 (it is free for them).

A useful test: if you can add *but you can if you want*, use *don't have to*.
:::
:::

::: quiz
*It's a holiday tomorrow, so the students ___ go to school.* Which is correct?
- [ ] *mustn't*
- [x] *don't have to*
- [ ] *must*
- [ ] *don't must*
::: solution
Going to school is not necessary (不必), but nothing forbids it. *Mustn't* would mean they are forbidden to go. *Don't must* is not English.
:::
:::

::: theorem *Should* for advice {#rule-should}
***Should*** gives **advice** or says what is the **right thing** to do: *You **should** see a doctor.* *We **shouldn't** waste food.* ***Should** I call her?* It is weaker than *must*: it is a recommendation, not an obligation. ***Ought to*** has the same meaning and is less common, especially in questions and negatives: *You **ought to** apologise.*
:::

::: proof
*Should* is the usual way to give advice, often with *I think*: *I think you should take a break.* For negative advice, English prefers *I don't think you should…* to *I think you shouldn't…*. *Should* is also used to talk about what is expected or probable (*The parcel should arrive tomorrow*), a meaning covered in [[modality/deduction]]. A stronger form of advice is *had better* (*You'd better hurry*), which often carries a warning: something bad will happen if you don't. Polite and indirect advice — *You might want to…*, *Have you thought about…?* — is treated in [[modality/modality-politeness]].
:::

- *You should drink more water.* (advice)
- *You shouldn't stay up so late.* (advice: don't)
- *What should I wear to the interview?* (asking for advice)
- *Students should hand in their essays by Friday.* (what is expected — this could also be a rule)

::: example Rules and advice {#ex-rules-advice}
A new student is starting at a language school. Rewrite the notes as sentences using *must*, *mustn't*, *have to*, *don't have to* or *should*. (a) Wear your ID card at all times (rule). (b) Eating in the classrooms: not allowed. (c) Bring a dictionary: not necessary — there are dictionaries in every room. (d) Join a club: a good idea. (e) Arrive on time: required. (f) Speak English during breaks: a good idea, not a rule.
::: solution
(a) *You must wear / You have to wear your ID card at all times.*

(b) *You mustn't eat in the classrooms.* (or *You can't eat in the classrooms.*)

(c) *You don't have to bring a dictionary — there are dictionaries in every room.*

(d) *You should join a club.*

(e) *You must arrive / You have to arrive on time.*

(f) *You should speak English during breaks.*

The choice between *must* and *have to* in (a) and (e) depends partly on variety and partly on who is speaking: a teacher stating the rule might say *must*; a student explaining it to a friend would more likely say *have to*.
:::
:::

::: example Can, could, must or should? {#ex-mixed}
Complete the conversation with *can*, *can't*, *could*, *must*, *have to*, *don't have to* or *should*.

**Lin:** *Excuse me, ___ (1) you tell me where the library is?*

**Student:** *Sure, it's next to the canteen. Have you got your card? You ___ (2) show it at the entrance.*

**Lin:** *Oh no, I left it at home. ___ (3) I get in without it?*

**Student:** *No, you ___ (4). But you ___ (5) go back for it — you can get a day pass at the front desk. You ___ (6) ask for one.*
::: solution
(1) *could* (or *can*) — a polite request.

(2) *have to* (or *must*) — a rule.

(3) *Can* — asking permission or possibility.

(4) *can't* — not allowed.

(5) *don't have to* — not necessary.

(6) *should* — advice (or *can* — possibility).
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence gives advice, not an order?
- [ ] *You must hand in your phone before the exam.*
- [x] *You should get more sleep before the exam.*
- [ ] *You mustn't talk during the exam.*
- [ ] *You have to write in pen.*
::: solution
*Should* gives advice. The others express rules: *must*, *mustn't* and *have to*.

::: note English and Chinese
- **能, 会, 可以 and *can*.** 会 is for learned skills (我会说英语 → *I can speak English*), 能 for ability or possibility in a situation (我今天不能来 → *I can't come today*), 可以 for permission (我可以进来吗？ → *Can I come in?* / *May I come in?*). English uses *can* for all three.
- **必须, 得 and *must* / *have to*.** 你必须戴头盔 → *You must / have to wear a helmet*. 我得走了 → *I have to go* / BrE *I've got to go*.
- **不必 vs 不能.** 不必, 不用 → *don't have to* or *needn't*; 不能, 不许, 不准 → *mustn't* or *can't*. Do not translate 不必 as *mustn't*.
- **应该 and *should*.** 你应该早点睡 → *You should go to bed earlier*. In negative advice, English often prefers *I don't think you should…* to 我觉得你不应该….
- **No inflection.** Like Chinese verbs, English modals do not change for person: *I can*, *she can*. Here the Chinese pattern helps — the error to avoid is adding *to*: ✗ *I can to swim* (perhaps influenced by *want to*, *need to*).
:::

::: history
The English modals were once ordinary verbs with full meanings. Old English *cunnan*, the ancestor of *can*, meant ‘to know’ (compare *know-how*, and the related word *cunning*), so *I can swim* originally meant something like ‘I know swimming’. *Mōtan*, the ancestor of *must*, meant ‘be allowed to’ and only later ‘be obliged to’; and *must* itself is in origin a past tense form. *Sceal*, the ancestor of *shall* and *should*, meant ‘owe’. During the Middle English and Early Modern English periods, these verbs gradually lost their infinitives, participles and *-s* forms and became the special class of auxiliaries we have today. By Shakespeare's time the process was largely complete, although forms like *to can* still appear occasionally in sixteenth-century texts. The rise of *have to* as an alternative to *must* is more recent; corpus studies of the last century show *must* declining and *have to* and *have got to* increasing, especially in American English (Leech et al. 2009).
:::

## Where this leads

The full system of modality begins in [[modality/what-modals-do]], with ability and possibility (*can*, *could*, *may*, *might*) in [[modality/ability-possibility]], obligation and necessity in [[modality/obligation-necessity]], and logical deduction (*must be*, *can't be*) in [[modality/deduction]]. Polite requests and offers in conversation are practised in [[everyday-english/requests-offers]].

::: summary
- Modal verbs take the base form without *to*, have no *-s*, form questions and negatives themselves, and have no *-ing* or *to* form ([[#rule-modal-form]]).
- *Can*: ability, permission, possibility, requests; *could*: past ability, polite requests; future: *will be able to* ([[#rule-can]]).
- *Must* and *have to*: obligation; BrE *must* tends to be the speaker's own obligation, *have to* external; AmE prefers *have to*; past *had to* ([[#rule-must]]).
- *Mustn't* = not allowed (不能); *don't have to* = not necessary (不必) — they are opposites, not synonyms ([[#rule-mustnt]]).
- *Should*: advice and what is right; weaker than *must* ([[#rule-should]]).
- Chinese 会, 能, 可以 all map onto *can*; 不必 is *don't have to*, never *mustn't*.
:::
