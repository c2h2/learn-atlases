Prepositions are short words — *in*, *on*, *at*, *to*, *from*, *under* — that link a noun phrase to the rest of the sentence. Chinese often expresses the same relations with a frame around the noun: 在桌子上 (‘at the table on’), 在家里, 在三点. English uses one word *before* the noun: *on the table*, *at home*, *at three o'clock*. The hard part is choosing the word. Why *on Monday* but *in July* and *at night*? Why *in the car* but *on the bus*? There is a system behind most of these choices, and this lesson sets it out; the rest are fixed phrases that must be learned one by one.

## Prepositions of time: *at*, *on*, *in*

::: definition Preposition {#def-preposition}
A **preposition** is a word placed before a noun phrase (or pronoun, or *-ing* form) to show its relation to something else in the sentence — in time, in space, or in some more abstract way: *at noon*, *under the bed*, *with my friend*, *after eating*. The preposition and its noun phrase together form a **prepositional phrase**.
:::

::: theorem *At*, *on* and *in* for time {#rule-time}
- ***at*** for a **point** in time: clock times and some fixed points — *at six o'clock*, *at noon*, *at midnight*, *at night*, *at the moment*, *at the end of the lesson*, and holiday periods: *at Christmas*, *at Spring Festival*.
- ***on*** for **days and dates**: *on Monday*, *on 3 May* (BrE) / *on May 3* (AmE), *on my birthday*, *on New Year's Day*, *on Friday evening*.
- ***in*** for **longer periods**: parts of the day (*in the morning*, *in the afternoon*, *in the evening*), months (*in July*), seasons (*in winter*), years (*in 2008*), decades and centuries (*in the 1990s*, *in the 21st century*).
:::

::: proof
The pattern goes from small to large: *at* a point, *on* a day, *in* a period that contains many days. The exceptions are fixed: we say *at night* but *in the night* is also used for one particular night (*I woke up in the night*); *on Friday evening* uses *on* because the day is named. For the weekend, British English says ***at** the weekend* and American English ***on** the weekend*; both say *over the weekend*. Some time expressions take **no preposition**: *this*, *next*, *last*, *every*, *tomorrow*, *yesterday*, *today* — *I'll see you **next** Monday*, not ✗ *on next Monday*; *We met **last** year*, not ✗ *in last year*. In informal American English, the preposition before a day is often dropped too: *See you Monday.*
:::

✗ *I'll call you in Monday.* ✓ *I'll call you on Monday.*

✗ *I was born on 1999.* ✓ *I was born in 1999.* (but ✓ *I was born on 12 March 1999*: a full date takes *on*)

✗ *We're going to Beijing in next month.* ✓ *We're going to Beijing next month.*

✗ *I go swimming in every Sunday.* ✓ *I go swimming every Sunday.*
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *The concert starts on eight o'clock.*
- [ ] *I'll finish it in tomorrow.*
- [x] *We usually go skiing in January.*
- [ ] *She called me at Friday.*
::: solution
A month takes *in*. The others should be *at eight o'clock* (a clock time), *tomorrow* with no preposition, and *on Friday* (a day).
:::
:::

### Other time prepositions

| preposition | use | example |
|---|---|---|
| *for* | a length of time | *I lived there **for** three years.* |
| *since* | from a starting point until now (with the present perfect) | *I've lived here **since** 2020.* |
| *during* | within a period, or at some time in it | *She slept **during** the film.* |
| *from … to / until* | start and end | *The shop is open **from** 9 **to** 5.* |
| *until* / *till* | up to a time | *Wait **until** Friday.* |
| *by* | not later than | *Please finish it **by** Friday.* |
| *before* / *after* | earlier / later than | ***after** lunch*, ***before** the exam* |
| *ago* | back from now (after the noun) | *two days **ago*** |

*For* and *since* are often confused: *for* + a length (*for ten minutes*, *for a week*), *since* + a starting point (*since nine o'clock*, *since Monday*). Both are used with the present perfect, explained in [[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect]]. *By* and *until* are also different: *by Friday* means ‘no later than Friday’ (once), while *until Friday* means ‘all the time up to Friday’ (continuing): *Hand in the essay **by** Friday* / *The library is closed **until** Friday*.

::: example Time prepositions {#ex-time}
Complete with *at*, *on*, *in*, *for*, *since* or no preposition (—). (a) *The meeting is ___ Tuesday ___ 10 a.m.* (b) *My grandfather was born ___ 1950.* (c) *I've known her ___ ten years.* (d) *We'll go to the beach ___ next weekend.* (e) *It often snows ___ February.* (f) *She has worked here ___ January.* (g) *What do you do ___ the weekend?*
::: solution
(a) *on* Tuesday, *at* 10 a.m. — a day, then a clock time.

(b) *in* 1950 — a year.

(c) *for* ten years — a length of time.

(d) — no preposition before *next*.

(e) *in* February — a month.

(f) *since* January — a starting point, with the present perfect.

(g) *at* the weekend (BrE) or *on* the weekend (AmE).
:::
:::

## Prepositions of place: *at*, *on*, *in*

::: theorem *At*, *on* and *in* for place {#rule-place}
- ***at*** treats a place as a **point** — a location where something happens, often a building used for its purpose: *at the bus stop*, *at the door*, *at home*, *at work*, *at school*, *at the station*, *at the top of the page*, *at the end of the street*.
- ***on*** for a **surface** or a **line**: *on the table*, *on the wall*, *on the floor*, *on page 12*, *on the Yangtze* (a town on a river), *on the coast*, *on the second floor*.
- ***in*** for a **space with limits** — inside a container, room, building or area: *in the box*, *in the kitchen*, *in my pocket*, *in the garden*, *in Chengdu*, *in China*, *in the photo*.
:::

::: proof
The choice depends on how the speaker sees the place, not only on the place itself. *She's **at** the cinema* treats the cinema as a point on today's map — she's there to see a film. *It's cold **in** the cinema* treats it as a space with walls. Similarly *I'll meet you **at** the station* (a meeting point) and *There's a café **in** the station* (inside the building). Cities and countries are almost always *in* (*in Shanghai*, *in Brazil*), but *at* is possible when a city is a stop on a journey (*The train stops **at** Nanjing*). For a street, British English uses *in* or *on* (*She lives **in**/**on** Park Road*) and American English usually *on* (*She lives **on** Park Avenue*); with a full address both use *at* (*She lives **at** 23 Park Road*).
:::

- *Wait for me **at** the entrance.*
- *There's a spider **on** the ceiling!*
- *My keys are **in** my bag.*
- *Is Wang Lei **at** home?*
- *Write your name **at** the top of the page.*
- *Their flat is **on** the fifth floor.*

::: warning Transport: *in* the car, *on* the bus
✗ *I left my umbrella in the bus.* ✓ *I left my umbrella on the bus.*

For **public transport** and large vehicles you can walk around in, English uses ***on***: *on the bus*, *on the train*, *on the plane*, *on the ship*, *on the metro*. For **cars**, **taxis** and **small boats**, English uses ***in***: *in the car*, *in a taxi*. Bicycles and motorbikes are *on*: *on my bike*. To say how you travel, use ***by*** with no article: *by bus*, *by train*, *by car*, *by plane* — but ***on foot***, not ✗ *by foot* (although *by foot* is sometimes heard).
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *She is sitting in the bus.*
- [ ] *I'll see you on the airport.*
- [x] *There's a picture on the wall above the sofa.*
- [ ] *My brother lives at Canada.*
::: solution
A picture hangs on a surface: *on the wall*. The others should be *on the bus* (public transport), *at the airport* (a meeting point) and *in Canada* (a country).
:::
:::

::: example Point, surface or space? {#ex-place}
Complete with *at*, *on* or *in* and say whether the place is seen as a point, a surface or a space. (a) *Your coat is ___ the chair.* (b) *There are twenty students ___ the classroom.* (c) *Turn left ___ the traffic lights.* (d) *The children are playing ___ the garden.* (e) *My name is ___ the list.* (f) *She's ___ the dentist's.*
::: solution
(a) *on* — a surface. (*in the chair* is also used for an armchair, which surrounds you.)

(b) *in* — a space with walls.

(c) *at* — a point on a route.

(d) *in* — an area with limits.

(e) *on* — a list is treated like a surface or line.

(f) *at* — a point where something happens (*the dentist's* = the dentist's surgery or office; similarly *at the hairdresser's*, *at my aunt's*).
:::
:::

### Fixed phrases: with and without *the*

Some common places are used **without *the*** after *at* or *in* when we mean their normal purpose:

- ***at home***, ***at work***, ***at school***, ***at university*** (BrE) / ***in college*** (AmE), ***in bed***, ***in prison***, ***at church***.
- *She's **in hospital*** (BrE: she is a patient) / *She's **in the hospital*** (AmE, same meaning).
- *I go **to school** / **to work** / **to bed** / **to church***.

With *the*, the place is just a building: *I went **to the school** to talk to the teacher* (as a visitor, not a pupil). And note: *go **home***, not ✗ *go to home*; *arrive **home***; *come **home***.

::: warning *Arrive in* and *arrive at*; *go to* and *go home*
✗ *We arrived to Beijing at midnight.* ✓ *We arrived in Beijing at midnight.*

✗ *I arrived to the hotel.* ✓ *I arrived at the hotel.*

✗ *What time do you go to home?* ✓ *What time do you go home?*

*Arrive* never takes *to*: *arrive **in*** a city or country, *arrive **at*** a building or point. *Home* after verbs of movement has no preposition (*go home*, *get home*, *walk home*), but *at home* is used for position (*I'm at home*).
:::

## Prepositions of position and movement

**Position** (where something is):

| | |
|---|---|
| *under* / *below* | *The cat is **under** the table.* |
| *over* / *above* | *There's a lamp **over** the desk.* *The temperature is **above** 30 degrees.* |
| *next to* / *beside* | *Sit **next to** me.* |
| *between* | *The bank is **between** the post office and the hotel.* |
| *in front of* | *There's a tree **in front of** our house.* |
| *behind* | *The car park is **behind** the supermarket.* |
| *opposite* (BrE) / *across from* (AmE) | *The school is **opposite** the park.* |
| *near* | *I live **near** the station.* |

**Movement** (where something goes):

| | |
|---|---|
| *to* | *I'm going **to** Guangzhou.* |
| *from* | *She came **from** Harbin by train.* |
| *into* / *out of* | *He walked **into** the room.* *Get **out of** the car.* |
| *onto* / *off* | *The cat jumped **onto** the table.* *Get **off** the bus.* |
| *up* / *down* | *We walked **up** the hill.* |
| *along* | *Walk **along** the river.* |
| *across* | *Swim **across** the river.* |
| *through* | *The train goes **through** a tunnel.* |
| *past* | *Go **past** the bank.* |
| *towards* (BrE) / *toward* (AmE) | *She ran **towards** me.* |
| *round* (BrE) / *around* | *We walked **around** the lake.* |

::: theorem Position or movement? {#rule-movement}
Use ***in***, ***on***, ***at*** for **position** (where something is) and ***to***, ***into***, ***onto*** for **movement** towards a place: *She is **in** the kitchen* (position) / *She went **into** the kitchen* (movement). After verbs of movement (*go*, *come*, *walk*, *run*, *drive*, *fly*) use *to* for the destination: *go **to** school*, *fly **to** Tokyo*.
:::

::: proof
English distinguishes "being somewhere" from "going there" with different prepositions, while Chinese often uses the same verb or a direction complement (进, 到, 上) attached to the verb: 走进房间 → *walk into the room*, 跳到桌子上 → *jump onto the table*. In informal English, *in* and *on* are often used after verbs of movement instead of *into* and *onto* — *He jumped in the pool*, *Put it on the table* — especially with *put*, *jump*, *fall* and *throw*, where the movement is clear from the verb. After *arrive*, as we saw, English uses *in* or *at*, not *to*. *Under* and *below*, *over* and *above* overlap: *under* and *over* usually mean directly beneath or above, often touching or covering (*a blanket over the bed*), while *below* and *above* compare levels (*above sea level*, *below zero*).
:::

::: example Directions {#ex-directions}
Look at the description and complete the directions with prepositions. *The museum is on Park Street. From the station, you walk for five minutes beside the river, then cross the bridge. The museum is the building on the other side of the road from the park, next to a bookshop.*

"Come ___ the station and turn left. Walk ___ the river for about five minutes, then go ___ the bridge. Go ___ the park; the museum is ___ the park, ___ a bookshop."
::: solution
"Come ***out of*** the station and turn left. Walk ***along*** the river for about five minutes, then go ***across*** (or *over*) the bridge. Go ***past*** the park; the museum is ***opposite*** (AmE *across from*) the park, ***next to*** a bookshop."

*Out of* shows movement from inside; *along* follows a line; *across* and *over* both work for a bridge; *past* means going by without stopping; *opposite* and *next to* show position. The language of giving directions is practised in [[everyday-english/directions-travel]].
:::
:::

::: example Choosing between similar prepositions {#ex-similar}
Choose the correct preposition. (a) *There are clouds (over / above) the mountains.* (b) *Put the bags (in / into) the boot of the car.* (BrE *boot* = AmE *trunk*) (c) *He jumped (in / into) the river.* (d) *The price is (under / below) ¥100.* (e) *I'll wait for you (at / in) the corner.*
::: solution
(a) Both are correct: *over* and *above* both mean ‘higher than’ here. *Above* is slightly more natural for things not directly overhead.

(b) *in* or *into*: both are used with *put*; *into* stresses the movement.

(c) *into* is standard; *in* is common in informal speech.

(d) Both: *under ¥100* is more common in everyday speech (*under a hundred yuan*), *below* is slightly more formal and common with levels and scales (*below average*, *below zero*).

(e) *at the corner* (a point) — or *on the corner* (especially AmE, and for buildings standing on the corner: *a shop on the corner*). *In the corner* means inside the corner of a room: *a desk in the corner*.
:::
:::

## Prepositions after other words

Many adjectives, nouns and verbs are followed by a particular preposition. These must be learned with the word:

| | |
|---|---|
| adjective + preposition | *good **at***, *interested **in***, *afraid **of***, *proud **of***, *different **from*** (AmE also *than*, BrE also *to*), *married **to***, *responsible **for*** |
| verb + preposition | *listen **to***, *look **at***, *wait **for***, *depend **on***, *belong **to***, *agree **with***, *think **about**/**of*** |
| noun + preposition | *a reason **for***, *a solution **to***, *the cause **of***, *an increase **in*** |
| fixed phrases | ***on** time* (punctual), ***in** time* (early enough), ***by** mistake*, ***on** holiday* (BrE) / ***on** vacation* (AmE), ***on** TV*, ***on** the phone*, ***in** English* |

Verbs with prepositions (*look at*, *wait for*) are treated in [[verb-patterns/prepositional-verbs]].

::: warning Prepositions after *listen*, *discuss* and *marry*
✗ *I like listening music.* ✓ *I like listening to music.*

✗ *We discussed about the plan.* ✓ *We discussed the plan.* (but *We talked **about** the plan.*)

✗ *She married with a doctor.* ✓ *She married a doctor.* or *She got married to a doctor.*

✗ *I'm good in maths.* ✓ *I'm good at maths* (BrE) / *math* (AmE).

Some verbs need a preposition where Chinese has none (*listen to*, *wait for*); others take a direct object where Chinese uses a preposition (*discuss*, *marry*, *enter*: *She entered the room*, not ✗ *entered into the room*).
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **在 … 上/里 and English prepositions.** Chinese uses a frame: 在 before the noun and a position word (上, 里, 下, 前, 后) after it — 在桌子上, 在盒子里. English uses a single preposition before the noun: *on the table*, *in the box*. Learners sometimes translate both parts (✗ *at the table on*) or choose the preposition from 在 alone (✗ *at the box*).
- **Time with no 在.** 我三点到 has no preposition, but English needs one: *I'll arrive **at** three*. On the other hand, 下个月 → *next month* (no preposition in English either).
- **Direction complements.** 走进, 跑出, 爬上 combine a verb and a direction. English uses verb + preposition or particle: *walk into*, *run out of*, *climb up*.
- **Position in the sentence.** The Chinese 在 phrase normally comes before the verb (我在图书馆学习); the English prepositional phrase of place usually comes after the verb and object (*I study **in** the library*).
- **从 … 到.** 从北京到上海 → *from Beijing to Shanghai*; 从九点到五点 → *from nine to five* (or *nine till five*, AmE also *nine through five* for inclusive periods: *Monday through Friday*).
:::

::: history
English prepositions have taken over much of the work done by case endings in Old English. Where Old English could show ‘to the king’ or ‘with a sword’ by an ending on the noun, Middle English increasingly used *to* and *with*, as the endings wore away. Many of today's prepositions are ancient (*in*, *on*, *at*, *of*, *to*), while others were formed later from nouns or phrases: *beside* from *by side*, *across* from *on cross*, and *during* from an old form of a verb meaning ‘to last’ (borrowed from French). Grammarians of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, including John Dryden, objected to ending a sentence with a preposition (*Who are you talking to?*), on the model of Latin, where this was impossible. The "rule" has no basis in English grammar: sentences such as *What are you looking at?* and *That's the house I grew up in* are natural and correct, and modern style guides reject the old prohibition.
:::

## Where this leads

Verbs followed by prepositions are covered in [[verb-patterns/prepositional-verbs]], and verbs with particles (*get up*, *look after*) in [[verb-patterns/phrasal-verbs]]. Useful language for travel and directions is practised in [[everyday-english/directions-travel]], and dates and times are read aloud in [[core-vocabulary/numbers-dates-money]]. *For* and *since* with the present perfect are explained in [[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect]].

::: summary
- Time: *at* + a point (*at six*, *at night*), *on* + a day or date (*on Monday*), *in* + a longer period (*in July*, *in 2010*, *in the morning*); no preposition before *this*, *next*, *last*, *every* ([[#rule-time]]).
- BrE *at the weekend*, AmE *on the weekend*; *for* + length, *since* + starting point; *by* = not later than, *until* = up to.
- Place: *at* + a point (*at the station*, *at home*), *on* + a surface or line (*on the wall*, *on the second floor*), *in* + an enclosed space or area (*in the box*, *in Beijing*) ([[#rule-place]]).
- *On* the bus/train/plane, *in* the car/taxi; *by* bus, *on* foot.
- Position *in*, movement *into*/*to*; *arrive in*/*at*, never *arrive to*; *go home* without *to* ([[#rule-movement]]).
- Learn fixed phrases (*at home*, *in hospital*/AmE *in the hospital*, *on time*) and word + preposition combinations (*listen to*, *good at*) as units.
:::
