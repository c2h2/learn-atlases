In Chinese you say 一本书 and 两本书: the noun 书 stays the same, and the number with its classifier (本) tells you how many. In English the noun itself changes: *a book*, *two books*. And in spoken Chinese, 他, 她 and 它 are all pronounced *tā*, so a speaker never has to choose between ‘he’, ‘she’ and ‘it’ while talking. In English the choice must be made every time, and getting it wrong — *My mother is a doctor. He works at night.* — confuses the listener.

This lesson covers the forms of nouns and pronouns: how to make plurals, which nouns have no plural, the difference between *I* and *me*, *my* and *mine*, the possessive *'s*, and the choice of *he*, *she*, *it* and *they*.

## Singular and plural

::: definition Singular, plural and countable noun {#def-countable}
A **countable noun** names something that can be counted: *one apple*, *two apples*. It has a **singular** form for one (*apple*) and a **plural** form for more than one (*apples*). A singular countable noun normally needs a determiner in front of it — *a*, *the*, *my*, *this* …: *a cat*, *my cat*, not just ✗ *cat*.
:::

### Regular plurals

::: theorem Spelling of regular plurals {#rule-plural-spelling}
1. Most nouns add ***-s***: *book* → *books*, *day* → *days*, *idea* → *ideas*.
2. Nouns ending in ***-s***, ***-ss***, ***-sh***, ***-ch***, ***-x*** or ***-z*** add ***-es***: *bus* → *buses*, *class* → *classes*, *dish* → *dishes*, *watch* → *watches*, *box* → *boxes*.
3. **Consonant + *-y*** → ***-ies***: *city* → *cities*, *baby* → *babies*, *country* → *countries*. **Vowel + *-y*** → *-ys*: *key* → *keys*, *boy* → *boys*.
4. Some nouns ending in ***-f*** or ***-fe*** change to ***-ves***: *knife* → *knives*, *leaf* → *leaves*, *wife* → *wives*, *life* → *lives*, *half* → *halves*, *shelf* → *shelves*, *wolf* → *wolves*. Others just add *-s*: *roof* → *roofs*, *chef* → *chefs*, *belief* → *beliefs*.
5. Some nouns ending in ***-o*** add ***-es***: *tomato* → *tomatoes*, *potato* → *potatoes*, *hero* → *heroes*. Many others add only *-s*: *photo* → *photos*, *piano* → *pianos*, *radio* → *radios*, *video* → *videos*, *zoo* → *zoos*.
:::

::: proof
Rules 1–3 are the same as for the verb ending *-s* ([[core-grammar/present-simple#rule-s-spelling]]), and the plural ending is pronounced in the same three ways: /s/ after voiceless sounds (*books*, *cats*), /z/ after voiced sounds (*days*, *dogs*), and /ɪz/ (GA often /əz/) after hissing sounds (*buses*, *watches*, *pages*). Rules 4 and 5 are lists rather than rules: the *-ves* plurals keep an old sound change, and the *-oes* plurals are mostly older, common words, while shortened or borrowed words (*photo* from *photograph*, *piano* from Italian) take *-s*. For a few words both are found (*zeros*/*zeroes*, *mosquitoes*/*mosquitos*); a dictionary gives the preferred form.
:::

### Irregular plurals

A small group of very common nouns have irregular plurals. They must be learned:

| singular | plural |
|---|---|
| *man* | *men* |
| *woman* /ˈwʊmən/ | *women* /ˈwɪmɪn/ |
| *child* | *children* |
| *person* | *people* (*persons* in legal and official language) |
| *foot* | *feet* |
| *tooth* | *teeth* |
| *mouse* | *mice* |
| *goose* | *geese* |

Some nouns have the **same form** in the singular and the plural: *one sheep*, *two sheep*; *a fish*, *many fish*; *a deer*, *ten deer*; *a series*, *two series*; *a species*, *many species*. (*Fishes* is used only in some scientific or literary contexts.)

Some nouns are **plural only**, especially things with two parts: *trousers* (BrE) / *pants* (AmE), *jeans*, *glasses*, *scissors*, *shorts*. They take a plural verb (*My jeans are new*), and to count them we say *a pair of*: *two pairs of jeans*.

::: example Making plurals {#ex-plurals}
Write the plural of each noun and say which rule it follows: *church*, *story*, *toy*, *knife*, *photo*, *child*, *box*, *sheep*.
::: solution
| singular | plural | rule |
|---|---|---|
| *church* | *churches* | ends in *-ch*: *-es* (pronounced /ɪz/) |
| *story* | *stories* | consonant + *y*: *-ies* |
| *toy* | *toys* | vowel + *y*: *-s* |
| *knife* | *knives* | *-fe* → *-ves* |
| *photo* | *photos* | *-o*, a shortened word: *-s* |
| *child* | *children* | irregular |
| *box* | *boxes* | ends in *-x*: *-es* |
| *sheep* | *sheep* | no change |
:::
:::

::: warning Forgetting the plural after a number or *many*
✗ *I have two brother.* ✓ *I have two brothers.*

✗ *There are many student in the library.* ✓ *There are many students in the library.*

✗ *She bought three new dress.* ✓ *She bought three new dresses.*

In Chinese, 两个哥哥 and 很多学生 show the number with the numeral or 很多, and the noun does not change. In English, a countable noun after *two*, *three*, *many*, *a few*, *several*, *both* and so on must be plural, even though the number already shows "more than one". Note also that *people* is already plural: *two people*, not ✗ *two peoples* (*peoples* means ‘nations’).
:::

## Countable and uncountable nouns: a first look

::: definition Uncountable noun {#def-uncountable}
An **uncountable noun** names something that English treats as a mass or a whole, not as separate units: *water*, *rice*, *money*, *information*. An uncountable noun has **no plural**, is **not used with *a* or *an*** or with numbers, and takes a **singular verb**.
:::

Common uncountable nouns that learners often treat as countable:

| | |
|---|---|
| substances and food | *water*, *milk*, *rice*, *bread*, *meat*, *sugar*, *coffee* (the substance) |
| abstract ideas | *information*, *advice*, *knowledge*, *news*, *progress*, *fun* |
| groups of things | *furniture*, *luggage* (BrE) / *baggage* (AmE), *equipment*, *homework*, *money*, *traffic* |
| others | *music*, *weather*, *work* (‘a job’ is countable), *travel* |

::: theorem Using uncountable nouns {#rule-uncountable}
With an uncountable noun:

1. do not add *-s*, and do not use *a*/*an* or a number: *some information*, not ✗ *an information* or ✗ *two informations*;
2. use a singular verb: *The news **is** good.* *Your advice **was** very useful.*
3. use *some*, *any*, *much*, *a lot of*, *a little* to talk about quantity: *a lot of homework*, *not much money*;
4. to count, use a **unit noun** + *of*: *a piece of advice*, *two pieces of furniture*, *a bottle of water*, *a cup of coffee*, *a loaf of bread*, *a bowl of rice*, *a slice of cake*.
:::

::: proof
Whether a noun is countable is a fact about the English word, not about the thing in the world. *Furniture* is a collection of separate objects (chairs, tables), yet the English word is uncountable, while *chair* is countable. Other languages draw the line differently: French *informations* and *conseils* (‘advice’) are plural nouns, so ✗ *informations* and ✗ *advices* are typical errors of French and Spanish speakers as well as Chinese speakers. Learner's dictionaries mark each noun [C] (countable) or [U] (uncountable), and many nouns are both, with different meanings: *paper* (the material, [U]) and *a paper* (a newspaper or an essay, [C]); *coffee* (the drink in general) and *a coffee* (a cup of it). This is explored fully in [[nouns-and-articles/countable-uncountable]].
:::

✗ *Can you give me some informations?* ✓ *Can you give me some information?*

✗ *My teacher gave me many advices.* ✓ *My teacher gave me a lot of advice.* or *… some good pieces of advice.*

✗ *We have too many homeworks.* ✓ *We have too much homework.*

✗ *The news are on at seven.* ✓ *The news is on at seven.* (*news* ends in *-s* but is uncountable and singular)
:::

::: example Countable or uncountable? {#ex-count}
Correct the sentences where necessary. (a) *I need a new furniture for my flat.* (b) *She drinks two cups of tea every morning.* (c) *There were a lot of traffics on the road.* (d) *Could I have a water, please?* (e) *I bought some bread and three apples.*
::: solution
(a) *Furniture* is uncountable: *I need some new furniture for my flat.* or *… a new piece of furniture* (or name the thing: *a new sofa*).

(b) Correct: the unit noun *cups* is countable and plural.

(c) *Traffic* is uncountable: *There was a lot of traffic on the road.* (singular verb *was* too)

(d) Strictly *water* is uncountable: *Could I have some water / a glass of water, please?* In a café or restaurant, however, *a water* (= a bottle or glass of water) is common in informal speech, just as *a coffee* is.

(e) Correct: *bread* is uncountable (*some bread*), *apple* is countable (*three apples*).
:::

## Personal pronouns

::: definition Subject and object pronouns {#def-pronoun-case}
English personal pronouns have two forms. The **subject form** is used as the subject of a verb; the **object form** is used as the object of a verb, after a preposition, and when the pronoun stands alone.
:::

| | subject | object | possessive determiner | possessive pronoun | reflexive |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1st singular | *I* | *me* | *my* | *mine* | *myself* |
| 2nd singular | *you* | *you* | *your* | *yours* | *yourself* |
| 3rd singular, male | *he* | *him* | *his* | *his* | *himself* |
| 3rd singular, female | *she* | *her* | *her* | *hers* | *herself* |
| 3rd singular, thing | *it* | *it* | *its* | — | *itself* |
| 1st plural | *we* | *us* | *our* | *ours* | *ourselves* |
| 2nd plural | *you* | *you* | *your* | *yours* | *yourselves* |
| 3rd plural | *they* | *them* | *their* | *theirs* | *themselves* |

- ***She** called **me** yesterday.* (subject *she*, object *me*)
- *I gave **them** a present.*
- *This letter is for **you** and **him**.* (after a preposition)
- *Who's there? — It's **me**.* / *Who wants tea? — **Me**!* (standing alone)

::: theorem Subject or object form? {#rule-pronoun-case}
Use the **subject form** (*I*, *he*, *she*, *we*, *they*) before the verb as its subject. Use the **object form** (*me*, *him*, *her*, *us*, *them*) after a verb, after a preposition, and alone. When two people are joined by *and*, use the form you would use for each one alone.
:::

::: proof
English nouns have lost their case endings, but these pronouns keep a subject form and an object form, so they still show who does what: *She likes him* is different from *He likes her*. The test with *and* works because each part of the pair must fit the position: *My sister and I went* (*I went*), *She invited my sister and me* (*invited me*). In informal speech, many native speakers say *Me and my sister went to the cinema*; this is very common in conversation but is not accepted in writing, where *My sister and I went* is standard (Swan 2016). Some speakers avoid *me* even where it is correct — *between you and I* — because they have been told that *me* is wrong after *and*; reference grammars note that this usage is widespread, but *between you and me* is the form recommended in careful writing. In formal English, *It is I* is possible, but *It's me* is normal in everyday English.
:::

::: example Choosing the pronoun {#ex-pronouns}
Choose the correct pronoun and explain. (a) *(He / Him) and his wife run a restaurant.* (b) *Can you help (I / me)?* (c) *My parents gave my brother and (I / me) some money.* (d) *Between you and (I / me), the food wasn't very good.* (e) *(Us / We) students need more time.*
::: solution
(a) *He* — subject of *run*: test alone, *He runs a restaurant*.

(b) *me* — object of *help*.

(c) *me* — object of *gave*: test alone, *My parents gave me some money*.

(d) *me* — after the preposition *between*. (*Between you and I* is common in speech but is avoided in careful English.)

(e) *We* — subject of *need*: test without *students*, *We need more time*.
:::
:::

### *He*, *she*, *it* and *they*

::: theorem Choosing a third person pronoun {#rule-he-she}
- Use ***he*** for a man or boy, and ***she*** for a woman or girl.
- Use ***it*** for things, places, ideas and usually for animals; but people often use *he* or *she* for their pets and for animals whose sex they know.
- Use ***they*** for plurals, and also for **one person** whose sex is unknown or unimportant, or who prefers *they*: *Someone left **their** umbrella — I hope **they** come back for it.*
:::

::: proof
Singular *they* has been used in English for centuries — it is found in Chaucer, Shakespeare and Jane Austen — and modern grammars and dictionaries treat it as standard, especially after *someone*, *anyone*, *everyone*, *a person* and *each student* (Huddleston & Pullum 2002; Swan 2016). Even when *they* refers to one person, it takes a plural verb: *they **are***, *they **come***. The older practice of using *he* for everyone (*Every student must bring his book*) is now avoided because it leaves out women; *he or she* is correct but heavy if repeated. Singular *they* is also used for a person who does not identify as a man or a woman.
:::

::: warning Mixing *he* and *she*
✗ *My mother is a doctor. He works at night.* ✓ *My mother is a doctor. She works at night.*

✗ *I met your brother. She was very friendly.* ✓ *I met your brother. He was very friendly.*

✗ *My father lost her keys.* ✓ *My father lost his keys.*

This is a speaking mistake more than a writing mistake: in writing there is time to choose, while in speech the Chinese habit of one sound, *tā*, takes over. It affects *his*/*her* too. Practise by retelling short stories about a man and a woman aloud and listening for every *he*, *she*, *his*, *her*, *him* and *her*.
:::

## Possessives

There are three ways to show who owns or has something: possessive determiners, possessive pronouns and the ending *'s*.

::: theorem Possessive determiners and possessive pronouns {#rule-possessive}
- A **possessive determiner** (*my*, *your*, *his*, *her*, *its*, *our*, *their*) comes **before a noun**: *This is **my** bag.*
- A **possessive pronoun** (*mine*, *yours*, *his*, *hers*, *ours*, *theirs*) **stands alone**, without a noun: *This bag is **mine**.* *Is this pen **yours** or **hers**?*
- A noun takes ***'s*** (singular) or ***s'*** (regular plural ending in *-s*): *Li Wei's phone*, *my sister's room*, *my parents' house*, *the students' books*. Irregular plurals take *'s*: *the children's toys*, *women's football*.
:::

::: proof
The possessive determiners are in the same position as *the* and *a*, so they cannot stand alone; the possessive pronouns replace a whole noun phrase (*mine* = *my bag*). The *'s* ending is pronounced like the plural: /s/, /z/ or /ɪz/ (*Mike's*, *Tom's*, *Rose's*). With plural nouns that already end in *-s*, only the apostrophe is added in writing, and nothing changes in speech: *the students' books* sounds the same as *the students' book* and *the student's books*; the context tells the listener which is meant. Names ending in *-s* can take either *'s* or *'*: *James's car* or *James' car*; both are accepted, and the choice is a matter of house style.
:::

- *Is this Amina's coat? — No, it's mine. **Hers** is the blue one.*
- *My friends' flat is near the station.* (several friends)
- *My friend's flat is near the station.* (one friend)
- *The dog wagged **its** tail.*

::: warning *Its* and *it's*
***Its*** (no apostrophe) is the possessive: *The company changed **its** name.* ***It's*** is the short form of *it is* or *it has*: ***It's** raining.* ***It's** been a long day.*

✗ *The cat hurt it's leg.* ✓ *The cat hurt its leg.*

The confusion is common even among native speakers. Test it: if you can say *it is* or *it has*, write *it's*; otherwise write *its*. The same logic separates *your* / *you're*, *their* / *they're* and *whose* / *who's*.
:::

::: example The possessive *'s* {#ex-possessive}
Rewrite using *'s* or *s'*. (a) *the bike of my brother* (b) *the room of the children* (c) *the office of our teachers* (several teachers) (d) *the car of Mr Jones* (e) *the end of the film*
::: solution
(a) *my brother's bike*

(b) *the children's room* (*children* is an irregular plural, so *'s*)

(c) *our teachers' office* (regular plural in *-s*: apostrophe only)

(d) *Mr Jones's car* or *Mr Jones' car* (both accepted)

(e) *the end of the film* is better left as it is. The *'s* is used mainly with people and animals (and with times, places and organisations: *today's news*, *China's economy*, *the company's profits*); for things, *of* is usual: *the end of the film*, *the top of the page*, not *the film's end*, which sounds unusual. The choice between *'s* and *of* is treated in [[nouns-and-articles/possessives-and-of]].
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *This is my parent's house.* (two parents)
- [ ] *Is this your's?*
- [x] *Their car is bigger than ours.*
- [ ] *The tree lost all it's leaves.*
::: solution
*Their car is bigger than ours* uses the determiner *their* before a noun and the pronoun *ours* alone. For two parents, write *my parents' house*. Possessive pronouns never have an apostrophe: *yours*, *hers*, *ours*, *theirs*. *Its leaves* (possessive) has no apostrophe.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Number on the noun.** Chinese nouns do not change for number; 们 is used only for people and is optional (学生们, or just 学生). English marks the plural on every countable noun, even after a number: *three students*.
- **Classifiers and unit nouns.** Chinese uses a classifier with every number + noun: 一本书, 一张纸, 一杯水. English uses no classifier with countable nouns (*a book*, *two books*), but needs a unit noun with uncountable ones (*a piece of paper*, *a glass of water*). So 一张纸 is *a piece of paper* or *a sheet of paper*, while 一张桌子 is just *a table*.
- **He, she and it.** 他, 她 and 它 are distinguished in writing (the distinction was introduced in the early twentieth century; 她 was promoted by the linguist 刘半农 around 1920), but in speech all are *tā*. English distinguishes them in speech, which is why errors appear mainly in speaking.
- **Object forms.** Chinese pronouns have one form for subject and object (我喜欢他 / 他喜欢我); English has *I* / *me*, *he* / *him* and so on.
- **的 and possessives.** 我的 corresponds both to *my* (before a noun: 我的书 → *my book*) and to *mine* (alone: 这本书是我的 → *This book is mine*). 李伟的 → *Li Wei's*.
:::

::: history
Most of the irregular plurals of English are survivors of Old English patterns. The ending *-s* comes from the Old English plural *-as* (*stānas* ‘stones’), which spread to almost all nouns in Middle English. *Feet*, *teeth*, *mice* and *geese* show an ancient vowel change (called *i*-mutation) that happened before Old English was written down. *Oxen* keeps the old *-en* plural, and *children* is a double plural: Old English *cildru* already had a plural ending, and *-en* was added later. Pronouns have their own history. *They*, *them* and *their* were borrowed from Old Norse during the Viking period, replacing native forms that had become too similar to *he* and *him*. And *you* was originally only the plural, and the polite form, of *thou*/*thee*; by the eighteenth century *thou* had almost disappeared from standard English, which is why English has the same *you* for one person and for many.
:::

::: summary
- Countable nouns have singular and plural forms; regular plurals add *-s* or *-es* (*-ies*, *-ves* for some), pronounced /s/, /z/ or /ɪz/ ([[#rule-plural-spelling]]).
- Learn the common irregular plurals: *men*, *women*, *children*, *people*, *feet*, *teeth*; some nouns do not change (*sheep*, *fish*).
- After numbers and *many*, a countable noun must be plural: *two brothers*.
- Uncountable nouns (*information*, *advice*, *furniture*, *news*, *homework*) have no plural, no *a*/*an*, a singular verb, and are counted with unit nouns: *a piece of advice* ([[#rule-uncountable]]).
- Use subject pronouns before the verb and object pronouns after verbs and prepositions: *My sister and I*, *for my sister and me* ([[#rule-pronoun-case]]).
- Choose *he*, *she*, *it* or *they* carefully in speech; singular *they* is standard for a person of unknown sex ([[#rule-he-she]]).
- *My*/*mine*, *her*/*hers*: determiner before a noun, pronoun alone; *'s* for nouns (*my parents' house*); *its* ≠ *it's* ([[#rule-possessive]]).
:::

*My best friend is called Li Hua. **She** is a nurse, and she works in a big hospital. Her parents **live** in a small town, and she visits **them** every month. Last week she gave me some **advice** about my studies. She said that I should read more **books** and drink more **water**. **She and I** are going to the cinema on Saturday. Is this umbrella **yours**? No, it's **hers**.*

The nine corrections: (1) *He* → *She*; (2) *lives* → *live* (*her parents* is plural); (3) *him* → *them* (it refers to her parents); (4) *advices* → *advice*; (5) *book* → *books*; (6) *waters* → *water*; (7) *Me and her* → *She and I* (subject; *Me and her* is informal speech only); (8) *your* → *yours*; (9) *her's* → *hers* (possessive pronouns have no apostrophe).

::: exercise Describe two people {level=3}
Write a short paragraph (six to eight sentences) about two people you know, one man and one woman. Use at least four different pronouns, two possessive forms with *'s*, one plural-only noun or irregular plural, and one uncountable noun with a unit noun. Then read it aloud, listening for *he*/*she* and *his*/*her*.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

*My uncle Jun and his wife Mei run a small noodle shop near my school. He makes the noodles by hand every morning, and she looks after the customers. Their children help them at weekends. Jun's noodles are famous in our area, and people queue for them at lunchtime. Mei always gives me an extra bowl of soup because I'm her favourite nephew. Last month she gave me a useful piece of advice: "Eat slowly and work hard." My parents' friends often ask me where the shop is, and I'm happy to tell them.*

Check: *he*/*his* for Jun, *she*/*her* for Mei; *Jun's noodles*, *my parents' friends*; irregular plurals *children*, *people*; uncountable *soup*, *advice* with unit nouns (*a bowl of*, *a piece of*).
