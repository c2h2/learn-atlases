To make a question in Chinese, you can add 吗 to a statement: 你喜欢咖啡 → 你喜欢咖啡吗？ The words stay in the same order. A question word also stays where the answer would be: 你去哪儿？ (‘you go where?’). English works differently:

- *You like coffee.* → ***Do** you like coffee?*
- *She is at home.* → ***Is** she at home?*
- *You are going **where**?* → ***Where** are you going?*

A new word appears (*do*), words change places (*she is* → *is she*), and the question word moves to the front. The same machinery makes negatives: *You like coffee* → *You **don't** like coffee.* This lesson explains that machinery. It is one of the most important things in English grammar, because the same few rules work for every tense: once you know them, you can make questions and negatives with any verb.

## Auxiliaries: the key to questions and negatives

::: definition Auxiliary verb and operator {#def-auxiliary}
An **auxiliary verb** is a verb that helps the main verb: ***be*** (*She **is** working*), ***have*** (*They **have** finished*), ***do*** (*I **don't** know*) and the **modal verbs** *can*, *could*, *will*, *would*, *shall*, *should*, *may*, *might* and *must*. In questions and negatives, the **first** auxiliary of the verb phrase has a special job; grammars call it the **operator**. The verb *be* acts as an operator even when it is the main verb (*She **is** a doctor*).
:::

::: theorem Questions and negatives with an operator {#rule-operator}
1. To make a **negative**, put ***not*** (or *n't*) after the operator: *She is working* → *She **isn't** working.* *I can swim* → *I **can't** swim.*
2. To make a **yes–no question**, put the operator **before the subject**: *She is working* → ***Is** she working?* *You can swim* → ***Can** you swim?*
3. If the statement has **no operator** — a present simple or past simple verb other than *be* — add ***do***, ***does*** or ***did***, and use the **base form** of the main verb: *You like it* → ***Do** you **like** it?* / *You **don't like** it.* *She lives here* → ***Does** she **live** here?* / *She **doesn't live** here.*
:::

::: proof
English questions and negatives need an auxiliary in a fixed position: *not* goes after it, and in a question it moves in front of the subject. When a sentence already has an auxiliary (*is working*, *can swim*), that auxiliary does the job. A present or past simple verb such as *likes* has no auxiliary, so English supplies an "empty" one, *do*, which carries tense and agreement and has no meaning of its own. That is why *-s* and *-ed* move to *do*: *She likes* → *She doesn't like*; *She liked* → *She didn't like*. Grammars call this **do-support**. *Be* is special: it behaves like an auxiliary even as a main verb, so we say *Is she a doctor?*, not ✗ *Does she be a doctor?* Main-verb *have* usually takes *do* (*Do you have a car?*); the *have got* pattern (*Have you got a car?*) is explained in [[core-grammar/be-and-have]].
:::

The whole system in one table (present tense):

| statement | negative | question |
|---|---|---|
| *I am late.* | *I'm not late.* | *Am I late?* |
| *She is a student.* | *She isn't a student.* | *Is she a student?* |
| *They are working.* | *They aren't working.* | *Are they working?* |
| *He can drive.* | *He can't drive.* | *Can he drive?* |
| *You will help.* | *You won't help.* | *Will you help?* |
| *We like jazz.* | *We don't like jazz.* | *Do we like jazz?* |
| *He likes jazz.* | *He doesn't like jazz.* | *Does he like jazz?* |
| *He liked jazz.* | *He didn't like jazz.* | *Did he like jazz?* |

The negative short forms are: *isn't*, *aren't*, *wasn't*, *weren't*, *don't*, *doesn't*, *didn't*, *haven't*, *hasn't*, *can't*, *couldn't*, *won't* (= *will not*), *wouldn't*, *shouldn't*, *mustn't*. There is no *amn't*: use *I'm not*. In formal writing, write the full forms: *do not*, *cannot* (usually one word), *will not*.

## Negatives

- *I don't eat meat.*
- *My brother doesn't like football.*
- *We aren't ready yet.*
- *She can't come tomorrow.*
- *It didn't rain yesterday.*

::: warning Negatives without *do*
✗ *I not like it.* ✓ *I don't like it.*

✗ *He no understand.* ✓ *He doesn't understand.*

✗ *She doesn't likes it.* ✓ *She doesn't like it.*

✗ *I don't can swim.* ✓ *I can't swim.*

In Chinese, 不 or 没 simply goes before the verb. In English, *not* needs an operator. With *do*, the main verb goes back to the base form, because *does* has already taken the *-s*. With a modal, *do* is not used.
:::

::: example Making negatives {#ex-negatives}
Make each sentence negative. (a) *My parents live in Beijing.* (b) *Li Wei is at work.* (c) *The shop opens on Sundays.* (d) *I can hear you.* (e) *We have a car.*
::: solution
Find the operator first. If there is none, add *do*/*does*.

(a) No operator; subject plural → *do*: *My parents **don't live** in Beijing.*

(b) Operator *is*: *Li Wei **isn't** at work.*

(c) No operator; subject *the shop* = *it* → *does*, and *opens* loses its *-s*: *The shop **doesn't open** on Sundays.*

(d) Operator *can*: *I **can't** hear you.* (*cannot* in formal writing)

(e) Main verb *have*: *We **don't have** a car.* (British English also: *We **haven't got** a car.*)
:::
:::

## Yes–no questions and short answers

::: definition Yes–no question and short answer {#def-yes-no}
A **yes–no question** is a question that can be answered with *yes* or *no*: *Are you tired?* A **short answer** is *yes* or *no* followed by the subject and the operator of the question, without the rest: *Yes, I am.* *No, she doesn't.* *Yes, they can.*
:::

- *Are you a student? — Yes, I am.*
- *Is it far? — No, it isn't.*
- *Do you speak Spanish? — No, I don't.*
- *Does your brother work here? — Yes, he does.*
- *Can you see the screen? — Yes, I can.*
- *Did they win? — No, they didn't.*

Short answers are polite and natural. A bare *Yes.* or *No.* can sound abrupt, though it is common between friends. Two details:

- In a positive short answer the operator is never contracted: *Yes, I am.* not ✗ *Yes, I'm.*; *Yes, it is.* not ✗ *Yes, it's.*
- The short answer repeats the operator, not the main verb: *Do you like it? — Yes, I do.* not ✗ *Yes, I like.*

::: quiz
Which answer to *Does your sister play tennis?* is correct?
- [ ] *Yes, she plays.*
- [ ] *Yes, she is.*
- [x] *Yes, she does.*
- [ ] *Yes, she do.*
::: solution
The question has the operator *does*, so the short answer repeats *does*: *Yes, she does.* (or *No, she doesn't.*) *She plays* repeats the main verb, *she is* uses the wrong operator, and *she do* lacks the *-s*. You can of course add information: *Yes, she does — she plays every week.*
:::
:::

::: example Forming yes–no questions {#ex-yes-no}
Turn each statement into a yes–no question and give a short positive answer. (a) *The museum is open today.* (b) *Your cousin studies medicine.* (c) *They can speak Japanese.* (d) *You live near here.*
::: solution
(a) Operator *is* → move it before the subject: ***Is** the museum open today? — Yes, it is.*

(b) No operator; subject *your cousin* (= *he/she*) → *does*; *studies* → base form *study*: ***Does** your cousin **study** medicine? — Yes, she does.* (or *he does*)

(c) Operator *can*: ***Can** they speak Japanese? — Yes, they can.*

(d) No operator; subject *you* → *do*: ***Do** you live near here? — Yes, I do.* (The answer changes *you* to *I*.)
:::
:::

## *Wh-* questions

::: definition *Wh-* question {#def-wh}
A ***wh-* question** asks for information, not just *yes* or *no*. It begins with a **question word**: *what*, *where*, *when*, *who*, *whose*, *which*, *why* or *how* (most begin with *wh-*), or a phrase such as *how much*, *how many*, *how long*, *how often*, *what time* or *which bus*.
:::

| question word | asks about | example |
|---|---|---|
| *what* | a thing, an action | *What do you want?* |
| *where* | a place | *Where does she work?* |
| *when* | a time | *When is your birthday?* |
| *what time* | a clock time | *What time does the film start?* |
| *who* | a person | *Who is that man?* |
| *whose* | the owner | *Whose bag is this?* |
| *which* | a choice from a limited group | *Which bus goes to the airport?* |
| *why* | a reason | *Why are you late?* |
| *how* | a manner or a state | *How do you spell it?* *How are you?* |
| *how much* / *how many* | a quantity (uncountable / countable) | *How much is it?* *How many brothers do you have?* |
| *how long* | a length of time | *How long does the trip take?* |
| *how often* | frequency | *How often do you go to the gym?* |

::: theorem Word order in *wh-* questions {#rule-wh-order}
In most *wh-* questions the order is:

**question word + operator + subject + main verb (+ rest)**

*Where **do** you **live**?* *What **is** she **doing**?* *When **can** we **meet**?* The operator rules are the same as in yes–no questions: use the auxiliary, *be* or a modal if there is one; otherwise add *do*, *does* or *did*.
:::

::: proof
A *wh-* question is a yes–no question with a question word added in front: *Do you live there?* → *Where do you live?* The question word replaces the missing information (*there*, *in Xi'an*) and moves to the start of the sentence, which is where English puts the most important part of a question. The rest keeps the yes–no question order, with the operator before the subject. This is why ✗ *Where you live?* is wrong (no operator) and ✗ *Where does she lives?* is wrong (*-s* on both *does* and *live*).
:::

### Subject questions

There is one important exception. When the question word **is the subject** — when we ask *who* or *what* did the action — there is no *do*, and the order is the same as in a statement.

::: theorem Subject questions {#rule-subject-question}
If *who*, *what* or *which* + noun is the **subject** of the question, do not add *do*: use the statement word order, with the verb in its normal form.

- *Who **lives** here?* (Someone lives here.) — not ✗ *Who does live here?*
- *What **happened**?* (Something happened.)
- *Which team **won**?*

If the question word is the **object**, use the normal *wh-* order with *do*: *Who **did you call**?* (You called someone.)
:::

::: proof
In a subject question the subject position is already filled by the question word, which is also at the front of the sentence. Nothing needs to move, so the operator does not need to come before the subject, and no *do* is required. Compare *Anna called Tom*: asking about Anna (the subject) gives *Who called Tom?*; asking about Tom (the object) gives *Who did Anna call?* The two questions ask for different people. (A stressed *do* is possible in a subject question for emphasis — *Well, who **did** take it, then?* — but this is rare.)
:::

::: example Subject or object question? {#ex-subject-object}
Here is a statement: *Wang Fang invited Carlos to the party.* Write a question that asks about (a) *Wang Fang* and (b) *Carlos*.
::: solution
(a) *Wang Fang* is the subject of *invited*. Replace it with *who* and keep the statement order, with no *do*: ***Who invited** Carlos to the party?* — *Wang Fang (did).*

(b) *Carlos* is the object. Replace it with *who*, move it to the front, and add the operator *did* (past) before the subject; *invited* becomes the base form *invite*: ***Who did** Wang Fang **invite** to the party?* — *Carlos.*

In formal English, *whom* is sometimes used for the object (*Whom did she invite?*), but in everyday English *who* is normal.
:::
:::

::: example Asking for the missing information {#ex-wh}
Write the question to which the words in brackets are the answer. (a) *She works [in a bank].* (b) *The concert starts [at eight].* (c) *She has [three] brothers.* (d) *I go swimming [twice a week].* (e) *[My uncle] lives in Canada.*
::: solution
(a) Place → *where*; present simple, *she* → *does*: *Where does she work?*

(b) Clock time → *what time* (or *when*); *the concert* = *it* → *does*: *What time does the concert start?*

(c) Number of countable things → *how many* + noun, which moves to the front with the question word; main verb *have*, subject *she* → *does*, and *has* becomes *have*: *How many brothers does she have?* (BrE also *How many brothers has she got?*)

(d) Frequency → *how often*; *I* in the statement becomes *you* in the question: *How often do you go swimming?*

(e) The answer is the subject → subject question, no *do*: *Who lives in Canada?*
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which question is correct?
- [ ] *Where your parents live?*
- [ ] *Where do your parents lives?*
- [x] *Where do your parents live?*
- [ ] *Where live your parents?*
::: solution
*Where* + operator *do* (plural subject) + subject *your parents* + base form *live*. The first question has no operator, the second puts *-s* on the main verb after *do*, and the last inverts the main verb, which English does only with *be* and auxiliaries (and in old or literary English).
:::
:::

## Answering negative questions

A **negative question** begins with a negative operator: *Don't you like it?* *Isn't she coming?* It often shows surprise, or that the speaker expects the answer *yes*: *Isn't it beautiful?*

English and Chinese answer such questions in opposite ways.

::: theorem *Yes* and *no* in answers to negative questions {#rule-yes-no}
In English, *yes* goes with a positive answer and *no* with a negative answer, whatever form the question has:

- *Don't you like coffee?* — ***No**, I don't.* (I do not like it.) / ***Yes**, I do.* (I like it.)
- *Isn't Tom coming?* — ***No**, he isn't.* (He is not coming.) / ***Yes**, he is.* (He is coming.)
:::

::: proof
English *yes* and *no* describe the answer itself: *yes* = the positive statement is true, *no* = the negative statement is true. The short answer that follows must agree with them: *No, I don't*, *Yes, I do*, never ✗ *Yes, I don't*. In Chinese, 对 or 是的 means ‘what you said is right’, so it agrees with the question: 你不喜欢咖啡吗？— 对，我不喜欢 (‘right, I don't’). Translating 对 as *yes* gives ✗ *Yes, I don't*, which an English listener cannot interpret. The safest strategy is to ignore *yes* and *no* at first and think of the short answer: if the true answer is *I don't*, start with *No*.
:::

::: warning Answering negative questions the Chinese way
**Anna:** *Aren't you hungry?*

✗ **Li Wei:** *Yes, I'm not hungry.* (meaning 对，我不饿)

✓ **Li Wei:** *No, I'm not.* (or *No, I'm fine, thanks.*)

✗ *Didn't you see the email? — Yes, I didn't see it.* ✓ *No, I didn't.* (if you did not see it)
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Particles and word order.** Chinese makes yes–no questions with 吗 or with the pattern A-not-A (你去不去？); the word order of the statement stays the same. English changes the word order and, in the simple tenses, adds *do*. Both systems work equally well; the English one simply has to be learned.
- **Question words stay in place in Chinese.** 你去哪儿？ and 他是谁？ keep the question word where the answer goes (‘you go where?’). In English the question word moves to the front: *Where are you going?* *Who is he?*
- **One negative word in Chinese, an operator in English.** 不 and 没(有) go straight before the verb. English needs *not* after an operator, so ✗ *I not know* must become *I don't know*.
- **Statement questions.** In informal English, a statement with rising intonation can be a question, usually to check or to show surprise: *You're leaving already?* *She lives in Paris?* This is normal in conversation, but it is not the neutral way to ask for information, and it is not used in writing.
- **Yes and no.** Chinese 对 confirms what the other person said; English *yes* confirms a positive answer ([[#rule-yes-no]]).
:::

::: history
Do-support is a fairly young part of English grammar. In Middle English, and still in Shakespeare's time, questions and negatives could be made by moving the main verb or putting *not* after it: *Know you the man?* and *I know not* are normal in Shakespeare's plays, side by side with *Do you know …?* and *I do not know*. The Swedish linguist Alvar Ellegård, in a famous corpus study, *The Auxiliary Do* (1953), counted the forms in thousands of examples and showed how the use of *do* in questions and negatives rose from the fifteenth century onwards and became the regular pattern in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. A few old forms survive in fixed expressions, such as *I know not why* in poetry and *How goes it?* in informal speech.
:::

## Where this leads

The operator system is used in every later lesson: with the past simple (*Did you go?* [[core-grammar/past-simple]]), the present continuous (*Are you working?* [[core-grammar/present-continuous]]), the future (*Will it rain?* [[core-grammar/talking-about-the-future]]) and the modal verbs (*Can I go?* [[core-grammar/first-modals]]). Question tags such as *isn't it?* and indirect questions such as *Do you know where she lives?* are treated in [[complex-sentences/noun-clauses]], and polite ways of asking in [[everyday-english/requests-offers]].

::: summary
- Questions and negatives need an **operator**: the first auxiliary, a modal, or *be* ([[#def-auxiliary]]).
- Negative: operator + *not* (*isn't*, *can't*, *don't*). Question: operator before the subject (*Is she…?*, *Can you…?*) ([[#rule-operator]]).
- With no operator (present or past simple of a verb other than *be*), add *do*, *does* or *did* and use the base form: *Does she live here?* *She doesn't live here.*
- Short answers repeat the operator: *Yes, I do.* *No, she isn't.* — never contracted at the end (✗ *Yes, I'm*).
- *Wh-* questions: question word + operator + subject + verb ([[#rule-wh-order]]); but when the question word is the subject, there is no *do* (*Who called you?*) ([[#rule-subject-question]]).
- In answers to negative questions, *yes* goes with a positive answer and *no* with a negative one: *Aren't you hungry? — No, I'm not.* ([[#rule-yes-no]]).
:::
