Read these two sentences aloud:

- ✗ *My sister work in a hospital.*
- ✓ *My sister works in a hospital.*

The difference is one letter, and every listener understands both. Yet the first sentence is one of the most frequent mistakes in learner English, and teachers, examiners and employers notice it at once. The small *-s* at the end of *works* tells the listener that the subject is one person or thing (*my sister*, not *my sisters*) and that we are talking about the present. Chinese has nothing like it, which is why it is so easy to forget.

This lesson is about the **present simple**: its forms, the *-s* ending and its spelling and pronunciation, and its meanings — habits, facts and states. Questions and negatives with *do* and *does* come in the next lesson, [[core-grammar/questions-and-negatives]].

## Form

::: definition Present simple {#def-present-simple}
The **present simple** is the form of a verb with no auxiliary that refers to the present. It has two forms: the **base form** of the verb (*work*, *play*, *go*) after *I*, *you*, *we*, *they* and plural nouns, and the base form + ***-s*** (*works*, *plays*, *goes*) after *he*, *she*, *it* and singular nouns. The form with *-s* is called the **third person singular**.
:::

| subject | verb |
|---|---|
| *I*, *you*, *we*, *they* | *work*, *live*, *watch* |
| *he*, *she*, *it* | *works*, *lives*, *watches* |
| plural nouns: *my parents*, *the shops* | *work*, *open* |
| singular nouns: *my father*, *the shop*, *Li Wei* | *works*, *opens* |

- *I live in Kunming. My brother lives in Shenzhen.*
- *We usually eat at home. Our neighbour usually eats out.*
- *The shops open at nine. The bank opens at ten.*

*Be* and *have* have their own forms (*am*, *is*, *are*; *have*, *has*): see [[core-grammar/be-and-have]]. The modal verbs (*can*, *must*, *should*, *will* …) never take *-s*: *She can swim*, not ✗ *She cans swim* (see [[core-grammar/first-modals]]).

### Spelling of the *-s* form

::: theorem Spelling of the third person *-s* {#rule-s-spelling}
1. Most verbs add ***-s***: *work* → *works*, *play* → *plays*, *read* → *reads*.
2. Verbs ending in ***-s***, ***-ss***, ***-sh***, ***-ch***, ***-x*** or ***-z*** add ***-es***: *miss* → *misses*, *wash* → *washes*, *watch* → *watches*, *fix* → *fixes*, *buzz* → *buzzes*.
3. Verbs ending in ***-o*** after a consonant add ***-es***: *go* → *goes*, *do* → *does*.
4. Verbs ending in **consonant + *-y*** change *y* to ***-ies***: *study* → *studies*, *try* → *tries*, *carry* → *carries*. After a vowel, *-y* stays: *play* → *plays*, *buy* → *buys*, *enjoy* → *enjoys*.
5. *Have* → ***has*** (irregular).
:::

::: proof
The *-es* spelling shows an extra syllable. After the hissing sounds /s/, /z/, /ʃ/, /tʃ/, /dʒ/ and the ending *-x* (= /ks/), the ending is pronounced /ɪz/, which is a whole syllable: *watch* is one syllable, *watches* is two. Writing *-es* makes this visible. *Goes* and *does* keep an old spelling with *-es* although no syllable is added. The change from *-y* to *-ies* is a general English spelling rule, also used for plurals (*city* → *cities*, see [[core-grammar/nouns-pronouns]]) and the past (*study* → *studied*, see [[core-grammar/past-simple]]). Verbs that end in *-se* or *-ge* already have an *e*, so they just add *-s*: *close* → *closes*, *change* → *changes* — both pronounced with /ɪz/.
:::

### Pronunciation of the *-s* form

The ending is written *-s* or *-es*, but it has three pronunciations. The choice depends on the last **sound** (not the last letter) of the verb.

::: theorem Pronunciation of the third person *-s* {#rule-s-sound}
1. After the hissing and hushing sounds /s/, /z/, /ʃ/, /ʒ/, /tʃ/ and /dʒ/, *-s* is pronounced **/ɪz/** (GA often /əz/), an extra syllable: *misses*, *uses*, *washes*, *watches*, *changes*.
2. After other **voiceless** sounds — /p/, /t/, /k/, /f/, /θ/ — it is pronounced **/s/**: *stops*, *gets*, *works*, *laughs*.
3. After all other sounds — **voiced** consonants and all vowels — it is pronounced **/z/**: *lives*, *reads*, *runs*, *plays*, *goes*, *sees*.
:::

::: proof
This is a rule of ease of pronunciation, and it is the same for the plural *-s* and the possessive *'s* (*cats* /s/, *dogs* /z/, *horses* /ɪz/). Saying /s/ after a voiceless sound and /z/ after a voiced sound keeps the vocal folds either off or on through the end of the word; adding the vowel /ɪ/ separates two hissing sounds that would otherwise run together (*wash* + /s/ is hard to hear). Two common verbs are irregular in pronunciation: *says* is /sez/, not /seɪz/, and *does* is /dʌz/, not /duːz/. Voiced and voiceless sounds are explained in [[sounds-of-english/stops-fricatives]].
:::

Many Chinese speakers find the /z/ ending difficult, because Mandarin syllables end only in a vowel, *-n*, *-ng* (or *-r*). A clear /s/ or /z/ is better than none: *She live_* without any final sound is the problem, not a slightly wrong /s/.

::: example Spelling and pronouncing *-s* forms {#ex-s-forms}

::: solution
| verb | *-s* form | why | ending |
|---|---|---|---|
| *teach* | *teaches* | ends in *-ch*: add *-es* | /ɪz/ (after /tʃ/) |
| *cry* | *cries* | consonant + *y*: *-ies* | /z/ (after the vowel /aɪ/) |
| *enjoy* | *enjoys* | vowel + *y*: just add *-s* | /z/ (after the vowel /ɔɪ/) |
| *drive* | *drives* | add *-s* | /z/ (after voiced /v/) |
| *cook* | *cooks* | add *-s* | /s/ (after voiceless /k/) |
| *fix* | *fixes* | ends in *-x*: add *-es* | /ɪz/ (after /s/ in /ks/) |
| *go* | *goes* | consonant + *o*: add *-es* | /z/ (after the vowel) |
:::
:::

::: quiz
In which verb is the *-s* ending pronounced /ɪz/?
- [ ] *helps*
- [ ] *sings*
- [x] *closes*
- [ ] *knows*
::: solution
*Close* ends in the sound /z/, a hissing sound, so *closes* is /ˈkləʊzɪz/ (GB), /ˈkloʊzəz/ (GA) — two syllables. *Helps* ends in /s/ (after voiceless /p/), *sings* and *knows* in /z/ (after /ŋ/ and a vowel). ## Meaning and use

::: theorem Uses of the present simple {#rule-ps-uses}
Use the present simple for:

1. **habits and routines** — things that happen regularly: *I get up at seven.* *She goes swimming every Saturday.*
2. **facts that are true for a long time**, and general truths: *My parents live in Lanzhou.* *Water boils at 100 °C.* *The sun rises in the east.*
3. **states**, with verbs of thinking, feeling and possessing: *I know the answer.* *He likes jazz.* *This bag belongs to Amina.*
4. **timetables** and fixed schedules, even for the future: *The train leaves at 9.15.* *The course starts on 3 March.*
:::

::: proof
What these uses have in common is that the situation is not tied to the moment of speaking: a habit, a fact or a state is true now, but also before now and after now. For actions happening at this moment, or for temporary situations, English uses the present continuous instead: *She's swimming right now* ([[core-grammar/present-continuous]]). Use 4 extends the idea: a timetable is a fixed fact, so the present simple can refer to a future event that is "on the schedule" (see [[core-grammar/talking-about-the-future]]). The present simple is also used in some special contexts — sports commentary (*Messi passes to Alba …*), instructions (*You turn left at the bank*), newspaper headlines and telling jokes or stories in a lively style — but these are less common.
:::

Habits are often marked by a time expression:

- **adverbs of frequency** before the main verb: *I **always** drink tea.* *He **often** works late.* *We **never** watch TV.* (Their position is explained in [[core-grammar/adjectives-adverbs]].)
- **expressions of frequency** at the end: *every day*, *twice a week*, *on Mondays*, *at weekends* (BrE) / *on weekends* (AmE), *once a month*.

Facts and states:

- *Amina speaks Arabic, French and English.*
- *Pandas eat bamboo.*
- *I want a new phone.*
- *Do you understand?* *I don't remember his name.*

::: example Which use? {#ex-uses}
Say which use of the present simple each sentence shows (habit, fact/general truth, state or timetable). (a) *Kenji cycles to work.* (b) *The museum closes at five on Sundays.* (c) *I think you're right.* (d) *Light travels faster than sound.* (e) *Our flight lands at 6 a.m. tomorrow.*
::: solution
(a) A **habit**: he does it regularly. (Without *every day*, the context still suggests a routine.)

(b) A **timetable** or regular fact: it happens every Sunday by rule. It could also be called a habit of the museum; both answers are acceptable.

(c) A **state**: *think* here means ‘have an opinion’, a state of mind, not an action. (In *I'm thinking about it*, *think* means ‘consider’ and can be progressive.)

(d) A **general truth**: a fact of physics.

(e) A **timetable**: a scheduled future event. The time is in the future (*tomorrow*), but the verb is present simple because the schedule is fixed.
:::
:::

::: example Sentences from a timetable {#ex-timetable}
Here is part of a weekend timetable for a sports centre. Write four sentences about it in the present simple.

| activity | day | time |
|---|---|---|
| swimming lessons | Saturday | 9.00–10.30 |
| yoga | Saturday and Sunday | 18.00 |
| the café | every day | opens 8.00, closes 21.00 |
| the centre | Sunday | closes early, 19.00 |
::: solution
A timetable describes fixed, regular events, so the present simple is right even when we talk about next weekend.

- *Swimming lessons start at nine on Saturday and finish at half past ten.* (*lessons* is plural: *start*, *finish*.)
- *There is a yoga class at six o'clock on Saturday and Sunday.* (or *Yoga starts at six …*: *yoga* is uncountable, so *starts*.)
- *The café opens at eight and closes at nine in the evening every day.* (*the café* is singular: *opens*, *closes* — with /ɪz/.)
- *On Sunday the centre closes early, at seven.*

Notice that the times are written as words or with *at*: *at nine*, *at half past ten*, *at six o'clock*. Prepositions of time are explained in [[core-grammar/prepositions-time-place]].
:::
:::

::: quiz
Why is the present simple used in *The concert begins at eight tomorrow*?
- [ ] Because *tomorrow* always needs the present simple.
- [x] Because the start time is part of a fixed schedule.
- [ ] Because *begin* is a state verb.
- [ ] It is a mistake; it should be *The concert will begin*.
::: solution
The present simple can refer to the future when the event is on a timetable or programme. *The concert will begin at eight tomorrow* is also correct, but it sounds more like an announcement or a prediction; the present simple presents the time as fixed. *Tomorrow* does not require any particular form (*I'll call you tomorrow*, *I'm meeting her tomorrow*), and *begin* is an action verb, not a state verb.
:::
:::

## Agreement: when to add *-s*

::: theorem Subject–verb agreement in the present {#rule-agreement}
Add *-s* to the verb when the subject is **third person singular**: *he*, *she*, *it*, a singular noun (*the teacher*), a name (*Li Wei*), an uncountable noun (*water*, *information*), or a word such as *everyone*, *somebody*, *nobody*, *each* or *this*. Do not add *-s* when the subject is *I*, *you*, *we*, *they*, a plural noun, or two subjects joined by *and*.
:::

::: proof
The *-s* is the only trace left in English of the old system of person endings (see the historical note below), and it marks a subject that is neither the speaker (*I*), the listener (*you*), nor a group (*we*, *they*). Words such as *everyone* and *nobody* refer to many people but are grammatically singular, so they take *-s*: *Everyone likes her.* Two singular subjects joined by *and* make a plural: *My brother and my sister live in Dalian.* Collective nouns such as *family*, *team* and *government* are singular in American English (*My family lives in Dalian*); in British English they can also take a plural verb when we think of the members (*My family live in Dalian*). This and other harder cases are treated in [[nouns-and-articles/agreement]].
:::

| subject | verb |
|---|---|
| *My best friend* | *works* in a bank. |
| *Everybody* | *knows* the answer. |
| *The information* | *changes* every day. |
| *Maths* | *is* my favourite subject. |
| *My parents* | *work* at the same school. |
| *Tom and Anna* | *live* together. |
| *People* | *say* it's a good film. (*people* is plural) |

::: warning Forgetting the *-s* — and adding it where it does not belong
✗ *He work in Shanghai.* ✓ *He works in Shanghai.*

✗ *My mother cook very well.* ✓ *My mother cooks very well.*

But do **not** add *-s*:

- after a modal verb: ✗ *She can speaks Japanese.* ✓ *She can speak Japanese.*
- after *does* and *doesn't*: ✗ *Does he likes it?* ✓ *Does he like it?* ✗ *She doesn't knows.* ✓ *She doesn't know.*
- after *to*: ✗ *He wants to goes.* ✓ *He wants to go.*
- with a plural subject: ✗ *My friends lives here.* ✓ *My friends live here.*

There is only one *-s* in each verb phrase, and it goes on the first verb: *she works*, *she does not work* (the *-s* moves to *do*).
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *My brother and I goes to the gym.*
- [ ] *Everyone in my class have a laptop.*
- [x] *Nobody knows the answer.*
- [ ] *She want to study medicine.*
::: solution
*Nobody* is grammatically singular, so *knows* is correct. *My brother and I* is plural (= *we*): *go*. *Everyone* is singular: *has*. *She* needs *-s*: *wants*.
:::
:::

::: example Correcting a profile {#ex-profile}
Correct the verb forms in this short profile.

*My friend Carlos come from Mexico. He study engineering and live with two other students. Every morning he drink a big cup of coffee and check his messages. His flatmates doesn't get up early, so the flat are very quiet.*
::: solution
Check every verb: what is its subject, and is it third person singular?

*My friend Carlos **comes** from Mexico. He **studies** engineering and **lives** with two other students. Every morning he **drinks** a big cup of coffee and **checks** his messages. His flatmates **don't** get up early, so the flat **is** very quiet.*

- *comes*, *studies* (consonant + *y* → *-ies*), *lives*, *drinks*, *checks*: subject *Carlos* / *he*.
- *don't*: the subject *his flatmates* is plural, so *do*, not *does*.
- *is*: the subject *the flat* is singular.

Notice that in *He studies engineering and lives with …* the second verb also needs *-s*: the subject *he* applies to both.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **No agreement in Chinese.** Chinese verbs never change for the subject: 我工作, 他工作, 他们工作. English marks only one person — *he/she/it* — and only in the present, which makes the *-s* easy to overlook. It carries little meaning, because the subject usually shows who is meant, so even advanced speakers sometimes drop it in fast speech. In writing, check it deliberately.
- **No tense marking in Chinese.** A Chinese sentence such as 他每天骑车上班 shows habit with 每天; the verb itself is the same as in 他昨天骑车上班. In English the verb form changes: *He cycles to work every day* / *He cycled to work yesterday*.
- **Habit and progress.** Chinese 他在看书 (‘he is reading’) and 他看书 (‘he reads’) are separated by 在; English separates them by *is reading* / *reads*. Learners sometimes use the present continuous for habits (✗ *I am getting up at seven every day*); the next lessons show the difference.
- **Final consonants.** Because standard Mandarin syllables end only in a vowel, *-n*, *-ng* or *-r*, final /s/ and /z/ are easy to drop in speech. Saying the ending clearly is a pronunciation task as much as a grammar one.
:::

::: history
Old English had a different verb ending for almost every person: roughly *ic singe* ‘I sing’, *þū singest* ‘you (singular) sing’, *hē singeþ* ‘he sings’ and *wē singaþ* ‘we sing’. Most of these endings disappeared in Middle English. For the third person singular, southern dialects used *-eth* (*he singeth*), while northern dialects used *-es*. The northern *-s* spread south and became normal in London English during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. The King James Bible (1611) still uses *-eth* (*he maketh me to lie down in green pastures*), but Shakespeare already uses both forms, and by the end of the seventeenth century *-s* had won in ordinary use. The single *-s* of modern English is the last survivor of the old endings. It is also one of the forms that children learning English as their first language master late: in Roger Brown's study *A First Language* (1973), the third person *-s* came after the plural *-s* and the *-ing* ending.
:::

## Where this leads

The next lesson, [[core-grammar/questions-and-negatives]], shows how to ask questions and make negatives in the present simple with *do* and *does*. [[core-grammar/present-continuous]] contrasts the present simple with *be* + *-ing*. ::: summary
- The present simple uses the base form (*I work*) and, after *he*, *she*, *it* and singular nouns, the base form + *-s* (*she works*) ([[#def-present-simple]]).
- Spelling: *-es* after *s*, *sh*, *ch*, *x*, *z* and in *goes*, *does*; consonant + *y* → *-ies*; *have* → *has* ([[#rule-s-spelling]]).
- Pronunciation: /ɪz/ after hissing sounds, /s/ after other voiceless sounds, /z/ elsewhere; *says* /sez/ and *does* /dʌz/ are irregular ([[#rule-s-sound]]).
- Use it for habits, long-term facts and general truths, states, and timetables ([[#rule-ps-uses]]).
- Add *-s* for every third person singular subject, including *everyone* and uncountable nouns, but never after modals, *does*, *to* or plural subjects ([[#rule-agreement]]).
- Chinese has no agreement and no tense on verbs, so the *-s* needs deliberate attention in writing and in speech.
:::
