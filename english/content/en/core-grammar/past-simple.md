Compare two Chinese sentences: 我昨天去北京 and 我明天去北京. The verb 去 is the same; only the time word changes. Now compare the English:

- *I **went** to Beijing yesterday.*
- *I'**m going** to Beijing tomorrow.*

In English the verb itself changes, even though *yesterday* already tells us the time. This seems like a waste: why say "past" twice? But in English the verb form is not optional. *I go to Beijing yesterday* sounds wrong to every English speaker, because the present form says "habit or now" while *yesterday* says "past", and the two contradict each other.

This lesson is about the **past simple**: the regular *-ed* ending, its spelling and its three pronunciations, the most common irregular verbs, questions and negatives with *did*, and when to use the past simple. It also explains why Chinese 了 is not the same as the English past.

## Form

::: definition Past simple {#def-past-simple}
The **past simple** is the past tense form of a verb used without an auxiliary. **Regular verbs** form it with the ending ***-ed***: *work* → *worked*, *live* → *lived*. **Irregular verbs** have their own past forms, which must be learned: *go* → *went*, *see* → *saw*. The past simple has the **same form for every subject** (*I worked*, *she worked*, *they worked*), except *be*, which has *was* and *were*.
:::

- *I **watched** a film last night.*
- *She **lived** in Japan for two years.*
- *We **went** to the beach on Sunday.*
- *They **were** very tired after the trip.*

There is no *-s* in the past: ✗ *He workeds.* The past forms of *be* (*was*, *were*) are in [[core-grammar/be-and-have]].

### Spelling of *-ed*

::: theorem Spelling of the regular past {#rule-ed-spelling}
1. Most verbs add ***-ed***: *work* → *worked*, *play* → *played*, *open* → *opened*.
2. Verbs ending in ***-e*** add only ***-d***: *live* → *lived*, *like* → *liked*, *agree* → *agreed*.
3. Verbs ending in **consonant + *-y*** change *y* to ***-ied***: *study* → *studied*, *try* → *tried*, *carry* → *carried*. After a vowel, *-y* stays: *play* → *played*, *stay* → *stayed*. (But *pay* → *paid*, *say* → *said*, *lay* → *laid* are irregular.)
4. Verbs ending in **one vowel letter + one consonant letter**, with the **stress on the last syllable**, double the consonant: *stop* → *stopped*, *plan* → *planned*, *prefer* → *preferred*. Not when the last syllable is unstressed: *visit* → *visited*, *open* → *opened*, *happen* → *happened*. Never double *w*, *x* or *y*: *fix* → *fixed*, *show* → *showed*.
5. British English doubles final *-l* after one vowel letter even when unstressed (*travel* → *travelled*, *cancel* → *cancelled*); American English usually does not (*traveled*, *canceled*).
:::

::: proof
These are the same spelling rules as for *-ing* ([[core-grammar/present-continuous#rule-ing-spelling]]) and for the *-s* ending (*study* → *studies*, [[core-grammar/present-simple#rule-s-spelling]]). Doubling keeps a short vowel short: *planned* /plænd/, not *planed* /pleɪnd/ (from *plane*). One difference from *-ing*: with *-ing* we keep the *y* (*studying*, to avoid *ii*), while with *-ed* we change it (*studied*).
:::

### Pronunciation of *-ed*

::: theorem Pronunciation of the regular past {#rule-ed-sound}
The ending *-ed* has three pronunciations, decided by the last **sound** of the verb:

1. After **/t/** or **/d/**: **/ɪd/** (GA often /əd/), an extra syllable: *wanted*, *needed*, *visited*, *decided*.
2. After other **voiceless** sounds — /p/, /k/, /f/, /s/, /ʃ/, /tʃ/, /θ/: **/t/**: *stopped*, *worked*, *laughed*, *missed*, *washed*, *watched*.
3. After **voiced** sounds — all other consonants and all vowels: **/d/**: *lived*, *played*, *opened*, *called*, *changed*, *cried*.
:::

::: proof
The logic is the same as for the *-s* ending ([[core-grammar/present-simple#rule-s-sound]]). /t/ is voiceless and /d/ is voiced, so the ending matches the voicing of the sound before it. After /t/ or /d/, a second /t/ or /d/ could not be heard (*want* + /t/ would sound like *want*), so a vowel is inserted. Note that the spelling does not show the pronunciation: *stopped* has one syllable, /stɒpt/ (GB), /stɑːpt/ (GA), even though it has six letters and two vowel letters. Two adjectives keep an old /ɪd/: *naked* /ˈneɪkɪd/ and *wicked* /ˈwɪkɪd/, and some adjectives in *-ed* have /ɪd/ where the verb has /d/ or /t/: *a learned* /ˈlɜːnɪd/ *professor* (‘very educated’), *an aged* /ˈeɪdʒɪd/ *relative* (‘very old’) — but *She learned* /lɜːnd/ *fast*.
:::

Chinese speakers often make one of two pronunciation errors: they add a vowel to every *-ed* (*worked* as ✗ "work-ed", two syllables) or they drop the ending altogether, so that *I worked* sounds like *I work*. Practise the clusters: *worked* /wɜːkt/ (GB), /wɜːrkt/ (GA) ends in /kt/, *lived* /lɪvd/ in /vd/.

::: example Spelling and pronouncing *-ed* {#ex-ed}
Write the past simple and say how *-ed* is pronounced: *hope*, *hop*, *need*, *cry*, *kiss*, *enjoy*, *permit*, *rain*.
::: solution
| verb | past | spelling rule | *-ed* sounds |
|---|---|---|---|
| *hope* | *hoped* | ends in *e*: add *-d* | /t/ (after /p/) |
| *hop* | *hopped* | one vowel + one consonant, stressed: double | /t/ (after /p/) |
| *need* | *needed* | add *-ed* | /ɪd/ (after /d/) |
| *cry* | *cried* | consonant + *y* → *-ied* | /d/ (after the vowel /aɪ/) |
| *kiss* | *kissed* | add *-ed* | /t/ (after /s/) |
| *enjoy* | *enjoyed* | vowel + *y*: add *-ed* | /d/ |
| *permit* | *permitted* | stress on *-mit*: double | /ɪd/ (after /t/) |
| *rain* | *rained* | add *-ed* | /d/ (after /n/) |

*Hoped* and *hopped* are pronounced differently in the vowel (/həʊpt/ and /hɒpt/ in GB), and the spelling shows it with the double *p*.
:::
:::

::: quiz
In which verb is *-ed* pronounced as an extra syllable, /ɪd/?
- [ ] *walked*
- [ ] *played*
- [x] *started*
- [ ] *watched*
::: solution
*Start* ends in /t/, so *started* is /ˈstɑːtɪd/ (GB), /ˈstɑːrtəd/ (GA) — two syllables. *Walked* and *watched* end in /t/ (after the voiceless /k/ and /tʃ/), *played* in /d/ (after a vowel). All three have one syllable.

### Irregular verbs

About two hundred English verbs are irregular, but most of them are rare. The most common verbs, however, are very often irregular: *be*, *have*, *do*, *say*, *go*, *get*, *make*, *know*, *think*, *take*, *see* and *come* are all irregular, and they are among the most frequent verbs of the language. Learning them in groups with the same pattern helps.

| pattern | verbs |
|---|---|
| no change | *cut* → *cut*, *put* → *put*, *hit* → *hit*, *let* → *let*, *cost* → *cost*, *set* → *set*, *shut* → *shut* |
| same spelling, new sound | *read* /riːd/ → *read* /red/ |
| *-ought* / *-aught* | *buy* → *bought*, *bring* → *brought*, *think* → *thought*, *fight* → *fought*, *catch* → *caught*, *teach* → *taught* |
| *-d* → *-t* | *send* → *sent*, *spend* → *spent*, *build* → *built*, *lend* → *lent* |
| long vowel → short + *t* | *keep* → *kept*, *sleep* → *slept*, *feel* → *felt*, *leave* → *left*, *meet* → *met*, *mean* → *meant* |
| vowel *i* → *a* | *begin* → *began*, *drink* → *drank*, *sing* → *sang*, *swim* → *swam*, *sit* → *sat*, *give* → *gave* |
| vowel → *o* | *write* → *wrote*, *drive* → *drove*, *ride* → *rode*, *speak* → *spoke*, *break* → *broke*, *choose* → *chose*, *sell* → *sold*, *tell* → *told* |
| vowel → *oo* / *ew* | *take* → *took*, *stand* → *stood*, *understand* → *understood*, *know* → *knew*, *grow* → *grew*, *fly* → *flew*, *throw* → *threw* |
| *-ay* → *-aid* | *say* → *said* /sed/, *pay* → *paid*, *lay* → *laid* |
| other | *go* → *went*, *do* → *did*, *see* → *saw*, *eat* → *ate*, *come* → *came*, *run* → *ran*, *get* → *got*, *find* → *found*, *have* → *had*, *make* → *made*, *hear* → *heard*, *lose* → *lost*, *wear* → *wore*, *forget* → *forgot* |

A few verbs have both a regular and an irregular past. In British English *learnt*, *dreamt*, *spelt*, *burnt* and *smelt* are common beside *learned*, *dreamed* and so on; American English usually prefers the *-ed* forms. Both are correct. American English also has some irregular forms that British English lacks, such as *dove* (beside *dived*) and, informally, *snuck* (beside *sneaked*). A learner's dictionary lists the past form of every irregular verb.

## Questions and negatives

::: theorem Questions and negatives in the past simple {#rule-did}
Questions and negatives use ***did*** for every subject, followed by the **base form** of the main verb:

- negative: *subject + did not (didn't) + base form*: *I **didn't go**.* *She **didn't like** it.*
- question: *(question word +) did + subject + base form*: ***Did** you **go**?* *Where **did** she **work**?*
- short answers: *Yes, I did.* *No, they didn't.*

*Be* uses *was* and *were* as its own operator: *I wasn't there.* *Were you at home?* Modal verbs such as *could* also need no *did*: *I couldn't come.*
:::

::: proof
This is the operator system of [[core-grammar/questions-and-negatives#rule-operator]] in the past. *Did* is the past of *do*: it carries the past tense, so the main verb goes back to the base form. Putting the past on both verbs (✗ *Did you went?*) marks the past twice, which English does not allow, just as it does not allow two *-s* endings (✗ *Does she likes?*). Subject questions, as always, need no *did*: *Who **told** you?* *What **happened**?*
:::

- *Did you see the match last night? — Yes, I did. It was great!*
- *I didn't hear the alarm, so I was late.*
- *Where did you buy that coat?*
- *Why didn't you call me?*
- *What time did the train arrive?*
- *Who ate the last dumpling?* (subject question: no *did*)

::: warning Two past forms in one verb phrase
✗ *Did you went to the party?* ✓ *Did you go to the party?*

✗ *I didn't saw him.* ✓ *I didn't see him.*

✗ *Where did she bought it?* ✓ *Where did she buy it?*

✗ *Who did tell you?* (as a subject question) ✓ *Who told you?*

After *did* or *didn't*, the main verb is always in the base form. The past is shown once, on *did*.
:::

::: example Questions and negatives {#ex-did}
Make the sentence negative, then make a yes–no question and a *wh-* question about the part in brackets. *Kenji bought [a new camera] last week.*
::: solution
1. Negative: no operator, so add *didn't* and use the base form *buy*: *Kenji **didn't buy** a new camera last week.*
2. Yes–no question: *did* before the subject, base form: ***Did** Kenji **buy** a new camera last week? — Yes, he did.*
3. *Wh-* question: the bracketed part is the object (a thing) → *what*: ***What did** Kenji **buy** last week?* — *A new camera.*

A subject question would need no *did*: *Who bought a new camera last week?* — *Kenji (did).*
:::
:::

## Meaning and use

::: theorem Uses of the past simple {#rule-past-uses}
Use the past simple for:

1. **completed actions and events at a definite time in the past**: *I met her in 2019.* *The shop closed at six yesterday.*
2. **past states and situations** that are over: *We lived in a small flat when I was a child.* *He was very shy at school.*
3. **past habits**: *I walked to school every day.* (Also *I used to walk*, see [[tense-and-aspect/past-habits]].)
4. **a series of events** in a story, in the order they happened: *She opened the door, looked around and switched on the light.*
:::

::: proof
The past simple places a situation in a time that is **finished and separated from now**. That time may be named (*yesterday*, *in 2019*, *two weeks ago*), or it may be clear from the context (a story, a conversation about someone's childhood). Because the time is over, the past simple says nothing about the present: *She lived in Paris* suggests that she no longer does. When an event is connected to the present rather than to a finished time — *I've lost my keys* (and I still don't have them) — English often uses the present perfect instead; that contrast is explained in [[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect]].
:::

Common time expressions with the past simple: *yesterday*, *last night*, *last week*, *last year*, *in 2015*, *on Monday*, *at three o'clock*, *when I was young*, and ***ago*** after a period of time: *two days ago*, *a long time ago*.

::: warning *Ago* and *before*
✗ *I came to Shanghai before three years.* ✓ *I came to Shanghai three years ago.*

To count back from now, put ***ago*** **after** the period of time. *Before* + a period (*before three years*) is not used this way; *before* goes before a point in time or an event (*before 2020*, *before lunch*). Chinese 三年前 has 前 after the number, like English *ago*.
:::

::: example Telling a story {#ex-story}
Put the verbs in the past simple to complete the story, and say which are irregular.

*Last Saturday Li Na (1 wake) up early. She (2 make) some tea, (3 pack) a small bag and (4 take) the bus to the mountains. The weather (5 be) perfect. She (6 walk) for three hours and (7 stop) for lunch by a lake. On the way back she (8 see) a group of monkeys, but they (9 not come) close. She (10 get) home at seven, tired but happy.*
::: solution
(1) *woke* (irregular); (2) *made* (irregular); (3) *packed* (regular, /t/); (4) *took* (irregular); (5) *was* (irregular, *be*, singular subject); (6) *walked* (regular, /t/); (7) *stopped* (regular, double *p*, /t/); (8) *saw* (irregular); (9) *didn't come* (negative with *didn't* + base form); (10) *got* (irregular).

In a story, every finite verb that describes the events is in the past, even though *Last Saturday* has already set the time. Chinese would not need to mark each verb.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *I see her two days ago.*
- [ ] *Did she called you yesterday?*
- [x] *We didn't have time to finish.*
- [ ] *He goed home early.*
::: solution
*We didn't have time* uses *didn't* + base form *have*. The other sentences need the past *saw* (*I saw her two days ago*), the base form after *did* (*Did she call you?*) and the irregular past *went* (*He went home early*). Forms such as ✗ *goed* are a natural mistake — young children learning English as a first language make them too, because they apply the regular rule to every verb.
:::
:::

::: example 了 and the English past {#ex-le}
Translate into English and say, for each sentence, whether 了 and the English past simple go together. (a) 我昨天买了一本书。 (b) 我昨天很累。 (c) 你吃了饭再走吧。 (d) 我以前不喜欢吃辣的。
::: solution
(a) *I bought a book yesterday.* Here 了 (completion) and the past (*bought*) appear together, because the completed action is in the past.

(b) *I was very tired yesterday.* No 了 in Chinese, but English needs the past *was*: the state belongs to a finished time.

(c) *Have something to eat before you go.* (or *Eat first, then go.*) 了 shows that eating will be complete before leaving, but the time is the future, so there is no English past.

(d) *I didn't like spicy food before.* (or *I never used to like spicy food*; *used to* is explained in [[tense-and-aspect/past-habits]].) No 了, but a past state: *didn't like*.

So the English past follows the **time** of the situation, while 了 follows **completion**. They often meet, as in (a), but neither one predicts the other.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **了 is not a past tense.** 了 after a verb shows that an action is complete (perfective aspect), not that it is in the past. It can appear in sentences about the future (明天吃了饭再走 ‘tomorrow, when you've eaten, then leave’), and many past sentences have no 了 (我以前住在上海 → *I lived in Shanghai before*; 他昨天很忙 → *He was very busy yesterday*). So "translate 了 as *-ed*" is not a safe rule. In English, every finite verb about the past is in a past form, whether or not Chinese would use 了.
- **Time words are not enough.** Chinese shows past time with words such as 昨天, 去年 and 以前, and the verb stays the same. English needs both the time word and the past form: *I went there last year.*
- **过 and experience.** 我去过北京 (‘I have been to Beijing at some time’) is usually translated with the present perfect, *I've been to Beijing*, unless a past time is named: *I went to Beijing in 2018*. See [[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect]].
- **Past states.** Chinese adjectives and 是 do not change for past time (他小时候很胖); English *be* does: *He was quite chubby as a child.* Learners who remember *-ed* on verbs often forget *was* and *were*.
- **Final consonant clusters.** Endings such as /kt/, /pt/ and /vd/ do not occur in Mandarin, so *-ed* is easy to drop in speech; a short, clear /t/ or /d/ is enough.

The relation between Chinese aspect particles and English tenses is treated in detail in [[tense-and-aspect/tense-and-chinese]].
:::

::: history
English verbs fall into two old families. **Strong verbs** formed the past by changing their vowel, as *sing* → *sang* and *drive* → *drove* still do; **weak verbs** added an ending with *-d* or *-t*, the ancestor of *-ed*. All new verbs join the weak (regular) type — *googled*, *texted*, *emailed* — and over the centuries many strong verbs have moved over too: *help* once had the past *holp*, and *climb* had *clomb*. A study by Erez Lieberman and colleagues in *Nature* (2007) traced 177 irregular verbs from Old English and found that 98 of them are still irregular today; the rarer a verb, the faster it became regular, while very frequent verbs such as *be*, *have*, *go* and *see* kept their irregular forms. Change also runs the other way now and then: American English has *dove* and *snuck* beside the older regular *dived* and *sneaked*.
:::

## Where this leads

The past simple is the basic tense of stories. *Tense and Aspect* builds on it with the past continuous and past perfect ([[tense-and-aspect/narrative-tenses]]), *used to* and *would* for past habits ([[tense-and-aspect/past-habits]]), and the difference between the past simple and the present perfect ([[tense-and-aspect/present-perfect]]). The past forms of modal verbs (*could*, *had to*) appear in [[core-grammar/first-modals]].

::: summary
- The past simple has one form for all subjects (except *be*: *was*/*were*): regular verbs add *-ed*; irregular verbs have their own forms (*went*, *saw*, *took*) ([[#def-past-simple]]).
- Spelling: *-d* after *e*, consonant + *y* → *-ied*, double the final consonant after a single stressed vowel (*stopped*), BrE *travelled* / AmE *traveled* ([[#rule-ed-spelling]]).
- Pronunciation: /ɪd/ after /t/ and /d/, /t/ after other voiceless sounds, /d/ elsewhere ([[#rule-ed-sound]]).
- Questions and negatives use *did* + base form for every subject: *Did you go?* *I didn't go.* Never ✗ *Did you went* ([[#rule-did]]).
- Use the past simple for finished actions and states at a definite past time, past habits and sequences of events ([[#rule-past-uses]]); count back with *ago* (*three years ago*).
- Chinese 了 marks completion, not past time; in English every verb about the past takes a past form, even with *yesterday* in the sentence.
:::
