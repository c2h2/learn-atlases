Chinese has several ways to talk about the future — 会, 要, 将, 打算, 准备 — but often it needs none of them: 我明天去上海 is a complete, natural sentence, and the word 明天 does all the work. English cannot leave the verb unmarked like this. *I go to Shanghai tomorrow* is possible only in special cases; normally the speaker must choose a future form: *I'll go*, *I'm going to go*, or *I'm going*. And the choice is not free. Each form says something different about how the speaker sees the future event: as a prediction, a decision, a plan or an arrangement.

This lesson introduces the three most common ways of talking about the future at this level — *will*, *be going to* and the present continuous — and the present simple for timetables. A fuller picture, with the future continuous and future perfect, is given in [[tense-and-aspect/future-forms]].

## Three future forms

::: definition Future forms {#def-future}
English has no single future tense. Instead it uses several forms to talk about future time:

| form | example |
|---|---|
| ***will*** + base form | *It **will rain** tomorrow.* / *I**'ll help** you.* |
| ***be going to*** + base form | *I**'m going to study** medicine.* |
| **present continuous** (*be* + *-ing*) | *We**'re flying** to Kunming on Friday.* |
| **present simple** | *The train **leaves** at 6.40.* |
:::

Grammarians debate whether *will* forms a "future tense". Because *will* is a modal verb, like *can* and *must*, and because English has no future ending on the verb itself, many modern grammars (Huddleston & Pullum 2002) say that English has only two tenses, present and past, and several ways of referring to the future. For learners, what matters is not the label but the choice between the forms.

### *Will*

::: theorem Form and uses of *will* {#rule-will}
**Form:** *will* + base form, the same for all persons. Contracted: *'ll* (*I'll*, *she'll*, *it'll*). Negative: ***won't*** (= *will not*). Question: *Will you…?*

**Uses:**
1. **Predictions** — what the speaker thinks or believes will happen, often with *I think*, *probably*, *I'm sure*: *I think it**'ll be** sunny tomorrow.* *She**'ll** probably **pass**.*
2. **Facts about the future**: *I**'ll be** twenty next month.* *The sun **will set** at 6.15.*
3. **Decisions made at the moment of speaking**: *The phone's ringing. — I**'ll get** it.*
4. **Offers, promises, requests and refusals**: *I**'ll carry** that for you.* *I **won't tell** anyone, I promise.* ***Will** you **close** the door, please?* *The car **won't start**.*
:::

::: proof
The common feature is that *will* expresses the speaker's own view or will at the moment of speaking: a belief about what is going to happen, or a decision or promise made now. Old English *willan* meant ‘want’, and that sense still shows in offers, promises and refusals (*The car won't start* = it ‘refuses’). In British English, ***shall*** is used with *I* and *we* in questions that offer or suggest: ***Shall** I open the window?* ***Shall** we go?* American English prefers *Should I…?* or *Do you want me to…?* in these cases; *shall* for plain future (*I shall be late*) is now rare and formal, mostly British. *Will* and *shall* are covered further in [[modality/will-would-shall]].
:::

- *Don't worry about the exam — you'**ll** be fine.* (prediction)
- *I'm cold. — I'**ll** close the window.* (instant decision)
- *I'**ll** call you as soon as I land.* (promise)
- *I **won't** be home for dinner tonight.* (negative prediction)

### *Be going to*

::: theorem Form and uses of *be going to* {#rule-going-to}
**Form:** *am/is/are* + *going to* + base form: *I'm going to*, *she's going to*, *they're going to*. Negative: *I'm not going to*, *she isn't going to*. Question: *Are you going to…?*

**Uses:**
1. **Plans and intentions** — something already decided before the moment of speaking: *I**'m going to learn** to drive this summer.* *We**'re going to paint** the kitchen.*
2. **Predictions based on present evidence** — the speaker can see that something is about to happen: *Look at those clouds — it**'s going to rain**.* *Watch out! You**'re going to fall**!*
:::

::: proof
*Be going to* looks at the future from the present: there is something in the present — a decision already made, or a visible sign — that leads to the future event. That is why it is natural for plans (the decision exists now) and for predictions with evidence (the clouds are there now). In informal speech *going to* is often pronounced /ˈɡʌnə/ or /ˈɡənə/ and sometimes written *gonna*; *gonna* is fine in song lyrics and very informal messages but should not be used in ordinary writing. Note that *going to go* is normal: *I'm going to go to the cinema*, though many speakers shorten it to *I'm going to the cinema* (the present continuous).
:::

::: example *Will* or *going to*? {#ex-will-going}
Choose *will* or *be going to* and explain. (a) *I've bought some paint. I (paint) my bedroom this weekend.* (b) *A: We've run out of milk. B: Oh, I (get) some on the way home.* (c) *She's got her coat on. She (go) out.* (d) *I'm sure you (enjoy) the film.* (e) *A: The bag looks heavy. B: Don't worry, I (help) you.*
::: solution
(a) *I'm going to paint* — a plan; the paint shows the decision was already made.

(b) *I'll get* — a decision made now, as a reaction to the news.

(c) *She's going to go out* (or *She's going out*) — a prediction from evidence: the coat.

(d) *you'll enjoy* — a prediction based on opinion (*I'm sure*). *You're going to enjoy* is also possible.

(e) *I'll help* — an offer made at the moment of speaking.

In many predictions both forms are correct (*It will rain* / *It's going to rain*). The difference is clearest with decisions: *I'll* for a new decision, *going to* for an existing plan.
:::
:::

::: warning *Will* for plans already made
✗ *A: What are you doing this summer? B: I'll visit my grandparents in Xi'an.*

✓ *I'm going to visit my grandparents in Xi'an.* or *I'm visiting my grandparents in Xi'an.*

If the decision was made earlier, *will* sounds as if you are deciding now, as you speak. Chinese learners often use *will* for every future meaning because they map it onto 会 or 要. Reserve *will* for predictions, promises, offers and instant decisions.
:::

### The present continuous for arrangements

::: theorem The present continuous for the future {#rule-pc-future}
Use the **present continuous** for **personal arrangements**: plans that are fixed with other people or booked, usually with a time expression: *I**'m meeting** Li Na at six.* *We**'re flying** to Kunming on Friday.* *What **are** you **doing** this evening?*
:::

::: proof
The present continuous shows an event as already in progress in some sense — the arrangement is in place: the ticket is bought, the time is agreed, it is in the diary. The difference from *going to* is small: *I'm going to see the doctor tomorrow* (my intention) and *I'm seeing the doctor tomorrow* (I have an appointment) are both correct, and native speakers often use them interchangeably. The present continuous is less common for events the speaker cannot arrange (✗ *It's raining tomorrow* is wrong for a prediction). With a time expression, it is clearly future; without one, it is usually understood as now (*What are you doing?*). See [[core-grammar/present-continuous#rule-pc-uses]].
:::

::: example Arrangement, plan or timetable? {#ex-arrangements}
Choose the better form and explain. (a) *I (see / 'm seeing) the dentist at four — I made the appointment last week.* (b) *The museum (opens / is opening) at nine every day.* (c) *A: Any plans for the holiday? B: Not really. I (stay / 'm going to stay) at home and relax.* (d) *What time (does / is) your class (start / starting) tomorrow?* (e) *It (rains / 's going to rain) — I can hear thunder.*
::: solution
(a) *'m seeing* — a fixed appointment with another person: present continuous.

(b) *opens* — an official schedule: present simple.

(c) *'m going to stay* — an intention, not arranged with anyone. (*I'm staying at home* is also common.)

(d) *does … start* — a class follows a timetable: present simple. *What time is your class starting?* is possible in speech but less usual.

(e) *'s going to rain* — a prediction from present evidence; the present simple cannot be used for a prediction.
:::
:::

### The present simple for timetables

For events fixed by a **timetable** or **official schedule** — transport, films, classes, opening times — the present simple is used: *The train **leaves** at 6.40.* *The film **starts** at eight.* *Term **begins** on 2 September.* This use was introduced in [[core-grammar/present-simple#rule-ps-uses]]. The present simple is **not** used for personal plans: ✗ *I meet Li Na at six tonight* (✓ *I'm meeting*).

::: example Four forms in one conversation {#ex-dialogue-future}
Read the conversation and explain each future form.

**Mei:** *What are you doing on Saturday?*

**Tom:** *I'm meeting some friends for lunch. Then I'm going to look for a new phone.*

**Mei:** *The shopping centre opens at ten. I'll come with you if you like.*

**Tom:** *Great! But it's going to be very busy — it's a holiday weekend.*

**Mei:** *Don't worry. I'm sure we'll find what you need.*
::: solution
- *What are you doing on Saturday?* / *I'm meeting some friends* — present continuous: arrangements already made.
- *I'm going to look for a new phone* — *going to*: an intention, decided but not arranged with anyone.
- *The shopping centre opens at ten* — present simple: a timetable.
- *I'll come with you* — *will*: an offer, decided now.
- *it's going to be very busy* — *going to*: a prediction from present evidence (it's a holiday weekend).
- *we'll find what you need* — *will*: a prediction based on opinion (*I'm sure*).

Each form has a reason. In Chinese, most of these sentences could have no future marker at all, or 要 or 会.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which form is best? *Look! That glass is right at the edge of the table. It ___.*
- [ ] *falls*
- [ ] *will fall*
- [x] *is going to fall*
- [ ] *is falling tomorrow*
::: solution
There is present evidence (the glass is at the edge), so *going to* is best. *Will fall* is possible but less natural here; *falls* is the present simple, not used for this kind of prediction; *is falling tomorrow* makes no sense.
:::
:::

## Future time clauses

::: theorem No *will* after *when*, *if*, *before* and similar words {#rule-time-clause}
After ***when***, ***as soon as***, ***before***, ***after***, ***until*** and ***if***, use the **present simple** for a future event, not *will*: *I'll call you **when** I **get** home.* *We'll start **as soon as** everyone **arrives**.* ***If** it **rains**, we'll stay in.*
:::

::: proof
In these clauses, the future meaning is already clear from the main clause (*I'll call you*) and from the conjunction, so English uses the present tense. ✗ *I'll call you when I will get home* is a very common learner error. There are exceptions with *if* when *will* means ‘be willing’ (*If you'll wait a moment, I'll check* = if you are willing to wait), but these are polite formulas, not ordinary future. *When* and *if* clauses are treated fully in [[complex-sentences/adverbial-clauses]] and [[complex-sentences/conditionals]].
:::

::: warning *Will* in time clauses
✗ *When I will finish university, I will travel.* ✓ *When I finish university, I'll travel.*

✗ *Let's wait until the rain will stop.* ✓ *Let's wait until the rain stops.*

✗ *If you will need help, call me.* ✓ *If you need help, call me.*
:::

::: example Time clauses {#ex-time-clauses}
Complete with the correct form of the verbs. (a) *I (phone) you as soon as I (arrive).* (b) *We (not start) dinner until Dad (get) home.* (c) *If the weather (be) nice tomorrow, we (go) to the beach.* (d) *Before you (leave), please (turn) off the lights.* (e) *She (be) very happy when she (hear) the news.*
::: solution
(a) *I'll phone you as soon as I arrive.*

(b) *We won't start dinner until Dad gets home.*

(c) *If the weather is nice tomorrow, we'll go to the beach.*

(d) *Before you leave, please turn off the lights.* (an imperative in the main clause)

(e) *She'll be very happy when she hears the news.*

In each case, *will* goes in the main clause and the present simple goes after *as soon as*, *until*, *if*, *before* and *when*.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is correct?
- [ ] *I'll text you when I'll get to the station.*
- [x] *I'll text you when I get to the station.*
- [ ] *I text you when I will get to the station.*
- [ ] *I'm texting you when I get to the station.*
::: solution
*Will* in the main clause, present simple after *when*. The present continuous (*I'm texting*) would mean an arrangement and sounds odd for a promise.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **No marker needed in Chinese.** 我明天去上海 is complete. In English, *I go to Shanghai tomorrow* sounds like a timetable; use *I'm going to Shanghai tomorrow* (arrangement) or *I'm going to go* (plan).
- **会 and *will*.** 会 often expresses a prediction (明天会下雨 → *It'll rain tomorrow*), so it overlaps with *will*. But 会 also means ‘can’ (我会游泳 → *I can swim*), which *will* does not.
- **要 and *going to* / *will*.** 要 can mean ‘want’, ‘be about to’ or ‘will’: 我要去北京 may be *I'm going to Beijing* or *I want to go to Beijing*. 快要下雨了 → *It's going to rain* (soon) — the evidence meaning.
- **打算 and 准备 → *going to*.** 我打算学开车 → *I'm going to learn to drive.*
- **将 is formal.** 将 in written Chinese (会议将于明天举行) corresponds to formal *will* or the present simple for schedules (*The meeting will be held / is tomorrow*).
- **的时候 and time clauses.** 我到家的时候给你打电话 → *I'll call you when I get home*: as in Chinese, the time clause has no future marker. Chinese learners do not usually need to add *will* — but they sometimes do, by analogy with the main clause.
:::

::: history
English lost its old future ending long before it was written down; even Old English used the present tense for the future, much as Chinese uses its unmarked verb. *Will* and *shall* began as ordinary verbs meaning ‘want’ (*willan*) and ‘owe, must’ (*sculan*), and gradually came to express the future in Middle English. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, grammarians such as John Wallis (1653) set out a rule that *shall* should be used with *I* and *we* and *will* with other persons for plain future, with the reverse for determination. This rule never matched usage well, especially in Scotland, Ireland and America, and modern grammars describe *will* as the normal form for all persons. *Be going to* developed from the literal meaning of going somewhere in order to do something (*I am going to buy bread* — walking to the shop) and was established as a future form by the seventeenth century; in recent decades it has become steadily more frequent in speech.
:::

## Where this leads

The future continuous (*I'll be working*), future perfect (*I'll have finished*), *be about to* and the full range of future meanings are explained in [[tense-and-aspect/future-forms]]. *Will*, *would* and *shall* as modal verbs are compared in [[modality/will-would-shall]], and *if* sentences about the future in [[complex-sentences/conditionals]].

::: summary
- English has no single future tense; it uses *will*, *be going to*, the present continuous and the present simple ([[#def-future]]).
- *Will*: predictions based on opinion, future facts, decisions made while speaking, offers and promises; negative *won't*; BrE *Shall I…?* for offers ([[#rule-will]]).
- *Be going to*: plans already decided, and predictions from present evidence ([[#rule-going-to]]).
- Present continuous: fixed personal arrangements, usually with a time (*I'm meeting her at six*) ([[#rule-pc-future]]).
- Present simple: timetables (*The train leaves at 6.40*).
- After *when*, *if*, *as soon as*, *before*, *after*, *until*, use the present simple, not *will* ([[#rule-time-clause]]).
- Chinese often marks the future only with a time word; English needs a future form, and the choice depends on meaning.
:::
