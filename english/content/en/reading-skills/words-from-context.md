Read this sentence:

> *After walking for twelve hours in the rain, the travellers were weary, and they wanted nothing more than a hot meal and a bed.*

Perhaps you do not know the word *weary*. But you can probably say what it means. People who have walked for twelve hours in the rain and want only food and a bed must be very tired. You worked out the meaning without a dictionary, from the words around it — its **context**.

Every reader meets unknown words. Research on coverage suggests that comfortable reading needs about 98% of the words in a text to be known (Hu & Nation 2000; see [[core-vocabulary/frequency-and-coverage]]). Even then, one word in fifty is unknown: about one word every four or five lines. If you stop and look up every one, reading becomes slow and you lose the thread of the text. If you skip every one, you may miss something important. This chapter shows how to decide which unknown words matter, how to work out their meaning from clues in the text, and when a dictionary is the better choice.

## First question: do I need this word?

Not every unknown word is worth your time. Before you try to guess, ask whether the word matters for your purpose ([[reading-skills/reading-with-a-purpose]]).

> *On the table there was a bowl of fruit: apples, oranges and a few kumquats.*

If you are reading a story, you do not need to know exactly what a *kumquat* is. The sentence tells you it is a kind of fruit, and the story will not change. You can read on.

::: theorem Decide first whether the word matters {#rule-matters}
When you meet an unknown word, first ask: **Can I understand the sentence and follow the text without it?**

1. If **yes** — the word is in a list, it is a detail, or the sentence makes sense without it — **read on**. If it appears again, you can think about it then.
2. If **no** — the word is in the title or a key sentence, it is repeated, or the sentence does not make sense without it — **work out its meaning** from the context, and look it up if you still need to.
:::

::: proof
Readers have limited attention. Every time you stop, you must hold the rest of the sentence in memory, and the longer the pause, the more of the text you lose. Nation (2009) advises learners to deal with an unknown word only when it matters for understanding, and to save careful study for words that are useful and frequent. A word that is important for the text is usually repeated or placed in a prominent position, so the first meeting is not the last chance.
:::

## Context clues

::: definition Context clue {#def-context-clue}
A **context clue** is information near an unknown word that helps you work out its meaning. Clues can be in the same sentence, in the sentences before and after, in the word itself (its parts), or in what you already know about the situation.
:::

The most useful kinds of clue are shown in the table. Many of them are signalled by small, frequent words that you already know.

| kind of clue | what it does | typical signal words |
|---|---|---|
| definition or restatement | the writer explains the word | *that is*, *in other words*, *or*, *means*, *is called*, commas, dashes, brackets |
| example | the writer gives examples of it | *for example*, *such as*, *including*, *like* |
| contrast | the writer gives its opposite | *but*, *however*, *although*, *unlike*, *instead of*, *not … but* |
| similarity | the writer gives a similar word | *like*, *also*, *similarly*, *and* |
| cause and effect | it causes something, or something causes it | *because*, *so*, *as a result*, *therefore* |
| the situation | general knowledge about what usually happens | none — you use what you know |

### Definition and restatement

Writers often explain a difficult word themselves, especially in texts for students and the general public.

- *pottery*: *The town is famous for its pottery — cups, plates and bowls made from clay.* (the dash introduces an explanation)
- *nocturnal*: *Bats are nocturnal, which means that they sleep during the day and are active at night.*
- *insomnia*: *The doctor said I had insomnia, or difficulty in sleeping.*

### Examples

- *citrus*: *Many citrus fruits, such as lemons, oranges and limes, are rich in vitamin C.* — *citrus* fruits are a group that includes lemons, oranges and limes.
- *percussion*: *She plays several percussion instruments, including drums and bells.*

### Contrast

- *talkative*: *My brother is very talkative, but my sister hardly says a word.* — the opposite of hardly saying a word: talking a lot.
- *reckless*: *Unlike his reckless older brother, Tom always drives carefully.* — the opposite of driving carefully: not careful about danger.

### Similarity

- *filthy*: *The first hotel was filthy, and the second one was just as dirty.* — *just as dirty* tells us that *filthy* means very dirty.

### Cause and effect

- *slippery*: *The road was slippery because of the ice, so we walked very slowly.* — ice causes it, and it makes people walk slowly: easy to fall on.

### The situation

- *bill*: *The waiter brought the bill, and Wang Lei paid with his card.* — what does a waiter bring that you pay? (In American English this is usually *the check*.)

::: example Using a contrast clue {#ex-contrast}
Work out the meaning of *frugal*: *Her parents were frugal and saved every yuan they could, but she spent her money as soon as she got it.*
::: solution
**Word class.** *Her parents were frugal* — after *were*, describing the parents: an **adjective**.

**Clues in the sentence.** *and saved every yuan they could* is a similarity clue: being frugal goes together with saving money. *but she spent her money as soon as she got it* is a contrast clue: the daughter is the opposite of her parents.

**Guess.** *frugal* = ‘careful with money, spending very little’.

**Check.** Put the guess in the sentence: *Her parents were careful with money and saved every yuan they could, but she spent her money as soon as she got it.* It makes sense.
:::
:::

::: quiz
What does *arid* probably mean? *The north of the country is green and wet, but the south is arid, and almost nothing grows there.*
- [ ] cold
- [ ] beautiful
- [x] very dry
- [ ] crowded
::: solution
*But* signals a contrast with *green and wet*, and *almost nothing grows there* is an effect. The opposite of wet, a place where plants cannot grow: very dry. *Cold* is possible from *nothing grows*, but it is not the opposite of *wet*.
:::
:::

## The word itself: word class and word parts

Two clues are inside the word: its grammar and its parts.

**Word class.** The position of a word and its ending tell you whether it is a noun, a verb, an adjective or an adverb ([[core-grammar/word-classes]]). This alone narrows the meaning. Even a made-up word has a word class:

- *glarb*: *The glarb was so heavy that two men had to carry it.* — after *the*, before *was*: a noun, a heavy thing.
- *glarbed*: *She glarbed the letter and threw it in the bin.* — after a subject, with *-ed* and an object: a verb, something you can do to a letter (tear it? screw it up?).
- *glarby*: *It was a very glarby day.* — after *very*, before a noun: an adjective.

**Word parts.** Many English words are built from parts you may already know ([[word-formation/morphemes]]):

| part | meaning | example |
|---|---|---|
| *un-*, *in-*, *im-*, *dis-* | not, the opposite | *unfriendly*, *incorrect*, *impossible*, *dishonest* |
| *re-* | again | *rewrite*, *rebuild* |
| *mis-* | wrongly | *misunderstand*, *misspell* |
| *pre-* | before | *preview*, *prehistoric* |
| *-less* | without | *homeless*, *careless* |
| *-ful* | full of, having | *helpful*, *painful* |
| *-able*, *-ible* | can be done | *washable*, *visible* |
| *-er*, *-or* | a person or thing that does | *teacher*, *heater*, *visitor* |

So *unbreakable* = *un-* (not) + *break* + *-able* (can be): ‘cannot be broken’. *Reheat* = heat again.

::: example Word class and word parts together {#ex-word-parts}
Work out *unaffordable*: *After the factory closed, many families moved away, because houses in the city had become unaffordable for people on low wages.*
::: solution
**Word class.** *had become unaffordable* — after *become*, describing *houses*: an **adjective**.

**Word parts.** *un-* (not) + *afford* + *-able* (can be). You may know *afford*: *I can't afford a new phone* — ‘have enough money for’. So *unaffordable* = ‘that people cannot pay for; too expensive’.

**Context check.** *for people on low wages* and *many families moved away* fit this meaning: the houses were too expensive for poor people, so they left.

**Meaning:** too expensive for people to buy or rent.
:::
:::

::: warning Word parts that mislead
Word parts are a good clue, but they can deceive you. Laufer (1997) called these words "deceptively transparent": they look as if their parts give the meaning, but they do not.

- *invaluable* does not mean ‘without value’; it means ‘extremely valuable’: *Your help has been invaluable.*
- *priceless* does not mean ‘with no price, cheap’; it means ‘worth a very large amount’: *a priceless painting*.
- *infamous* does not mean ‘not famous’; it means ‘famous for something bad’: *an infamous prison*.
- *nevertheless* has nothing to do with *never* + *less*; it means ‘in spite of that’ ([[reading-skills/following-the-thread]]).
- *outline* is not ‘a line outside’ in most texts; it usually means ‘a short description of the main points’.

Always check a guess from word parts against the sentence. If the guess does not fit, the parts have misled you.
:::

## A step-by-step strategy

Clarke and Nation (1980) described a simple procedure for guessing, which is still widely taught. Here is a version of it.

::: theorem Steps for working out an unknown word {#rule-steps}
1. **Find the word class.** Is it a noun, a verb, an adjective or an adverb?
2. **Look at the clause.** What does it do, or what is done to it? What describes it, or what does it describe?
3. **Look at the wider context.** Look at the sentences before and after, and at signal words (*but*, *because*, *for example*, *that is*) that link them.
4. **Guess** a meaning, in English or Chinese — a general meaning is enough.
5. **Check** the guess: (a) is it the same word class? (b) does it make sense if you put it in place of the word? (c) do the word parts agree? If not, go back to step 2.
:::

::: proof
Steps 1–3 move from the word outwards, so that the guess uses all the clues, not just the first one that comes to mind. Step 5 is the most important: learners often make a guess and keep it even when the next sentence shows it is wrong. Substituting the guess into the sentence is a quick test that catches many errors. The procedure does not promise success. Guessing is easier when most other words are known (Nation 2013), when the clues are close to the word, and when the reader knows the topic; a guess is often only partly right, but a partly right meaning is often enough to keep reading.
:::

::: example The full strategy in a short text {#ex-full-strategy}
Work out *scarce*:

> *In the dry season, water becomes scarce in the villages. Women sometimes walk for two hours to find a well that still has water, and every drop is used carefully.*
::: solution
**Do I need it?** Yes: it is in the first sentence, which gives the main point.

**Step 1 — word class.** *water becomes scarce*: after *becomes*, describing *water*, so an **adjective**.

**Step 2 — the clause.** *In the dry season, water becomes scarce.* Something happens to water in the dry season. We know that in a dry season there is little rain.

**Step 3 — wider context.** The next sentence gives effects: women *walk for two hours to find a well that still has water*, and *every drop is used carefully*. People must go far to find water and use it carefully: there is not much of it.

**Step 4 — guess.** *scarce* = ‘there is not enough of it; hard to find’ (缺少的).

**Step 5 — check.** (a) Adjective: yes. (b) *In the dry season, water becomes hard to find in the villages.* It makes sense. (c) No word parts to check. The guess fits.
:::
:::

::: example When the first guess is wrong {#ex-wrong-guess}
A reader meets *fine* in this text and thinks it means ‘good’:

> *Mr Zhou parked his car outside the hospital for only ten minutes, but when he came back there was a fine of 200 yuan on the window.*

Use step 5 to check the guess.
::: solution
**First guess:** *fine* = ‘good, OK’ (the meaning the reader knows, as in *I'm fine*).

**Check (a) word class.** *there was a fine of 200 yuan*: after *a*, followed by *of*: a **noun**. But *fine* ‘good’ is an adjective. The guess fails the first test.

**Back to the clause.** *A fine of 200 yuan* on the car window after parking outside a hospital. Something with an amount of money, left on a car that was parked where it should not be.

**New guess.** *fine* (noun) = ‘money you must pay as a punishment’ (罚款).

**Check.** Noun: yes. *there was a punishment of 200 yuan on the window* makes sense. *But* in the first half also fits: he parked *for only ten minutes, but* he still got the punishment.

This is a common situation: the word is not new, but this **meaning** is new. Many frequent words have several meanings, and the context tells you which one is meant ([[core-vocabulary/using-a-dictionary]]).
:::
:::

::: quiz
In *She was so exhausted after the race that she fell asleep on the bus*, which step gives the most help with *exhausted*?
- [ ] the word parts *ex-* and *-ed*
- [x] the effect: *she fell asleep on the bus*
- [ ] a definition after a dash
- [ ] an example after *such as*
::: solution
The structure *so … that* shows cause and effect: she was so exhausted that she fell asleep. After a race, falling asleep suggests ‘very tired’. The word parts do not help much here, and there is no definition or example in the sentence.
:::
:::

## When to use a dictionary

Guessing is a skill, not a rule against dictionaries. A good reader guesses most of the time and looks up some words.

::: theorem When to look a word up {#rule-look-up}
Look a word up when:

1. it is **important** for the text (in the title, in a key sentence, or repeated several times) and the context does not give a clear meaning;
2. your guess **does not fit** when you check it, and you cannot find a better one;
3. you need the **exact** meaning — in a contract, instructions, a medical or legal text, or a question you must answer;
4. you want to **learn** the word, because it is frequent or useful to you.

When you look a word up, read the context first and choose the dictionary meaning that fits it.
:::

::: proof
Guessing gives a meaning quickly but often only roughly, and sometimes wrongly. Dictionaries are exact but slow. The rule uses each where it is strongest. Looking up words that are important or that you want to learn also helps memory: a word that is noticed, checked and met again is more likely to be remembered (Nation 2013). A word met only once in a story, guessed roughly, is usually soon forgotten, and that is fine, because if it is frequent you will meet it again. How to read a dictionary entry and find the right sense is the subject of [[core-vocabulary/using-a-dictionary]].
:::

::: example To guess or to look up? {#ex-guess-or-look}
Liu Yang is reading a short story for pleasure and meets three unknown words. What should she do with each? (a) *patter*: *She heard the gentle patter of rain on the roof.* (b) *Lighthouse*: *The story is called "The Lighthouse Keeper", and the word appears on almost every page.* (c) *drowsiness*: *A note on the medicine box says: "Do not take on an empty stomach. May cause drowsiness."*
::: solution
(a) *patter*: the sentence already says it is a sound that rain makes on a roof. That is enough for a story. **Read on.**

(b) *lighthouse*: it is in the title and on every page, so it is important. Word parts help: *light* + *house*, a building with a light. The story may make it clear (by the sea, a light for ships). **Guess, and look it up** if the guess is still unclear after a page; it is worth learning.

(c) *drowsiness*: this is not part of the story — it is about her health, and she needs the exact meaning. **Look it up**: ‘feeling sleepy’ (so she should not drive after taking it).
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Characters and word parts.** Chinese readers already use a kind of word-part clue: a character with the 氵 radical is often connected with water (河, 湖, 洗), and many Chinese words are compounds whose parts show the meaning (电脑, 火车). English word parts work in a similar way, but less reliably: many parts come from Latin or Greek (*bene-* ‘good’, *chron-* ‘time’) and are not obvious, and some words mislead (see the warning above). Use word parts as one clue among several, and always check.
- **A rough meaning is enough.** Many learners feel they must find the exact Chinese word for every English word. When you guess from context, a general meaning — ‘some kind of fruit’, ‘a sound’, ‘very tired’ — is often all you need to keep reading.
- **Signal words matter more in English.** Chinese often leaves the link between two clauses unsaid, and the reader understands it from the meaning. English writers more often state it with *but*, *because*, *for example* or *that is*, and these words are some of the best clues to a new word. Notice them.
- **Known words with new meanings.** Many guessing problems come from a familiar word used in an unfamiliar meaning (*fine* ‘money paid as a punishment’, *bill* ‘a list of what you must pay’). If a word you know does not fit the sentence, suspect a second meaning.
:::

::: history
The idea that readers can work out meaning from context is old, but its place in teaching has changed. In 1967 Kenneth Goodman described reading as "a psycholinguistic guessing game", in which readers predict what comes next and check only some of the print. Later research changed this picture. Keith Stanovich (1980) found that it was weaker readers, not skilled ones, who relied most on context to recognise words; skilled readers recognise familiar words quickly and accurately from the print itself. Eye-movement studies agreed: skilled readers look at almost every word ([[reading-skills/reading-with-a-purpose]]). So guessing from context is not a way to avoid reading the words carefully. It is a tool for the words you genuinely do not know. For that purpose, Clarke and Nation (1980) proposed the step-by-step procedure in this chapter, and Laufer (1997) showed how learners are misled by words that only look familiar.
:::

## Where this leads

How many words a reader needs, and why unknown words are unavoidable, is covered in [[core-vocabulary/frequency-and-coverage]]. Word parts are studied in depth in [[word-formation/morphemes]] and the following chapters of *Word Formation*. The signal words used as clues here — *however*, *because*, *for example* — are the subject of [[reading-skills/following-the-thread]]. Reading large amounts of easy text, where most words can be guessed or skipped and words are met many times, is the subject of [[reading-skills/extensive-reading]].

::: summary
- Unknown words are normal: even at 98% coverage, one word in fifty is new.
- First decide whether the word **matters**; if you can follow the text without it, read on ([[#rule-matters]]).
- Use **context clues**: definitions and restatements, examples, contrasts, similarities, causes and effects, and the situation; small signal words point to them.
- Use the word's **class** and its **parts**, but check: some words, such as *invaluable* and *priceless*, mislead.
- Follow the steps: word class → clause → wider context → guess → **check** by substituting the guess ([[#rule-steps]]).
- Look a word up when it is important and unclear, when your guess fails, when you need the exact meaning, or when you want to learn it ([[#rule-look-up]]).
:::

What kind of clue (definition, example, contrast, similarity, cause and effect) helps with the word given before each sentence? (a) *reptiles*: *Some reptiles, such as snakes and lizards, live in the desert.* (b) *tedious*: *The film was so tedious that half the audience fell asleep.* (c) *frail*: *The old man was frail, but his wife was still strong and active.* (d) *cartographer*: *He works as a cartographer — a person who draws maps.* (e) *spotless*: *The room was spotless; everything was perfectly clean.*

(a) noun (after *a new*); (b) verb (after the subject, with *-ed*); (c) adjective (after *too*, with the ending *-y*, describing *the soup*); (d) adverb (with *-ly*, describing how she answered).

(a) write again; (b) read wrongly; (c) without power; (d) that cannot be believed (often used to mean ‘very surprising’); (e) heat before (*Preheat the oven*); (f) not honest.

::: exercise Work out the meaning {level=2}
Use the context to work out *remote*, *blocked*, *isolated* and *migrated*. Say which clues you used.

> *The village was very remote: the nearest town was three hours away by car, and there was no bus. In winter the road was often blocked by snow, and the villagers could be isolated for weeks, with no visitors and no way to get out. Most young people had migrated to the cities to find work, so the village was quiet.*
::: solution
*remote*: ‘far away from other places’ — the colon introduces an explanation: three hours from the nearest town, no bus.

*blocked*: ‘closed so that nothing can get through’ — cause (snow) and situation (a road in winter).

*isolated*: ‘cut off, alone, without contact’ — restatement after the comma: *with no visitors and no way to get out*.

*migrated*: ‘moved to live in another place’ — *to the cities to find work*, and the effect: *so the village was quiet*.
:::

::: exercise Read on or look it up? {level=2}
Lin Tao is reading an online article about saving money for his own interest. For each word, say whether he should read on, guess, or look it up, and why. (a) *deposited*: *a sum of money deposited in a bank account* (the article is about bank accounts, and the word appears five times) (b) *brioche*: *a café selling croissants and brioche* (c) *interest*: *the interest rate* (he knows *interest* only as ‘wanting to know about something’)
::: solution
(a) **Guess, then look it up**: it is repeated and central to the topic. The context suggests ‘put into’ (money put into a bank account); a dictionary will confirm it, and it is worth learning.

(b) **Read on**: it is in a list after *croissants* and is clearly something to eat; it does not matter for an article about saving money.

(c) **Look it up**: this is a known word with a new meaning, the topic depends on it, and the exact meaning matters (‘money paid by a bank for keeping your money, as a percentage’).
:::

::: exercise Misleading words {level=2}
Each sentence uses a word whose parts may mislead. Give the real meaning. (a) *The old map in the museum is priceless.* (b) *Your advice was invaluable.* (c) *He is an infamous criminal.* (d) *It was raining; nevertheless, we went for a walk.*
::: solution
(a) *priceless*: ‘worth so much money that no price can be given’, not ‘cheap’. (b) *invaluable*: ‘extremely useful’, not ‘without value’. (c) *infamous*: ‘famous for being bad’, not ‘unknown’. (d) *nevertheless*: ‘in spite of that’ (we went for a walk although it was raining).

::: exercise The full strategy {level=3}
Use all five steps of [[#rule-steps]] to work out *reluctant*, and write down each step.

> *Mei had never spoken in public before, and she was reluctant to give the speech. She told her teacher she was ill, she asked her friend to do it instead, and in the end she agreed only because her teacher promised to stand next to her.*
::: hint
Look at what Mei does in the second sentence. Do her actions show that she wants to give the speech?
:::
::: solution
**Do I need it?** Yes: it describes Mei's feelings, which the rest of the text explains.

**Step 1.** *she was reluctant to give the speech*: after *was*, describing Mei, followed by *to* + verb: an **adjective**.

**Step 2.** *She was reluctant to give the speech*: it is a feeling about doing something. The first half says she *had never spoken in public before*.

**Step 3.** The next sentence gives her actions: she said she was ill, she asked her friend to do it, and she agreed *only because* her teacher promised to help. These are things people do when they do not want to do something.

**Step 4.** *reluctant* = ‘not wanting to do something; unwilling’ (不情愿的).

**Step 5.** Adjective: yes. *She was unwilling to give the speech* makes sense and fits all the actions. No word parts to check.
:::

::: exercise Write your own clues {level=3}
Write one sentence for each word that would let a reader who does not know the word work out its meaning. Use a different kind of clue each time (definition, example, contrast, cause and effect). Words: *vegetarian*, *furious*, *ancient*, *fragile*.
::: solution
Models (many answers are possible):

- definition: *My cousin is a vegetarian, which means she does not eat meat or fish.*
- cause and effect: *He was furious when he saw his broken car, and he shouted at everyone in the street.*
- contrast: *The town has some modern buildings, but most of them are ancient, built more than a thousand years ago.* (contrast plus restatement)
- example or situation: *Please be careful with that box — it contains fragile things such as glasses and plates that break easily.*

A good answer uses a clear signal word (*which means*, *when*, *but*, *such as*) and a clue close to the word.
