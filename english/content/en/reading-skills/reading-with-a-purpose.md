Zhang Min is at a railway station. On the big screen there are forty trains, but she does not read forty lines. Her eyes go straight down the list until they find *Hangzhou*, and then across to the time and the platform. It takes her five seconds. That evening she gets a long email from a friend who has had some bad news. She reads it slowly, twice, and thinks about every sentence.

Zhang Min is the same reader in both places, but she reads in two very different ways, and both are right. In her own language she does this without thinking. In English, many learners lose this freedom: they read every text in the same way, slowly, from the first word to the last, and stop at every word they do not know. This chapter shows that good reading starts with a question — **why am I reading this?** — and that the answer decides how you read.

## Reasons for reading

::: definition Reading purpose {#def-purpose}
Your **reading purpose** is what you want to get from a text. The most common purposes are:

1. to **find one piece of information** (a time, a price, a name, an answer to a question);
2. to **get the general idea** (what the text is about and what its main point is);
3. to **understand fully** (every important detail, for example to follow instructions or to study);
4. to **enjoy** a story or learn about a subject, at a comfortable speed.
:::

The purpose usually comes from outside the text. Think about these situations:

- You open a train timetable. *When does the last train leave?* You need one fact.
- Your teacher gives you a newspaper article and asks: *Is this article for or against the new airport?* You need the general idea.
- You get a letter from your bank that says your card will stop working. You need every detail.
- You take a novel on holiday. You want to enjoy the story.

The same text can be read for different purposes. A recipe is scanned in the shop (*Do I need eggs?*) and read closely in the kitchen (*How long in the oven, and at what temperature?*).

## Three ways of reading

There are three main ways of reading a text. Each one fits a different purpose.

::: definition Skimming {#def-skimming}
**Skimming** (略读) is reading quickly to get the **general idea** (the **gist**) of a text. You do not read every word. You look at the parts that carry the most meaning: the title, the headings, the first paragraph, the first sentence of each paragraph, the last paragraph, and any pictures, tables or words in bold.
:::

::: definition Scanning {#def-scanning}
**Scanning** (寻读) is looking quickly through a text to **find a particular piece of information**. You know what you are looking for before you start — a number, a name, a date, a key word — and your eyes move over the text until they find it. You ignore everything else.
:::

::: definition Close reading {#def-close-reading}
**Close reading** (细读) is reading slowly and carefully to **understand everything that matters**: the details, how the ideas are connected, and the exact meaning of important sentences. You may read some parts twice and stop to think or check a word.
:::

Skimming and scanning are both fast, but they are different. When you skim, you want the big picture and you do not know exactly what you will find. When you scan, you know exactly what you want and you do not care about the rest. In everyday life, most reading is a mix: you skim a web page, scan it for the part you need, and read that part closely.

### Skimming in practice

Here is a short article written for this lesson. Before you read it all, try to skim it in 30 seconds: read the title, the first paragraph, the first sentence of each other paragraph and the last paragraph.

> **Trees in the City**
>
> *Many cities around the world are planting more trees in their streets and parks. There are several good reasons for this, but there are also some problems.*
>
> *First, trees make streets cooler. Their leaves give shade, and they release water into the air, so a street with tall trees can feel much cooler on a hot summer day.*
>
> *Second, trees are good for people. Many people feel calmer in green places, and they are more likely to walk in a street that is pleasant and shady.*
>
> *Third, trees help when there is heavy rain. Their roots take in water, so less water runs over the ground and into the drains.*
>
> *However, trees in cities also cause problems. Roots can break pavements, and falling leaves can block drains in autumn. Trees also need water and care, and this costs money.*
>
> *Most city planners believe that the benefits of trees are greater than the costs. But they agree that a city must choose the right tree for the right place.*

::: example Skimming for the gist {#ex-skim-trees}
Skim *Trees in the City*. What is the article about, and what is the writer's main point?
::: solution
**Step 1: the title.** *Trees in the City* tells us the topic: trees in towns and cities.

**Step 2: the first paragraph.** *Many cities … are planting more trees … There are several good reasons for this, but there are also some problems.* This tells us the plan of the whole article: first reasons, then problems.

**Step 3: the first sentence of each middle paragraph.** *First, trees make streets cooler.* *Second, trees are good for people.* *Third, trees help when there is heavy rain.* *However, trees in cities also cause problems.* The words *First*, *Second*, *Third* and *However* show the structure: three benefits and then the problems.

**Step 4: the last paragraph.** *Most city planners believe that the benefits of trees are greater than the costs.* This is the conclusion.

**Gist:** The article explains why cities are planting more trees (they cool streets, help people and reduce flooding), mentions some problems, and concludes that the benefits are greater than the costs if the right trees are chosen. We found this without reading most of the words.
:::
:::

Skimming works because most writers put the important information in predictable places. In English texts that explain or argue, the main point of a paragraph is very often in its first sentence. This is not always true (see [[reading-skills/main-ideas]]), but it is true often enough to make skimming useful.

::: quiz
You skimmed *Trees in the City*. Which sentence gives the best summary of the gist?
- [ ] Tree roots break pavements, so cities should plant fewer trees.
- [x] Trees bring cities several benefits and some problems, and the benefits are usually greater.
- [ ] Trees release water into the air on hot days.
- [ ] City planners do not agree about trees.
::: solution
The second option includes the topic (trees in cities), the structure (benefits and problems) and the conclusion. The first option is the opposite of the conclusion. The third is one detail from one paragraph. The fourth is wrong: the last paragraph says that most planners agree.
:::
:::

### Scanning in practice

When you scan, you need to know **what the information will look like**. A time looks like *10:30* or *half past ten*. A price has a currency sign. A name starts with a capital letter. A date has a month name or numbers with slashes. If you know the shape you want, your eyes can jump to it.

> **Riverside Library — opening hours**
>
> | day | opening hours |
> |---|---|
> | Monday | closed |
> | Tuesday to Friday | 9:00 – 20:00 |
> | Saturday | 10:00 – 17:00 |
> | Sunday | 13:00 – 17:00 |
>
> *Story time for children (ages 3–7): every Saturday at 11:00 in the Children's Room. Free, no booking needed.*
>
> *Computers can be booked for one hour at a time at the front desk.*

::: example Scanning for details {#ex-scan-library}
Scan the library notice to answer: (a) Can you visit the library on Monday? (b) What time does it open on Sunday? (c) How long can you use a computer? (d) Does a child aged 5 need a ticket for story time?
::: solution
For each question, decide first what kind of word or number you are looking for, then find it.

(a) Look for the word *Monday*: it says *closed*. **No.**

(b) Look for *Sunday*, then the first time on that line: **13:00** (1 p.m.).

(c) Look for *computer*, then a length of time near it: *one hour at a time*. **One hour** (you can book again).

(d) Look for *story time*, then the words about cost or booking: *Free, no booking needed*. The age range *3–7* includes 5. **No ticket is needed.**

We did not read the notice from top to bottom. For each question we looked for a key word (*Monday*, *Sunday*, *computer*, *story time*) and read only the words next to it.
:::
:::

::: warning Scanning for the exact words of the question
Scanning for key words works well for names, numbers and dates, but the text does not always use the same words as the question. If you are asked *Is the library open on weekends?*, the notice does not contain the word *weekend*; you must look for *Saturday* and *Sunday*. If you are asked *How much does story time cost?*, the answer is the word *Free*, not a number. When the first scan fails, think of other words with the same meaning (*cost* → *price*, *free*, *£*, *¥*) and scan again.
:::

### Close reading in practice

Some texts must be read closely, because a small word changes what you must do. Here is a notice in a hostel kitchen.

> **Kitchen rules**
>
> *Guests may use the kitchen between 7 a.m. and 10 p.m. Please wash, dry and put away everything you use. Label your food with your name and the date you will leave. Food without a label, and food left after the date on the label, will be thrown away when the fridge is cleaned every Friday morning.*

::: example Reading closely {#ex-close-kitchen}
Li Na arrives on Monday and will leave on Thursday. On Wednesday she puts some milk in the fridge but forgets to label it. On Thursday morning she leaves and forgets her yoghurt, which has a label with *Li Na — Thursday*. What happens to the milk and the yoghurt, and when?
::: solution
Here we need every detail, so we read every word of the last two sentences.

**The rule has two parts.** Food is thrown away if (1) it has no label, *or* (2) it is still there after the date on the label. The word *and* in *Food without a label, and food left after the date* joins the two groups of food that will be thrown away.

**When?** *when the fridge is cleaned every Friday morning*. Nothing is thrown away before Friday morning.

**The milk** has no label, so it belongs to group (1). It will be thrown away on **Friday morning** — Li Na could still have used it on Wednesday and Thursday, but she has left by then.

**The yoghurt** has a label with Thursday. After Thursday it belongs to group (2), so it will also be thrown away on **Friday morning**.

A reader who skimmed the notice would remember "label your food" but might miss *every Friday morning* and *after the date on the label*, the two details that answer the question.
:::
:::

## Choosing how to read

::: theorem Let the purpose choose the way of reading {#rule-choose}
Before you read, ask yourself what you need from the text, and choose:

1. **one fact** → **scan** for its key word or its shape (a number, a name, a date);
2. **the general idea** → **skim** the title, headings, first and last paragraphs and first sentences;
3. **every important detail** → **read closely**, usually after skimming first;
4. **a story or a subject you enjoy** → read **fluently** at a comfortable speed and do not stop for every unknown word.

You can change your way of reading in the middle of a text when your purpose changes.
:::

::: proof
Reading is limited by time and attention. A reader who reads every text closely spends a long time on parts that do not matter for the task and may finish without the answer. A reader who only skims misses details that matter. Research on skilled readers shows that they change their speed and attention to fit the task; Carver (1990) called these different ways of reading "gears", like the gears of a bicycle. Teachers of second-language reading, such as Nuttall (2005) and Nation (2009), make the same point: learners often read everything slowly and closely, and they gain a lot when they learn to choose. The rule is a guide, not a law: some tasks need two ways of reading, one after the other.
:::

::: example Choosing for four tasks {#ex-choose-four}
Which way of reading fits each task? (a) Checking the closing date for a job application on a company web page. (b) Deciding in two minutes which of five articles is about learning languages. (c) Following the instructions to set up a new printer. (d) Reading a detective story on the train.
::: solution
Ask *what do I need?* for each task, then use [[#rule-choose]].

(a) One fact: a date. **Scan** for the words *closing date* or *deadline*, or for a month name.

(b) The general idea of five texts, with little time. **Skim** each article: the title, the first paragraph, perhaps the headings.

(c) Every detail, in the right order: a mistake could mean the printer does not work. **Skim first** to see how many steps there are and what you need, **then read closely**, one step at a time.

(d) Enjoyment. **Read fluently**: follow the story, do not stop for every unknown word, and look up a word only if you cannot follow the story without it.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Wang Jun has a 20-page report. His manager asks: *Does the report recommend opening a new office in Chengdu?* What is the best way to start?
- [ ] Read the whole report closely from page 1.
- [x] Scan the contents, summary and conclusion for *Chengdu* and *recommend*.
- [ ] Read the report fluently for pleasure.
- [ ] Look up every unknown word in the first paragraph.
::: solution
He needs one answer (yes or no), and reports usually put their recommendations in the summary and the conclusion. Scanning those parts for the key words *Chengdu* and *recommend* (or *recommendation*) will probably find it in a minute or two. If the answer is not clear, he can then read the relevant section closely.
:::

## What the eyes do

When we read, it feels as if our eyes move smoothly along the line. They do not. Laboratory studies with eye-tracking cameras show three kinds of movement (Rayner 1998):

- **fixations**: the eyes stop on a word. In silent reading a fixation usually lasts about a quarter of a second (roughly 200–250 milliseconds). We take in information only during fixations;
- **saccades**: quick jumps from one fixation to the next, usually about 7–9 letters forward. During a saccade we see almost nothing;
- **regressions**: jumps back to an earlier part of the text. In skilled readers about 10–15% of saccades go backwards, often because something was not understood.

Skilled readers fixate most content words and skip many short, predictable words such as *the* and *of*. They look at almost every word, but they do it quickly.

::: remark Reading speed
How fast do people read? A large review of 190 studies by Brysbaert (2019) estimated that adults reading English silently as their first language read on average about **238 words per minute** for non-fiction and about **260 words per minute** for fiction. Carver (1990) described different speeds for skilled adult readers depending on the purpose: roughly 600 words per minute for scanning, 450 for skimming, 300 for normal reading, 200 for reading in order to learn, and about 140 for memorising. These figures are for first-language readers; second-language readers are usually slower, and that is normal.
:::

::: warning Believing in "speed reading"
Some courses promise that you can read 1,000 words per minute or more with full understanding. A review of the research by Rayner, Schotter, Masson, Potter and Treiman (2016) found no good evidence for this. There is a **trade-off** between speed and comprehension: when people read much faster than normal, they understand and remember less. What "speed readers" do well is skim. Skimming is a useful skill, but it is not the same as reading every sentence. The best way to read faster with good understanding is to know more words and to practise reading a lot of easy text ([[reading-skills/extensive-reading]]).
:::

::: quiz
According to eye-movement research, which statement is true of skilled readers?
- [ ] Their eyes move smoothly along each line.
- [ ] They read only the first and last word of each line.
- [x] They stop briefly on most words and jump between them.
- [ ] They never go back to an earlier word.
::: solution
The eyes make fixations (short stops) and saccades (jumps). Skilled readers fixate most content words, skip some short ones, and make some regressions (backward jumps) — about 10–15% of their saccades (Rayner 1998).
:::
:::

## Before you read: predicting

Skimming is also a good start for close reading. Before you read a text carefully, spend half a minute on its title, headings and pictures, and ask: *What do I already know about this? What will the writer probably say?* This is called **predicting**. A reader who has a rough plan of the text in mind finds the details easier to place. If the prediction is wrong, that is useful too: you notice the surprise, and you read that part more carefully.

::: example Predicting from a title and headings {#ex-predict}
An article has the title *Learning to Swim as an Adult* and three headings: *Why adults are afraid*, *Finding the right teacher*, *Your first five lessons*. What do you expect to find in each part?
::: solution
**Title:** the article is for adults who cannot swim yet. It will probably be encouraging, because it is about *learning*.

**Why adults are afraid:** probably reasons such as fear of deep water, a bad experience as a child, or feeling embarrassed in front of others.

**Finding the right teacher:** probably advice on what kind of teacher or class to choose, perhaps private lessons or adult classes.

**Your first five lessons:** probably a step-by-step plan, from getting used to water to floating and the first strokes.

We have not read a word of the article, but we now have questions in mind, and we know where to look for the answers. When we read closely, we check our predictions.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Translating every sentence.** Many Chinese learners were taught English through 精读, careful study of a short text sentence by sentence, often by translating it into Chinese. This is a good way to study grammar and vocabulary, and close reading has its place. But if it becomes the only way of reading, every text takes a long time and the reader often loses the main idea in the details. Try reading for the gist first, in English, and translate only the sentences you really need to.
- **Capital letters help scanning.** English writes names of people, places, days and months with capital letters (*Hangzhou*, *Friday*, *March*). Chinese has no capital letters, so this signal is new for Chinese readers. When you scan for a name or a date, look for capital letters and numbers: they stand out on the page.
- **First sentences carry the main point.** English writing that explains or argues often puts the main point at the start of a paragraph, which is why skimming first sentences works. Chinese writing more often builds up to the point. When you skim English, trust the first sentences more than you would in Chinese ([[reading-skills/main-ideas]]).
:::

::: history
The study of how we read began with the eyes. In 1879 the French eye doctor Louis Émile Javal reported that the eyes move along a line of print in short jumps, not smoothly. In 1908 the American psychologist Edmund Burke Huey summarised the first experiments on eye movements and word recognition in *The Psychology and Pedagogy of Reading*, a book still read today. Modern eye-tracking cameras made it possible to measure every fixation; Keith Rayner's review *Eye Movements in Reading and Information Processing* (1998) summarised twenty years of this work, and later reviews showed why promises of very fast reading with full understanding are not supported by the evidence (Rayner et al. 2016). In language teaching, the terms *skimming* and *scanning* became common in the 1970s and 1980s, in books such as Christine Nuttall's *Teaching Reading Skills in a Foreign Language* (first edition 1982).
:::

::: summary
- Before you read, ask **why** you are reading: to find one fact, to get the general idea, to understand every detail, or to enjoy.
- **Skimming** is fast reading for the gist: the title, headings, first and last paragraphs and first sentences.
- **Scanning** is a fast search for one piece of information; know what its shape is (a number, a name, a date) and look for it.
- **Close reading** is slow, careful reading of everything that matters; skim first to see the plan.
- Skilled readers stop on most words for about a quarter of a second and jump between them; fluent adult readers of English as a first language read about 240–260 words per minute silently (Brysbaert 2019).
- Much faster "speed reading" loses comprehension; reading faster comes from more vocabulary and more practice.
:::

::: exercise Match the task and the way of reading {level=1}
Choose skimming, scanning, close reading or fluent reading for each task. (a) Finding a friend's phone number in a long message. (b) Reading a contract before you sign it. (c) Looking at the front page of a news website to see what has happened today. (d) Reading a comic for fun. (e) Finding which platform your train leaves from. (f) Following a recipe while you cook.
::: solution
(a) scanning (for a number); (b) close reading (every detail matters; skimming first is a good start); (c) skimming (the general idea of the headlines); (d) fluent reading; (e) scanning (for the name of your destination, then the platform number); (f) close reading (skim the recipe first to check you have everything, then follow each step closely).

::: exercise Scan a timetable {level=1 check="3"}
Here is part of a bus timetable. How many buses leave Central Station between 8:00 and 9:00 (including 8:00 and 9:00)?

| bus | leaves Central Station | arrives at the University |
|---|---|---|
| 12 | 7:40 | 8:05 |
| 12 | 8:00 | 8:25 |
| 31 | 8:20 | 8:50 |
| 12 | 8:50 | 9:15 |
| 31 | 9:20 | 9:50 |
::: solution
Look only at the column *leaves Central Station*: 7:40 (no), 8:00 (yes), 8:20 (yes), 8:50 (yes), 9:20 (no). There are **3** buses.
:::

::: exercise Skim and say the gist {level=1}
Skim this paragraph and write its gist in one sentence.

> *Sleep is important for learning. While we sleep, the brain goes over what we did during the day. Students who sleep well after studying usually remember more the next day than students who stay up late. So if you have a test tomorrow, an extra hour of sleep may help you more than an extra hour of study.*
::: solution
A good answer: *Sleep helps us remember what we have learned, so sleeping well before a test can be better than studying late.* The first sentence gives the topic and the main point; the last sentence gives the advice that follows from it. Answers that say only *It is about sleep* are too general; answers that give only one detail (*the brain goes over the day*) miss the main point.
:::

::: exercise How long will it take? {level=2 check="25"}
Chen Hao reads English novels at about 160 words per minute. The next chapter of his novel has about 4,000 words. About how many minutes will it take him to read it?
::: solution
4,000 ÷ 160 = **25** minutes. (A fluent first-language reader at about 260 words per minute would need about 15 minutes; Chen Hao's speed is normal for a learner and will grow with practice.)
:::

::: exercise Read a notice closely {level=2}
Read the notice and answer the questions.

> *The swimming pool is open to members from 6 a.m. to 9 p.m. every day except public holidays. Children under 12 must be with an adult at all times. Children aged 12 to 15 may swim alone after 4 p.m. on weekdays and at any time at weekends.*

(a) Can a 10-year-old swim alone at 5 p.m. on a Tuesday? (b) Can a 13-year-old swim alone at 2 p.m. on a Wednesday? (c) Can a 13-year-old swim alone at 2 p.m. on a Saturday? (d) Is the pool open on a public holiday?
::: solution
(a) **No.** Children under 12 must be with an adult *at all times*.

(b) **No.** On weekdays, 12- to 15-year-olds may swim alone only *after 4 p.m.*

(c) **Yes.** At weekends they may swim alone *at any time* (during opening hours).

(d) **No** (or: not according to this notice). It is open *every day except public holidays*.
:::

::: exercise Predict from headings {level=2}
An article is called *Working from Home* and has these headings: *More freedom*, *The problem of loneliness*, *Making it work*. Write one or two sentences for each heading saying what you expect it to contain. Then say what you think the writer's overall opinion will be.
::: solution
Model answers:

*More freedom*: the advantages — no travel to work, choosing your own hours, more time with family.

*The problem of loneliness*: a disadvantage — no colleagues to talk to, feeling cut off from the team.

*Making it work*: advice — keeping regular hours, having a separate place to work, staying in contact with colleagues.

Overall opinion: probably balanced but positive. The article has one heading for advantages, one for a problem, and a last heading that gives advice on how to *make it work*, which suggests the writer thinks working from home can be a good thing if it is done well. Other reasonable predictions are acceptable; the point is to use the headings.
:::

::: exercise Plan your reading {level=3}
You have a meeting in 15 minutes about a 12-page report on customer complaints. You have not read it. At the meeting you will be asked: (1) what the main problems are, and (2) whether the number of complaints went up or down last year. Describe, step by step, how you will use your 15 minutes.
::: hint
Two questions need two different ways of reading. Which one needs one fact? Which one needs the general idea? Where in a report are these usually found?
:::
::: solution
A good plan:

1. **Skim** the contents page, the summary (if there is one) and the conclusion: 4–5 minutes. Note the main problems, which are usually listed there. This answers question (1).
2. **Scan** for numbers and words such as *increase*, *decrease*, *rose*, *fell*, *last year* or the year itself, and look at any table or chart of complaints per year: 3–4 minutes. This answers question (2).
3. **Read closely** only the paragraph or table that answers question (2), to be sure of the exact figures: 2–3 minutes.
4. Use the last minutes to write down the two answers in your own words.

What matters is that the plan uses skimming for the main problems, scanning for the figure, and close reading only where exactness matters, instead of starting at page 1.
:::
:::

::: exercise Your own reading week {level=3}
For one week, notice three different things you read in English or Chinese (for example a message, a web page, a notice, a book). For each one, write two or three sentences in English: what it was, why you read it, and how you read it (skimming, scanning, close reading or fluent reading). Did you choose the best way? What would you do differently?
::: solution
Answers will differ. A model entry:

*On Tuesday I read an email from my landlord about the water being turned off. I needed every detail — the day and the times — so I read it closely, twice. That was the right choice, because the time was in the last sentence and I nearly missed it.*

A good answer names the text, the purpose and the way of reading, and reflects on the choice using the ideas in [[#rule-choose]].
:::
