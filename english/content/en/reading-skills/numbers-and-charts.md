A small shop sells 100 cakes a week. In March, after a television programme mentions it, sales **rise by 50%**. In April the programme is forgotten, and sales **fall by 50%**. Is the shop back where it started?

Many readers say yes. In fact it is not: 100 cakes plus 50% is 150, and 150 minus 50% is 75. The shop now sells a quarter fewer cakes than before the programme. The words were simple and the numbers were small, but the meaning depended on one question that is easy to forget: **50% of what?**

Numbers appear in almost every kind of text: news reports, reviews, instructions, reports at work, textbooks. They are often the most important facts in the text, and they are also where readers make the most mistakes. This chapter explains how percentages and changes are described in English, how to read tables and charts, and how to check whether the words of a text match its numbers. How to *say* numbers, dates and prices is covered in [[core-vocabulary/numbers-dates-money]].

## Percentages, fractions and proportions

English texts express proportions in several ways, and a reader should move easily between them:

| percentage | fraction | other ways of saying it |
|---|---|---|
| 50% | a half | *one in two*, *half of* |
| 33% | about a third | *one in three*, *a third of* |
| 25% | a quarter | *one in four*, *a quarter of* |
| 20% | a fifth | *one in five* |
| 10% | a tenth | *one in ten* |
| 75% | three quarters | *three in four*, *three out of four* |
| 66–67% | two thirds | *two in three* |

Texts often round numbers and use **approximation words**: *about*, *around*, *roughly*, *approximately* (close to); *nearly*, *almost*, *just under* (a little less than); *just over*, *more than*, *over* (a little more than). *Nearly half* might be 47%; *just over a third* might be 35%.

::: definition Percentage and base {#def-percentage}
A **percentage** is a number out of 100: 30% means 30 out of every 100. Every percentage is a percentage **of** something, called its **base**: 30% **of the students**, 30% **of last year's sales**. The same percentage of a different base is a different amount.
:::

::: theorem Always ask: per cent of what? {#rule-base}
When you read a percentage, find its base. In particular:

1. in a **change** (*rose by 20%*, *fell by 10%*), the base is the value **before** the change;
2. after two changes, the second percentage is of the **new** value, so equal rises and falls do not cancel out;
3. two percentages can be compared directly only if they have the **same base** or you know both bases.
:::

::: proof
A percentage is a ratio, so it means nothing without the quantity it is a ratio of. Point 2 follows from point 1: a rise of 50% multiplies by 1.5 and a fall of 50% multiplies by 0.5, so the result is 1.5 × 0.5 = 0.75 of the original, a fall of 25% overall. Point 3 is the source of many misleading claims: *Sales of electric bikes grew by 200%, while sales of cars grew by only 2%* may describe 300 extra bikes and 20,000 extra cars, if the town sold 150 bikes and a million cars last year.
:::

::: example Two changes in a row {#ex-two-changes}
A train ticket cost 200 yuan. The price rose by 10% in January and by another 10% in July. A newspaper says the price *rose by 20% this year*. Is that right?
::: solution
**January.** The base is 200. 10% of 200 = 20, so the new price is 200 + 20 = **220 yuan**.

**July.** The base is now 220 ([[#rule-base]], point 2). 10% of 220 = 22, so the new price is 220 + 22 = **242 yuan**.

**Whole year.** The rise is 242 − 200 = 42 yuan. As a percentage of the original price: 42 ÷ 200 = 0.21 = **21%**.

So the newspaper's *20%* is close but not exact: two rises of 10% make a rise of 21%, because the second 10% is of a larger number. With small percentages the difference is small; with large ones it is large (two rises of 50% make 125%, not 100%).
:::
:::

## Per cent and percentage points

::: definition Percentage point {#def-percentage-point}
When a quantity that is itself a percentage changes — an unemployment rate, an interest rate, a share of votes — the simple difference between the two percentages is measured in **percentage points**. If a rate goes from 10% to 12%, it has risen by **2 percentage points**. As a proportion of the old rate, that is a rise of **20%** (2 is 20% of 10).
:::

::: example Points or per cent? {#ex-points}
*Last year 40% of the town's residents used the bus at least once a week. This year the figure is 50%.* Which statements are correct? (a) Bus use rose by 10%. (b) Bus use rose by 10 percentage points. (c) Bus use rose by 25%. (d) The number of weekly bus users rose by a quarter.
::: solution
**Percentage points:** 50 − 40 = 10. So (b) is **correct**.

**Per cent:** the change (10 points) as a proportion of the old figure (40): 10 ÷ 40 = 0.25 = 25%. So (c) is **correct**, and (a) is **wrong** (or at best careless — many writers say *10%* when they mean 10 points, and readers must notice).

(d) **Probably correct but not certain.** If the population of the town is the same in both years, then a rise from 40% to 50% of the residents is a rise of a quarter in the number of users. If the population changed, the number of users changed by a different amount. The text does not say, so (d) goes beyond the evidence ([[reading-skills/inference]]).
:::
:::

::: quiz
An interest rate falls from 4% to 3%. Which description is accurate?
- [ ] It fell by 1%.
- [x] It fell by 1 percentage point, which is a fall of 25%.
- [ ] It fell by 33%.
- [ ] It fell by 4 percentage points.
::: solution
The difference is 4 − 3 = 1 percentage point. As a proportion of the old rate, 1 ÷ 4 = 25%. *It fell by 1%* is a common but inexact way of saying it; strictly it would mean a fall from 4% to 3.96%.
:::
:::

## The language of change

Texts that describe numbers use a small set of verbs, nouns and adverbs again and again.

| direction | verbs | nouns |
|---|---|---|
| up | *rise*, *increase*, *grow*, *go up*, *climb*, *double* (×2), *triple* (×3) | *a rise*, *an increase*, *growth* |
| down | *fall*, *decrease*, *drop*, *decline*, *go down*, *halve* (÷2) | *a fall*, *a decrease*, *a drop*, *a decline* |
| no change | *remain stable*, *stay the same*, *level off* (stop changing) | *stability* |
| highest / lowest point | *peak*, *reach a peak*, *hit a low* | *a peak*, *a low* |
| up and down | *fluctuate* | *fluctuation* |

How big or fast the change was is shown by adverbs (with verbs) and adjectives (with nouns):

- large or fast: *sharply*, *steeply*, *dramatically*, *rapidly* — *a sharp rise*, *a dramatic fall*;
- small: *slightly*, *a little*, *marginally* — *a slight increase*;
- steady: *steadily*, *gradually*, *slowly* — *a steady decline*, *gradual growth*.

These words are not exact. *Dramatically* in one text may mean 30%, in another 300%. Always look for the number behind the word.

::: theorem *By*, *to*, *from* … *to* {#rule-by-to}
The prepositions after a verb or noun of change say what the number is:

1. ***by*** + the **size of the change**: *Prices rose by 5%.* *Sales fell by 2,000 units.*
2. ***to*** + the **new value**: *Prices rose to 105 yuan.* *Unemployment fell to 4%.*
3. ***from*** + the **old value** … ***to*** + the new value: *The population grew from 8 million to 10 million.*
4. with nouns, ***of*** + the size of the change, and ***in*** + what changed: *a rise of 5% in prices*, *an increase in demand*.
:::

::: proof
The difference between *by* and *to* is small in form and large in meaning. *Unemployment fell by 4%* and *Unemployment fell to 4%* describe completely different situations. Reference grammars and learner's dictionaries list these prepositions with the verbs of change (Swan 2016); they are among the most important small words for reading reports, because the number that follows means nothing until you know which preposition it belongs to.
:::

::: quiz
*The number of visitors rose to 12,000, an increase of 2,000 on last year.* How many visitors were there last year?
- [ ] 12,000
- [ ] 14,000
- [x] 10,000
- [ ] 2,000
::: solution
*Rose to 12,000* gives the new value; *an increase of 2,000* gives the size of the change. So last year's figure was 12,000 − 2,000 = 10,000.
:::
:::

Comparisons between two amounts use these patterns:

- *twice as many / much as*, *three times as many / much as*: *Shanghai has about twice as many metro stations as our city.*
- *half as many / much as*: *The new phone uses half as much power as the old one.*
- *X% more / less than*: *Rents are 30% higher than in 2020.*
- *more than double*, *nearly triple*, *a twofold / threefold increase*.

Be careful with *three times more than*. Many writers use it to mean the same as *three times as many as* (×3), but some readers, and some style guides, take it as ×4 (the original plus three times more). When the exact number matters, look for the figures themselves.

## Reading tables

A table arranges numbers in **rows** and **columns** so that they can be compared. Read it in this order:

1. the **title**: what is being measured, where and when;
2. the **headings** of the columns and rows, and the **units** (%, yuan, thousands, per 1,000 people);
3. any **notes** and the **source**: who collected the data, how, and when;
4. only then, the numbers — first the overall pattern, then particular cells.

Here is a table of invented data, made for this lesson.

> **How students travel to Hillside College (% of students, survey of 800 students each year)**
>
> | way of travelling | 2015 | 2020 | 2025 |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | car | 38 | 31 | 22 |
> | bus | 30 | 28 | 25 |
> | bicycle | 12 | 18 | 27 |
> | on foot | 15 | 17 | 18 |
> | other | 5 | 6 | 8 |

::: example Reading a table {#ex-table}
Use the table to answer. (a) What do the numbers measure? (b) Which way of travelling was most common in 2015, and which in 2025? (c) Describe the change in cycling in one sentence with a percentage-point figure. (d) A student newspaper says: *Cycling has more than doubled since 2015.* Is this supported?
::: solution
**(a) Title and units first.** The numbers are **percentages of students** at Hillside College who travel in each way, from a survey of 800 students in each year. They are not numbers of students.

**(b)** 2015: **car** (38%). 2025: **bicycle** (27%), just ahead of bus (25%) and car (22%).

**(c)** *The share of students who cycle rose steadily from 12% in 2015 to 27% in 2025, an increase of 15 percentage points.* (Note *from … to* for the values and *of* for the size of the change — [[#rule-by-to]].)

**(d)** **Yes.** 27 ÷ 12 = 2.25, so the share of cyclists is more than twice as large. Because the surveys asked the same number of students (800), the number of cyclists in the survey also more than doubled (from about 96 to about 216). Whether it is true of all students at the college depends on how well the survey represents them, which the table does not tell us.
:::
:::

## Reading charts

The three most common charts each do a different job:

- a **line chart** shows **change over time**: the horizontal axis (the *x*-axis) is usually time, the vertical axis (the *y*-axis) the quantity;
- a **bar chart** **compares** amounts in different categories (countries, products, age groups);
- a **pie chart** shows the **parts of a whole**, which add up to 100%.

Before reading the shape of a chart, read its title, the labels of the axes, the units and the **scale** — the numbers along the axes. The same data can look very different on different scales.

The figure below is a line chart of a different kind: it lets you see what a steady percentage change does over time.

::: widget plot
f: 100*(1+p/100)^x; 100+p*x
x: 0, 20
y: 0, 1000
sliders: p=5:1:12:1
labels: rises by p% every year; rises by p every year
caption: Two ways for a quantity that starts at 100 to grow over 20 years. The curve rises by $p$% of its **current** value every year, so each year's rise is bigger than the last; the straight line adds the same amount ($p$) every year. With the slider at 5, the curve reaches about 265 after 20 years and the line reaches 200; at 10, the curve reaches about 673 and the line 300. This is why "a steady rise of 10% a year" means much more than "a rise of 10 every year", and why repeated percentage changes cannot simply be added ([[#rule-base]]).
:::

::: warning Charts that mislead by their scale
A bar chart shows sales of 98 and 102. If the vertical axis starts at 0, the two bars look almost the same, which is honest: the difference is about 4%. If the axis starts at 95, the second bar looks more than twice as tall as the first. Nothing in the numbers is false, but the picture exaggerates.

✗ *The chart shows that Product B sells more than twice as well as Product A.*

✓ *Product B sold 102 units and Product A 98, a difference of about 4%; the axis of the chart starts at 95.*

Always read the numbers on the axis before you trust the shape. Other warning signs: no units, no source, a time period chosen to begin at an unusually low or high point, and pictures (icons, 3-D bars) whose size is not proportional to the numbers.
:::

::: quiz
A chart shows how a family spends its monthly income: rent, food, transport, savings and other. Which kind of chart is it most likely to be?
- [ ] a line chart
- [x] a pie chart
- [ ] a timetable
- [ ] a map
::: solution
The categories are parts of one whole (the monthly income) and together make 100%, which is what a pie chart shows. A bar chart could also be used. A line chart would be used if the text showed how spending changed month by month.
:::
:::

## Checking the words against the numbers

Writers summarise numbers in words, and words are less exact than numbers. A careful reader checks that the description fits the data.

::: theorem Check the description {#rule-check}
When a text describes data, compare each statement with the numbers:

1. **quantity words**: *most* and *a majority* mean more than half; *a minority*, less than half; *almost all*, perhaps 90% or more; *few*, a small number;
2. **change words**: is a *sharp* rise really large? did the figure *double*, or only rise by half?
3. **causes**: a text may say that one thing **caused** another when the data show only that they happened **together**;
4. **missing information**: what is the base, the source, the size of the sample, the time period?
:::

::: proof
Descriptions of data choose and interpret; they are never neutral copies of the numbers. Point 1 gives the usual meaning of the quantity words, but writers are not always careful, which is why the numbers must be checked. Point 3 is the most important for understanding news about research: if people who drink green tea live longer, it may be because tea-drinkers also do other healthy things. The data show a **connection**; they do not by themselves show the cause. Darrell Huff's *How to Lie with Statistics* (1954) became famous for describing these traps for general readers, and they are just as common today. The same checks become central in academic reading: [[academic-reading/reading-data]].
:::

::: example Does the text match the table? {#ex-check}
A report on the Hillside College survey says: *(1) Most students now travel by bicycle. (2) Car use has fallen sharply. (3) The college's new bicycle sheds, built in 2018, have caused this change.* Check each sentence against the table.
::: solution
Use [[#rule-check]].

(1) **Not supported.** In 2025, 27% of students cycled. That is the largest single group, but *most* means more than half. A correct version: *Cycling is now the most common way of getting to the college, used by just over a quarter of students.*

(2) **Supported.** Car use fell from 38% to 22%, a fall of 16 percentage points, or about 42% of the 2015 figure. *Sharply* is a reasonable word for this.

(3) **Not supported by the table.** The table shows that cycling rose and car use fell, and the sheds were built in 2018. It does not show that the sheds **caused** the change. Other things may have changed too: fuel prices, parking rules, the students' attitudes. A careful version: *The rise in cycling began before the new sheds were built and continued after; the sheds may have helped.*
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Large numbers.** Chinese groups large numbers by 万 (10,000) and 亿 (100 million); English by thousands and millions. A report giving *3.2 million* means 320万; *1.4 billion* means 14亿. Converting quickly and correctly is a skill in itself ([[core-vocabulary/numbers-dates-money]]).
- **百分点 and per cent.** Chinese also distinguishes 百分之 and 百分点, in the same way as English distinguishes *per cent* and *percentage points*. The concept transfers directly; the English words simply have to be recognised.
- **倍 and *times*.** *增加了两倍* in careful Chinese usage means that the result is three times the original, while *增加到两倍* means it is twice the original — and everyday usage is not always careful. English has a similar uncertainty with *two times more*. When the size matters, work from the actual figures, not from the word.
- **Discounts.** Chinese 八折 means you pay 80%; English describes the same thing as *20% off*. The English phrase gives the part you do **not** pay.
:::

::: history
Charts are younger than most people think. The Scottish engineer and writer William Playfair is usually credited with inventing the line chart and the bar chart in *The Commercial and Political Atlas* (1786), which showed England's trade over the eighteenth century, and the pie chart in his *Statistical Breviary* (1801). Before him, numbers were almost always presented in tables. In the following century, Florence Nightingale used diagrams of her own design to show that more soldiers in the Crimean War died of disease than of wounds. The dangers of charts were described for general readers by Darrell Huff in *How to Lie with Statistics* (1954), whose chapters on scales that do not start at zero and on percentages without a base remain good advice for any reader of the news.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Fractions and percentages {level=1}
Write each as a percentage. (a) a quarter (b) one in five (c) three quarters (d) nine out of ten (e) about a third (f) just over half (give a likely figure)
::: solution
(a) 25%; (b) 20%; (c) 75%; (d) 90%; (e) about 33%; (f) a little more than 50%, for example 51–55%.
:::
:::

::: exercise By or to? {level=1}
Complete with *by* or *to*. (a) *The price of petrol rose ___ 8.20 yuan a litre.* (b) *The number of students fell ___ 15%, from 2,000 to 1,700.* (c) *The temperature dropped ___ minus 5 degrees.* (d) *Exports grew ___ 3 billion dollars, to a total of 40 billion.*
::: solution
(a) *to* (new value); (b) *by* (size of the change: 300 is 15% of 2,000); (c) *to* (new value); (d) *by* (size of the change; *to a total of* gives the new value).
:::
:::

::: exercise A percentage of a number {level=1 check="120"}
A town has 800 shops. 15% of them sell food. How many shops sell food?
::: solution
15% of 800 = 0.15 × 800 = **120**.
:::
:::

::: exercise Percentage points {level=2 check="5"}
The share of households with a pet rose from 20% to 25%. By how many percentage points did it rise?
::: solution
25 − 20 = **5** percentage points. (As a proportion of the old figure, that is a rise of 5 ÷ 20 = 25%.)
:::
:::

::: exercise Up and down {level=2 check="80"}
A share price is 100 yuan. It **rises by 60%** in one month and **falls by 50%** the next. What is the price at the end, in yuan?
::: solution
After the rise: 100 + 60% of 100 = **160**. After the fall: 160 − 50% of 160 = 160 − 80 = **80** yuan. The price is now 20% lower than at the start, even though "60% up, 50% down" sounds like a gain ([[#rule-base]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Describe a table {level=2}
Use the Hillside College table to write three sentences: one about bus travel, one about walking, and one comparing car and bicycle in 2025. Use at least one verb of change with an adverb and one *from … to* phrase.
::: solution
Models: *The share of students who travel by bus fell slightly, from 30% in 2015 to 25% in 2025.* *Walking increased gradually, by 3 percentage points over ten years.* *In 2025 slightly more students cycled (27%) than drove (22%), the opposite of the situation in 2015.* Any accurate sentences that use the language of change and the prepositions correctly are acceptable.
:::
:::

::: exercise Check the headline {level=3}
A news website gives this headline and first sentence (both invented for this exercise). Use [[#rule-check]] to say what is supported, what is not, and what information is missing.

> *COFFEE MAKES YOU LIVE LONGER, SAYS STUDY*
>
> *A new study of 5,000 adults found that people who drank two or more cups of coffee a day were 12% less likely to die during the ten years of the study than people who drank none.*
::: hint
Is the study about cause or about connection? 12% less likely than what? What do we not know about the two groups of people?
:::
::: solution
**Supported:** in this study, coffee drinkers (two or more cups a day) died less often during ten years than non-drinkers; the difference was 12% (a relative difference: 12% lower than the risk of the non-drinkers).

**Not supported:** the headline's claim of **cause** (*makes you live longer*). The study shows a connection. Coffee drinkers may differ from non-drinkers in other ways — health, income, work, smoking — that affect how long they live. Also, *live longer* goes beyond *less likely to die during ten years*.

**Missing:** the base (how many people in each group died — 12% less of a small risk is a small difference in absolute terms); who the 5,000 adults were (one country? one age group?); whether the researchers took other differences into account; who carried out and paid for the study.

A careful headline: *Coffee drinkers less likely to die during ten-year study*.
:::
:::

::: exercise Write a short report {level=3}
Here is invented data for a small town library: visitors per year — 2019: 40,000; 2020: 15,000; 2021: 22,000; 2022: 31,000; 2023: 38,000; 2024: 45,000. Write a paragraph of 60–90 words describing the data, with a topic sentence, at least three verbs or nouns of change, *by* and *to* used correctly, and one sentence that gives a possible reason **without** claiming it as certain.
::: solution
A model:

*Visitor numbers at the library fell sharply in 2020 but have recovered steadily since then. In 2020 the number of visitors dropped from 40,000 to 15,000, a fall of more than 60%. It then rose every year, reaching 38,000 in 2023 and 45,000 in 2024, which is 5,000 more than in 2019. The fall in 2020 may have been connected with the closure of public buildings that year, although the figures alone do not show the cause.*

A good answer has a topic sentence giving the overall pattern ([[reading-skills/main-ideas]]), accurate figures, correct *by*/*to*/*from … to*, and a hedged reason (*may have been connected with*).
:::
:::
