Read these three sentences:

> *Chen Jing opened a small bakery near the university in 2019. At first it struggled, because few students knew about it. Then she started selling bread at half price after 8 p.m., and this changed everything.*

To understand them, you must make several connections without noticing. *It* in the second sentence is the bakery, not the university. *She* in the third sentence is Chen Jing. *This* is not a thing at all: it is the whole idea of selling bread at half price in the evening. *Then* tells you that the third sentence happens later than the second, and *because* tells you why the bakery struggled.

Words like these are the **thread** of a text. They connect each sentence to the sentences around it. A reader who follows the thread knows at every moment who is doing what and how each idea is related to the last. A reader who loses it may understand every word and still not understand the text. This chapter shows the main ways English connects sentences — pronouns, *this* and *that*, synonyms and linking words — and how to track what each one refers to.

## Cohesion

::: definition Cohesion and cohesive devices {#def-cohesion}
**Cohesion** (衔接) is the way the sentences of a text are linked by words that point to other parts of the text. The words that make these links are **cohesive devices** (衔接手段). Halliday and Hasan (1976) described the main kinds:

1. **reference**: words such as *he*, *it*, *they*, *this*, *that*, *the*, which point to something already mentioned (or about to be mentioned);
2. **substitution and ellipsis**: replacing words with *one*, *do*, *so*, or leaving them out (*I wanted to go, but I couldn't* [go]);
3. **conjunction**: linking words such as *and*, *but*, *because*, *however*, *then*;
4. **lexical cohesion**: repeating a word, or using a synonym or a more general word for the same thing (*the bakery* … *the shop* … *the business*).
:::

When you read, each cohesive device asks you a question. *It* asks: *which thing?* *However* asks: *what is this in contrast with?* *The shop* asks: *have I met this shop before?* Answering these questions quickly and correctly is what "following the thread" means.

## Pronouns

::: definition Reference and referent {#def-reference}
**Reference** (指代) is the use of a word to point to something mentioned elsewhere in the text. The thing it points to is its **referent**. In *Chen Jing opened a bakery. She was 26*, the pronoun *she* refers to *Chen Jing*; *Chen Jing* is the referent of *she*.
:::

Most pronouns point **back** to something already mentioned. Sometimes a pronoun points **forward**, especially at the start of a sentence: *When she arrived at the station, Mei realised she had left her bag on the bus.* Here the first *she* refers to *Mei*, who is named only later.

::: theorem Finding the referent of a pronoun {#rule-pronoun}
To find what a pronoun refers to:

1. **Match the form.** *He* and *him* refer to a male person (or animal); *she* and *her* to a female; *it* to a thing, an animal, an idea or a situation; *they* and *them* to a plural noun or a group — or, as singular *they*, to one person whose gender is not known or not stated (*Someone has left their umbrella*).
2. **Look back** to the nearest earlier noun phrases that match the form, usually in the same or the previous sentence.
3. **Prefer the main person or thing** — often the subject of the previous sentence, or the topic of the paragraph.
4. **Check the meaning.** Put the referent in place of the pronoun. If the sentence does not make sense, try the next candidate.
:::

::: proof
Writers use a pronoun when they expect the reader to identify the referent easily; if it is not easy, careful writers repeat the noun. Form (step 1) removes many candidates at once: *she* cannot refer to *the bakery*. Distance (step 2) and prominence (step 3) are strong tendencies, not rules: the subject of the previous sentence and the main topic are the most likely referents (Halliday & Hasan 1976; Grabe 2009). Step 4 is needed because meaning can override all the other clues. In *The cup fell off the table, but it didn't break*, *it* is the cup, although *the table* is nearer, because we know that the thing that falls is the thing that might break. Singular *they* is standard English (Swan 2016) and is common in modern texts.
:::

::: example Tracking pronouns in a short text {#ex-pronouns}
Say what each numbered pronoun refers to: *Wang Fang and her brother wanted to visit their grandparents in Guilin. (1) They had not seen (2) them for two years. Her brother booked the train tickets, but (3) he forgot to check the date, and (4) they were for the wrong day. Wang Fang was angry, but (5) she managed to change (6) them at the station.*
::: solution
Use [[#rule-pronoun]]: match the form, look back, prefer the main person, check the meaning.

(1) *They*: plural, a person or people who can *see*. Candidates: *Wang Fang and her brother*, *their grandparents*. The subject of the previous sentence is *Wang Fang and her brother*. Check: *Wang Fang and her brother had not seen …* — makes sense. **Wang Fang and her brother.**

(2) *them*: plural, and different from the subject of its own clause (otherwise it would be *themselves*). **Their grandparents.**

(3) *he*: male. **Her brother.**

(4) *they*: plural, and it *were for the wrong day*. People are not "for a day"; tickets are. **The train tickets.** (Note that *they* in (1) and (4) refer to different things.)

(5) *she*: female. **Wang Fang.**

(6) *them*: plural, something you can *change at the station*. **The train tickets.**
:::
:::

::: warning "A pronoun always refers to the nearest noun"
✗ In *The cup fell off the table, but it didn't break*, *it* = the table (because *table* is nearer).

✓ *It* = the cup. The cup is the subject of the first clause, and the cup is what might break.

The nearest noun is a good first guess, but the subject and the main topic are often better ones, and meaning decides in the end. Look at this sentence: *The police stopped the drivers because they were driving too fast.* *They* = the drivers, because police do not stop people for their own speeding. Change one word — *The police stopped the drivers because they were looking for a stolen car* — and *they* = the police. The grammar is the same; only our knowledge of the world tells the two apart.
:::

## *This*, *that* and other pointing words

Pronouns usually point to a noun phrase. But *this*, *that*, *it* and *which* can also point to a **whole idea**: a clause, a sentence, or even a paragraph.

- *She started selling bread at half price in the evening, and this changed everything.* — *this* = selling bread at half price in the evening.
- *Many students do not eat breakfast. This can make it hard to concentrate in the morning.* — *This* = not eating breakfast.
- *He said he had finished the report, which was not true.* — *which* = that he had finished the report.

Writers very often put a noun after *this*: *this problem*, *this decision*, *this change*, *these results*. The noun is a summary of what came before, and it tells you how the writer sees it.

::: theorem *This* + noun as a summary {#rule-this-summary}
When *this*, *these*, *that*, *those* or *such* are followed by a general noun — *this problem*, *these changes*, *such an approach*, *this view* — the phrase usually refers back to an idea in the previous sentence or sentences, and the noun **labels** that idea. To understand it:

1. find the earlier idea that fits the label;
2. notice how the label describes it (a *problem*, a *success*, a *claim*, a *mistake*), because the label often shows the writer's opinion.
:::

::: proof
These "labelling" nouns are very common in written English, especially in news and academic texts; Biber et al. (1999) note that *this* + noun at the start of a sentence is a typical way in which written texts refer back to whole ideas. The label is not neutral: calling the same plan *this solution* or *this risky idea* tells the reader what to think of it. Recognising labels is therefore useful both for following the thread and for understanding the writer's attitude ([[reading-skills/inference]]).
:::

::: example What does *this* refer to? {#ex-this-summary}
What does the phrase in each second sentence refer to, and what does the label tell you? (a) *Last year the company moved its office out of the city centre. This decision saved money but made many workers' journeys longer.* (b) *Some students copy their homework from the internet. This practice is becoming more common.* (c) *Prices rose by 8% in six months. Such an increase has not been seen for twenty years.*
::: solution
(a) *This decision* = moving the office out of the city centre. The label *decision* is neutral; the rest of the sentence gives a good result and a bad one.

(b) *This practice* = copying homework from the internet. *Practice* means something done regularly; the context (*becoming more common*) suggests the writer sees it as a problem, though the label itself is neutral.

(c) *Such an increase* = prices rising by 8% in six months. *Such* means ‘of this kind’, and *increase* summarises *prices rose*. The second sentence tells us the increase is unusual.
:::
:::

Other words that point back without repeating the noun:

- **one / ones** replace a countable noun: *I don't like the red shirt; I prefer the blue one.* (one = shirt)
- **do / do so** replace a verb phrase: *Doctors advise eating less salt, and most people know they should do so.* (do so = eat less salt)
- **so / not** replace a clause after *think*, *hope*, *be afraid*: *Will it rain? — I hope not.* (= I hope it will not rain)
- **the former / the latter** mean the first and the second of two things just mentioned, mostly in formal writing: *Li and Zhou both applied; the former got the job.* (the former = Li)

::: quiz
*The first plan was cheap but slow; the second was fast but expensive. In the end the committee chose the latter.* Which plan did the committee choose?
- [ ] the first plan
- [x] the second plan
- [ ] a cheap and fast plan
- [ ] neither plan
::: solution
*The latter* means the second of the two things just mentioned: the fast but expensive plan. *The former* would mean the first.
:::
:::

## Synonyms and repetition

Good English writers often avoid repeating the same noun again and again. Instead they use a **synonym** or a **more general word** for the same thing. A reader must recognise that the different words refer to the same thing.

> *Chengdu is famous for its food. The city has thousands of restaurants, and the capital of Sichuan Province attracts food lovers from all over the world. Visitors to the town often say they came for the pandas and stayed for the hotpot.*

*Chengdu*, *the city*, *the capital of Sichuan Province* and *the town* all refer to the same place. A learner who does not know that Chengdu is the capital of Sichuan might think the third phrase is a new place; the word *the* is a clue that it is not new ([[nouns-and-articles/a-and-the]]).

::: theorem Following chains of words {#rule-lexical-chains}
A noun phrase with ***the*** or ***this*** that seems new often refers to something already mentioned under another name. When you meet one, ask: **Have I met this before under a different word?** Look back for a more specific word (*Chengdu*) that the new phrase (*the city*) could describe. Chains of words for the same thing — *a bakery* … *the shop* … *the business* … *it* — run through a text and keep it together.
:::

::: proof
The definite article signals that the reader can identify the referent ([[nouns-and-articles/a-and-the]]); if it has not been mentioned in those words, it was probably mentioned in other words. Halliday and Hasan (1976) called this **lexical cohesion**, and noted that general words such as *the thing*, *the place*, *the man*, *the idea* are often used in this way. Avoiding repetition is a matter of style: academic and technical texts repeat key terms more, to be exact, while news writing and stories vary them more.
:::

::: example Finding chains in a news report {#ex-lexical-chain}
Find the chains of words that refer to the same thing:

> *A fire broke out in a restaurant in the old town on Tuesday night. The blaze started in the kitchen and spread quickly to the dining room. Firefighters arrived within ten minutes, and the owner, Ms Huang, praised their speed. The building was badly damaged, but nobody was hurt.*
::: solution
Use [[#rule-lexical-chains]]: for each phrase with *the* that seems new, ask whether it was mentioned before under another word.

**Chain 1: the fire.** *a fire* → *The blaze*. A reader who does not know *blaze* can still see from *The* and from *started in the kitchen and spread* that it is the same fire ([[reading-skills/words-from-context]]). *Blaze* means a large, strong fire.

**Chain 2: the restaurant.** *a restaurant* → *the kitchen*, *the dining room* (parts of it) → *The building*. *The building* is a more general word for the restaurant.

**Chain 3: the firefighters.** *Firefighters* → *their* (in *their speed*).

**Chain 4: the owner.** *the owner* = *Ms Huang*: the two phrases stand side by side and refer to the same person. *The owner* is new information here, but *the* is used because a restaurant has an owner, so the reader can identify who is meant.
:::
:::

## Linking words

::: definition Connective {#def-connective}
A **connective** (连接词) — also called a linking word or a discourse marker — is a word or phrase that shows how a sentence or clause is related to what came before: by addition, contrast, cause, result, example, time or restatement.
:::

| relation | connectives | what to expect next |
|---|---|---|
| addition | *and*, *also*, *in addition*, *moreover*, *furthermore*, *besides* | another point of the same kind |
| contrast | *but*, *however*, *although*, *though*, *yet*, *on the other hand*, *nevertheless*, *whereas*, *instead* | something different or unexpected |
| cause | *because*, *since*, *as*, *because of*, *due to* | a reason |
| result | *so*, *therefore*, *as a result*, *consequently*, *thus* | a consequence |
| example | *for example*, *for instance*, *such as*, *in particular* | a particular case |
| time and order | *first*, *then*, *next*, *after that*, *finally*, *meanwhile* | the next step or event |
| restatement | *in other words*, *that is*, *i.e.*, *to put it simply* | the same idea said differently |
| conclusion | *in short*, *in conclusion*, *overall*, *to sum up* | a summary or the main point |

::: theorem Connectives tell you what is coming {#rule-connectives}
A connective at the start of a sentence tells you **how to read the sentence before you read it**. Use it to predict:

1. after a **contrast** word, expect the opposite of what you just read, and expect the more important point — the writer's own view often comes after *but* or *however*;
2. after a **result** word, expect a consequence of the previous sentence;
3. after an **example** word, expect a particular case of the general statement before it — so the general statement is likely to be the main idea;
4. after a **restatement** word, expect easier words for an idea you may not have understood.
:::

::: proof
Connectives are some of the most useful words a reader can know, because they are frequent and they carry the logic of a text. Research on reading comprehension has repeatedly found that readers who notice and use them understand and remember expository texts better (Grabe 2009). Point 1 reflects a common pattern of English argument: a concession followed by the writer's position (*Some people say X. However, Y.*), the same pattern that puts many topic sentences after an opening contrast ([[reading-skills/main-ideas]]).
:::

::: example Predicting from connectives {#ex-connectives}
Read the first sentence and the connective, and say what you expect next. Then compare with the real continuation. (a) *Electric cars produce no exhaust fumes. However, …* (b) *Our town has very little public transport. As a result, …* (c) *Some animals change colour with the seasons. For example, …* (d) *The film was not a commercial success. In other words, …*
::: solution
(a) *However* = contrast. Expect a disadvantage or a problem with electric cars. Continuation: *… however, the electricity they use may still come from burning coal.*

(b) *As a result* = result. Expect a consequence of poor public transport. Continuation: *… almost every family owns a car.*

(c) *For example* = example. Expect a particular animal. Continuation: *… the Arctic fox is brown in summer and white in winter.*

(d) *In other words* = restatement. Expect a simpler way of saying *not a commercial success*. Continuation: *… it did not make much money.*

Each time, the connective let us guess the type of sentence before reading it, which makes reading faster and easier.
:::
:::

::: quiz
*Most people think of deserts as hot. ______, the largest desert in the world is Antarctica, which is extremely cold.* Which connective fits best?
- [ ] Therefore
- [ ] For example
- [x] In fact
- [ ] In addition
::: solution
The second sentence corrects the common belief in the first, so we need a contrast or correction: *In fact* (or *However*, *Actually*). *Therefore* would make it a result of the first sentence, *For example* an example of hot deserts, and *In addition* another point of the same kind.
:::
:::

::: quiz
*The museum is free on Sundays. ______, it is usually very crowded then.* Which connective fits best?
- [x] As a result
- [ ] However
- [ ] For instance
- [ ] In other words
::: solution
Being crowded is a consequence of being free: *As a result* (or *So*, *Consequently*). *However* would suggest that the crowds are surprising, which they are not.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Subjects that Chinese leaves out.** Chinese often leaves out a subject or object that is clear from context — 他进了门，（他）坐下就吃 — and the reader understands who is meant. Linguists call this a **zero pronoun**; it is very common in Chinese (Li & Thompson 1981). English almost always needs the pronoun: *He came in, sat down and started eating.* So English texts contain many more pronouns, and each one has to be tracked.
- ***He*, *she* and *it*.** Spoken Mandarin uses one sound, *tā*, for 他, 她 and 它. Written Chinese distinguishes them, but in speech they sound the same, and Chinese learners sometimes pay less attention to the difference. In English the form of the pronoun is one of the best clues to its referent (step 1 of [[#rule-pronoun]]), so notice it.
- **Paired connectives.** Chinese often marks a relation twice: 虽然 … 但是, 因为 … 所以. English uses one connective: *Although it was raining, we went out* or *It was raining, but we went out*, not both (✗ *Although it was raining, but we went out*). When reading, remember that one English connective already does the whole job.
- **Implicit links.** Chinese frequently leaves the relation between two clauses unstated (意合), while English more often states it with a connective (形合). Connectives are therefore especially important clues when Chinese readers read English: they say what Chinese would leave to the reader.
:::

::: history
Pronoun reference looks simple until you try to explain it. In 1972 the computer scientist Terry Winograd, in *Understanding Natural Language*, discussed the sentence *The city councilmen refused the demonstrators a permit because they feared violence*: *they* is the councilmen, but if *feared* is changed to *advocated*, *they* becomes the demonstrators. No rule of grammar decides this; only knowledge of the world does. Pairs like this, later called "Winograd schemas", became a test for computer programs that try to understand language. Four years later, M. A. K. Halliday and Ruqaiya Hasan's *Cohesion in English* (1976) gave the first full description of the ties that link English sentences — reference, substitution, ellipsis, conjunction and lexical cohesion — and its categories are still used in teaching reading and writing today.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Who is it? {level=1}
Say what each numbered word refers to. *Li Ming bought a new laptop last week, but (1) it stopped working after two days. (2) He took (3) it back to the shop, and the assistant gave (4) him a new one. Now (5) he is very happy with (6) it.*
::: solution
(1) *it* = the new laptop; (2) *He* = Li Ming; (3) *it* = the laptop (the broken one); (4) *him* = Li Ming; (5) *he* = Li Ming; (6) *it* = the new one, i.e. the replacement laptop. Note that (1) and (6) are both *it* but refer to two different laptops.
:::
:::

::: exercise Choose the connective {level=1}
Choose the best connective: *however*, *for example*, *as a result*, *in addition*. (a) *The hotel was close to the beach. ______, it had a large pool.* (b) *Many fruits are rich in vitamin C. ______, a single orange gives you most of what you need for a day.* (c) *The bridge was closed for repairs. ______, traffic in the city centre was very heavy.* (d) *The test was difficult. ______, almost everyone passed.*
::: solution
(a) *In addition* (another good point); (b) *For example* (a particular case); (c) *As a result* (a consequence); (d) *However* (an unexpected result).
:::
:::

::: exercise Find the chain {level=1 check="3"}
Apart from *a young panda*, how many noun phrases (not pronouns) in this text refer to the same animal? *A young panda escaped from its enclosure at the zoo on Monday. Staff found the animal two hours later in a tree. The cub was hungry but unhurt, and keepers say the little bear is now back with its mother.*
::: solution
*the animal*, *The cub* and *the little bear*: **3** noun phrases. (The pronoun *its*, used twice, also refers to the panda.) Each phrase starts with *the*, which shows that the animal has already been mentioned ([[#rule-lexical-chains]]).
:::

::: exercise What does *this* refer to? {level=2}
Explain what the words in each second sentence refer to. (a) *Many young people now rent bicycles with their phones instead of buying them. This trend has changed the look of city streets.* (b) *Ma Lin forgot her sister's birthday. She has never forgotten it since — that mistake taught her to use a calendar.* (c) *Some schools have stopped giving homework to young children. Not all parents support such a policy.*
::: solution
(a) *This trend* = young people renting bicycles with their phones instead of buying them. (b) *it* = her sister's birthday; *that mistake* = forgetting her sister's birthday. (c) *such a policy* = not giving homework to young children.
:::
:::

::: exercise Meaning decides {level=2}
Decide what *they* refers to in each sentence, and explain why. (a) *The teachers punished the students because they had cheated in the test.* (b) *The teachers punished the students because they wanted to set an example.* (c) *The parents thanked the doctors because they had saved their son's life.*
::: solution
(a) *they* = the students: cheating is a reason to be punished. (b) *they* = the teachers: setting an example is a reason for punishing someone. (c) *they* = the doctors (doctors save lives; *their son* = the parents' son, so *their* refers to the parents). The grammar is the same in (a) and (b); our knowledge of the world decides.
:::
:::

::: exercise Predict and continue {level=2}
Write a sensible continuation for each sentence, using the connective as a guide. (a) *Learning to drive is expensive. Nevertheless, …* (b) *The shop's prices are very low. Consequently, …* (c) *Not everyone enjoys travelling. Some people, for instance, …* (d) *The instructions were written in very technical language. In other words, …*
::: solution
Models: (a) *… most young people in my town want to learn.* (b) *… it is always full of customers.* (c) *… feel nervous in places they do not know.* (d) *… most users could not understand them.* Any continuation is correct if it fits the relation: contrast for (a), result for (b), example for (c), restatement for (d).
:::

::: exercise Map a paragraph {level=3}
Read the paragraph and (a) list every pronoun and *this*/*these* phrase with its referent, (b) list every connective and the relation it shows.

> *Wu Lei started learning the guitar at forty. At first he practised only on Sundays, so he made little progress. Then a friend suggested playing for ten minutes every day instead. This small change made a big difference: within a year, he could play twenty songs. He still finds some of them difficult; however, he now plays in a small band with three of his colleagues, and they perform at the company party every December.*
::: hint
Remember that *this* can refer to a whole idea, and that *them* and *they* may refer to different groups.
:::
::: solution
(a) Reference: *he* (all four times) = Wu Lei; *This small change* = playing for ten minutes every day instead of only on Sundays; *them* (in *some of them*) = the twenty songs; *his* = Wu Lei's; *they* = Wu Lei and his three colleagues (the band).

(b) Connectives: *At first* (time — the beginning); *so* (result: little progress because he practised only on Sundays); *Then* (time — the next stage); *instead* (contrast/replacement: every day rather than only on Sundays); the colon after *difference* (restatement/explanation: what the big difference was); *however* (contrast: although some songs are difficult, he plays in a band); *and* (addition).
:::

::: exercise Repair the thread {level=3}
This text is hard to follow because it repeats nouns and has no connectives. Rewrite it so that it reads smoothly, using pronouns, a synonym or general word, *this* + noun, and at least three connectives.

*Zhao Min wanted to save money. Zhao Min stopped buying coffee at the café near Zhao Min's office. Zhao Min started making coffee at home. Making coffee at home saved Zhao Min about 400 yuan a month. Zhao Min missed talking to the people at the café. Zhao Min now goes to the café once a week.*
::: solution
A model:

*Zhao Min wanted to save money, so she stopped buying coffee at the café near her office and started making it at home instead. This simple change saved her about 400 yuan a month. However, she missed talking to the people at the café. As a result, she now goes there once a week.*

The rewrite uses pronouns (*she*, *her*, *it*, *there*), a summary phrase (*This simple change*), and connectives of result (*so*, *As a result*), replacement (*instead*) and contrast (*However*). (*She* is chosen here; the original does not say whether Zhao Min is a man or a woman, so *he* is equally correct.)
:::
:::
