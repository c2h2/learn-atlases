Read this short passage:

> *Anna opened the fridge. There was half a lemon and a bottle of milk that smelled strange. She looked at the clock — nearly eight — sighed, and picked up her car keys.*

The writer does not say that Anna is hungry, that there is no food in the house, or that she is going out to buy something to eat. But almost every reader understands all three. You put together what the text says (an almost empty fridge, the time, the car keys) with what you know about life (people look in the fridge when they want to eat; eight o'clock is dinner time; car keys mean going somewhere), and you reach a conclusion the text never states.

This is **inference**: reading between the lines. Writers rely on it all the time, because saying everything would be long and dull. They also show their opinions without always stating them, through the words they choose. This chapter explains how to make inferences that the text really supports, how to tell facts from opinions, and how to recognise the writer's attitude and tone.

## Inference

::: definition Inference {#def-inference}
An **inference** (推断) is a conclusion that a reader draws from what a text says, together with the reader's general knowledge, about something the text does not state directly. What a text states directly is **explicit**; what it suggests without stating is **implicit** (or **implied**).
:::

Inferences are not wild guesses. A good inference is one that the text gives you reasons for. In the Anna passage, *She is going to buy food* is a good inference; *She is going to visit her mother* is possible but has no support in the text; *She is a doctor* has nothing to do with it at all.

::: theorem What makes a good inference {#rule-good-inference}
A good inference:

1. is **supported by evidence** in the text — you can point to the words that led you to it;
2. **fits all the evidence**, not just one detail, and nothing in the text contradicts it;
3. is the **most likely** explanation, not merely a possible one;
4. does **not go further** than the evidence allows: *Anna has no food at home* is supported; *Anna never cooks* is not.
:::

::: proof
Readers infer constantly, often without noticing: to connect pronouns with their referents, to work out unknown words, to supply causes and purposes that the writer leaves out. Most of these inferences are needed simply to make the text coherent. The risk is in inferences that go beyond what is needed — the reader adds their own expectations and remembers them as if they were in the text. Bartlett (1932) found exactly this when people retold an unfamiliar story: they changed details to fit their own experience. The four tests above keep inferences tied to the text. Point 4 is the hardest: a statement can be consistent with the text and still be more than the text says.
:::

::: example Making inferences from a short story {#ex-infer-story}
What can you infer about the place, the time and the relationship between the people?

> *Grandpa Liu put another piece of wood on the fire and pulled his coat tighter. Outside, the snow had covered the path to the gate. "Your mother called," he said, without looking up from the fire. "The roads are closed. You'll have to stay with your old grandfather a few more days." Xiao Yu tried not to smile.*
::: solution
For each inference, find the evidence and check that nothing contradicts it ([[#rule-good-inference]]).

**Time of year: winter.** Evidence: *fire*, *pulled his coat tighter*, *snow had covered the path*, *roads are closed*.

**Place: probably a house in the countryside or a small village, far from where Xiao Yu's mother is.** Evidence: a wood fire, *the path to the gate*, roads that close because of snow, a mother who calls on the phone rather than being there. "Far from the city" is likely but not certain; "in the countryside" is the safest statement.

**Relationship: Grandpa Liu is Xiao Yu's grandfather; Xiao Yu is visiting.** Evidence: *your old grandfather*, *stay … a few more days*.

**Feelings: Xiao Yu is happy to stay longer.** Evidence: *tried not to smile* — a smile is a sign of pleasure, and trying to hide it suggests that showing pleasure might seem rude (perhaps because the mother is worried or the situation is a problem). We can infer the pleasure; the reason for hiding it is less certain.

**Not supported:** that Xiao Yu dislikes their mother, or that the grandfather is ill. Nothing in the text points to either.
:::
:::

::: quiz
*When Mr Gao got home, the front door was open, the window beside it was broken, and his television was gone.* Which inference is best supported?
- [ ] Mr Gao forgot to close the door.
- [x] Someone broke into Mr Gao's house and stole the television.
- [ ] Mr Gao sold his television.
- [ ] Mr Gao's neighbours are criminals.
::: solution
The broken window, the open door and the missing television all fit a burglary, and nothing contradicts it. *He forgot to close the door* explains only one detail and not the broken window. *He sold it* does not explain the window. *His neighbours are criminals* goes far beyond the evidence.
:::
:::

::: warning Reading in what is not there
✗ *The writer says Anna is lazy.* (from the Anna passage)

✓ *The text suggests Anna has no food at home and is going out to get some.*

Two common mistakes: (1) stating an inference as if the text said it (*the writer says …*); (2) adding your own opinion or experience as if it were evidence. Nothing in the passage judges Anna; *lazy* is the reader's idea. When you report an inference, use words that show it is an inference: *the text suggests*, *it seems that*, *probably*, *we can infer that*.
:::

## What readers bring to the text

Inference depends on **background knowledge**: what the reader already knows about the world, about the topic and about how texts of this kind usually go. The same text can be easy for one reader and very hard for another, not because of the words but because of what each reader can infer.

::: remark Background knowledge and comprehension
In a well-known experiment, Bransford and Johnson (1972) gave people a short passage that described a familiar activity in very general words, without naming it. Readers who were not told the topic found the passage hard to understand and remembered little of it. Readers who were given the title first — the passage was about washing clothes — found it easy and remembered much more. The words were the same; only the readers' ability to connect them to what they knew had changed. For learners of English, this means that reading about familiar topics is easier, and that a quick look at the title, pictures and first lines ([[reading-skills/reading-with-a-purpose]]) helps by activating what you know.
:::

Background knowledge can also mislead. Readers from different cultures may draw different inferences from the same detail. In the story above, a reader might infer from *Grandpa Liu* and *Xiao Yu* that the story is set in China — probably true, but the text does not say so. A dinner invitation for *seven o'clock* means different things in different places. When an inference depends on cultural knowledge, ask whether the writer could have expected it of their readers.

## Fact and opinion

::: definition Fact and opinion {#def-fact-opinion}
A **statement of fact** says something that can, at least in principle, be checked and shown to be true or false: *The bridge is 1,200 metres long.* *Prices rose by 8% last year.*

A **statement of opinion** expresses a judgement, a feeling or a belief that cannot be proved true or false in the same way: *The bridge is beautiful.* *Prices are far too high.* *The government should lower taxes.*
:::

Note that a statement of fact is not always **true**. *The Great Wall can be seen from the Moon with the naked eye* is a statement of fact in form — it can be checked — but it is false. Deciding whether a statement is a fact or an opinion is a question about **what kind** of statement it is; deciding whether it is true is a separate question.

::: theorem Signals of opinion {#rule-opinion-signals}
A sentence probably expresses an opinion if it contains:

1. **evaluative words**: *good*, *bad*, *best*, *terrible*, *beautiful*, *important*, *excellent*, *disappointing*, *too* (*too expensive*);
2. words of **recommendation or obligation**: *should*, *ought to*, *must*, *need to*;
3. words that show **belief or uncertainty**: *I think*, *I believe*, *in my view*, *probably*, *seem*, *appear*, *clearly*, *obviously*;
4. **predictions** about what will or would happen: *This will ruin the city.*

A sentence probably states a fact if it contains numbers, dates, names, measurements, or descriptions of events that could be observed — but always ask whether the claim **could be checked**.
:::

::: proof
These signals come from the way English expresses evaluation and stance (Biber et al. 1999 call this "stance": the writer's attitudes, feelings, judgements and certainty). They are tendencies, not proof. *Clearly* and *obviously* signal the writer's certainty, but they are often used precisely where the point is a matter of opinion. Numbers can be used in opinions (*Ten yuan is too much for a coffee*), and evaluative words can appear in reported facts (*The film won the prize for best picture* is a fact about a prize). Many sentences contain both: *The new phone, which costs 6,000 yuan, is far too expensive* gives a fact (the price) and an opinion (*far too expensive*).
:::

::: example Sorting fact and opinion {#ex-fact-opinion}
Label each sentence F (fact), O (opinion) or F + O (both), and explain. (a) *The new metro line has 24 stations.* (b) *It is the most useful thing the city has done in years.* (c) *More than 300,000 people used it in the first week.* (d) *The tickets, which cost 4 yuan, are a real bargain.* (e) *The city should build another line to the airport.*
::: solution
Use [[#rule-opinion-signals]] and ask *could this be checked?*

(a) **F.** A number of stations; it could be checked on a map.

(b) **O.** *the most useful thing* is an evaluation; another person could disagree.

(c) **F.** A number of passengers; it could be checked in the company's records.

(d) **F + O.** *which cost 4 yuan* is a fact; *a real bargain* is a judgement.

(e) **O.** *should* expresses a recommendation.
:::
:::

## Attitude and tone

::: definition Attitude and tone {#def-attitude-tone}
The writer's **attitude** is what the writer thinks or feels about the topic: in favour, against, neutral, mixed, worried, enthusiastic. The **tone** is the feeling of the writing itself, as the reader hears it: serious, light, humorous, angry, sad, formal, friendly, ironic.
:::

Writers often show their attitude without stating it, through **word choice**. Many words have a neutral meaning plus a positive or negative feeling — their **connotation** ([[collocation-and-register/connotation]]):

| positive | neutral | negative |
|---|---|---|
| *slim* | *thin* | *skinny* |
| *determined* | *firm* | *stubborn* |
| *thrifty* | *careful with money* | *mean* (BrE), *stingy* |
| *confident* | *sure of himself* | *arrogant* |
| *a lively area* | *a busy area* | *a noisy area* |

Two writers can describe the same facts with very different attitudes:

- *The council has spent 2 million yuan on a new park, giving families a green space in the heart of the city.*
- *The council has spent 2 million yuan of taxpayers' money on yet another park, while the roads are full of holes.*

The facts are the same (2 million yuan, a park). The first writer approves: *giving families a green space*. The second disapproves: *taxpayers' money*, *yet another*, and the contrast with the roads.

::: theorem Clues to the writer's attitude {#rule-attitude-clues}
Look for:

1. **word choice**: words with positive or negative connotations, and labels such as *this mistake* or *this success* ([[reading-skills/following-the-thread]]);
2. **what is emphasised and what is left out**: which facts come first, which get the most space, which are missing;
3. **comparisons and contrasts**: what the topic is set against (*while the roads are full of holes*);
4. **certainty and doubt**: *clearly*, *undoubtedly* versus *perhaps*, *may*, *it is claimed that*;
5. **direct statements of opinion**, often near the beginning or the end.
:::

::: proof
Attitude is rarely in one word; it builds up from many small choices, and the clues work together. Choice of words is the most frequent clue, which is why the same event can be reported very differently by different writers. Selection and order matter because readers give most weight to what comes first and what takes most space ([[reading-skills/main-ideas]]). Expressions of doubt such as *it is claimed that* often show that the writer does not accept a claim, while *clearly* shows that the writer wants the reader to accept one. These clues are described as features of stance in corpus grammars (Biber et al. 1999) and are taken further in [[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]].
:::

::: example Recognising attitude from word choice {#ex-attitude}
Read two short reviews of the same restaurant, written for this lesson. What is each writer's attitude, and which words show it?

> *Review 1: The Golden Spoon is a small, cosy restaurant tucked away in a quiet lane. The menu is short, but every dish is cooked with care, and the friendly owner remembers regular customers by name. It is not cheap, but it is worth every yuan.*

> *Review 2: The Golden Spoon is a cramped little restaurant hidden in a back street. The menu offers very little choice, and prices are high for what you get. The owner chats with his regulars while other customers wait.*
::: solution
Both reviews mention the same facts: a small restaurant, a narrow street, a short menu, high prices, an owner who knows regular customers.

**Review 1: positive.** *cosy* (small but pleasant), *tucked away* (hidden in a nice way), *quiet lane*, *cooked with care*, *friendly*, *remembers … by name*, *worth every yuan*. The negative fact (price) is placed in a concession (*It is not cheap, but …*) and then outweighed.

**Review 2: negative.** *cramped* (small and uncomfortable), *hidden in a back street*, *very little choice*, *high for what you get*. The owner's friendliness to regulars becomes a fault: *while other customers wait*.

The facts are almost identical. The attitude comes from word choice, from what is emphasised, and from how the same detail (the owner knows his regulars) is framed.
:::
:::

::: quiz
What is the writer's attitude? *The new rules, which the company describes as "simplifying" the process, will add three more forms for every customer to complete.*
- [ ] strongly in favour of the new rules
- [x] doubtful or critical of the new rules
- [ ] neutral, with no opinion
- [ ] confused about what the rules are
::: solution
The writer puts *simplifying* in quotation marks and gives it as the company's description (*which the company describes as*), then gives a fact that contradicts it: three more forms. This contrast shows that the writer does not believe the rules simplify anything.
:::
:::

::: example Naming the tone {#ex-tone}
Both texts below, written for this lesson, are about the same problem: a lift that has not worked for weeks. Describe the tone of each and say which features create it.

> *Text A: For the third week running, residents of Building 5 have climbed nine floors to reach their homes. Elderly people are trapped in their flats. Repeated complaints have produced nothing but promises. This is unacceptable, and the management company must act now.*

> *Text B: Good news for the residents of Building 5: they are now the fittest people in the city. Thanks to the lift, which has been "under repair" for three weeks, even Grandma Zhao on the ninth floor can now climb stairs faster than her grandson.*
::: solution
**Text A: serious and angry.** Features: plain facts stated forcefully (*For the third week running*, *nine floors*); strong words (*trapped*, *unacceptable*); a contrast between *complaints* and *nothing but promises*; a direct demand with *must act now*. There is no joke anywhere.

**Text B: humorous and ironic, but still critical.** Features: the opening *Good news* and *the fittest people in the city* present a problem as if it were a benefit — the literal meaning does not fit the situation, so we infer irony. Quotation marks around *under repair* suggest that no real repair is happening. The picture of an old woman beating her grandson on the stairs is meant to make readers smile.

Both writers disapprove of the situation; their **attitude** is similar, but their **tone** is very different.
:::
:::

Some writing says the opposite of what it means, to make a point or a joke: *Great — another meeting about meetings.* This is **irony**. Irony is understood by inference: the reader notices that the literal meaning does not fit the situation. Irony and understatement are discussed in [[pragmatics/irony-understatement]].

::: quiz
*After three hours in the traffic jam, Li Wei arrived at the party just as the last guests were leaving. "Perfect timing," he said.* What does Li Wei mean?
- [ ] He arrived at exactly the right time.
- [x] He arrived far too late.
- [ ] He enjoyed the traffic jam.
- [ ] The party had not started yet.
::: solution
He arrived as the party was ending, which is the opposite of perfect timing. The literal meaning does not fit the situation, so we infer that he is being ironic: his timing was very bad.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Understatement.** English speakers, especially in British English, often say less than they mean: *not bad* can mean ‘good’, *a bit of a problem* can mean ‘a serious problem’, and *quite good* can be lukewarm in British English (‘fairly good’) but positive in American English. Chinese also has polite indirectness, but the particular expressions differ, so a learner may take an English understatement literally.
- **Connotation is not translated by the dictionary.** A Chinese translation often gives the neutral meaning only: *thin*, *slim* and *skinny* may all be glossed as 瘦. Learner's dictionaries mark many words as *approving* or *disapproving*; use these labels when you want to understand a writer's attitude ([[core-vocabulary/using-a-dictionary]]).
- **Familiar school questions.** Chinese school reading often asks for the 中心思想 and the 作者态度 of a text. These are exactly the skills of this and the previous chapter. The difference to watch is that in English the evidence for attitude is often in small words — *yet another*, *so-called*, *claims*, quotation marks — that are easy to miss when reading for content alone.
- **Culture and inference.** Inferences that depend on cultural knowledge (what a gift means, what is polite, what time "evening" starts) may not transfer between Chinese and English contexts. When an inference feels uncertain, look for more evidence in the text before relying on your own expectations.
:::

::: history
Psychologists began to study inference in reading in the twentieth century. In *Remembering* (1932), Frederic Bartlett asked English university students to read and later retell *The War of the Ghosts*, a story from a Native American tradition that was unfamiliar to them. Their retellings became shorter and more conventional, and details that did not fit their expectations were changed or dropped; Bartlett explained this with the idea of a **schema**, an organised body of knowledge that readers use to understand and remember. Forty years later, John Bransford and Marcia Johnson (1972) showed how strongly comprehension depends on having the right schema active, with a passage that was nearly meaningless without its title. In philosophy, Paul Grice's lecture *Logic and Conversation* (published 1975) explained how listeners infer what speakers mean beyond what they say, an idea taken up in [[pragmatics/meaning-beyond-words]].
:::

## Where this leads

Inference in conversation — what speakers mean beyond their words — is the subject of [[pragmatics/meaning-beyond-words]], and irony of [[pragmatics/irony-understatement]]. Different text types show attitude in different ways, from neutral news reports to opinion pieces: [[reading-skills/text-types]]. In academic texts, writers show certainty and doubt with hedges and boosters: [[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]], and claims must be weighed against their evidence: [[academic-reading/claims-and-evidence]]. Connotation is covered in [[collocation-and-register/connotation]].

::: summary
- An **inference** is a conclusion from what the text says plus the reader's knowledge, about something the text does not state.
- A good inference is supported by evidence, fits all of it, is the most likely explanation and goes no further than the evidence ([[#rule-good-inference]]).
- **Background knowledge** makes inference possible; it can also mislead, so check inferences against the text.
- A **fact** can be checked; an **opinion** is a judgement. Evaluative words, *should*, *I think*, *probably* and predictions signal opinion; many sentences contain both ([[#rule-opinion-signals]]).
- **Attitude** is the writer's view of the topic; **tone** is the feeling of the writing. Look at word choice and connotation, emphasis and omission, comparisons, certainty and doubt ([[#rule-attitude-clues]]).
- **Irony** says the opposite of what it means; the reader infers it from a mismatch with the situation.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Explicit or implied? {level=1}
Read the text, then say whether each statement is stated (S), implied (I) or not supported (N).

> *Zhou Ping closed his laptop, stretched, and looked out at the dark street. The office was silent; even the cleaners had gone home. He had finished the report at last.*

(a) Zhou Ping finished a report. (b) It was late in the evening or at night. (c) Zhou Ping works too hard every day. (d) Zhou Ping was the last person in the office.
::: solution
(a) S (*He had finished the report at last*). (b) I (*dark street*, *the office was silent*, *even the cleaners had gone home*). (c) N (we know only about this day; *every day* goes beyond the evidence). (d) I (*The office was silent; even the cleaners had gone home* strongly suggests that everyone else had left).
:::
:::

::: exercise Fact or opinion? {level=1}
Label each sentence F (fact), O (opinion) or F + O. (a) *Hangzhou is in Zhejiang Province.* (b) *Hangzhou is the most beautiful city in China.* (c) *The West Lake, which covers about 6 square kilometres, is a wonderful place for a walk.* (d) *Visitors should come in spring.* (e) *More visitors come in October than in any other month.*
::: solution
(a) F; (b) O (*the most beautiful*); (c) F + O (the size is a fact; *a wonderful place* is an opinion); (d) O (*should*); (e) F (a claim about numbers that could be checked — whether it is true is a separate question).
:::
:::

::: exercise Positive, neutral or negative? {level=1}
Say whether each word is positive (+), neutral (0) or negative (−) when used about a person. (a) *curious* (b) *nosy* (c) *talkative* (d) *chatty* (e) *childish* (f) *young* (g) *generous* (h) *wasteful*
::: solution
(a) + or 0 (interested in learning); (b) − (too interested in other people's business); (c) 0, sometimes slightly −; (d) + or 0 (friendly and informal); (e) − (behaving like a child in a bad way); (f) 0; (g) +; (h) −. Some words can shift with context — *curious* can also mean ‘strange’ — so the context always matters.
:::

::: exercise Which inference? {level=2}
Read the text and decide which inferences are well supported, and why.

> *The interview was at ten. At five to ten, Sun Jie was still standing at the bus stop, checking her phone every few seconds. When the bus finally came, she ran up the stairs to the top deck, sat down and closed her eyes.*

(a) Sun Jie was going to be late for her interview. (b) Sun Jie did not want the job. (c) Sun Jie was worried or stressed. (d) The bus was late.
::: solution
(a) **Well supported**: at 9:55 she was still at the bus stop, so she cannot arrive by ten. (b) **Not supported**: nothing suggests she did not want the job; her checking and hurrying suggest the opposite. (c) **Well supported**: checking her phone every few seconds, *finally*, running, closing her eyes. (d) **Likely but not certain**: *finally* suggests the wait was long, but we do not know the timetable; she may simply have left home late. A careful answer says *probably*.
:::
:::

::: exercise Same facts, different attitude {level=2}
Rewrite this neutral sentence twice, once with a positive attitude and once with a negative one. Do not change the facts. *The city has built a 20-storey office tower in the old market square.*
::: solution
Models:

Positive: *The city has given the old market square a striking new landmark: a modern 20-storey office tower that will bring jobs and life to the area.*

Negative: *The city has dropped a 20-storey office block into the old market square, blocking the view of the historic streets around it.*

The facts (a 20-storey office building in the old market square) stay the same; word choice (*striking*, *landmark*, *bring life* / *dropped*, *block*, *blocking the view*) and added consequences create the attitude.
:::
:::

::: exercise Clues to attitude {level=2}
What is the writer's attitude to the plan, and what clues show it?

> *The so-called "Green City" plan promises to plant 10,000 trees by next year. Yet the same council cut down 300 old trees last spring to widen a road. Perhaps this time the promises will be kept.*
::: solution
**Attitude: doubtful, critical (sceptical).** Clues: *so-called* (the writer does not accept the name); quotation marks around *Green City*; *promises* (not *will plant*); the contrast introduced by *Yet* with what the council did before; and the last sentence, which is ironic — *Perhaps this time* suggests that the writer expects the promises not to be kept, as before.
:::

::: exercise Explain an inference {level=3}
Read the passage and answer: How does Mrs Ma feel about her son's decision? Give at least three pieces of evidence, and say what you cannot infer.

> *"So you're really going," Mrs Ma said. She folded his shirts again, though they were already folded, and put a bag of dried fruit between them. "They'll have proper food in Canada, I suppose." At the door she held his arm for a moment longer than necessary, then pushed him gently towards the taxi. "Go on. You'll miss your plane."*
::: hint
Look at what Mrs Ma does, not only what she says. Why would someone fold shirts that are already folded?
:::
::: solution
**Inference: she is sad that he is leaving and worried about him, but she accepts his decision and supports him.**

Evidence:
- *So you're really going* — she has perhaps hoped he would not go, but accepts it.
- She folds shirts that are already folded — she is keeping busy, which suggests nervousness or sadness, and wants to care for him.
- She puts in dried fruit and says *They'll have proper food in Canada, I suppose* — she worries he will not eat well; *I suppose* shows doubt.
- She holds his arm longer than necessary — she does not want to let go.
- She pushes him gently and tells him to go — she supports the decision in the end.

**Cannot infer:** why he is going (study? work?), how long he will stay, or whether she has argued with him about it. A good answer separates what the evidence shows from what it does not.
:::
:::

::: exercise Write between the lines {level=3}
Write a short passage (60–90 words) in which a character feels one of these emotions without the word, or any synonym, being used: *embarrassed*, *proud*, *disappointed*. Then write one or two sentences listing the clues you used. Ask a classmate to name the emotion.
::: solution
Answers will differ. A model for *proud*:

*When the results were put on the wall, Lin's father read the list twice, from the bottom up, before he found her name near the top. He took a photo of it with his phone, then another. On the way home he stopped to talk to three neighbours, and each time, somehow, the conversation came round to exams.*

Clues: reading the list twice and taking two photos (he wants to keep the moment), talking to neighbours and steering the conversation to exams (he wants to tell people), her name *near the top* (a good result). A good answer shows the emotion through actions and details that support one clear inference.
:::
