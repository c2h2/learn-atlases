Look at these four openings, all written for this lesson:

> *A. Preheat the oven to 180°C. Mix the flour and sugar in a large bowl.*
>
> *B. Firefighters rescued six people from a burning flat in Wuhan early on Sunday morning.*
>
> *C. The old woman had lived alone at the end of the lane for as long as anyone could remember.*
>
> *D. It is time we stopped pretending that homework helps young children learn.*

You know at once what kind of text each one begins: a recipe, a news report, a story and an opinion piece. You did not need to read to the end. You recognised them from a few features — the verb forms, the kind of information, the way the writer addresses you. And as soon as you recognised the type, you knew what to expect: steps in order, the facts of an event, a plot with characters, an argument.

Recognising the **type** of a text is one of the fastest ways to understand it. It tells you why the text was written, where the important information will be, and how to read it. This chapter describes six common types of text — narratives, descriptions, instructions, news reports, reviews and opinion pieces — and the features that signal each.

## What makes a text type

::: definition Text type and genre {#def-text-type}
A **text type** (语篇类型) is a group of texts that share a main **purpose** — to tell a story, to describe, to instruct, to inform, to evaluate, to persuade — and therefore tend to share a **structure** and typical **language features**. A **genre** (体裁) is a more specific, socially recognised kind of text with its own conventions: a recipe, a news report, a film review, a job advertisement, a letter of complaint.
:::

The two terms overlap, and different books use them in slightly different ways. In this chapter, *text type* means the broad group and *genre* the particular kind. A recipe and a set of instructions for a printer are different genres of the same broad type: instructions.

::: theorem Three questions to identify a text type {#rule-identify-type}
To identify the type of a text, ask:

1. **Purpose.** What is the writer trying to do — tell, describe, instruct, inform, evaluate or persuade?
2. **Structure.** How is it organised — in time order, by place, in numbered steps, from most to least important, or as a claim with reasons?
3. **Language.** Which tense and person dominate (past or present; *I*, *you* or *he/she/they*)? Are there imperatives, evaluative adjectives, dates and numbers, quotations, *should* and *must*?

Look also at the **layout**: headlines, numbered lists, star ratings, a byline (the writer's name), a date.
:::

::: proof
Texts of a given type look alike because they do the same job for readers who have the same needs. A reader following instructions needs the steps in the right order, so instructions are numbered and use imperatives; a reader of the news wants the most important facts first, so reports put them in the first sentence. Corpus research shows that these features cluster: Biber (1988) found that texts can be described by how often they use groups of features such as past tense and third-person pronouns (typical of narratives) or *should*, *must* and conditional clauses (typical of persuasion). Real texts often mix types — a review contains description, an opinion piece contains a short narrative — so the question is which purpose is **main**.
:::

## Six common types

The table summarises the six types. The sections that follow give a short example of each.

| type | main purpose | typical structure | typical language |
|---|---|---|---|
| narrative | to tell a story | events in time order: setting, problem, events, ending | past tenses; *he*, *she*, *they* or *I*; time words (*then*, *later*, *suddenly*); dialogue |
| description | to show what someone or something is like | by parts or by place (left to right, near to far) | adjectives; prepositions of place; *have*, *there is*; present or past simple |
| instructions | to tell the reader how to do something | numbered steps in order; a list of what you need | imperatives (*Mix*, *Press*); *you*; sequence words (*first*, *next*); exact numbers |
| news report | to inform about a recent event | most important facts first, then details, quotations and background | past simple and present perfect; third person; names, places, dates, numbers; *said*, *according to* |
| review | to describe and evaluate a product, book, film, place … | what it is, what it is like, strengths and weaknesses, a recommendation | evaluative adjectives; present simple; *but*, *although*; a rating |
| opinion piece | to persuade the reader to accept a view | a claim, reasons and evidence, an opposing view answered, a conclusion | *I*, *we*; *should*, *must*; *because*, *however*; questions to the reader |

### Narratives

> *The old woman had lived alone at the end of the lane for as long as anyone could remember. Every morning she fed the cats; every evening she sat at her window. Then, one Tuesday in March, the window stayed dark. By Thursday the children of the lane had decided to find out why.*

Past tenses (*had lived*, *fed*, *stayed*, *had decided*), a setting (*the end of the lane*), a change (*Then, one Tuesday*) and a question that makes the reader want to continue. Narratives are read for the plot and the characters; their main ideas are usually implied rather than stated in topic sentences ([[reading-skills/inference]]). Stories and novels are the subject of the course *Reading Literature* ([[reading-literature/narrative]]).

### Descriptions

> *The flat is on the fourth floor of an old building near the river. The front door opens into a bright living room with two large windows. On the left is a small kitchen; on the right, a short corridor leads to the bedroom and the bathroom.*

Present simple (*is*, *opens*, *leads*), prepositions of place (*on the fourth floor*, *on the left*), adjectives (*bright*, *large*, *small*), and an order that follows a visitor through the flat. A reader of a description builds a picture; it often helps to draw it.

::: example Turning a description into a picture {#ex-description}
Using the description of the flat, answer: (a) You open the front door. What do you see first? (b) Where is the kitchen? (c) How do you get to the bathroom? (d) Can you see the river from the flat?
::: solution
Follow the description in the order the writer gives it, which is the order of a visitor walking in.

(a) The living room: *The front door opens into a bright living room with two large windows.*

(b) To the left of the living room: *On the left is a small kitchen.* (The point of view is that of someone standing in the living room, facing in from the front door.)

(c) Through the living room, then right, along the short corridor: *on the right, a short corridor leads to the bedroom and the bathroom.*

(d) **The text does not say.** The building is *near the river*, and the living room has large windows, but nothing tells us what they look out on. Drawing the plan makes this gap visible; a reader who answers "yes" has made an inference that goes beyond the evidence ([[reading-skills/inference]]).
:::
:::

### Instructions

> **How to reset your password**
>
> *1. Go to the login page and click "Forgot password".*
>
> *2. Enter the email address you used to register.*
>
> *3. Open the email we send you and click the link within 30 minutes.*
>
> *4. Choose a new password of at least eight characters.*

A title starting *How to*, numbered steps, imperatives (*Go*, *Enter*, *Open*, *Choose*), exact details (*30 minutes*, *eight characters*). Instructions must be read closely, in order, and usually only once you are ready to act ([[reading-skills/reading-with-a-purpose]]).

### News reports

> *SIX RESCUED FROM FLAT FIRE*
>
> *Firefighters rescued six people from a burning flat in Wuhan early on Sunday morning. The fire started in the kitchen of a fifth-floor flat at about 3 a.m. and spread to two neighbouring flats. All six residents, including two children, were taken to hospital but were not seriously hurt. "The smoke alarm woke us up just in time," said one resident, Mr Tang. Investigators believe the fire was caused by a faulty electric heater.*

::: theorem How a news report is organised {#rule-news}
Most news reports follow the **inverted pyramid**:

1. the **headline** gives the event in very few words;
2. the **first sentence** (the **lead**) answers most of the main questions: *who*, *what*, *where*, *when*, and often *how* or *why*;
3. the following sentences add **details** in order of importance: numbers, consequences, quotations from people involved, background;
4. the least important information comes **last**.

So the main idea of a report is almost always in its first sentence, and you can stop reading whenever you have enough.
:::

::: proof
The inverted pyramid lets readers get the essential facts quickly and lets editors shorten a story from the end without losing anything important. It is the standard structure taught in journalism and is used in news writing in English, Chinese (倒金字塔) and many other languages. Not all news writing follows it: feature articles often begin with a scene or a person, and longer reports may tell events in time order after the lead. But for ordinary news, the first sentence carries the main idea.
:::

**Headlines** have their own grammar, designed to save space. Knowing it prevents misunderstandings:

- the **present simple** for past events: *Firefighters rescue six* = firefighters rescued six;
- a **past participle** for the passive, without *be*: *Six rescued from fire* = six people were rescued;
- ***to* + verb** for the future: *Mayor to open new bridge* = the mayor will open a new bridge;
- **articles and *be*** left out: *Bridge closed after storm* = the bridge has been closed after a storm;
- **noun strings**: *City bus fare rise row* = an argument about a rise in city bus fares ([[nouns-and-articles/noun-phrases]]).

::: example Reading a news report {#ex-news}
Use the inverted pyramid to answer questions about the fire report. (a) Which sentence gives the main facts? (b) Answer *who*, *what*, *where* and *when* from that sentence alone. (c) What do the later sentences add? (d) Rewrite the headline as a full sentence.
::: solution
(a) The first sentence: *Firefighters rescued six people from a burning flat in Wuhan early on Sunday morning.* It is the lead ([[#rule-news]]).

(b) *Who?* firefighters and six people. *What?* a rescue from a fire. *Where?* a flat in Wuhan. *When?* early on Sunday morning.

(c) Details in order of importance: how the fire started and spread (sentence 2); what happened to the people (sentence 3 — taken to hospital, not seriously hurt); a quotation from a resident (sentence 4); the probable cause (sentence 5).

(d) *SIX RESCUED FROM FLAT FIRE* = *Six people were rescued from a fire in a flat.* The participle *rescued* is passive, with *were* left out; *FLAT FIRE* is a noun + noun phrase meaning ‘a fire in a flat’.
:::
:::

::: quiz
What does the headline *Hospital to close children's ward* mean?
- [ ] The hospital has closed its children's ward.
- [x] The hospital is going to close its children's ward.
- [ ] Someone went to the hospital to close a ward.
- [ ] The children's ward is close to the hospital.
::: solution
In headlines, *to* + verb refers to the future: the hospital *will* (or *is going to*) close the ward. *Close* here is a verb (/kləʊz/, GA /kloʊz/), not the adjective *close* ‘near’ (/kləʊs/, GA /kloʊs/).
:::
:::

### Reviews

> *The Lantern Festival, the new film by director Zhang Hui, follows three generations of a family over one evening in a small southern town. The photography is beautiful, and the two lead actors are excellent. However, the story moves very slowly, and the last half hour adds little. Worth seeing for its images, but be ready to be patient. ★★★☆☆*

A review describes (what the film is about) and evaluates (*beautiful*, *excellent*, *moves very slowly*, *adds little*), balances strengths and weaknesses (*However*), and ends with a recommendation and often a rating. Read a review for the writer's overall judgement — usually at the end — and for the reasons behind it, which may matter more to you than the rating.

### Opinion pieces

> *It is time we stopped pretending that homework helps young children learn. Children of seven or eight already spend six hours a day at school. When they come home, they need to play, read for pleasure and spend time with their families. Supporters of homework say it teaches discipline. But discipline can be learned in many ways, and a tired child learns very little at nine in the evening. Schools should end homework for children under ten.*

An opinion piece (in newspapers, an **editorial** if it gives the paper's own view, or a **column** if it is signed by a writer) states a position (*It is time we stopped pretending …*), gives reasons, mentions the opposing view (*Supporters of homework say …*) and answers it (*But …*), and ends with a recommendation (*should*). Read it as an argument: find the claim, the reasons and the evidence, and ask whether they convince you ([[academic-reading/claims-and-evidence]]).

Instructions sit outside all three circles: they tell you what to do. Real texts often spread over several regions; try *events & facts* to shade the region of news reports.

::: example Identifying the type {#ex-identify}
Identify the type of each extract and give two features that show it. (a) *Turn off the power before you remove the cover. Then unscrew the four screws at the corners.* (b) *The library, which reopened yesterday after a two-year renovation, will be free for all residents, the city council announced.* (c) *I lay awake listening to the rain. Somewhere downstairs, a door opened and closed.* (d) *The phone's camera is outstanding, but the battery barely lasts a day. If photos matter more to you than battery life, it is a good buy.*
::: solution
Use the three questions of [[#rule-identify-type]].

(a) **Instructions.** Imperatives (*Turn off*, *unscrew*); sequence word (*Then*); exact details (*four screws*).

(b) **News report.** Recent event (*yesterday*); facts and third person (*the library*, *the city council*); reporting verb (*announced*); future plan stated as fact (*will be free*).

(c) **Narrative.** Past tenses (*lay*, *opened*); first person (*I*); events in time and a mysterious detail that makes us want to continue.

(d) **Review.** Evaluative words (*outstanding*, *barely lasts*); strengths and weaknesses joined by *but*; a recommendation (*it is a good buy*), with a condition.
:::
:::

::: theorem Reading according to type {#rule-read-by-type}
Once you know the type, read it accordingly:

1. **narrative** — follow the characters and the order of events; infer motives and feelings;
2. **description** — build a picture; note the organising principle (place, parts);
3. **instructions** — read closely and in order; check numbers and conditions before acting;
4. **news report** — get the main facts from the headline and the first sentence; read on only for the details you need; separate facts from the opinions of people quoted;
5. **review** — find the overall judgement and the reasons for it; decide which reasons matter to you;
6. **opinion piece** — find the claim, the reasons and the answer to the opposing view; judge whether the evidence is enough.
:::

::: proof
Each type puts its main idea in a predictable place and asks a different kind of attention of the reader, which is why the purpose-based reading of [[reading-skills/reading-with-a-purpose]] and the type of text work together. Swales (1990) described genres as defined by their communicative purpose and showed that readers who know a genre's conventions find its key information faster. Knowing the type also protects against misreading: an opinion presented in the language of a report is still an opinion.
:::

::: example Report or opinion? {#ex-report-opinion}
Both extracts are about the same event. Which is a news report and which is an opinion piece? Which sentences in the report are facts, and which give someone's opinion?

> *Extract 1: The city council voted on Monday to close the old swimming pool on Park Road at the end of the year. Repairs would cost an estimated 12 million yuan, a council spokesperson said. "We simply cannot afford it," said Councillor Lin. Local resident Ms Ho said the decision was "a disaster for families".*

> *Extract 2: Closing the Park Road pool is a short-sighted decision that the council will regret. Twelve million yuan sounds a lot, but it is less than the city spent on a new car park last year. Where will our children learn to swim now?*
::: solution
**Extract 1 is a news report.** It reports a recent event (*voted on Monday*), in the third person, with a cost figure and quotations. The facts are: the vote, the closure date, the estimated cost (attributed: *a council spokesperson said*). The opinions in it belong to people who are **quoted**: Councillor Lin (*We simply cannot afford it*) and Ms Ho (*a disaster for families*). The reporter does not give an opinion.

**Extract 2 is an opinion piece.** The writer judges the decision directly (*short-sighted*, *will regret*), uses a comparison as evidence (*less than … a new car park*), uses *our*, and ends with a question to the reader (*Where will our children learn to swim now?*).

The important skill: in a report, opinions appear inside quotation marks with a name; in an opinion piece, they are the writer's own.
:::
:::

::: warning Taking an opinion for a report
✗ *The newspaper says the decision was a disaster for families.*

✓ *A local resident, quoted in the newspaper, said the decision was "a disaster for families".*

In news reports, opinions are usually given by quoted people, not by the reporter. Reporting a quotation as the paper's own view changes who is responsible for the claim. The opposite mistake is also common: reading an opinion piece, a sponsored article or an advertisement as if it were neutral news. Check the type before you trust the content — look for a byline with *comment* or *opinion*, words such as *sponsored* or *advertisement*, and the language of persuasion (*should*, *must*, evaluative adjectives).
:::

::: quiz
Where is the writer's overall judgement usually found in a review?
- [ ] in the first sentence, which describes the product
- [ ] in the middle, among the details
- [x] at the end, often with a rating or recommendation
- [ ] nowhere — reviews give only facts
::: solution
Reviews usually start by saying what is being reviewed, then describe and evaluate it, and end with an overall judgement or recommendation, often with a rating. This is different from news reports, which put the main point first.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **School categories.** Chinese schools usually teach three broad types: 记叙文 (narrative), 说明文 (exposition, which includes descriptions, instructions and explanations) and 议论文 (argument). These correspond well to the broad types of this chapter. English teaching tends to name more specific genres — the review, the news report, the letter of complaint — each with its own conventions; it is worth learning these, because they tell you where to look for information.
- **News structure is shared.** Chinese news writing also uses the inverted pyramid (倒金字塔), so the habit of reading the lead first transfers directly.
- **Headline grammar is different.** Chinese headlines also compress, but English headline grammar — present simple for the past, *to* for the future, a participle for the passive — has to be learned. A Chinese reader who takes *Minister quits* as a habit (‘the minister quits regularly’) instead of an event (‘the minister has resigned’) has misread the headline's tense.
- **Reviews and opinion.** Online reviews in English are often more direct than Chinese readers expect, with strong evaluative words. They are still opinions; the reasons given are more useful than the strength of the language.
:::

::: history
People have classified kinds of writing since ancient times, but the modern study of text types and genres began in the twentieth century. In *Variation across Speech and Writing* (1988), Douglas Biber counted dozens of grammatical features in large collections of texts and showed that they cluster: narrative texts use more past tense and third-person pronouns, persuasive texts more modals such as *should* and *must*. Two years later, John Swales's *Genre Analysis* (1990) defined a genre by its communicative purpose and showed how genres such as the research article are built from typical "moves". In language teaching, the "genre approach", developed especially in Australian schools in the 1980s and 1990s, taught pupils the structure and language of each type explicitly. These ideas reach learners in the form used in this chapter: recognise the purpose, expect the structure, notice the language.
:::

::: summary
- A **text type** groups texts by main purpose — telling, describing, instructing, informing, evaluating, persuading; a **genre** is a specific kind with its own conventions ([[#def-text-type]]).
- Identify the type from purpose, structure, language and layout ([[#rule-identify-type]]).
- **Narratives**: past tense, events in time order. **Descriptions**: adjectives and place. **Instructions**: imperatives and numbered steps.
- **News reports** follow the inverted pyramid: headline, a lead that answers who, what, where and when, then details ([[#rule-news]]). Headlines use the present for the past and *to* for the future.
- **Reviews** describe and evaluate and end with a judgement; **opinion pieces** argue a claim with reasons and answer the opposing view.
- Read each type according to its purpose ([[#rule-read-by-type]]), and do not take a quoted opinion or an opinion piece for the reporter's facts.
:::

::: exercise Find the structure {level=2}
Put the parts of this opinion piece in the usual order (claim, reasons, opposing view, answer, recommendation). (a) *Some people say that cycling in the city is too dangerous.* (b) *Cities should build more cycle lanes.* (c) *But the danger comes from cars, and separate lanes remove most of it.* (d) *More cycle lanes would mean less traffic and cleaner air.* (e) *The city must put cycle lanes in next year's budget.*

(b) claim — (d) reason — (a) opposing view — (c) answer — (e) recommendation. Order (d) before (a) is usual, but the opposing view sometimes comes straight after the claim; any order in which the answer follows the opposing view and the recommendation comes last is acceptable.

In this news report, which statements are facts reported by the journalist, and which are opinions of quoted people?

::: solution
Facts reported by the journalist: the centre opened on Friday; it has 120 shops; around 15,000 people visited on the first day (attributed to the owners: *according to the owners*). Opinions of others: Wu Fang's *It's the best thing that has happened to this area*; the shopkeepers' fear that they will lose customers (reported, not quoted, but still their view). (a)

> *RIVERSIDE BOOKS TO CLOSE AFTER 30 YEARS*
>
> *Riverside Books, the city's only English-language bookshop, will close on 30 June after almost three decades. Owner Mr Fu said the rent had doubled in three years. "We cannot continue," he said. The report uses headline grammar (*to close*), a lead with who, what and when, third person and a quotation; the opinion piece uses evaluative language (*shameful mistake*), a strong claim and no attributed sources.

::: exercise Write instructions {level=3}
Write clear instructions (5–7 numbered steps) for something you know how to do — making a cup of tea, sending a photo by email, folding a paper boat. Use a *How to* title, imperatives and sequence words. Then exchange with a classmate: can they follow your instructions without asking a question?
::: solution
Answers will differ. A model:

> **How to make a cup of green tea**
>
> *1. Boil some water and let it cool for two or three minutes.*
>
> *2. Put one teaspoon of green tea leaves into a cup.*
>
> *3. Pour a little water over the leaves and wait ten seconds.*
>
> *4. Fill the cup with water.*
>
> *5. Leave the tea for two minutes, then drink it.*
>
> *6. Add more hot water when the cup is half empty.*

A good answer has a title, numbered steps in the right order, imperatives, and exact details where they matter (times, amounts).
