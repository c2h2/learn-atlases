Two students start the same year with the same level of English. The first reads one difficult article a week, slowly, with a dictionary, writing every new word in a notebook. In a year she reads about 50 texts of 1,000 words: **50,000 words**. The second reads easy books for twenty minutes every evening, at about 120 words a minute, looking up almost nothing. In a year he reads about **876,000 words** — more than seventeen times as much.

The first student studies English through reading. The second student *reads* in English. Both are useful, but they do different things. Careful study teaches you how a difficult text works; the earlier chapters of this course are about that kind of reading. Reading a lot of easy text does something careful study cannot: it lets you meet the same words and patterns again and again, in many contexts, until you recognise them without effort. This is how readers become fluent.

This last chapter explains what **extensive reading** is, why it works, how to choose texts at the right level and how to build a habit that lasts.

## Two kinds of reading

::: definition Intensive and extensive reading {#def-extensive}
**Intensive reading** (精读) is the slow, careful study of a short text, usually difficult, with attention to its words, grammar and structure. The aim is to understand that text completely and to learn language from it.

**Extensive reading** (泛读) is reading **large amounts** of **easy** text, quickly and for the meaning, usually material the reader has chosen and enjoys. The aim is not to study the text but to read a lot: to understand the story or the information, to build speed and to meet known language again and again.
:::

The two are not opposites; they are partners. Intensive reading teaches new language and new skills — the strategies of [[reading-skills/words-from-context]], [[reading-skills/following-the-thread]] and [[reading-skills/long-sentences]]. Extensive reading gives those skills and words thousands of hours of practice in real reading. A learner who does only intensive reading knows a lot about English but reads slowly; a learner who does only extensive reading reads fluently but may not learn new, difficult language. Most learners in China have had plenty of the first and very little of the second.

| | intensive reading | extensive reading |
|---|---|---|
| amount | a short text | many books and articles |
| difficulty | difficult; many new words | easy; very few new words |
| speed | slow | fast, comfortable |
| aim | learn language from the text | read for meaning and pleasure |
| dictionary | often | rarely |
| who chooses | usually the teacher | usually the reader |
| what happens after | exercises, questions | usually nothing; the next book |

::: theorem Why extensive reading works {#rule-why}
Reading a lot of easy text helps in three ways:

1. **Repetition.** A word or phrase needs to be met many times, in different contexts, before it is known well. Only a large amount of reading provides these meetings.
2. **Fluency.** Recognising words and sentences becomes faster and more automatic with practice, which frees attention for understanding.
3. **Confidence and habit.** Easy, interesting reading is enjoyable, so learners read more, which gives more repetition and fluency.
:::

::: proof
**Repetition.** Studies of learning words from reading agree that one meeting with a word is rarely enough. Waring and Takaki (2003), for example, gave learners a graded reader that contained words they did not know and tested them afterwards: the learners picked up the meanings of some of the words, especially words they met more often, but forgot many of them within three months. The conclusion is not that reading is useless for vocabulary, but that it works through **quantity**: a single book teaches little; hundreds of books teach a great deal. Nation and Wang (1999), who studied the vocabulary of a series of graded readers, suggested that learners need to read a lot — of the order of a graded reader every week or two — to meet new words often enough.

**Fluency.** Reading speed and automatic word recognition develop with practice, like any skill, and practice at a comfortable level is the only kind that can be done in large amounts.

**Habit.** In a well-known study in Fiji, Elley and Mangubhai (1983) gave primary school classes a large number of illustrated storybooks in English, which the children read and shared with their teachers. After a year, these children had made clearly larger gains in English reading than children in classes taught with the usual course, and the difference grew in the second year. Many later studies, collected by Day and Bamford (1998) and Nation and Waring (2020), point the same way.
:::

## Graded readers

For most learners, the most important material for extensive reading is the **graded reader**.

::: definition Graded reader {#def-graded-reader}
A **graded reader** (分级读物) is a book written or rewritten for learners, in which the vocabulary and grammar are kept within the limits of a **level**. Each series has several levels, described by the number of headwords used (for example, about 200 at the lowest level and 2,000–3,000 at the highest) or by a level of a scale such as the Common European Framework (A1–C1). Many graded readers are simplified versions of well-known novels; others are new stories written at the level.
:::

Graded readers are short. Books at the lowest levels may be only a few thousand words long, and books at the highest levels a few tens of thousands; a typical adult novel is often 80,000 words or more. This means a learner can finish a book in a few days and feel the satisfaction of having read a whole book in English — an important part of the motivation that keeps extensive reading going.

Here is the opening of a story, written for this lesson, at two levels:

> *Version A. Anna lives in a small town by the sea. She is sixteen. Every morning she walks to school along the beach. One morning she sees something on the sand. It is a bottle, and there is a letter in it.*

> *Version B. Anna had lived all her sixteen years in the little harbour town, and she knew every rock and pool along the shore. So when, one grey October morning on her way to school, she noticed something glinting among the seaweed, she stopped. It was a bottle, sealed with wax, and through the green glass she could see a rolled-up sheet of paper.*

::: example What makes a text easier {#ex-grading}
Compare versions A and B. Which features make version A easier to read? What does version B give the reader that version A does not?
::: solution
**Version A is easier because of:**

- **vocabulary**: only very frequent words (*small*, *town*, *sea*, *walks*, *beach*, *bottle*, *letter*); version B has less frequent ones (*harbour*, *glinting*, *seaweed*, *sealed*, *wax*);
- **grammar**: present simple only, and short simple sentences of 5–10 words; version B has the past perfect (*had lived*), a long sentence with a *when*-clause inside a main clause, and a participle phrase (*sealed with wax*) — the kind of sentence studied in [[reading-skills/long-sentences]];
- **information**: one fact per sentence, given in time order.

**Version B gives** more detail and atmosphere (*grey October morning*, *through the green glass*), a feeling of the character's long familiarity with the place (*knew every rock and pool*) and a more natural style.

Both are good extensive reading — **for different readers**. A reader for whom version B has fifteen unknown words should read version A or something like it, and move on to texts like B later.
:::
:::

Graded readers are not the only material. As your level rises, you can add:

- **young adult and children's books** written for native speakers, which often have simple language and strong stories;
- **news written for learners**, and short, simple news items on topics you already know;
- **non-fiction about your interests** — sport, cooking, science, history — where your knowledge of the topic makes the language easier;
- **books you have already read in Chinese**, or whose story you know from a film, so that the content is familiar and attention can go to the language;
- eventually, **original novels and non-fiction** for adults.

Old public-domain classics are often recommended because they are free, but their English is usually much harder than it looks: long sentences, old-fashioned words and a style that modern readers of any language find slow. For extensive reading, a simplified version is usually better than the original until your level is high.

## Choosing the right level

The most important decision in extensive reading is the **level**. If a text is too hard, reading becomes intensive reading: slow, tiring, full of dictionary work, and soon abandoned.

::: theorem Choosing a text for extensive reading {#rule-level}
A text is at the right level for extensive reading when:

1. you know **almost all the words** — about **98%** of the running words, which is about **one unknown word in fifty**, or roughly five on a page of 250 words;
2. you can read it at a **comfortable speed**, without stopping often;
3. you **understand** the story or the information without a dictionary;
4. you **want** to read on.

To test a book, read one page and count the words you do not know. More than about five on a 250-word page means the book is too hard for extensive reading; it can wait, or be used for intensive reading instead.
:::

::: proof
The figure of 98% comes from research on **lexical coverage** — the share of the words in a text that a reader knows — described in [[core-vocabulary/frequency-and-coverage]]. Hu and Nation (2000) found that most learners needed about 98% coverage to understand a story well without help; around 95% (one unknown word in twenty), reading is possible but needs effort. Extensive reading is meant to be fluent and enjoyable, so it aims at the higher figure. Nation and Waring (2020) give the same advice for choosing graded readers. Points 2–4 matter as much as point 1: a text can have few unknown words and still be hard (complex sentences, an unfamiliar topic) or dull (and a dull book will not be finished).
:::

::: example Testing a page {#ex-page-test}
Mei wants to read two books. She reads the first page of each — both pages have about 250 words — and marks the words she does not know. In book 1 she marks 4 words; in book 2 she marks 14. What percentage of the words does she know on each page? Which book should she choose for extensive reading?
::: solution
**Book 1.** Known words: 250 − 4 = 246. Coverage: 246 ÷ 250 = 0.984 = **98.4%**. This is about one unknown word in sixty — the right level for extensive reading ([[#rule-level]], point 1).

**Book 2.** Known words: 250 − 14 = 236. Coverage: 236 ÷ 250 = 0.944 = **94.4%**, about one unknown word in eighteen. That is below even the 95% level at which reading without help becomes difficult. Book 2 would be slow, intensive work.

**Choice:** book 1 for extensive reading. Book 2 can wait a few months, or Mei can use one chapter of it for intensive reading with a dictionary. One page is a small sample, so she should also check points 2–4: if book 1 is boring, a different book at the same level is better than forcing herself through it.
:::
:::

::: quiz
On a page of 300 words, a reader does not know 15 words. Is the page at a good level for extensive reading?
- [ ] Yes — 15 is a small number of words.
- [ ] Yes — the reader knows 95% of the words, which is the ideal level.
- [x] No — 95% coverage means one unknown word in twenty; extensive reading should be easier, at about 98%.
- [ ] It is impossible to say without a dictionary.
::: solution
300 − 15 = 285 known words; 285 ÷ 300 = 95%. Research suggests that 95% coverage allows reading with effort, but comfortable, fluent reading needs about 98% — on a 300-word page, about 6 unknown words or fewer. This page is better used for intensive reading.
:::
:::

## How to read extensively

::: theorem How to read extensively {#rule-how}
The following principles, adapted from Day and Bamford (1998), describe good extensive reading:

1. **Easy.** Choose texts at the level of [[#rule-level]], or even below it.
2. **Interesting.** Choose what you want to read; read a variety of things; stop reading a book you do not enjoy and choose another.
3. **A lot.** Read as much as possible, regularly: a little every day is better than a lot once a week.
4. **For meaning.** Read for the story, the information or the pleasure, not to study the language. Do not translate in your head.
5. **Fast.** Read at a comfortable speed; do not go back over every sentence.
6. **Without stopping.** Skip unknown words that do not matter; guess the ones that do; look up a word only if it appears again and again and you cannot follow the text without it.
7. **For its own sake.** Do not set yourself exercises or tests on what you read. A short record of what you have read — title, number of pages, a word about whether you liked it — is enough.
:::

::: proof
Each principle protects the main purpose: reading **large amounts** fluently. A difficult text (against point 1) or a boring one (against point 2) slows reading or stops it altogether. Stopping for every unknown word (against point 6) turns extensive reading back into intensive reading and reduces the amount read. Tests and exercises (against point 7) make reading feel like homework, and learners read less. Day and Bamford's principles are not rules from experiments; they are a description of programmes that teachers have found successful, and the research on quantity and coverage explains why they work.
:::

The advice to read without a dictionary is the hardest for many learners to follow. The steps of [[reading-skills/words-from-context]] apply here, but more lightly: the question is not "What exactly does this word mean?" but "Can I understand the story without knowing it?"

::: example Skip, guess or look up? {#ex-skip-guess}
A learner is reading a graded reader for pleasure and meets these unknown words. What should she do with each?

> *The old fisherman pulled his boat up onto the shingle and sat down to mend his nets. Gulls circled overhead, crying. "Storm's coming," he muttered, looking at the dark clouds in the west. "The storm," he said again, as Anna came closer. "It always brings something in."*

The unknown words: *shingle*, *mend*, *gulls*, *muttered*.
::: solution
**shingle — skip.** It is clearly some kind of ground at the edge of the sea where a boat can be pulled. Knowing that it means ‘small round stones on a beach’ adds little to the story.

**mend — guess.** *Sat down to ___ his nets*: something a fisherman does to nets while sitting. ‘Repair’ fits, and the guess is good enough to continue. If *mend* appears again with clothes or a fence, the guess will be confirmed.

**gulls — skip or guess.** Something that circles in the air over the sea and cries: a kind of seabird. That is all the story needs.

**muttered — guess.** It is followed by words in quotation marks, so it is a way of speaking; the old man is talking to himself, so ‘said quietly’ fits.

None of these words needs a dictionary. If a word keeps appearing — if *storm* were unknown and appeared on every page — then it is worth looking up, because the story depends on it and the reader will meet it again.
:::
:::

::: warning "Hard books teach more"
✗ *I'm reading an original novel with a dictionary — I look up about thirty words on every page, so I learn a lot.*

This feels productive, and it is a good way to study one chapter intensively. But it is not extensive reading, and as a way of reading whole books it usually fails. At thirty unknown words on a page, the reader cannot follow the story, reads perhaps a page in twenty minutes, meets each new word only once or twice, and rarely finishes the book.

✓ *I'm reading graded readers at a level where I know almost all the words. I finish a book every week, and I keep meeting the same words in new contexts.*

The words that are learned from reading are mostly words that are met **many times**. Easy texts provide those meetings; hard ones do not. Many learners also make the opposite mistake of staying too long at one level: when reading feels very easy and you no longer meet any new words, move up a level.
:::

## How much is enough?

The amount of reading depends on two things: **time** and **speed**. Fluent first-language readers of English read silently at about 240–260 words per minute ([[reading-skills/reading-with-a-purpose]]); second-language readers reading easy texts are often slower, perhaps 100–200 words per minute, and become faster with practice.

The slider sets the reading speed in words per minute. The horizontal line marks **one million words** a year. At 120 words per minute, 20 minutes a day gives about 876,000 words a year, close to the line; at 200 words per minute, 15 minutes a day passes it. A graded reader at a middle level might be 10,000–20,000 words long, so a million words is something like fifty to a hundred graded readers.
:::

::: example Planning a year of reading {#ex-plan}
Jun reads graded readers at about 100 words per minute. He has 15 minutes every day on the bus. (a) How many words does he read in a day, and in a year (365 days)? (b) If an average book at his level has 15,000 words, how many books is that in a year? (c) After six months, his speed has risen to 150 words per minute. How many words a day does he read now in the same time?
::: solution
**(a)** In a day: 100 × 15 = **1,500 words**. In a year: 1,500 × 365 = **547,500 words** — over half a million.

**(b)** 547,500 ÷ 15,000 = 36.5, so about **36 books** a year, or roughly one book every ten days.

**(c)** 150 × 15 = **2,250 words** a day — half as much again in the same time. Speed and quantity reinforce each other: reading more makes you faster, and reading faster lets you read more.

This plan follows principle 3 of [[#rule-how]]: a short time **every day** produces a large total. Fifteen minutes is easy to keep up; three hours on Sunday is not.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which plan is most likely to make a learner a fluent reader?
- [ ] reading one difficult newspaper article a week with a dictionary
- [x] reading easy books for 20 minutes every day
- [ ] reading for three hours once a month
- [ ] learning 50 words a day from a list and reading nothing
::: solution
Fluency comes from large amounts of reading at an easy level, done regularly. The first option is useful intensive reading, but the amount is small. The third gives a similar total to 20 minutes on a few days, but irregular reading is harder to keep up. The fourth teaches words but never practises reading them.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A learner reading for pleasure meets an unknown word. It does not seem important, and the story is still clear. What should she do?
- [x] Keep reading without looking it up.
- [ ] Stop and look it up in a dictionary.
- [ ] Go back to the beginning of the chapter.
- [ ] Write it in a notebook and learn it before continuing.
::: solution
In extensive reading, unknown words that do not stop you from understanding are skipped ([[#rule-how]], point 6). If the word is useful, you will meet it again, and each meeting will add to what you know about it.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **泛读 and 精读.** Many Chinese universities have long taught separate 精读 and 泛读 courses. In practice, 泛读 courses have often used texts that were too difficult and were followed by comprehension questions, so they became a second kind of intensive reading. Real extensive reading is easier, longer and freer than most learners have experienced in class.
- **A habit from Chinese reading.** Most Chinese readers became fluent in Chinese by reading a great deal for pleasure — stories, comics, novels, magazines — not only by studying textbooks. Extensive reading in English is the same process, with books chosen at the right level.
- **Familiar stories help.** Reading the English version (or a graded version) of a book you know in Chinese — a classic novel, a popular story — is a good way to start: the content is familiar, so attention is free for the language.
- **Translating in your head.** Learners who were taught through translation often translate every sentence silently into Chinese. This is slow and tiring. Extensive reading at an easy level is the best way to break the habit, because the language is simple enough to understand directly.
:::

::: history
The idea of reading a lot of easy text to learn a language is about a century old. The British linguist Harold Palmer, who worked in Japan in the 1920s and 1930s, is often credited with using the term *extensive reading* in language teaching, in contrast with the slow study of texts. At about the same time, Michael West, teaching in Bengal, wrote readers with a strictly controlled vocabulary so that children could read whole stories in English; his later *General Service List* (1953) became the basis of many graded reader series. Interest grew again in the 1980s, after Elley and Mangubhai's "book flood" in Fiji (1983), and Stephen Krashen argued in *The Power of Reading* (1993) for "free voluntary reading". Richard Day and Julian Bamford's *Extensive Reading in the Second Language Classroom* (1998) gave teachers a practical framework and ten principles, and Paul Nation and Rob Waring's *Teaching Extensive Reading in Another Language* (2020) brought the research up to date.
:::

::: summary
- **Intensive reading** is the careful study of short, difficult texts; **extensive reading** is reading large amounts of easy text quickly, for meaning and pleasure ([[#def-extensive]]). Learners need both.
- Extensive reading works through **repetition** of words in many contexts, growing **fluency** and the **habit** of reading ([[#rule-why]]); a single book teaches little, hundreds of books a great deal.
- **Graded readers** keep vocabulary and grammar within a level and are the main material for most learners ([[#def-graded-reader]]).
- Choose texts at about **98% coverage** — roughly five unknown words on a page of 250 — that you can read comfortably and want to read ([[#rule-level]]).
- Read easy, interesting texts, a lot, regularly, for meaning, fast and without stopping; skip or guess most unknown words ([[#rule-how]]).
- A little every day adds up: 20 minutes a day at 120 words per minute is close to a million words a year.
:::

::: exercise Which book? {level=2}
A learner tests three books by reading one page (about 250 words) of each. Book A: 2 unknown words, a story about a detective, which she likes. Book B: 6 unknown words, a book about the history of science, which she finds dull. Book C: 12 unknown words, a famous novel she has always wanted to read. Which book should she read for extensive reading, and what should she do with the others?
::: solution
**Book A.** It is at the right level (2 unknown words in 250 is over 99% coverage) and she likes it — both conditions of [[#rule-level]] are met.

**Book B** is at a possible level (6 in 250 is about 97.6%), but she finds it dull, and a dull book will not be finished (principle 2 of [[#rule-how]]); she can leave it.

**Book C** is too hard for extensive reading (12 in 250 is about 95%). She can keep it for later, read a graded version of the same novel now, or use one chapter of it for intensive reading.
:::

::: exercise Skip or guess {level=2}
In this extract from a story written for this lesson, decide for each of the words *trudged*, *drenched*, *kettle* and *shivering* whether to skip it, guess it (give the guess), or look it up.

> *Tom trudged up the hill in the rain. By the time he reached the cottage, he was drenched. His grandmother opened the door, took one look at him and put the kettle on. "Sit by the fire," she said. "You're shivering."*
::: solution
*trudged* — **guess**: a way of walking (*up the hill*), probably slowly and with effort in the rain; ‘walked heavily’ is enough.

*drenched* — **guess**: after walking in the rain, he was … ‘very wet’. The context makes this almost certain.

*kettle* — **guess or skip**: something you *put on* when someone comes in cold and wet; it is for making a hot drink. ‘Something for boiling water’ is enough.

*shivering* — **guess**: he is cold and wet and is told to sit by the fire; ‘shaking with cold’.

None needs a dictionary; the story is completely clear. All four are useful words that will appear again in other stories, where the guesses will be confirmed ([[reading-skills/words-from-context]]).

::: exercise Plan your own reading {level=3}
Write a plan for one month of extensive reading. Include: (a) what you will read and how you will check the level; (b) when and for how long you will read each day; (c) an estimate of how many words you will read in the month, with your calculation; (d) what you will do if a book is too hard or boring; (e) how you will record what you read.
::: solution
Answers will differ. A model:

(a) Graded readers at the intermediate level and short news stories for learners about sport. I will test each book by reading the first page and counting unknown words; I will only read books with five or fewer on a page.

(b) Twenty minutes on the bus home every weekday and thirty minutes on Saturday and Sunday mornings.

(c) My speed is about 110 words per minute. Weekdays: 110 × 20 = 2,200 words × 22 days = 48,400. Weekends: 110 × 30 = 3,300 × 8 days = 26,400. Total about **75,000 words**, or about five books of 15,000 words.

(d) If I don't like a book after twenty pages, I will stop and choose another. If I find more than five unknown words on most pages, I will go down a level.

(e) A one-line record for each book: title, level, number of words, date finished and a score out of five.

A good plan is realistic, regular, at the right level, and shows the calculation.

::: exercise Answer the sceptic {level=3}
A classmate says: *Reading easy books is a waste of time. If I already know 98% of the words, I learn almost nothing new. I'd rather read hard texts and learn thirty new words a page.* Write a reply of 80–120 words, using at least two ideas from this chapter.
::: solution
A model:

*Easy reading is not a waste of time, for three reasons. First, at 98% coverage you still meet a new word every fifty words — several on every page — and you can guess most of them from context, because the rest of the text is clear. Second, you meet the words you half know again and again, and that repetition is what makes them really known. Third, easy reading builds speed: if you read four times faster, you read four times as much. Hard texts with thirty new words a page are useful for study, but you read very little, you forget most of the words, and you rarely finish the book. We need both kinds of reading.*

A good answer uses the ideas of repetition, coverage and quantity ([[#rule-why]], [[#rule-level]]), and accepts that intensive reading has its own place.
