A friend sees you reading an article and asks, *What's it about?* You could answer *It's about bats.* Or you could answer *It says bats are not blind — they can all see, and some see very well.* The first answer gives the **topic**. The second gives the **main idea**: what the writer says about the topic. The second answer is much more useful, and it is also much harder to give, because you must decide which of the many sentences in the text matter most.

Finding main ideas is the centre of reading for understanding. It is what makes skimming possible ([[reading-skills/reading-with-a-purpose]]), it is what you remember a week later, and it is what you need when you summarise a text for someone else. This chapter explains the difference between a topic and a main idea, where writers put the main idea of a paragraph, how they support it with examples, reasons and evidence, and how to find the main idea when the writer does not state it.

## Topic and main idea

::: definition Topic and main idea {#def-topic-main-idea}
The **topic** of a text or paragraph is what it is about. It can usually be given in a few words, often a noun phrase: *bats*, *learning to cook*, *the new library*.

The **main idea** (主旨) is the most important point the writer makes about the topic. It is a complete statement, usually one sentence: *Bats are not blind.* *Learning to cook is one of the most useful skills a young person can have.*
:::

One topic can have many main ideas. Three writers can write about the same topic and make three different points:

- Topic: *working from home*.
- Main idea 1: *Working from home saves time and money.*
- Main idea 2: *Working from home can make people lonely.*
- Main idea 3: *Working from home works best for people who can organise their own time.*

A good test: if you can answer the question *What does the writer say about it?*, you have a main idea; if you can only answer *What is it about?*, you have a topic.

::: quiz
Which of these is a main idea, not just a topic?
- [ ] *the history of tea*
- [ ] *tea in China and Britain*
- [x] *Tea became popular in Britain in the eighteenth century, when it became cheaper.*
- [ ] *drinking tea*
::: solution
The third option is a complete statement that says something about the topic. The other three are noun phrases that name a topic but make no point about it.
:::
:::

## Topic sentences

::: definition Topic sentence {#def-topic-sentence}
A **topic sentence** (主题句) is a sentence that states the main idea of a paragraph. The other sentences of the paragraph — the **supporting sentences** (支撑句) — explain, prove or develop it.
:::

Here is a paragraph written for this lesson. Its first sentence is the topic sentence.

> *Learning to cook is one of the most useful skills a young person can have. People who can cook usually eat better, because they know what goes into their food and can choose fresh ingredients. They also save money, since a meal made at home usually costs much less than the same meal in a restaurant. Finally, cooking for other people is a good way to make friends and keep them.*

Every other sentence gives a reason why cooking is useful: better food, less money, friendship. The words *also* and *Finally* show that the reasons are a list. If you removed the first sentence, the paragraph would still make sense, but you would have to work out its point yourself.

::: theorem Where the topic sentence goes {#rule-topic-sentence-position}
In English texts that explain or argue (textbooks, articles, reports, essays):

1. the topic sentence is **most often at the start** of the paragraph;
2. it is sometimes **at the end**, as a conclusion after the details;
3. it may come **after an opening contrast**, often after a sentence about what *many people think* and a signal such as *But*, *However* or *In fact*;
4. sometimes there is **no topic sentence** at all, and the main idea is **implied**: the reader must work it out from the details.

Stories and descriptions often have no topic sentences; their paragraphs follow events or places instead.
:::

::: proof
Putting the main point first helps readers: they know what the details are for before they read them. English composition teaching has recommended this at least since Alexander Bain's *English Composition and Rhetoric* (1866), and it is still taught in schools and in writing courses ([[sentences-and-paragraphs/topic-sentences]]). But professional writers do not always follow the school model. Braddock (1974) studied paragraphs in essays from American magazines and found that only a minority of them — about 13% — opened with a simple topic sentence; many main ideas were stated later, spread over several sentences, or left implied. So the first sentence is the best place to look, but not the only one. Rule 1 is a strong tendency in textbooks and academic writing, and a weaker one in journalism and essays.
:::

**Topic sentence at the end.** Here the details come first and build up to the point.

> *In 2010 the town had one small bookshop, and it was closing. Today it has four. The old cinema has become a library with a café, and every Saturday there is a book market in the main square. Last year the town held its first book festival, and over ten thousand visitors came. In fifteen years, this quiet place has become a town of readers.*

The last sentence collects all the details (more bookshops, a library, a market, a festival) into one statement. That is the main idea.

**Topic sentence after a contrast.** A writer often starts with a common belief and then corrects it.

> *Many people believe that bats are blind. In fact, all bats can see, and many of them see quite well. Most bats are active at night, and many find their way in the dark by making high sounds and listening for the echoes. But this does not mean they cannot use their eyes, and some large fruit bats rely mainly on sight and smell.*

The first sentence is not the main idea — it is the idea the writer disagrees with. The signal *In fact* introduces the real main idea: *all bats can see*.

::: example Finding the topic sentence {#ex-find-topic-sentence}
Which sentence is the topic sentence? *(1) Every year thousands of birds fly into the glass walls of tall buildings. (2) Birds do not see glass; they see the sky or the trees reflected in it. (3) Some cities now require new buildings to use special glass with patterns that birds can see. (4) Others ask office buildings to turn off their lights at night during the months when birds migrate. (5) Cities are starting to take action to protect birds from their buildings.*
::: solution
**Find the topic.** All the sentences are about birds and buildings with glass.

**Ask what the writer says about it.** Sentences 1 and 2 describe a problem (birds fly into glass) and its cause (they do not see it). Sentences 3 and 4 give two examples of what cities do: special glass, lights turned off.

**Look for the sentence that covers the others.** Sentence 5, *Cities are starting to take action to protect birds from their buildings*, is general enough to include sentences 3 and 4, and sentences 1 and 2 explain why the action is needed.

**Topic sentence: sentence 5**, at the end. Sentence 1 is not the topic sentence, although it is first: it introduces the problem but does not say what the paragraph is mainly about (the action cities are taking).
:::
:::

## Supporting details

::: definition Supporting details {#def-supporting-details}
**Supporting details** are the sentences that develop the main idea. The most common kinds are:

1. **examples**: particular cases of a general statement (*for example*, *for instance*, *such as*);
2. **reasons**: why something is true or happens (*because*, *since*, *the reason is*);
3. **evidence**: facts, figures, research results or expert opinion (*according to*, *a study found*, *research shows*, numbers);
4. **explanation and description**: what something means, how it works, what it is like.
:::

Details also have levels. **Major details** support the main idea directly; **minor details** support a major detail. In the cooking paragraph, *People who can cook usually eat better* is a major detail, and *because they know what goes into their food* is a minor detail that explains it.

::: theorem Main idea or detail? {#rule-main-or-detail}
To decide whether a sentence states the main idea or a detail, ask:

1. Is it **general enough to cover** the other sentences of the paragraph? A main idea covers them; a detail covers only itself.
2. Do the other sentences **answer a question** about it — *Why?*, *How?*, *For example?*, *How do we know?* If so, it is the main idea.
3. Is it **too general**? A statement that would fit many different paragraphs (*Cooking is interesting*) is a topic or a vague comment, not the main idea of this one.
:::

::: proof
A main idea and its details form a hierarchy: each detail is an answer to a question raised by the main idea. This is why the test in point 2 works. Readers who mistake a detail for the main idea usually choose a striking or memorable sentence — a surprising number, a vivid example — because it stands out. Readers who choose something too general usually repeat the topic. Grabe (2009) describes this hierarchy of ideas as central to comprehension, and research since the 1970s has found that readers remember the higher levels of a text — its main ideas — better than its details (Kintsch & van Dijk 1978).
:::

::: example Kinds of supporting detail {#ex-kinds-of-support}
Say what kind of supporting detail each sentence gives for the main idea *Walking is good for your health.* (a) *A study of older adults found that those who walked regularly had fewer heart problems.* (b) *For example, a walk after dinner can help you sleep better.* (c) *This is because walking makes the heart and lungs work harder.* (d) *According to many doctors, thirty minutes of walking a day is a good goal.*
::: solution
Use the signal words and ask what each sentence adds.

(a) **Evidence**: a research result (*A study … found*).

(b) **Example**: one particular case (*For example*).

(c) **Reason**: why walking is healthy (*This is because*).

(d) **Evidence** in the form of expert opinion (*According to many doctors*). It also gives practical advice.
:::
:::

::: warning Choosing an interesting detail as the main idea
✗ Main idea of the town paragraph: *Over ten thousand visitors came to the book festival.*

✓ Main idea: *In fifteen years, the town has become a town of readers.*

A number or a vivid example is easy to remember, so readers often choose it as the main idea. But it supports only part of the paragraph. Use the test in [[#rule-main-or-detail]]: does the sentence cover the bookshops, the library and the book market too? The festival sentence does not; the last sentence does.

The opposite mistake is to choose something too general: ✗ *The paragraph is about a town.* That is the topic, not what the writer says about it.
:::

::: quiz
What is the main idea of the bats paragraph?
- [ ] Many people believe that bats are blind.
- [x] Bats are not blind: all of them can see.
- [ ] Some large fruit bats rely on sight and smell.
- [ ] Bats are animals.
::: solution
The paragraph opens with a common belief and then corrects it with *In fact*: the correction is the main idea. The first option is the belief the writer rejects; the third is a detail; the fourth is only a topic, and too general.
:::
:::

## When the main idea is not stated

Some paragraphs have no topic sentence. The writer gives details and trusts the reader to see what they have in common.

> *Li Hua woke up at five, two hours before her alarm. She read her notes again while she ate breakfast, but she could not finish her noodles. On the bus she checked three times that she had her identity card and two pens. When she reached the building, she stood outside for ten minutes before she went in.*

No sentence says *Li Hua was nervous about an important exam* — but every detail points to it: waking early, reading notes, not eating, checking her card and pens, waiting outside. Working out an implied main idea is a kind of inference, which is the subject of [[reading-skills/inference]].

::: example Stating an implied main idea {#ex-implied}
Write the implied main idea of the Li Hua paragraph in one sentence, and show how the details support it.
::: solution
**Step 1: list the details.** She woke two hours early. She read her notes at breakfast. She could not finish her food. She checked her identity card and pens three times. She waited outside the building for ten minutes.

**Step 2: ask what they have in common.** All of them are signs of worry. *Notes*, *identity card* and *two pens* suggest an exam or a test.

**Step 3: write a sentence that covers all the details.** *Li Hua was very nervous on the morning of an important exam.*

**Step 4: check.** Does each detail support this sentence? Waking early — nerves. Notes — exam. Not eating — nerves. Checking her card and pens — exam, and worry. Waiting outside — nerves. Yes. The sentence is not too general (*Li Hua had a busy morning* would miss the feeling) and not too narrow (*Li Hua did not eat her noodles* is one detail).
:::
:::

## The main idea of a whole text

A longer text has a main idea too, often called its **central idea** or, in an essay, its **thesis**. It is usually stated in the introduction (often at the end of the first paragraph) and repeated in the conclusion. The topic sentences of the middle paragraphs support it, just as details support a topic sentence.

::: theorem Finding the main idea of a whole text {#rule-whole-text}
1. Read the **title**, the **first paragraph** and the **last paragraph**; the main idea is usually stated in one of them.
2. Find the **topic sentence** of each middle paragraph.
3. Check that the main idea you chose **covers** all the topic sentences. If it does not, it may be the main idea of one paragraph only.
4. Write it in **one sentence** in your own words.
:::

::: proof
The structure of a text repeats the structure of a paragraph at a larger scale: one general statement, several supporting points, and details under each point. This is why skimming the first and last paragraphs and the first sentences of the others gives the gist ([[reading-skills/reading-with-a-purpose]]). Steps 3 and 4 protect against the two common errors — choosing the point of one paragraph, or giving only the topic.
:::

::: example The main idea of a short article {#ex-whole-text}
Find the main idea of this short article.

> **Why Children Should Learn to Swim**
>
> *Swimming is often seen as a sport or a holiday activity. But it is more than that: every child should learn to swim, because it can save lives and it brings benefits that last a lifetime.*
>
> *The most important reason is safety. Many accidents happen near rivers, lakes and the sea, and a child who can swim is much more likely to reach safety after a fall into water.*
>
> *Swimming is also very good exercise. It uses almost every muscle in the body, and because water supports the body, it is gentle on the joints.*
>
> *Finally, swimming is a social activity. Children who can swim can join their friends at the pool or the beach, and many keep swimming as adults.*
>
> *For all these reasons, swimming lessons should be part of every child's education.*
::: solution
**Step 1: title, first and last paragraphs.** The title asks *why*. The first paragraph ends with a clear statement: *every child should learn to swim, because it can save lives and it brings benefits that last a lifetime.* The last paragraph repeats this: *swimming lessons should be part of every child's education.*

**Step 2: topic sentences of the middle paragraphs.** *The most important reason is safety.* *Swimming is also very good exercise.* *Finally, swimming is a social activity.*

**Step 3: check coverage.** Safety = *save lives*; exercise and social life = *benefits that last a lifetime*. The statement covers all three.

**Step 4: in one sentence.** *Every child should learn to swim, mainly for safety but also for exercise and friendship.*

Note that the first sentence of the article (*Swimming is often seen as a sport*) is not the main idea: like the bats paragraph, the article opens with a common view and then, after *But*, gives its own.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Where the point comes.** Teaching in English-speaking schools asks writers to put the main point early, in a topic sentence and a thesis. Many Chinese essays, especially in school writing, begin with background, a scene or a quotation, and state the point later; the traditional pattern 起承转合 (opening, development, turn, conclusion) is one model of this. Neither way is better, but they lead readers to look in different places. When you read English expository writing, look for the main idea in the first sentences first. (The claim, made by Kaplan in 1966, that whole cultures "think" in straight lines or in circles has been strongly criticised; the difference is one of conventions learned at school, not of thinking.)
- **Signals of a correction.** *In fact*, *Actually*, *But* and *However* after a sentence such as *Many people believe …* usually introduce the writer's own main idea. Chinese uses similar signals (其实, 事实上, 但是), so this pattern is easy to transfer — but notice it in English, where it is very common in articles.
- **One sentence in your own words.** Writing the main idea in English, not translating a sentence from the text into Chinese, is good practice: it forces you to decide what the point is.
:::

::: history
The idea that a paragraph should be a unit with one main point stated near its beginning comes from nineteenth-century rhetoric. The Scottish philosopher Alexander Bain set out rules for the paragraph in *English Composition and Rhetoric* (1866), including that the theme of a paragraph should usually be indicated at its opening; his rules entered school textbooks and are the origin of the "topic sentence". A century later, Richard Braddock (1974) tested the rule against real writing and found that professional writers state the main idea at the start of a paragraph much less often than textbooks suggest. Reading research added another view: Walter Kintsch and Teun van Dijk (1978) described comprehension as building a "macrostructure", a mental summary of a text's main ideas, which readers remember much better than its details.
:::

## Where this leads

The links between sentences inside a paragraph — pronouns, synonyms and linking words — are the subject of [[reading-skills/following-the-thread]]. Implied main ideas lead to [[reading-skills/inference]]. Different text types organise their main ideas in different ways: [[reading-skills/text-types]]. On the writing side, [[sentences-and-paragraphs/topic-sentences]] shows how to write a clear topic sentence, and in academic texts main ideas become claims supported by evidence: [[academic-reading/claims-and-evidence]].

::: summary
- The **topic** is what a text is about (a few words); the **main idea** is what the writer says about it (a sentence).
- A **topic sentence** states the main idea of a paragraph. In expository English it is most often first, but it can be last, after an opening contrast, or missing ([[#rule-topic-sentence-position]]).
- **Supporting details** are examples, reasons, evidence and explanation; major details support the main idea and minor details support them.
- A main idea **covers** the other sentences, and they answer questions about it; it is neither one detail nor just the topic ([[#rule-main-or-detail]]).
- An **implied** main idea is what all the details have in common.
- The main idea of a whole text is usually in the first and last paragraphs and covers all the topic sentences ([[#rule-whole-text]]).
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Topic or main idea? {level=1}
Say whether each is a topic (T) or a main idea (M). (a) *city parks* (b) *City parks make people healthier and happier.* (c) *the price of houses in Shanghai* (d) *Smartphones have changed the way people travel.* (e) *learning a musical instrument* (f) *It is never too late to learn a musical instrument.*
::: solution
(a) T; (b) M; (c) T; (d) M; (e) T; (f) M. The main ideas are complete statements that say something about a topic.
:::
:::

::: exercise Which sentence? {level=1 check="3"}
Which sentence (give its number) is the topic sentence?

*(1) Many people think that you need expensive equipment to start running. (2) All you need is a pair of comfortable shoes. (3) In fact, running is one of the cheapest sports there is. (4) You do not have to pay to use a gym or a pool. (5) And you can run almost anywhere: in a park, along a river or in your own street.*
::: solution
Sentence **3**. Sentence 1 is the common belief that the writer corrects; *In fact* in sentence 3 introduces the writer's own point. Sentences 2, 4 and 5 support it: cheap shoes, no gym fees, no special place.
:::
:::

::: exercise Kind of support {level=1}
Main idea: *Learning a second language has many benefits.* Name the kind of support (example, reason, evidence). (a) *For instance, it can help you get a job with an international company.* (b) *Some studies have found that bilingual people are good at switching between tasks.* (c) *This is because you must think about how words and sentences work.*
::: solution
(a) example (*For instance*); (b) evidence (a research finding: *Some studies have found*); (c) reason (*This is because*).
:::
:::

::: exercise The odd one out {level=2 check="4"}
One sentence does not support the main idea of this paragraph. Which one (give its number)?

*(1) Our new office is much better than the old one. (2) It is closer to the train station, so most of us spend less time travelling. (3) The rooms are larger and have more light. (4) The old office was built in 1985. (5) And there is a kitchen where we can make tea and heat our lunch.*
::: solution
Sentence **4**. The main idea is that the new office is better. Sentences 2, 3 and 5 give reasons (location, rooms, kitchen). The date the old office was built does not show that the new one is better.
:::
:::

::: exercise Write the implied main idea {level=2}
Write the implied main idea in one sentence.

> *The streets were empty. All the shops had closed early, and their windows were covered with wooden boards. People had moved their cars into garages or away from the trees. On the radio, a woman's voice repeated the same message every ten minutes. The sky in the east had turned a strange dark yellow.*
::: solution
A good answer: *A big storm was coming, and the town had prepared for it.* (Also acceptable: *The town was waiting for a dangerous storm.*) The details — empty streets, boarded windows, cars moved away from trees, a repeated radio message, a strange sky — all point to danger from the weather. An answer such as *The town was quiet* is too general and misses the reason.
:::
:::

::: exercise Major and minor details {level=2}
Sort the details into major (M) and minor (m) for the main idea *Our school's new reading club has been a success.* (a) *More than sixty students joined in the first month.* (b) *Some of them had never read a book in English before.* (c) *The club has helped students improve their English.* (d) *In a survey, most members said they now read faster.* (e) *Students have made new friends through the club.*
::: solution
Major: (a), (c), (e) — each gives one way the club is a success (many members, better English, friendship). Minor: (b) supports (a) (it says something about the members), and (d) supports (c) (evidence that English improved). Other sensible analyses are possible if they show which detail supports which.
:::
:::

::: exercise A text map {level=3}
Read the article and draw a map of its ideas (as in the figure in this chapter): the main idea at the top, the topic sentences below it, and one or two details under each topic sentence.

> **The Night Market**
>
> *Every town in the region seems to have a night market now, and they have become far more than places to buy cheap food.*
>
> *For local families, the market is a place to meet. After work, parents bring their children, grandparents sit at the tables, and neighbours who rarely see each other in the day stop to talk.*
>
> *For small businesses, it is a chance to start with little money. A stall costs much less to rent than a shop, so young cooks and craftspeople can try out their ideas.*
>
> *For visitors, the night market is often the most memorable part of a trip. They can taste local dishes, see how people live, and buy things they will not find anywhere else.*
>
> *The night market, in short, has become the living room of the town.*
::: solution
Main idea: *Night markets have become far more than places to buy food: they are the living room of the town.* (stated in the first and last paragraphs)

- Topic sentence 1: *For local families, the market is a place to meet.* Details: parents bring children; grandparents sit at tables; neighbours stop to talk.
- Topic sentence 2: *For small businesses, it is a chance to start with little money.* Details: a stall is cheaper than a shop; young cooks and craftspeople try out ideas.
- Topic sentence 3: *For visitors, the night market is often the most memorable part of a trip.* Details: local dishes; how people live; unusual things to buy.

The parallel openings *For local families*, *For small businesses*, *For visitors* show the structure: one group of people per paragraph.
:::
:::

::: exercise Write a paragraph {level=3}
Write a paragraph of 80–100 words on one of these topics: *living in a big city*, *learning online*, *keeping a pet*. Start with a clear topic sentence, give two or three supporting details of different kinds (an example, a reason, a piece of evidence), and underline (or mark with brackets) your topic sentence. Then ask a classmate to find your main idea without looking at the brackets.
::: solution
Answers will differ. A model:

*[Keeping a dog is good for older people living alone.] A dog gives them company, so they feel less lonely, especially in the evenings. It also makes them go out: a dog needs a walk at least twice a day, and on these walks they often stop and talk to neighbours. For example, my grandfather has made three new friends in the park since he got his dog last year. Many doctors also say that regular walking helps older people stay healthy.*

A good answer has one general topic sentence, details that answer *why?* or *for example?*, and no sentence that goes off the topic.
:::
:::
