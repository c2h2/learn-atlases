Here is a sentence of 42 words:

> *The new bridge across the river, which the city had been planning for more than ten years and which cost far more than expected, finally opened last Saturday to cars, buses and the thousands of cyclists who cross the river every day.*

You probably know every word. Yet many readers reach the end and have to start again, because by the time they get to *finally opened*, they have forgotten what opened. The problem is not the vocabulary but the structure. The heart of the sentence is only four words long — *The new bridge … opened* — and everything else is extra information hung around it.

This chapter shows how to find that heart in any long sentence: the subject and the main verb. It shows which parts can be set aside on a first reading — phrases, clauses, information between commas — and how to read a long subject by finding its head noun. These are skills for close reading ([[reading-skills/reading-with-a-purpose]]); with practice they become fast and automatic.

## The core of a sentence

::: definition Main clause, subject and main verb {#def-core}
Every complete English sentence has at least one **main clause** (主句). A main clause has a **subject** (主语), the person or thing the sentence is about, and a **main verb** (谓语动词), a verb that shows tense (*opened*, *is*, *has been planning*, *will cost*). Together, the subject and the main verb — with an object or complement if the verb needs one — make the **core** of the sentence. Everything else adds information to the core.
:::

The core of the bridge sentence is *The new bridge opened*. Compare:

- *The bridge opened.* (core)
- *The new bridge across the river opened.* (the subject is longer)
- *The new bridge across the river, which the city had been planning for more than ten years, opened.* (a clause has been added to the subject)
- *The new bridge across the river, which the city had been planning for more than ten years and which cost far more than expected, finally opened last Saturday to cars, buses and the thousands of cyclists who cross the river every day.* (more information about when and for whom)

Each version is a correct sentence, and each one has the same core. The basic clause patterns — SV, SVO, SVC and the others — are in [[core-grammar/sentence-patterns]]; long sentences are these same patterns with a great deal added.

::: definition Finite and non-finite verbs {#def-finite}
A **finite verb** (限定动词) shows tense and can be the main verb of a clause: *opens*, *opened*, *is opening*, *has opened*, *can open*. A **non-finite verb** (非限定动词, 非谓语动词) does not show tense on its own: the *to*-infinitive (*to open*), the *-ing* form (*opening*) and the past participle (*opened* in *a bridge opened in 2024*). A non-finite verb cannot be the main verb of a sentence by itself.
:::

This difference is the key to finding the main verb. In *The bridge opened last year*, *opened* is finite: past tense, main verb. In *The bridge opened last year is already busy*, *opened last year* is a participle phrase describing the bridge (= *which was opened last year*), and the main verb is *is*.

## A procedure for untangling

::: theorem Untangling a long sentence {#rule-untangle}
1. **Find the main verb.** Look for a finite verb that is not inside a clause beginning with *who*, *which*, *that*, *because*, *when*, *if*, *although* and similar words. If there are several finite verbs, the main one is the one that is left after you set those clauses aside.
2. **Find its subject.** Ask *Who or what* + the verb? The subject is usually before the verb and may be long.
3. **Find what completes the verb**, if it needs something: an object (*opened **the door***) or a complement (*is **expensive***).
4. **Bracket the extras**: phrases beginning with a preposition (*across the river*), clauses beginning with a relative word or a conjunction (*which cost …*, *because …*), *-ing* and *-ed* phrases (*sitting by the window*, *built in 1990*), and anything between commas, dashes or brackets.
5. **Read the core**, then add the extras back one at a time, asking what each one tells you about.
:::

::: proof
The procedure works because English sentences are built in layers around a main clause. The main clause carries the central statement; phrases and subordinate clauses add details of who, which, when, where, why and how. Reference grammars describe this layered structure in detail (Quirk et al. 1985; Huddleston & Pullum 2002). Setting the layers aside temporarily reduces the amount the reader must hold in memory, which is the real difficulty of long sentences. Step 1 starts with the verb rather than the subject because the verb is usually easier to recognise, while the subject may be a long phrase with no clear end. Step 5 matters: the extras are not unimportant — they often contain the details you need — but they are easier to understand once you know what they belong to.
:::

::: example Untangling the bridge sentence {#ex-bridge}
Apply [[#rule-untangle]] to the sentence at the start of this chapter.
::: solution
**Step 1 — main verb.** The finite verbs are *had been planning*, *cost*, *opened* and *cross*. *had been planning* and *cost* are inside clauses beginning with *which*; *cross* is inside a clause beginning with *who*. Set those aside: the main verb is **opened** (*finally* is an adverb that goes with it).

**Step 2 — subject.** *What opened?* **The new bridge across the river.** Its head noun is *bridge*.

**Step 3 — completion.** *opened* needs nothing more here (*The bridge opened* is complete).

**Step 4 — extras.**
- [*across the river*] — prepositional phrase: which bridge.
- [*which the city had been planning for more than ten years*] — relative clause: more about the bridge.
- [*and which cost far more than expected*] — a second relative clause about the bridge.
- [*finally*] [*last Saturday*] — when.
- [*to cars, buses and the thousands of cyclists*] — for whom.
- [*who cross the river every day*] — relative clause about the cyclists.

**Step 5 — core, then extras.** *The bridge opened.* Which bridge? The new one across the river, which was planned for over ten years and cost more than expected. When? Finally, last Saturday. For whom? Cars, buses and many cyclists, who cross every day.

**Paraphrase in short sentences:** *The new bridge across the river finally opened last Saturday. The city had planned it for over ten years, and it cost far more than expected. Cars, buses and thousands of cyclists can now use it every day.*
:::

## What can be set aside

These are the most common kinds of extra information in a long sentence. Most of them are introduced by a small word that you can learn to notice.

| kind of extra | begins with | example (extra in brackets) |
|---|---|---|
| prepositional phrase | *in*, *on*, *of*, *with*, *for*, *across*, *after* … | *The house [at the end of the street] is empty.* |
| relative clause | *who*, *which*, *that*, *whose*, *where*, *when* (or nothing) | *The man [who called you] is my uncle.* / *The book [I bought] was cheap.* |
| participle phrase | an *-ing* or *-ed* form | *The girl [sitting by the window] is new.* / *Cars [made in this factory] are exported.* |
| adverbial clause | *because*, *when*, *if*, *although*, *while*, *after*, *so that* … | *[Although it was late,] the shop was still open.* |
| apposition | a noun phrase after a comma | *Dr Liu, [our family doctor,] has retired.* |
| an aside | dashes or brackets | *The test — [as everyone expected] — was difficult.* |

Some extras come **before** the subject. An adverbial clause or a participle phrase at the start of a sentence is followed by a comma, and the subject comes after the comma:

- *Although the weather was terrible, the match went ahead.* — core: *the match went ahead*.
- *Tired after the long journey, the children fell asleep in the car.* — core: *the children fell asleep*.
- *Because of the strike, which lasted three days, most trains were cancelled.* — core: *most trains were cancelled*.

::: example A sentence that starts with extras {#ex-front}
Find the core: *Having spent three years working in a hospital in a small town in the north, where she was the only doctor who could speak English, Dr Wang decided to return to Shanghai.*
::: solution
**Step 1 — main verb.** Finite verbs: *was*, *could speak*, *decided*. *was* is inside the clause *where she was the only doctor*; *could speak* is inside *who could speak English*. The main verb is **decided**.

**Step 2 — subject.** *Who decided?* **Dr Wang.** It comes after the comma, late in the sentence.

**Step 3 — completion.** *decided* + *to return to Shanghai*.

**Step 4 — extras.** Everything before *Dr Wang* is a long opening participle phrase: [*Having spent three years working in a hospital in a small town in the north*], with a relative clause inside it: [*where she was the only doctor*] and another inside that: [*who could speak English*].

**Core:** *Dr Wang decided to return to Shanghai.* Then the extras: she had spent three years in a small northern town, at a hospital where she was the only English-speaking doctor. The *Having spent* phrase tells us what happened **before** her decision.
:::
:::

::: warning Taking the first verb-like word as the main verb
✗ Reading *The books written by her grandfather* as a sentence: "The books wrote …"

✓ *The books written by her grandfather are now in a museum.* — *written by her grandfather* describes the books (= *which were written by her grandfather*); the main verb is *are*.

Past participles (*written*, *given*, *made*, *built*) and *-ing* forms often appear right after a noun, where a main verb might be expected. Two quick tests: (1) Does the sentence still have another finite verb later? If so, the first one is probably a participle. (2) Can you put *who is / which was* before it? *The books (which were) written by her grandfather* — yes, so it is a participle phrase. Regular verbs are especially confusing, because the past tense and the past participle have the same form: *The students asked to leave early were from Class 3* (= the students **who were asked** to leave early).
:::

## Long subjects

In many long sentences the problem is not the extras at the end but a long subject at the start. The reader has to wait a long time for the verb.

::: theorem The head of a long subject {#rule-head}
A long subject is usually a noun phrase with one **head noun** (中心词) and modifiers before and after it ([[nouns-and-articles/noun-phrases]]). To read it:

1. find the **first noun** that is not inside an earlier phrase — after *the*, *a*, *my*, adjectives and modifier nouns — and test whether it is the head;
2. treat what follows the head (*of …*, *in …*, *who …*, *that …*, *-ing* and *-ed* phrases) as its description;
3. the main verb **agrees with the head**, not with the nearest noun: *The number of students … **was** …*, *The results of the study … **were** …*.
:::

::: proof
In English noun phrases, determiners and short modifiers come before the head, and longer modifiers come after it ([[nouns-and-articles/noun-phrases]]), so the head is usually near the beginning of the phrase. Subject–verb agreement is with the head (Quirk et al. 1985; Swan 2016), which gives the reader a useful check: if the verb is singular, the head is probably singular, and vice versa. There are a few exceptions with quantity expressions such as *a number of* (*A number of students **were** late*, where *students* controls the verb), which are covered in [[nouns-and-articles/agreement]].
:::

::: example Finding the head of a long subject {#ex-long-subject}
Find the head noun of the subject and the main verb: *The number of young people in the region who choose to study at universities in other countries after finishing secondary school has risen sharply in the last five years.*
::: solution
**First noun:** *number* (after *The*). Test it: everything after it — *of young people*, *in the region*, *who choose to study …*, *after finishing secondary school* — describes which number. So the head is **number**.

**Main verb:** *choose* and *finishing* are inside the description (*who choose …*; *after finishing …* is non-finite). The finite verb after the whole subject is **has risen**.

**Check agreement:** *number* is singular, *has* is singular. (Do not choose the verb by looking at the nearest noun. Here the noun just before the verb, *school*, happens to be singular too, but the plural nouns *young people* and *universities* in the description show how easily a reader could be misled.)

**Core:** *The number has risen sharply.* Which number? The number of young people in the region who go abroad to university after secondary school.
:::
:::

Subjects can also be whole clauses or infinitives:

- *What surprised me most was the silence.* — subject: *What surprised me most*; verb: *was*.
- *Whether the plan will work is still unclear.* — subject: *Whether the plan will work*.
- *To finish the work on time will be difficult.*

English often avoids a long subject of this kind by putting *it* in the subject position and the long part at the end: *It will be difficult to finish the work on time.* *It is still unclear whether the plan will work.* Here *it* does not refer back to anything; it points forward to the clause at the end ([[reading-skills/following-the-thread]]).

::: quiz
Which verb form is correct? *The results of the survey, which was carried out among 2,000 people in five cities, ______ surprising.*
- [ ] was
- [x] were
- [ ] is
- [ ] has been
::: solution
The head of the subject is *results* (plural), so the verb is *were*. *Was* inside the relative clause agrees with *the survey*, which is a different noun. The extra information between the commas can be set aside: *The results … were surprising.*
:::
:::

## Reading in chunks

When you read a long sentence, try not to translate it word by word, and do not jump to the end to find the verb first if you can avoid it. Instead, read in **chunks** — groups of words that belong together, such as a noun phrase, a prepositional phrase or a short clause — and build the meaning as you go:

> *The new bridge across the river | which the city had been planning | for more than ten years | … | finally opened | last Saturday …*

Skilled readers do this automatically. At first it helps to mark the chunks with a pencil. The procedure of [[#rule-untangle]] is for the sentences that still do not make sense after one reading in chunks.

::: example Reading in chunks {#ex-chunks}
Divide this sentence into chunks and say what each chunk adds: *It is not surprising that many people who move to a big city from the countryside feel lonely during their first year there.*
::: solution
Read from left to right, one meaningful group at a time.

1. *It is not surprising* — the frame: something is not surprising. *It* points forward, as in *It will be difficult to finish the work on time* above: we expect a *that*-clause to tell us what.
2. *that many people* — here it comes: the subject of the clause is *many people*.
3. *who move to a big city* — which people? Those who move to a big city …
4. *from the countryside* — … from the countryside.
5. *feel lonely* — the verb of the *that*-clause: back to *many people*, who *feel lonely*.
6. *during their first year there* — when.

**Core of the whole sentence:** *It is not surprising that many people feel lonely*, with *it* standing for the *that*-clause. Notice that in chunk 5 we had to connect *feel* back to *many people* across chunks 3 and 4; that is the step that goes wrong when a reader treats *the countryside feel lonely* as one group.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence is a correct paraphrase of *Built in 1904 and closed in 1990, the station, now a museum, attracts thousands of visitors every year*?
- [ ] The station was built in 1990 and attracts visitors.
- [ ] Thousands of visitors built the station in 1904.
- [x] The station, which was built in 1904 and closed in 1990, is now a museum that thousands of people visit every year.
- [ ] The museum closed in 1990 and attracts no visitors.
::: solution
Core: *the station attracts thousands of visitors every year*. *Built in 1904 and closed in 1990* is an opening participle phrase describing the station; *now a museum* is in apposition to *the station*. The third option keeps all this information.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Modifiers before and after.** Chinese puts all modifiers, even long clauses, before the noun with 的: 那座市政府计划了十多年、花费远超预期的过江新桥. English puts long modifiers after the noun: *the new bridge across the river, which the city had been planning for more than ten years*. When you read English, you meet the head noun first and then its description. Hold the head in mind and read what follows as information about it — do not wait for the noun, as you would in Chinese.
- **One main clause, many layers.** A long Chinese sentence often strings clauses together with commas, each with its own verb, in time order (sometimes called 流水句). A long English sentence usually has one main clause, and the other clauses are attached to it with words such as *which*, *who*, *because* and *although*, or reduced to *-ing* and *-ed* phrases. Finding the main clause first is therefore more important in English than in Chinese.
- **Don't reorder while reading.** Translating a long English sentence into Chinese means moving the modifiers in front of the noun, which is slow and makes you lose the thread. Read in English order, chunk by chunk; translate only when you must, and then translate the core first.
- **Verb forms tell you the structure.** Chinese verbs do not change form, but English *opened*, *opening* and *to open* do different jobs in a sentence. The difference between finite and non-finite forms ([[#def-finite]]) is one of the best clues to where the main verb is.
:::

::: history
Teachers have long tried to make the structure of sentences visible. In 1877 Alonzo Reed and Brainerd Kellogg, two American teachers, published *Higher Lessons in English*, which taught pupils to draw sentences as diagrams: the subject and the main verb on a horizontal line, with modifiers hanging below on slanting lines. "Reed–Kellogg diagrams" were used in American schools for a century. Psychologists later studied what happens when the structure misleads the reader. In 1970 Thomas Bever discussed the sentence *The horse raced past the barn fell*, which most readers first cannot understand: they take *raced* as the main verb, and then *fell* makes no sense. In fact *raced past the barn* is a participle phrase (= *the horse that was raced past the barn*), and *fell* is the main verb. Such "garden-path" sentences, which lead the reader up the wrong path, became a standard tool for studying how readers build sentence structure as they read.
:::

The structure of noun phrases is covered in detail in [[nouns-and-articles/noun-phrases]], and subject–verb agreement in [[nouns-and-articles/agreement]]. Relative clauses, adverbial clauses and participle clauses are the subject of the course *Complex Sentences*: [[complex-sentences/relative-clauses]], [[complex-sentences/adverbial-clauses]] and [[complex-sentences/participle-infinitive-clauses]]. Academic texts pack even more information into noun phrases: [[academic-reading/dense-sentences]].

::: summary
- Every long sentence has a **core**: a subject and a main (finite) verb, with an object or complement if needed ([[#def-core]]).
- **Finite** verbs show tense and can be main verbs; *to*-infinitives, *-ing* forms and past participles cannot by themselves ([[#def-finite]]).
- To untangle: find the main verb, find its subject, find what completes it, bracket the extras, read the core and add the extras back ([[#rule-untangle]]).
- Extras include prepositional phrases, relative clauses, participle phrases, adverbial clauses, apposition and asides; some come before the subject, followed by a comma.
- A long subject has a **head noun** near its start; the verb agrees with the head, not with the nearest noun ([[#rule-head]]).
- Read in chunks, in English order; Chinese puts modifiers before the noun, English puts long ones after it.
:::

::: exercise Find the main verb {level=1}
Write the main verb of each sentence. (a) *The woman living next door works at the airport.* (b) *The letter that arrived this morning was for my father.* (c) *When the rain stopped, the children ran outside.* (d) *Tickets bought online are cheaper.* (e) *My friend, a nurse at the city hospital, often works at night.*
::: solution
(a) *works* (*living next door* is a participle phrase); (b) *was* (*arrived* is inside the relative clause *that arrived this morning*); (c) *ran* (*stopped* is inside the clause *When the rain stopped*); (d) *are* (*bought online* is a participle phrase); (e) *works* (*a nurse at the city hospital* is apposition).
:::

::: exercise Bracket the extras {level=1}
Put brackets around the extra information and write the core. (a) *The shop on the corner sells excellent bread.* (b) *The boy who won the prize is only eleven.* (c) *Although she was tired, Li Na finished her homework.* (d) *Our teacher, Mr Brown, comes from Scotland.*
::: solution
(a) *The shop [on the corner] sells excellent bread.* Core: *The shop sells excellent bread.* (b) *The boy [who won the prize] is only eleven.* Core: *The boy is only eleven.* (c) *[Although she was tired,] Li Na finished her homework.* Core: *Li Na finished her homework.* (d) *Our teacher, [Mr Brown,] comes from Scotland.* Core: *Our teacher comes from Scotland.*

How many finite verbs are there in this sentence? *The film that we watched last night, which my sister had recommended, was much longer than I expected.* (Count *had recommended* as one verb.)
::: solution
*watched* (in *that we watched last night*), *had recommended* (in *which my sister had recommended*), *was* (the main verb) and *expected* (in *than I expected*): **4** finite verbs. Only *was* is outside the extra clauses, so the core is *The film was much longer*.

::: exercise Find the head {level=2}
Write the head noun of the subject and choose the correct verb. (a) *The price of the tickets for the concerts (is / are) too high.* (b) *The students in the class that I teach on Mondays (is / are) very friendly.* (c) *A list of the books that you need for the course (is / are) on the website.* (d) *The opinions of my oldest friend (was / were) always important to me.*
::: solution
(a) head *price* → *is*; (b) head *students* → *are*; (c) head *list* → *is*; (d) head *opinions* → *were*. In each case, the nouns after the head (*tickets*, *concerts*, *class*, *books*, *course*, *friend*) belong to its description and do not control the verb.

Write the core of each sentence, then rewrite the sentence as two or three short sentences. (a) *The museum, which reopened last month after a two-year renovation that cost 30 million yuan, now has a large hall for children.* (b) *Worried that she would miss her flight, Zhang Yu, who had never travelled abroad before, left for the airport five hours early.*
::: solution
(a) Core: *The museum now has a large hall for children.* Short sentences: *The museum reopened last month after a two-year renovation. The renovation cost 30 million yuan. The museum now has a large hall for children.*

(b) Core: *Zhang Yu left for the airport five hours early.* Short sentences: *Zhang Yu had never travelled abroad before. She was worried that she would miss her flight, so she left for the airport five hours early.*

In each sentence, is the word in brackets the main verb (M) or part of a participle phrase (P)? (a) *The man [arrested] last night has been released.* (b) *The police [arrested] a man last night.* (c) *The parcel [sent] from Beijing never arrived.* (d) *She [sent] the parcel from Beijing.* (e) *Students [asked] about the test said it was fair.*
::: solution
(a) P (main verb: *has been released*); (b) M; (c) P (main verb: *arrived*); (d) M; (e) P (= *students who were asked about the test*; main verb: *said*).

::: exercise Untangle and paraphrase {level=3}
Use all the steps of [[#rule-untangle]] on this sentence, then explain it in two or three simple sentences.

*The report published by the city government last week, which looked at the travel habits of more than 5,000 residents over a whole year, found that people who live within walking distance of a metro station drive less than half as often as those who do not.*
::: hint
There are four finite verbs outside the subject's description, but only one of them is the main verb. What did the report do?
:::
::: solution
**Step 1 — main verb.** Finite verbs: *looked* (in the *which* clause), *found*, *live* (in *who live …*), *drive*, *do not*. The main verb is **found**.

**Step 2 — subject.** *What found?* *The report published by the city government last week, which looked at …* — head **report**.

**Step 3 — completion.** *found* + an object clause: *that people who live within walking distance of a metro station drive less than half as often as those who do not*. Inside it, the core is *people … drive less than half as often as those who do not* (*those who do not* = people who do not live near a station).

**Step 4 — extras.** [*published by the city government last week*] — participle phrase; [*which looked at the travel habits of more than 5,000 residents over a whole year*] — relative clause about the report; [*who live within walking distance of a metro station*] — relative clause about *people*.

**Core:** *The report found that people near metro stations drive less.*

**Paraphrase:** *Last week the city government published a report. It studied how more than 5,000 residents travelled during one year. It found that people who live close to a metro station drive less than half as often as other people.*
:::

::: exercise Build a long sentence {level=3}
Combine these short sentences into one long sentence, then mark its core. *The library is in the old town. It was built in 1935. It has just been repaired. It will open again on Monday. Students and visitors can use it free of charge.*
::: solution
A model: *The library in the old town, which was built in 1935 and has just been repaired, will open again on Monday, and students and visitors will be able to use it free of charge.*

Core: *The library will open again on Monday* (main clause 1) and *students and visitors will be able to use it* (main clause 2, joined by *and*).

Other good versions: *Built in 1935 and just repaired, the library in the old town will reopen on Monday, free of charge for students and visitors.* (one main clause: *the library will reopen*). Any version is correct if it is grammatical, keeps all the information and has a clear core.
:::
