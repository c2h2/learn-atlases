Liu Yang reads a shopping list aloud to a friend: *a bag of rice*. The friend writes *a back of rice* and laughs. Liu Yang is sure he said /ɡ/ at the end of *bag*. But at the end of an English word, /ɡ/ and /k/ are not kept apart mainly by voice. What English listeners actually listen for is the **vowel before them**: long before /ɡ/, short before /k/. Liu Yang said a short vowel, so his *bag* sounded like *back*.

This chapter is about the consonants made by stopping the air or forcing it through a narrow gap: the six plosives /p b t d k ɡ/, the nine fricatives /f v θ ð s z ʃ ʒ h/, and the two affricates /tʃ dʒ/. They are 17 of the 24 English consonants. Most come in voiceless–voiced pairs ([[sounds-of-english/how-speech-is-made#rule-pairs]]), and the main lesson of the chapter is how English really keeps these pairs apart: by a puff of air at the start of a word, by the length of the vowel at the end, and by voice in between.

## Plosives

::: definition Plosive {#def-plosive}
A **plosive** (or **stop**) is a consonant made in three stages: the articulators **close** completely, so that no air can escape through the mouth (the soft palate is raised, so none escapes through the nose either); the air pressure builds up behind the closure for a moment (the **hold**); then the closure opens suddenly (the **release**). English has six plosives: bilabial /p b/, alveolar /t d/ and velar /k ɡ/.
:::

| | voiceless | voiced | closure |
|---|---|---|---|
| bilabial | /p/ *pen* | /b/ *bed* | both lips |
| alveolar | /t/ *ten* | /d/ *day* | tongue tip on the alveolar ridge |
| velar | /k/ *key* | /ɡ/ *go* | back of the tongue on the soft palate |

The English alveolar plosives are made on the **ridge**, a little further back than the Mandarin *d* and *t*, which many speakers make with the tongue tip touching the back of the top teeth. Try it: feel where your tongue touches for Pinyin *dà* (大), then move it a few millimetres back, onto the bump, for English *day*.

### At the start of a word: aspiration

Hold a piece of paper (or your hand) in front of your mouth and say *pin*, then *bin*. On *pin* the paper moves: a short puff of air comes out after the /p/, before the vowel. On *bin* the paper hardly moves.

::: theorem Aspiration {#rule-aspiration}
The voiceless plosives /p/, /t/, /k/ are **aspirated** — followed by a short puff of breath before the vowel begins — at the start of a stressed syllable: *pin* [pʰɪn], *ten* [tʰen], *key* [kʰiː], *repeat* [rɪˈpʰiːt]. They are **not** aspirated after /s/ in the same syllable: *spin* [spɪn], *stop*, *sky*. In other positions (unstressed syllables, the end of a word) the puff is weak or absent.

The voiced plosives /b/, /d/, /ɡ/ are never aspirated. At the start of a word, after a pause, many speakers make them with little or no voicing, so the main difference between *pin* and *bin* is the puff of air.
:::

::: proof
The puff is a delay: after the release of /p/, the vocal folds wait a little before they start to vibrate for the vowel, and during the wait only breath comes out. This delay is called the **voice onset time** (VOT). In a famous cross-language study, Lisker and Abramson (1964) measured average VOTs in English words of about 60 ms for /p/, 70 ms for /t/ and 80 ms for /k/, and of only about 0–20 ms for /b/, /d/ and /ɡ/, which most of their English speakers made without voicing before the release. After /s/ the delay disappears, because the vocal folds are already apart for /s/ and come together in time for the vowel. This is why *spin* can sound to a Mandarin speaker like *sbin*: the /p/ of *spin* is unaspirated, like Pinyin *b*.
:::

::: example Puff or no puff? {#ex-aspiration}
Which /p/, /t/ or /k/ in these words is aspirated? *pen*, *spend*, *top*, *stop*, *cake*, *escape*, *happy*.
::: solution
Apply [[#rule-aspiration]] position by position:

1. *pen*: /p/ starts a stressed syllable — aspirated [pʰ].
2. *spend*: /p/ comes after /s/ — not aspirated.
3. *top*: the first /t/ starts a stressed syllable — aspirated; the final /p/ is at the end of the word — weak or no puff.
4. *stop*: /t/ after /s/ — not aspirated; final /p/ — weak.
5. *cake*: the first /k/ — aspirated; the final /k/ — weak.
6. *escape* /ɪˈskeɪp/: /k/ after /s/ — not aspirated, even though the syllable *-scape* is stressed.
7. *happy* /ˈhæpi/: /p/ starts an **unstressed** syllable — only weakly aspirated.

A paper test confirms it: the paper moves most for *pen*, *top* and *cake*.
:::
:::

::: quiz
In which word is the /t/ **not** aspirated?
- [ ] *tea*
- [ ] *attack*
- [x] *steam*
- [ ] *time*
::: solution
In *steam* the /t/ comes after /s/ in the same syllable, so it is unaspirated. In *tea*, *time* and *attack* (/əˈtæk/, stress on *-tack*) the /t/ begins a stressed syllable and is aspirated.
:::
:::

### At the end of a word: the vowel tells

::: theorem Final voiceless and voiced consonants {#rule-final}
At the end of a word, the difference between a voiceless consonant (/p t k f θ s ʃ tʃ/) and its voiced partner (/b d ɡ v ð z ʒ dʒ/) is carried mainly by the **vowel before it**: the vowel (and any /l/, /m/, /n/ or /ŋ/ between) is clearly longer before the voiced consonant. Compare *back – bag*, *cap – cab*, *right – ride*, *rice – rise*, *safe – save*, *bus – buzz*, *H* /eɪtʃ/ – *age* /eɪdʒ/. The voiced consonant itself is often only partly voiced, and the voiceless one is often made with a short, strong closure.
:::

::: proof
This is the same pre-fortis clipping that shortens long vowels ([[sounds-of-english/long-vowels-diphthongs#rule-clipping]]); it also affects short vowels (*bag* has a longer vowel than *back*, though both are TRAP). Final voiced consonants lose much of their voicing because the vocal folds stop vibrating as speech comes to an end. In addition, final /p/, /t/, /k/ are often made with no audible release, or with the vocal folds closed at the same time (a "glottal" closure), especially in GB and GA conversation. Listeners therefore depend on the vowel, and a learner who keeps every vowel the same length makes every final pair sound alike.
:::

::: warning Do not drop or "decorate" final consonants
✗ /bæ/ for *back* or *bag* — the consonant is dropped.
✗ /ˈbæɡə/ for *bag* — an extra vowel is added after the consonant.
✓ *bag* /bæɡ/: a **long** vowel, then a gentle closure; *back* /bæk/: a **short** vowel, then a firm closure.

Both mistakes come from languages, including Mandarin, in which syllables do not end in plosives. Adding a vowel can change the word or add a syllable (*bag* → *bagger*); dropping the consonant makes *back*, *bag*, *bat* and *bad* all sound the same.
:::

::: example Hearing the difference at the end {#ex-final}
Liu Yang wants to say *a bag of rice*, *a cab to the airport* and *I need to rise early*. What should he do with each vowel?
::: solution
1. *bag*: /ɡ/ is voiced, so make the TRAP vowel long: [bæːɡ]. A short vowel would give *back*.
2. *cab*: /b/ is voiced, so make the TRAP vowel long; a short vowel would give *cap*.
3. *rise*: /z/ is voiced, so make the PRICE diphthong long and let the /z/ buzz; a short diphthong and a hissing /s/ would give *rice*.

In all three, the long vowel does most of the work; a light voicing of the final consonant helps but is not enough on its own.
:::
:::

### Two common variants of /t/

- **The tap (GA).** Between vowels, after a stressed syllable, GA usually turns /t/ into a very quick tap of the tongue on the ridge, which sounds like a short /d/: *water*, *city*, *getting*, *a lot of*. LPD writes it t̬; phoneticians write [ɾ]. As a result, *writer* and *rider* can sound alike in GA (they are often kept apart by the length of the vowel, as [[#rule-final]] predicts). GB does not use the tap in careful speech.
- **The glottal stop.** Before another consonant, both GB and GA often replace or reinforce /t/ with a closure of the vocal folds, [ʔ]: *football* [ˈfʊʔbɔːl], *not now*, *Gatwick*. Many British speakers also use [ʔ] between vowels (*water* [ˈwɔːʔə]); this is common in everyday speech but is not part of the GB model.

You do not need to produce these variants, but you need to **recognise** them. They are discussed further in [[connected-speech/elision-assimilation]].

## Fricatives

::: definition Fricative {#def-fricative}
A **fricative** is a consonant made by bringing two articulators so close that the air is forced through a narrow gap and becomes turbulent, making a hissing noise. A fricative can be held as long as the breath lasts: *ffff*, *ssss*, *shhhh*. English has nine fricatives: /f v/ (labiodental), /θ ð/ (dental), /s z/ (alveolar), /ʃ ʒ/ (postalveolar) and /h/ (glottal).
:::

### /f/ and /v/

Top teeth on the inside of the lower lip; air through the gap. /f/ is voiceless, /v/ voiced. **Pairs:** *fan – van*, *fine – vine*, *ferry – very*, *safe – save*, *leaf – leave*, *half – have*.

/v/ is not /w/: for /w/ the lips are rounded and do not touch the teeth (see [[sounds-of-english/nasals-approximants]]). **Pairs:** *vest – west*, *vine – wine*, *verse – worse*, *veil – whale*.

- *I've got a very old van.*
- *We went west, wearing warm vests.*

### /θ/ and /ð/: the *th* sounds

**How to make them:** put the tip of the tongue lightly against the back of the top front teeth, or just between the top and bottom teeth. Do not bite. Blow gently, so that air passes between the tongue and the teeth. /θ/ is voiceless (*think*), /ð/ is voiced (*this*).

/θ/: *think*, *thank*, *three*, *Thursday*, *both*, *month*, *health*, *mouth* (noun), *bath*.
/ð/: *the*, *this*, *that*, *they*, *there*, *mother*, *weather*, *with* (often also /θ/), *breathe*, *smooth*.

The two are distinguished mainly in a handful of pairs: *thigh – thy*, *mouth* (noun) – *mouth* (verb, /maʊð/), *bath* – *bathe* (/beɪð/), *breath* – *breathe*.

**Substitutes and confusions:** learners who do not have these sounds usually replace /θ/ with /s/, /f/ or /t/, and /ð/ with /z/, /d/ or /l/. Each replacement makes real minimal pairs collapse: *think – sink – fink*, *thank – sank – tank*, *three – free – tree*, *then – den – Zen*, *they – day*.

::: warning *Th* is not *s*
✗ *I sink so.* (for *I think so.*)
✗ *Sank you very much.*
✓ *I think so.* *Thank you very much.*

/s/ is made with the tongue tip behind the ridge, the teeth close together; /θ/ with the tongue tip touching the teeth. In a mirror you should see the tip of your tongue for *th*. Not every English speaker uses /θ/ and /ð/ — some accents of London, Ireland, the Caribbean and elsewhere use /f/, /t/, /v/ or /d/ — but in both models (GB and GA) they are distinct phonemes, and listeners rely on them.
:::

### /s/, /z/, /ʃ/ and /ʒ/

/s/ and /z/: tongue tip (or blade) close to the alveolar ridge, sides of the tongue against the side teeth, a very narrow groove down the middle; the teeth are close together; the lips are spread. /ʃ/ and /ʒ/: the same, but further back, with a wider gap, and with the lips **rounded and pushed forward**.

**Pairs /s/–/ʃ/:** *see – she*, *sip – ship*, *sort – short*, *seat – sheet*, *save – shave*, *mess – mesh*.
**Pairs /s/–/z/:** *sip – zip*, *Sue – zoo*, *rice – rise*, *bus – buzz*, *place – plays*.

/ʒ/ is the rarest English consonant. It appears in the middle of words such as *measure*, *pleasure*, *usual*, *vision*, *television*, *decision*, *Asia* (GB /ˈeɪʃə/ or /ˈeɪʒə/), and at the end of some French borrowings: *beige*, *garage* (GB /ˈɡærɑːʒ/ or /ˈɡærɪdʒ/, GA /ɡəˈrɑːʒ/).

::: example /s/ or /ʃ/? {#ex-s-sh}
Find every /s/ and /ʃ/ in the sentence *Sam's sister sells fresh fish in the shop.*, and say what the tongue and lips do for each.
::: solution
Transcribe first (GB): /sæmz ˈsɪstə selz freʃ fɪʃ ɪn ðə ʃɒp/.

- /s/: *Sam's* (first sound), *sister* (two /s/), *sells* (first sound). Tongue tip close to the ridge, teeth close together, lips spread.
- /ʃ/: *fresh*, *fish*, *shop*. Tongue further back, wider gap, lips rounded and pushed forward.
- Not /s/: the *s* at the end of *Sam's* and *sells* is /z/, because it follows a voiced sound (see [[sounds-of-english/spelling-and-sound]]).

Say the sentence slowly in a mirror: the lips should move forward on *fresh*, *fish* and *shop* and spread again on *Sam's*, *sister* and *sells*. A speaker who rounds the lips on every *s* sound will say *shister* and *shells*.
:::
:::

### /h/

/h/ is a breath of air through the open vocal folds, with the mouth already in the position of the next vowel: *hat* is just a breathy start to /æt/. There is almost no friction in the mouth. /h/ appears only before a vowel (*hat*, *behind*), never at the end of a syllable. In some words the letter *h* is silent: *hour*, *honest*, *honour*, *heir*; and in unstressed *he*, *him*, *her*, *his* it is often dropped in connected speech (*tell him* /ˈtel ɪm/).

### Final fricatives

[[#rule-final]] applies here too. *Rice* and *rise*, *bus* and *buzz*, *safe* and *save*, *half* and *have* differ in the length of the vowel before the fricative more than in the voicing of the fricative itself.

::: example The *th* sounds {#ex-th}
Say *Thursday the thirteenth of March*. Which sounds are /θ/ and which /ð/? Where will a learner who replaces *th* with /s/ be misunderstood?
::: solution
- *Thursday* /ˈθɜːzdeɪ/ (GB), /ˈθɜːrzdeɪ/ (GA): /θ/ at the start.
- *the*: /ð/ (weak form /ðə/).
- *thirteenth* /ˌθɜːˈtiːnθ/ (GB), /ˌθɜːrˈtiːnθ/ (GA): /θ/ at the start **and** at the end.
- *of March*: no *th*.

A learner who uses /s/ will say something like /ˈsɜːzdeɪ zə ˌsɜːˈtiːns/. The listener may still understand from the context (there is no day called *Sursday*), but the word *thirteenth* ends like *teens*, and with numbers context does not help: *thirteenth* and *thirtieth*, or *three* and *free*, are easy to confuse. Dates, numbers and names are exactly where clear /θ/ matters most.
:::
:::

## Affricates

::: definition Affricate {#def-affricate}
An **affricate** is a plosive whose release is slow, so that the closure opens into a fricative at the same place: a stop and a fricative joined into one sound. English has two: voiceless /tʃ/ (*cheap*, *church*, *watch*) and voiced /dʒ/ (*jeep*, *judge*, *age*). Both are postalveolar, with rounded lips, like /ʃ/ and /ʒ/.
:::

**Pairs /tʃ/–/dʒ/:** *cheap – jeep*, *chin – gin*, *choke – joke*, *rich – ridge*, *H – age*. **Pairs /tʃ/–/ʃ/:** *chip – ship*, *cheap – sheep*, *watch – wash*, *chair – share*.

**Spellings:** /tʃ/ — *ch* (*chair*), *tch* (*watch*), *t* before *-ure* and *-ion* (*picture*, *question*). /dʒ/ — *j* (*job*), *g* before *e*, *i*, *y* (*age*, *giant*, *gym* — with exceptions: *get*, *give*, *girl*), *dge* (*bridge*), *d* before *-ule*, *-ual* in many words (*schedule* GA, *individual*).

The clusters /tr/ and /dr/ (*tree*, *dream*) sound very like affricates, because the /t/ and /d/ are made further back before /r/ and released into it. Many Chinese textbooks list them as separate sounds; they are clusters of two phonemes, but you can practise them as one movement.

::: quiz
Which word **begins** with an affricate?
- [ ] *ship*
- [x] *juice*
- [ ] *year*
- [ ] *zoo*
::: solution
*Juice* begins with /dʒ/, a stop released into a fricative. *Ship* begins with the fricative /ʃ/, *year* with the approximant /j/ and *zoo* with the fricative /z/.
:::
:::

## A phone call

Read the dialogue aloud. It has many *th* sounds, final consonants and /s/–/ʃ/ pairs.

**Receptionist:** *Good morning, Thornton Dental. How can I help?*

**Liu Yang:** *Hello. I'd like to change my appointment, please. It's on Thursday the third.*

**Receptionist:** *Could I have your name?*

**Liu Yang:** *Liu Yang. That's L-I-U, and then Yang, Y-A-N-G.*

**Receptionist:** *Thank you. I can see it — Thursday at three. When would you like to come instead?*

**Liu Yang:** *Is there anything the following week? Maybe the tenth?*

**Receptionist:** *I have Tuesday the eighth at half past ten, or the ninth in the morning.*

**Liu Yang:** *The eighth is fine. Thanks so much.*

::: note English and Chinese
- **Aspiration, not voice.** Mandarin keeps *b/p*, *d/t*, *g/k* apart by aspiration, as English does at the start of words. So at the start of a stressed syllable you can use your Pinyin *p, t, k* for English /p t k/ and your Pinyin *b, d, g* for English /b d ɡ/. Problems begin in other positions: after /s/ (*speak*, *study*, *school*, where English uses an **unaspirated** sound like Pinyin *b, d, g*) and at the end of words, where Mandarin has no plosives at all.
- **Final consonants.** Standard Mandarin syllables end only in a vowel, *-n*, *-ng* (or *-r*). Speakers tend to drop final plosives and fricatives or to add a vowel after them. Many Cantonese, Hakka and southern Min speakers have final /p t k/ in their home language, but these are unreleased, so they may need to practise releasing them clearly and making the vowel-length difference of [[#rule-final]].
- **Missing fricatives.** Standard Mandarin has no /θ/, /ð/, /v/, /z/ or /ʒ/ as separate sounds. Its *s* is close to English /s/, its *sh* is retroflex (the tongue tip curled back) and its *x* is made with the tongue blade raised towards the hard palate; English /ʃ/ is between the two, with rounded lips. Its *h* is velar, with friction at the back of the mouth; English /h/ is a gentle breath.
- **Before /iː/ and /ɪ/.** Mandarin has *xi* but no *si* (syllables like 西 are written *xi*), so some learners say *see*, *seat* and *sip* like *she*, *sheet* and *ship*. Keep the tongue tip forward and the lips spread for /s/.
- **ch, j, q, zh in Pinyin.** Pinyin *ch* and *zh* are retroflex affricates; *q* and *j* are made against the hard palate. English /tʃ/ and /dʒ/ are between them, with rounded lips. Pinyin *j* is voiceless, so English *jeep* needs a little more voice, especially between vowels (*major*, *enjoy*).
:::

::: history
Old English wrote the *th* sounds with two letters of its own: **þ** (thorn) and **ð** (eth), used for both /θ/ and /ð/ without a clear rule. Under the influence of French scribes after the Norman Conquest, the spelling *th* gradually replaced them, and thorn disappeared from printing; early printers, whose type came from the Continent and had no thorn, sometimes used *y* in its place. That is the origin of the *ye* in pseudo-old signs such as *Ye Olde Shoppe*: it was always pronounced *the*. The voiced /ð/ at the start of *the*, *this*, *they* and *there* comes from a later change in which very common function words voiced their first consonant.
:::

## Where this leads

The remaining consonants — nasals, /l/, /r/, /w/ and /j/ — are in [[sounds-of-english/nasals-approximants]]. Plosives and fricatives join into clusters in [[sounds-of-english/syllables-clusters]] (*str-*, *-sts*, *-ksθs*). In fast speech /t/ and /d/ are often dropped or changed ([[connected-speech/elision-assimilation]]). The difficulties of Chinese speakers with these sounds are collected in [[sounds-of-english/sounds-for-chinese-speakers]].

::: summary
- A **plosive** closes, holds and releases: /p b t d k ɡ/ ([[#def-plosive]]). English /t d/ are made on the alveolar ridge.
- /p t k/ are **aspirated** at the start of a stressed syllable and unaspirated after /s/: *pin* [pʰɪn], *spin* [spɪn] ([[#rule-aspiration]]). At the start of a word, *pin* and *bin* differ mainly by the puff of air — as Pinyin *p* and *b* do.
- At the end of a word, voiceless–voiced pairs differ mainly in the **length of the vowel before them**: *back – bag*, *rice – rise* ([[#rule-final]]). Do not drop final consonants or add vowels after them.
- A **fricative** forces air through a narrow gap: /f v θ ð s z ʃ ʒ h/ ([[#def-fricative]]). For /θ ð/ the tongue touches the teeth; for /ʃ ʒ/ the lips are rounded; /h/ is just breath.
- An **affricate** is a stop released into a fricative: /tʃ dʒ/ ([[#def-affricate]]).
- GA taps /t/ between vowels (*water*); GB and GA often use a glottal stop for /t/ before consonants (*football*).
- Mandarin lacks /θ ð v z ʒ/ and final plosives; its *sh*, *x*, *h*, *ch*, *zh*, *j*, *q* differ from the nearest English sounds.
:::

::: exercise Correct the transcription {level=3}
A learner transcribed what he says, and his teacher marked the words that were misunderstood. Explain each problem using the rules of this chapter, and say what he should change.

1. *bag* said as [bæk]  2. *three* said as [sriː]  3. *very* said as [ˈweri]  4. *speak* said as [spʰiːk]  5. *rise* said as [raɪs]
::: solution
1. The vowel was short and the closure strong, so listeners heard *back*. Lengthen the vowel before /ɡ/ and make a gentle closure ([[#rule-final]]).
2. /θ/ was replaced by /s/, giving a non-word close to *free*/*tree*. Put the tongue tip against the teeth and blow ([[#def-fricative]]).
3. /v/ was replaced by /w/, which sounds like *wary*. Put the top teeth on the lower lip and add voice; do not round the lips.
4. The /p/ after /s/ was aspirated. This does not usually cause misunderstanding, but it sounds unusual; after /s/, use an unaspirated sound like Pinyin *b* ([[#rule-aspiration]]).
5. The PRICE vowel was short and the /z/ hissed like /s/, so listeners heard *rice*. Lengthen the diphthong and let the /z/ buzz.
:::
:::

::: exercise A tongue twister of your own {level=3}
Write a short sentence (six to ten words) that contains at least three words with /θ/ or /ð/ and two words ending in a voiced plosive (/b/, /d/ or /ɡ/). Transcribe the key words and say it five times, faster each time, without losing the *th* sounds or the long vowels.
::: solution
Answers will differ. A model: *Their three dogs dragged the thick bag.* — *Their* /ð/, *three* /θ/, *the* /ð/, *thick* /θ/; *dogs* ends in /ɡz/ and *dragged* in /ɡd/ (both voiced), *bag* in /ɡ/. Key transcriptions (GB): /ðeə θriː dɒɡz dræɡd ðə θɪk bæɡ/. Check yourself: the tongue should touch the teeth four times, and the vowels of *dogs*, *dragged* and *bag* should be noticeably long.
:::
