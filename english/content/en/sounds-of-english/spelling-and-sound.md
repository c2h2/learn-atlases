Read these two sentences aloud: *I read a book every evening.* *Yesterday I read a book about Mars.* The word *read* is spelled the same in both, but it is /riːd/ in the first and /red/ in the second. Now look at *hoping* and *hopping*, *diner* and *dinner*: one extra letter, and a different vowel. English spelling is not a simple code for sounds — but it is not chaos either. Most words follow patterns, and the patterns can be learned. The difficult words are mostly very common ones, which you will meet so often that you can learn them one by one.

This chapter gives the main patterns: how consonant letters are pronounced, what doubled consonants and the "silent *e*" tell you about the vowel, the most common vowel spellings, silent letters, and the endings *-s* and *-ed*. It ends with the history that explains why so many everyday words break the patterns. It uses the symbols of [[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet]] and the syllables of [[sounds-of-english/syllables-clusters]].

## Letters and sounds

English has 26 letters and, in GB, 44 phonemes. So some phonemes must be written with groups of letters, and some letters must stand for more than one sound.

::: definition Grapheme {#def-grapheme}
A **grapheme** is a letter or group of letters that stands for one phoneme in a word. In *ship* /ʃɪp/ there are three graphemes: *sh*, *i*, *p*. In *night* /naɪt/ there are three: *n*, *igh*, *t*. A group of two letters for one sound (*sh*, *ee*, *ck*) is called a **digraph**; a group of three (*igh*, *tch*, *dge*) a **trigraph**.
:::

The relation goes both ways. One phoneme can have many spellings: the figure shows the main spellings of /iː/. And one grapheme can have several pronunciations: *ea* is /iː/ in *meat*, /e/ in *bread* and /eɪ/ in *great* ([[sounds-of-english/long-vowels-diphthongs#ex-ea]]).

The first three are by far the most common; the others appear in a small number of words, and the last one in one common word only. Most English vowels have a similar fan of spellings.

## Consonant letters

Most consonant letters have one main value, the one in the phonemic alphabet: *b* /b/, *d* /d/, *f* /f/, *m* /m/ and so on. The main exceptions follow patterns.

::: theorem Soft and hard *c* and *g* {#rule-c-g}
1. *c* is "soft" /s/ before *e*, *i* or *y* (*cent*, *city*, *cycle*, *place*) and "hard" /k/ elsewhere (*cat*, *cold*, *cut*, *class*, *music*).
2. *g* is usually "hard" /ɡ/ before *a*, *o*, *u*, a consonant or at the end (*game*, *go*, *gun*, *glass*, *bag*). Before *e*, *i* or *y* it is often "soft" /dʒ/ (*gem*, *giant*, *gym*, *age*, *page*), but there are many common exceptions with /ɡ/: *get*, *give*, *girl*, *gift*, *begin*, *together*, *tiger*.
3. To keep *g* hard before *e* or *i*, English sometimes adds *u*: *guess*, *guide*, *guitar*. To show a soft *g* or *c* before *a* or *o*, it keeps a silent *e*: *changeable*, *noticeable*.
:::

::: proof
The rule for *c* is very reliable; the rule for *g* is only a tendency. The reason is history. The soft values came into English mainly through French and Latin words (*city*, *gem*), while many native English words kept a hard *g* before *e* and *i* (*get*, *give*, *begin*), sometimes under the influence of Old Norse. So for *g* before *e*, *i* or *y*, check the dictionary when a word is new.
:::

The single letter *s* is /s/ at the start of a word (*sun*) but often /z/ between vowels (*rose*, *music*, *easy*, *busy*) and in the endings *-s* and *-es* after voiced sounds (see [[#rule-s-ed]]).

### What doubled consonants tell you

::: theorem Doubled consonants and short vowels {#rule-double}
In a stressed syllable, a **doubled** consonant letter (or *ck*, *tch*, *dge*) after a single vowel letter usually shows that the vowel is **short**; a **single** consonant letter followed by another vowel letter usually shows that it is **long** (a long vowel or diphthong):

| short vowel | long vowel |
|---|---|
| *hopping* /ˈhɒpɪŋ/ (GB) | *hoping* /ˈhəʊpɪŋ/ (GB) |
| *dinner* /ˈdɪnə/ | *diner* /ˈdaɪnə/ |
| *latter* /ˈlætə/ | *later* /ˈleɪtə/ |
| *filling* /ˈfɪlɪŋ/ | *filing* /ˈfaɪlɪŋ/ |
| *back*, *catch*, *badge* | *bake*, *coach*, *page* |

The doubled consonant is pronounced as **one** consonant: *dinner* has one /n/.
:::

::: proof
The double letter is a spelling device, not a sound: it closes the stressed syllable, which signals a short ("checked") vowel ([[sounds-of-english/short-vowels#rule-checked]]). It is the reason for the familiar spelling rule of adding endings: *hop* + *-ing* → *hopping* (double the consonant to keep the vowel short), but *hope* + *-ing* → *hoping* (drop the *e*; the single consonant keeps the vowel long). There are exceptions in both directions — *very*, *many*, *body*, *city* and *river* have short vowels before single consonants — so the rule is reliable for reading words with endings (*-ing*, *-er*, *-ed*), less so for other words. Spelling changes with endings are taught in [[word-formation/spelling-changes]].
:::

## Vowel letters

Each of the five vowel letters has a "short" value and a "long" value. The long value is usually the **name** of the letter.

| letter | name / long value | short value |
|---|---|---|
| *a* | /eɪ/ *name*, *late* | /æ/ *cat*, *hat* |
| *e* | /iː/ *these*, *Pete* | /e/ *bed*, *pet* |
| *i* (and *y*) | /aɪ/ *time*, *bite*, *my* | /ɪ/ *sit*, *bit*, *gym* |
| *o* | /əʊ/ (GA /oʊ/) *home*, *hope* | /ɒ/ (GA /ɑː/) *hot*, *hop* |
| *u* | /juː/ *cute*, *tube*; /uː/ *rule* | /ʌ/ *cut*, *tub*; /ʊ/ *put* |

::: theorem The silent *e* {#rule-silent-e}
A final *e* after a single consonant letter is usually silent and shows that the vowel letter before the consonant has its long ("name") value: *hat* /hæt/ – *hate* /heɪt/; *pet* – *Pete*; *bit* – *bite*; *hop* – *hope*; *cut* – *cute*. In longer words it works the same way in the stressed syllable: *mistake*, *complete*, *arrive*, *alone*.
:::

::: proof
The final *e* was once pronounced; in Middle English *name* had two syllables. When the final vowel was lost (in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries), the preceding long vowel stayed long, and the *e* remained in the spelling as a sign of it. Writers then added *e* to other words by analogy. Several very common words break the pattern, because their vowels later became short or changed in other ways: *have*, *give*, *live* (the verb), *come*, *some*, *done*, *love*, *gone*, *one*, *were*, *there*. A silent *e* also has other jobs: it keeps *c* and *g* soft (*dance*, *large*), and it stops a word ending in *v* or *u* (*have*, *blue*), which English spelling avoids.
:::

::: quiz
Which word does **not** follow the silent-*e* pattern?
- [ ] *same*
- [ ] *note*
- [x] *come*
- [ ] *wide*
::: solution
In *same* /seɪm/, *note* /nəʊt/ (GB) and *wide* /waɪd/, the final *e* makes the vowel long. *Come* /kʌm/ has the short STRUT vowel, although it is spelled like *home*. It is one of the common exceptions (with *some*, *done*, *love*, *have*, *give*).
:::
:::

::: example Predicting the pronunciation of new words {#ex-predict}
You meet these words for the first time: *mope*, *glitch*, *strand*, *flaw*, *sleet*, *pounce*. Use the patterns to predict how they are pronounced, then say how sure you can be.
::: solution
1. *mope*: *o* + consonant + silent *e* → long /əʊ/ ([[#rule-silent-e]]): /məʊp/ (GB), /moʊp/ (GA). Very likely.
2. *glitch*: *tch* after *i* → short /ɪ/ and /tʃ/: /ɡlɪtʃ/. Very likely.
3. *strand*: *a* before a cluster, no final *e* → short /æ/: /strænd/. Very likely (GB does not use /ɑː/ here).
4. *flaw*: *aw* → /ɔː/: /flɔː/. Very likely.
5. *sleet*: *ee* → /iː/: /sliːt/. Almost certain: *ee* is very regular.
6. *pounce*: *ou* → usually /aʊ/; *ce* → soft /s/ with silent *e*: /paʊns/. Likely, but *ou* has other values (*touch*, *soup*), so a dictionary check is wise.

All six predictions are in fact correct. Regular patterns work best for words like these — less common words of one syllable. The most common words (*one*, *two*, *have*, *does*) are the most irregular.
:::
:::

## Silent letters

::: theorem Common silent letters {#rule-silent}
These letters are not pronounced in the patterns shown:

| pattern | examples |
|---|---|
| *kn-*, *gn-* at the start | *know*, *knee*, *knife*; *gnaw*, *sign*, *foreign* |
| *wr-* at the start | *write*, *wrong*, *wrist* |
| *-mb* at the end | *lamb*, *climb*, *thumb*, *bomb* |
| *b* before *t* | *doubt*, *debt*, *subtle* |
| *-stle*, *-sten* | *castle*, *whistle*, *listen*, *fasten* |
| *l* in *-alk*, *-alm*, *-alf*, *-ould* | *walk*, *talk*, *calm*, *half*, *could*, *should* |
| *gh* | *night*, *though*, *daughter* (but /f/ in *laugh*, *enough*, *cough*) |
| *h* | *hour*, *honest*, *honour*; *what*, *why* (in GB and most GA) |
| others | *psychology* (*p*), *autumn* (*n*), *island* (*s*), *answer*, *two* (*w*), *Wednesday* (first *d*), *receipt* (*p*) |
:::

::: example Finding silent letters {#ex-silent}
Find the silent letters in this sentence and give the transcription of each word that contains one: *The knight could not climb the castle wall, so he went home to listen to the rain.*
::: solution
- *knight* /naɪt/: *k* and *gh* silent.
- *could* /kʊd/: *l* silent.
- *climb* /klaɪm/: *b* silent.
- *castle* /ˈkɑːsl/ (GB), /ˈkæsl/ (GA): *t* silent (and *e* — the syllable is a syllabic /l/).
- *home* /həʊm/: final *e* silent (it marks the long vowel).
- *listen* /ˈlɪsn/: *t* silent.
- *rain* /reɪn/: *ai* is one vowel; no letter is silent, but two letters stand for one sound.

In *the*, the *e* is not silent: it stands for schwa (/ðə/). In *wall* /wɔːl/, two letters *l* stand for one /l/ — a doubled letter, not a silent one.
:::
:::

::: warning Do not pronounce what you see
✗ /ˈwednzdeɪ/ for *Wednesday*, ✗ /ˈsælmən/ for *salmon*, ✗ /ˈaɪrən/ in GB for *iron*, ✗ /ˈlɪstən/ with /t/ for *listen*.
✓ *Wednesday* /ˈwenzdeɪ/, *salmon* /ˈsæmən/, *iron* /ˈaɪən/ (GB), /ˈaɪərn/ (GA), *listen* /ˈlɪsn/.

Pronouncing silent letters is called a **spelling pronunciation**. A few spelling pronunciations have become accepted over time (many speakers now pronounce the *t* in *often*: LPD gives both /ˈɒfn/ and /ˈɒftən/), but most of them simply sound wrong. When a common word looks strange, check its transcription once and remember it.
:::

## The endings *-s* and *-ed*

The most frequent spellings in English are the grammatical endings *-s* (plurals, the third person, possessives) and *-ed* (past tense, past participle). They have three pronunciations each, chosen by the **last sound** of the word they are added to.

::: theorem Pronouncing *-s* and *-ed* {#rule-s-ed}
**The ending *-s* / *-es***:
1. /ɪz/ (GA also /əz/) after the hissing and hushing sounds /s z ʃ ʒ tʃ dʒ/: *buses*, *roses*, *wishes*, *garages*, *watches*, *judges*. This adds a syllable.
2. /s/ after other voiceless sounds /p t k f θ/: *cups*, *cats*, *books*, *laughs*, *months*.
3. /z/ after other voiced sounds, including all vowels: *dogs*, *bags*, *days*, *runs*, *calls*, *goes*.

**The ending *-ed***:
1. /ɪd/ (GA often /əd/) after /t/ or /d/: *wanted*, *needed*, *started*. This adds a syllable.
2. /t/ after other voiceless sounds /p k f θ s ʃ tʃ/: *stopped*, *looked*, *laughed*, *missed*, *washed*, *watched*.
3. /d/ after other voiced sounds, including all vowels: *played*, *cleaned*, *lived*, *called*, *changed*.
:::

::: proof
The pattern follows from what English allows at the end of a syllable. After /s/, /z/ and the other hissing sounds, another /s/ or /z/ would be almost impossible to hear (*bus* + /z/), so a vowel is added; after /t/ and /d/, the same happens with *-ed*. Elsewhere, the ending simply takes on the voicing of the sound before it: voiceless after voiceless (*cats*, *looked*), voiced after voiced (*dogs*, *played*), because a cluster in the coda must be either all voiceless or (for /z/ and /d/) voiced at its end ([[sounds-of-english/syllables-clusters#rule-codas]]). Note that it is the **sound** that decides, not the letter: *laughs* ends in the sound /f/, so it takes /s/; *raced* ends in the sound /s/, so it takes /t/.
:::

::: example Choosing the ending {#ex-endings}
Pronounce *-s* or *-ed* in: *boxes*, *thinks*, *pens*, *hoped*, *ended*, *fixed*, *judged*.
::: solution
Find the last sound of the base word first:

1. *box* ends in /ks/ — the last sound /s/ is hissing → /ɪz/: *boxes* /ˈbɒksɪz/ (GB), 2 syllables.
2. *think* ends in /k/, voiceless → /s/: *thinks* /θɪŋks/.
3. *pen* ends in /n/, voiced → /z/: *pens* /penz/.
4. *hope* ends in /p/ (the *e* is silent), voiceless → /t/: *hoped* /həʊpt/ (GB), 1 syllable.
5. *end* ends in /d/ → /ɪd/: *ended* /ˈendɪd/, 2 syllables.
6. *fix* ends in /ks/, voiceless → /t/: *fixed* /fɪkst/, 1 syllable.
7. *judge* ends in /dʒ/, voiced but not /t/ or /d/ → /d/: *judged* /dʒʌdʒd/, 1 syllable.
:::
:::

::: warning *-ed* is not always a syllable
✗ /ˈwɒʃɪd/ for *washed*, ✗ /ˈplɑːnɪd/ for *planned*, ✗ /ˈlʊkɪd/ for *looked*.
✓ *washed* /wɒʃt/, *planned* /plænd/, *looked* /lʊkt/ — one syllable each.

Only after /t/ and /d/ does *-ed* add a syllable. (A few adjectives keep an old /ɪd/: *a learned professor* /ˈlɜːnɪd/, *a naked eye*, *wicked*, *a crooked line*, *my beloved* — but the verbs *learned*, *crooked* are one syllable.)
:::

## Same spelling, different words

**Homographs** are words with the same spelling and different pronunciations. Some are different words; many are a noun and a verb with different stress or a different final consonant.

- *read* /riːd/ (present) – *read* /red/ (past)
- *live* /lɪv/ (verb) – *live* /laɪv/ (adjective: *live music*)
- *lead* /liːd/ (to guide) – *lead* /led/ (a metal)
- *tear* /tɪə/ (from the eye) – *tear* /teə/ (to rip)
- *wind* /wɪnd/ (moving air) – *wind* /waɪnd/ (to turn)
- *close* /kləʊz/ (to shut) – *close* /kləʊs/ (near)
- *use* /juːz/ (verb) – *use* /juːs/ (noun)
- *record* /rɪˈkɔːd/ (verb) – *record* /ˈrekɔːd/ (noun), GB; see [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics]]

::: example Choosing between homographs {#ex-homograph}
Choose the pronunciation of the word in bold in each sentence, and say what in the sentence tells you.

1. *The **wind** was so strong that the trees bent.*
2. *Don't forget to **wind** the clock.*
3. *Please **close** the window.*
4. *The station is very **close** to our house.*
5. *What's the **use** of a map without a compass?*
::: solution
1. /wɪnd/ — *the* shows it is a noun ('moving air').
2. /waɪnd/ — after *to*, it is a verb ('turn something round').
3. /kləʊz/ (GB), /kloʊz/ (GA) — after *please*, an instruction: the verb 'shut', with /z/.
4. /kləʊs/ (GB), /kloʊs/ (GA) — after *very*, an adjective ('near'), with /s/.
5. /juːs/ — after *the*, a noun, with /s/; the verb (*Can I use it?*) has /juːz/.

The grammar of the sentence — a noun after *the*, a verb after *to* or as an instruction, an adjective after *very* — chooses the pronunciation. Noun–verb pairs like *use* and *close* often differ in the voicing of the last consonant: voiceless for the noun or adjective, voiced for the verb (also *advice – advise*, *belief – believe*).
:::
:::

## Reading aloud: a short notice

> *Welcome to Riverside Library. Please switch off your phone, keep your bags on the floor, and speak quietly. Children under eight must be with an adult. We close at half past nine on weekdays and at noon on Saturdays.*

Patterns in the notice: a silent *e* marking a long vowel in *side*, *phone*, *close* and *nine* (but not in *welcome* /ˈwelkəm/, which contains the exception *come*); *ph* in *phone*; *tch* in *switch*; *igh* in *eight* (/eɪt/, with *ei* + silent *gh*); *oo* in *floor* (/ɔː/ — an exception) and *noon* (/uː/); silent *l* in *half*; *-s* as /z/ in *bags*, *weekdays*, *Saturdays*; *-ly* in *quietly*.

::: note English and Chinese
- **Characters and sound.** Chinese characters do not show their pronunciation reliably either: the phonetic part of a character such as 清 gives a clue, but many characters with the same phonetic part are said differently. Chinese readers therefore already know that writing can be a rough guide to sound. English spelling is more regular than many learners think: use the patterns of this chapter, and learn the very common exceptions one by one.
- **Pinyin habits.** Pinyin is almost perfectly regular, and learners sometimes expect English letters to be the same. In particular, they expect every written vowel to be pronounced (*Wednesday*, *chocolate*), every letter to have one value (*a* in *cat*, *car*, *care*, *was*, *about*), and *e* at the end of a word to be a vowel (it is usually silent).
- **Phonics.** Many Chinese primary schools now teach **phonics** (自然拼读): letter–sound patterns such as the ones in this chapter. Phonics is very useful for reading new words aloud, and for spelling words you hear; it cannot replace the dictionary for the irregular common words, or for stress, which spelling does not show.
- **Learning new words.** When you learn a new word, learn three things together: its spelling, its transcription (with the stress mark), and its meaning. A word learned only from its spelling is often learned with the wrong sound.
:::

::: history
Why is English spelling like this? Several layers of history lie on top of each other. Old English spelling was fairly regular. After the Norman Conquest of 1066, French-trained scribes introduced new spellings: *qu* for older *cw* (*queen*), *ou* for the vowel /uː/ (*hous*, later *house*), *gh* for a sound like the *ch* of Scottish *loch* (*night*), and *c* before *e* and *i* for /s/. William Caxton set up the first printing press in England in 1476, and printers gradually fixed the spelling of many words — but during the same centuries the Great Vowel Shift (c. 1400–1700) changed the long vowels, and sounds such as the *k* of *knight* and the *gh* of *night* were lost. The spelling stayed while the pronunciation moved. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, scholars added letters to show the Latin origin of words: *b* in *debt* and *doubt* (Latin *debitum*, *dubitare*), and, by mistake, *s* in *island* (by false association with *isle*, which comes from Latin *insula*; *island* is in fact a native English word). Samuel Johnson's *Dictionary* (1755) helped to standardise British spelling, and Noah Webster's *American Dictionary* (1828) established some American forms (*color*, *center*).
:::

## Where this leads

The patterns of this chapter work only for the **sounds**; they do not show which syllable is stressed. That is the subject of [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics]]. Spelling changes when endings are added (*hopping*, *happier*) are in [[word-formation/spelling-changes]], capital letters and spelling in writing in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/capitals-spelling]], and the history of English spelling in [[history-of-english/early-modern-english]] and [[history-of-english/dictionaries-grammars]].

::: summary
- A **grapheme** is a letter or letter group for one phoneme (*sh*, *igh*, *tch*); English has 26 letters for 44 phonemes (GB) ([[#def-grapheme]]).
- *c* is /s/ before *e*, *i*, *y* and /k/ elsewhere; *g* before *e*, *i*, *y* is often /dʒ/ but has many exceptions ([[#rule-c-g]]).
- A doubled consonant (or *ck*, *tch*, *dge*) usually follows a short vowel: *hopping*, *dinner*, *back* ([[#rule-double]]).
- A final silent *e* usually makes the vowel before it long: *hat – hate*, *bit – bite* — except in common words such as *have*, *give*, *come*, *love* ([[#rule-silent-e]]).
- Vowel letters before *r* have their own values (*car*, *her*, *for*, *care*); common vowel digraphs (*ee*, *ai*, *oa*, *ou*, *ow*, *oo*) have a main value and some exceptions.
- Silent letters follow patterns: *kn-*, *wr-*, *-mb*, *-stle*, *l* in *walk*, *gh* in *night* ([[#rule-silent]]).
- *-s* is /ɪz/, /s/ or /z/ and *-ed* is /ɪd/, /t/ or /d/, chosen by the last **sound** of the word ([[#rule-s-ed]]).
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Soft or hard? {level=1}
Is the *c* or *g* in each word soft (/s/, /dʒ/) or hard (/k/, /ɡ/)?

*city*, *cup*, *face*, *give*, *page*, *garden*, *gym*, *cycle*
::: solution
Soft: *city* /s/, *face* /s/, *page* /dʒ/, *gym* /dʒ/, *cycle* (the first *c*) /s/. Hard: *cup* /k/, *give* /ɡ/ (an exception before *i*), *garden* /ɡ/. In *cycle* the second *c* is hard (/ˈsaɪkl/).
:::
:::

::: exercise Long or short vowel? {level=1}
Is the stressed vowel long (a long vowel or diphthong) or short? Use [[#rule-double]] and [[#rule-silent-e]].

*hoping*, *hopping*, *later*, *latter*, *cute*, *cut*, *ride*, *ridden*
::: solution
Long: *hoping* (/əʊ/), *later* (/eɪ/), *cute* (/juː/), *ride* (/aɪ/). Short: *hopping* (/ɒ/, GB), *latter* (/æ/), *cut* (/ʌ/), *ridden* (/ɪ/).
:::
:::

::: exercise Silent letters {level=1}
Write the silent letter(s) in each word.

*knife*, *write*, *thumb*, *listen*, *half*, *hour*, *island*, *doubt*
::: solution
*knife* — *k* (and final *e*, which marks the long vowel); *write* — *w* (and final *e*); *thumb* — *b*; *listen* — *t*; *half* — *l*; *hour* — *h*; *island* — *s*; *doubt* — *b*.
:::
:::

::: exercise *-s* endings {level=2}
Is the ending /s/, /z/ or /ɪz/?

*maps*, *eggs*, *classes*, *laughs*, *trees*, *bridges*, *clothes*, *weeks*
::: solution
/s/: *maps*, *laughs* (last sound /f/), *weeks*. /z/: *eggs*, *trees*, *clothes* (/kləʊðz/; many speakers say /kləʊz/). /ɪz/: *classes*, *bridges*.
:::
:::

::: exercise *-ed* endings {level=2}
Is the ending /t/, /d/ or /ɪd/?

*asked*, *rained*, *visited*, *dressed*, *loved*, *decided*, *reached*, *stayed*
::: solution
/t/: *asked*, *dressed*, *reached*. /d/: *rained*, *loved*, *stayed*. /ɪd/: *visited*, *decided*.
:::
:::

::: exercise Count the extra syllables {level=2 check="3"}
In the sentence *She washed the dishes, watched the news and painted the fences.*, how many of the endings *-s*, *-es* and *-ed* add a syllable?
::: hint
Check the last sound of each base word: *wash*, *dish*, *watch*, *news* (already plural), *paint*, *fence*.
:::
::: solution
- *washed* /wɒʃt/ — no extra syllable (/ʃ/ is voiceless, not /t/ or /d/).
- *dishes* /ˈdɪʃɪz/ — **extra syllable** (after /ʃ/).
- *watched* /wɒtʃt/ — no extra syllable.
- *news* — not an ending here.
- *painted* /ˈpeɪntɪd/ — **extra syllable** (after /t/).
- *fences* /ˈfensɪz/ — **extra syllable** (after /s/).

The answer is 3.
:::
:::

::: exercise Explain the exceptions {level=3}
A friend says: "English spelling has no rules: look at *have* and *gave*, *come* and *home*, *put* and *but*." Write a short reply (four to six sentences) that explains what the patterns are, why these particular words break them, and how a learner should deal with such words.
::: solution
A model answer: "There are rules, but some very common words break them. A final silent *e* usually makes the vowel long, as in *gave* and *home*; *have* and *come* are exceptions because their vowels became short centuries ago, after the spelling had been fixed. The letter *u* usually has the STRUT vowel, as in *but*; *put* kept an older vowel, because the change from the FOOT vowel to STRUT did not happen after lip sounds like /p/. Exceptions like these are mostly very frequent words, so you hear them often enough to learn them one by one. For less common words, the patterns work well, and when you are not sure, a dictionary transcription gives the answer."
:::
:::

::: exercise Read aloud {level=3}
Read the following text aloud, recording yourself. Before you read, mark (1) every silent letter, (2) every *-s* and *-ed* ending with its pronunciation, and (3) any word you are not sure about. Check those words in a dictionary.

> *Last autumn my neighbour planted twelve lemon trees in his garden. He watered them daily and talked to them while he worked. Now he answers every question about lemons, whether you ask or not.*
::: solution
Silent letters: *autumn* (final *n*), *neighbour* (*gh*; GA *neighbor*), *talked* (*l*), *answers* (*w*), *whether* and *while* (*h*, in GB and most GA), *twelve* (final *e*). Endings: *planted* /ɪd/; *trees* /z/; *watered* /d/; *talked* /t/; *worked* /t/; *answers* /z/; *lemons* /z/. Words learners often check: *autumn* /ˈɔːtəm/, *neighbour* /ˈneɪbə/, *daily* /ˈdeɪli/, *question* /ˈkwestʃən/, *whether* /ˈweðə/ (GB). A good reading keeps *planted* at two syllables, *talked* and *worked* at one, and does not pronounce the silent letters.
:::
:::
