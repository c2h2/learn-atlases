"I'm sinking about it," says Huang Mei, and her friend wonders what is sinking. Huang Mei meant *thinking*, and she has made two small changes: /θ/ became /s/ at the start, and at the end the *ng* became /n/. The first change was the subject of [[sounds-of-english/stops-fricatives]]. This chapter is about the second kind, and about the other sounds that learners of English often find slippery: the nasals /m/, /n/, /ŋ/, the two kinds of /l/, the English /r/, and the glides /w/ and /j/.

These seven sounds have something in common: the air flows freely, through the nose or over the tongue, without the noise of a fricative or the burst of a plosive. They are all voiced. And they are the consonants whose **position in the word** matters most: /ŋ/ can never begin a word, GB says /r/ only before a vowel, and /l/ changes its sound at the end of a word.

## Nasals

::: definition Nasal {#def-nasal}
A **nasal** is a consonant made with a complete closure in the mouth and the soft palate lowered, so that all the air goes out through the nose. English has three nasals, all voiced: bilabial /m/ (*map*), alveolar /n/ (*nap*) and velar /ŋ/ (*sing*).
:::

The three nasals are made in exactly the same three places as the three pairs of plosives. The only difference is the soft palate: up for the plosive, down for the nasal.

::: widget graph
nodes: lips@-3,2.6; alveolar ridge@0,2.6; soft palate@3,2.6; p b@-3,1.2; t d@0,1.2; k ɡ@3,1.2; m@-3,-0.2; n@0,-0.2; ŋ@3,-0.2
edges: lips-p b; alveolar ridge-t d; soft palate-k ɡ; p b-m; t d-n; k ɡ-ŋ
caption: Plosives and nasals share their places. Each column is one place of closure: the lips, the alveolar ridge, the soft palate. Above, the plosives (the soft palate is raised, air is stopped); below, the nasal made at the same place (the soft palate is lowered, air goes through the nose). To find /ŋ/, say *k*, hold the closure at the back of the mouth, and hum. You can drag the points.
:::

### /m/ and /n/

/m/ (both lips closed) and /n/ (tongue tip on the alveolar ridge) are easy for most learners at the start of a syllable. At the end of a word they need care:

- /m/ and /n/ at the end of a word must be kept apart: *some – sun*, *team – teen*, *home* – *hone*, *Tim – tin*. Close the lips for /m/; keep them open, with the tongue on the ridge, for /n/.
- Hold a final nasal long enough to be heard, especially before a pause. A final nasal that is only a nasal colour on the vowel (as in French *bon*) may not be heard as a consonant.

### /ŋ/

To make /ŋ/, raise the back of the tongue against the soft palate, as for /k/ or /ɡ/, and let the air out through the nose. The tongue tip stays down, behind the lower teeth.

::: theorem Where /ŋ/ can appear {#rule-ng}
1. /ŋ/ **never begins** an English word or syllable. It comes only after a vowel: *sing*, *long*, *thank*, *finger*.
2. It is spelled *ng* (*sing*, *young*) or *n* before /k/ or /ɡ/ (*think* /θɪŋk/, *uncle* /ˈʌŋkl/, *anger* /ˈæŋɡə/ (GB)).
3. In GB and GA, *ng* at the **end of a word** is /ŋ/ with no /ɡ/: *sing* /sɪŋ/, *long* /lɒŋ/ (GB). This stays true when an ending is added to the word: *singer* /ˈsɪŋə/, *singing* /ˈsɪŋɪŋ/, *longing*.
4. *ng* in the **middle of a word** that is not made from a shorter word usually has /ŋɡ/: *finger* /ˈfɪŋɡə/, *English* /ˈɪŋɡlɪʃ/, *hungry* /ˈhʌŋɡri/, *language*. The comparatives *longer*, *stronger*, *younger* also have /ŋɡ/.
:::

::: proof
In Middle English, *ng* was always /ŋɡ/. Between the sixteenth and the seventeenth centuries the /ɡ/ was lost at the end of words, and words formed from them kept the new pronunciation (*sing* → *singer*). Words that were not formed in this way, such as *finger*, kept the /ɡ/. The comparatives are an old exception. In the north-west of England and the English Midlands, many speakers still pronounce /ŋɡ/ in *sing* and *singer* — another reminder that GB is one model among many accents. Points 3 and 4 are therefore tendencies with a history, and LPD shows the exceptions word by word.
:::

::: example *Singer* and *finger* {#ex-ng}
Transcribe these words (GB): *thing*, *thinking*, *finger*, *singer*, *younger*, *bank*.
::: solution
Apply [[#rule-ng]] word by word:

1. *thing* /θɪŋ/: *ng* at the end of a word — /ŋ/ only.
2. *thinking* /ˈθɪŋkɪŋ/: *n* before /k/ is /ŋ/; final *-ing* is /ɪŋ/.
3. *finger* /ˈfɪŋɡə/: *ng* in the middle of a word not made from a shorter word — /ŋɡ/.
4. *singer* /ˈsɪŋə/: *sing* + *-er*; the /ŋ/ of *sing* is kept, with no /ɡ/.
5. *younger* /ˈjʌŋɡə/: a comparative — /ŋɡ/ (compare *young* /jʌŋ/).
6. *bank* /bæŋk/: *n* before /k/ is /ŋ/.

In GA the transcriptions are the same except for the final /ər/ of *finger*, *singer*, *younger*.
:::
:::

::: warning Keep final /n/ and /ŋ/ apart
✗ *I'm thinkin about it.* said with /n/ in careful speech, or *thing* said as *thin*.
✓ *thin* /θɪn/ – *thing* /θɪŋ/; *ran* /ræn/ – *rang* /ræŋ/; *win* /wɪn/ – *wing* /wɪŋ/; *ton* /tʌn/ – *tongue* /tʌŋ/; *kin* /kɪn/ – *king* /kɪŋ/.

For /n/ the tongue **tip** goes up to the ridge; for /ŋ/ the tip stays down and the **back** of the tongue goes up. Do not add a /ɡ/ or /k/ after /ŋ/ (✗ /θɪŋk/ for *thing* sounds like *think*). Note that many native speakers do say *-ing* as /ɪn/ in informal speech (*goin'*, *nothin'*); this is a feature of style, not a different word, but *thin* and *thing* must still be kept apart.
:::

### Syllabic nasals

After /t/ or /d/, an unstressed syllable spelled *-en*, *-on* or *-an* is often said without any vowel: the tongue stays on the ridge for /t/ or /d/ and the air is released through the nose. The /n/ then forms a syllable by itself, written /n̩/ or, in LPD, simply /n/ with no vowel before it: *button* /ˈbʌtn/, *garden* /ˈɡɑːdn/ (GB), *sudden* /ˈsʌdn/, *written* /ˈrɪtn/. A /l/ can be syllabic in the same way: *little* /ˈlɪtl/, *middle* /ˈmɪdl/.

## /l/: the lateral

::: definition Lateral approximant {#def-lateral}
For English /l/, the tongue tip touches the alveolar ridge, as for /t/ and /n/, but the sides of the tongue are lowered, so the air flows out over one or both sides. It is voiced and has no friction: a **lateral approximant**.
:::

English has two main kinds of /l/, and they are allophones of one phoneme ([[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet#def-phoneme]]).

::: theorem Clear and dark /l/ {#rule-dark-l}
In GB, /l/ is **clear** [l] before a vowel or /j/: *light*, *leaf*, *alive*, *value*. It is **dark** [ɫ] at the end of a word and before a consonant: *feel*, *bell*, *milk*, *help*, *table*. For dark /l/, the back of the tongue is also raised towards the soft palate, which gives the /l/ an *oo*-like colour. In GA, /l/ is fairly dark in all positions.
:::

::: proof
The contrast can be heard in pairs such as *leaf* [liːf] and *feel* [fiːɫ], or *lip* and *pill*. The two sounds never change the meaning of a word — a clear /l/ at the end of *feel* still means *feel* — so they are allophones. But a learner who drops the dark /l/, or replaces it with a vowel, changes the word: *feel* becomes *fee*, *bell* becomes *bay* or *bear*, *milk* becomes something like *miuk*. In many London and south-eastern English accents dark /l/ itself becomes a vowel like [ʊ] ("*l*-vocalisation": *milk* [mɪʊk]); this is common in speech, but the GB and GA models keep a consonant.
:::

To make a good dark /l/: say *feel* slowly and keep the tongue tip on the ridge at the end. You should be able to hold the /l/ — *feeeelllll* — and feel the tip against the ridge throughout.

::: example Clear or dark? {#ex-l}
Which /l/ in each word is clear and which dark (GB)? *little*, *lily*, *please*, *world*, *usually*.
::: solution
Use [[#rule-dark-l]]: before a vowel, clear; at the end or before a consonant, dark.

1. *little* /ˈlɪtl/: the first /l/ is before a vowel — clear; the second is syllabic at the end of the word — dark.
2. *lily* /ˈlɪli/: both before vowels — clear.
3. *please* /pliːz/: before a vowel — clear (and partly voiceless after the aspirated /p/).
4. *world* GB /wɜːld/, GA /wɜːrld/: before /d/ — dark.
5. *usually* /ˈjuːʒuəli/ or /ˈjuːʒəli/: before a vowel — clear.
:::
:::

## /r/

::: definition English /r/ {#def-r}
The English /r/ of GB and GA is a **postalveolar approximant**, written [ɹ] by phoneticians. The tongue tip is raised towards the back of the alveolar ridge **without touching it** (many speakers instead bunch the body of the tongue up and back); the lips are slightly rounded; the sides of the tongue touch the upper back teeth; and there is no friction, no tap and no trill. It is voiced.
:::

::: theorem Where /r/ is pronounced {#rule-r}
1. In both GB and GA, /r/ is pronounced before a vowel: *red*, *very*, *story*, *tree*.
2. In GA (rhotic), /r/ is also pronounced after a vowel, at the end of a word or before a consonant: *car* /kɑːr/, *hard* /hɑːrd/, *teacher* /ˈtiːtʃər/.
3. In GB (non-rhotic), a written *r* after a vowel is **not** pronounced unless a vowel follows: *car* /kɑː/, *hard* /hɑːd/. If the next word begins with a vowel, the /r/ is pronounced to link the words: *far away* /ˌfɑːr əˈweɪ/, *for ever*. This is **linking r**.
4. Many GB speakers also insert an /r/ between two vowels where there is no *r* in the spelling, after /ə/, /ɑː/ or /ɔː/: *the idea of it* /ði aɪˈdɪər əv ɪt/, *law and order*. This **intrusive r** is very common in natural GB speech, although some people dislike it.
:::

::: proof
Rules 2 and 3 are the rhoticity difference of [[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet#rule-rhotic]]. Linking and intrusive /r/ show that the /r/ of GB has not simply disappeared: speakers now treat it as a sound that appears between vowels. Because intrusive /r/ appears exactly where linking /r/ would appear if there were an *r* in the spelling (*idea of* rhymes with *ear of* for these speakers), it is a natural extension of the system, not carelessness. More examples are in [[connected-speech/linking]].
:::

### /l/ or /r/?

/l/ and /r/ are both voiced approximants made near the ridge, so learners whose language does not separate them (Japanese and Korean speakers, and speakers of some Chinese varieties) can find them hard. The difference: for /l/ the tip **touches** the ridge and air flows over the sides; for /r/ the tip **does not touch** anything and the lips are rounded.

**Pairs:** *light – right*, *long – wrong*, *lead – read*, *glass – grass*, *fly – fry*, *collect – correct*, *clown – crown*, *alive – arrive*.

- *Turn right at the lights.*
- *Is this the right answer, or the wrong one?*
- *Please collect the tests and correct them.*

::: example Following the tongue tip {#ex-lr}
Read *Laura left her red gloves on the rail.* Find each /l/ and /r/, say whether the tongue tip touches the ridge, and say which /r/ sounds are pronounced in GB.
::: solution
Transcribe first: GB /ˈlɔːrə left hə red ɡlʌvz ɒn ðə reɪl/.

- *Laura*: /l/ at the start — tip touches, clear /l/. The *r* is before a vowel — /r/ pronounced, tip does not touch.
- *left*: /l/ before a vowel — tip touches, clear.
- *her*: in GB the *r* is not pronounced, because a consonant (*r* of *red*) follows; in GA it is /hər/ (weak form).
- *red*: /r/ before a vowel — pronounced, lips slightly rounded.
- *gloves*: /ɡl/ — the /l/ is before a vowel, so clear; tip touches just after the /ɡ/ closure.
- *rail*: /r/ at the start (no touch), and a dark /l/ at the end (tip touches, back of the tongue raised).

The sentence alternates between touching and not touching. Saying it slowly with a mirror, you should see the tip of the tongue go up for every /l/ and the lips round for every /r/.
:::
:::

::: quiz
For which sound does the tongue tip **not** touch the roof of the mouth?
- [ ] /l/ in *light*
- [ ] /n/ in *night*
- [x] /r/ in *right*
- [ ] /t/ in *tight*
::: solution
For /r/ the tip is raised towards the back of the alveolar ridge but does not touch it (or the tongue is bunched), so the air flows freely. For /l/, /n/ and /t/ the tip touches the ridge: /t/ stops the air, /n/ sends it through the nose, and /l/ lets it out over the sides.
:::
:::

## /w/ and /j/: the glides

::: definition Glides {#def-glides}
/w/ and /j/ are **glides** (or semivowels): approximants that start from a vowel-like position and move quickly into the following vowel. For /w/, the lips are rounded and the back of the tongue is raised, as for /uː/ (*wet*, *away*). For /j/, the front of the tongue is raised towards the hard palate, as for /iː/ (*yes*, *beyond*). They always come before a vowel.
:::

The hardest cases for learners are a glide before the vowel it resembles:

- /w/ before /uː/ or /ʊ/: *woo*, *wood*, *would*, *woman*, *wool*, *wolf*. The lips must be rounded **more** at the start than for the vowel and then open a little: *wood* /wʊd/ is not *ood*.
- /j/ before /iː/ or /ɪ/: *year*, *yeast*, *yield*, *yes*. The tongue must be **higher** at the start than for the vowel: *year* /jɪə/ (GB), /jɪr/ (GA) is not *ear*; *yeast* is not *east*.

**Pairs:** *year – ear*, *yeast – east*, *Yale – ale*, *wood – could* (vowel the same, first sound different), *woo – ooh*.

GB and most GA speakers say *which* and *witch*, *where* and *wear*, *whale* and *Wales* the same, with /w/. Some speakers in Scotland, Ireland and parts of the United States keep a voiceless sound [ʍ] for *wh*; dictionaries such as LPD show it as an option.

::: example *Ear* or *year*? {#ex-glides}
Huang Mei says *I studied English for one ear* and *I'd like a wooden toy* (said as *ooden*). What should she change?
::: solution
1. *year*: she left out /j/. Start with the front of the tongue very close to the hard palate — closer than for the vowel — and glide into the vowel: /jɪə/ (GB), /jɪr/ (GA). Without /j/ the word is *ear*, a part of the head.
2. *wooden* /ˈwʊdn/: she left out /w/. Start with the lips tightly rounded and pushed forward, then relax them a little into /ʊ/. A useful exercise: say *we*, *woo*, *wood* in a row, keeping the same strong lip-rounding at the start of each.

Both mistakes come from Pinyin habits: syllables written *yi* and *wu* in Pinyin are pronounced [i] and [u], with no separate glide before the vowel.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which word begins with a **consonant** sound?
- [ ] *hour*
- [ ] *ear*
- [x] *year*
- [ ] *honest*
::: solution
*Year* begins with the glide /j/, a consonant. *Hour* and *honest* begin with a vowel sound because the *h* is silent, and *ear* begins with the vowel /ɪə/ (GB). That is why we say *a year* but *an hour*, *an ear* and *an honest answer*.
:::
:::

## A dialogue in a shop

**Huang Mei:** *Hello. I'm looking for a long wool scarf. Something warm.*

**Assistant:** *We've got a lovely one in yellow. Would you like to see it?*

**Huang Mei:** *Yes, please. Oh, it's really soft. Is there a larger one?*

**Assistant:** *That's the longest we have, I'm afraid. We'll have more in the winter.*

**Huang Mei:** *All right, I'll take it. Can I pay with my phone?*

**Assistant:** *Of course. Would you like a bag?*

**Huang Mei:** *No, thanks. I'll wear it now. It's cold outside!*

Listen for: /ŋ/ in *looking*, *long*, *something*, *longest*; /l/ clear in *looking*, *lovely*, *larger*, *yellow* and dark in *wool*, *all*, *I'll*, *we'll*, *cold*; /r/ in *really*, *right* (and, in GA, in *larger*, *more*, *winter*, *wear*); /w/ in *wool*, *we've*, *would*, *we'll*, *winter*, *wear*; /j/ in *yellow*, *yes*.

::: note English and Chinese
- **Final nasals.** Standard Mandarin syllables can end in *-n* and *-ng*, so English final /n/ and /ŋ/ are familiar. But speakers of many southern varieties, and of Wu dialects such as Shanghainese, do not keep *-in* and *-ing* or *-en* and *-eng* apart in their own speech, and may find *thin – thing* or *ran – rang* hard. Standard Mandarin has no final *-m*: Mandarin speakers sometimes say *some* like *sun* or *time* like *tine*. Cantonese and Hakka speakers have final *-m* and usually find it easy.
- **/ŋ/ at the start.** Some Chinese varieties allow *ng* at the start of a syllable (Cantonese 我 *ngo*), but English never does; this mainly matters for names.
- **/l/ and /n/.** In many varieties of south-western and southern China — for example in much of Sichuan, Chongqing, Hunan and Hubei — *n* and *l* are not separate phonemes, and speakers may say *night* and *light*, or *no* and *low*, alike. Many younger Hong Kong Cantonese speakers also pronounce *n* as *l*. The final chapter gives practice ([[sounds-of-english/sounds-for-chinese-speakers]]).
- **Final /l/.** Mandarin has no final /l/ (the final *-r* of 儿 is different). Learners tend to drop the dark /l/ or to add a vowel after it (*feel* → *fee*, *feel-o*). Keep the tongue tip on the ridge at the end.
- **Pinyin *r* is not English /r/.** Pinyin *r* (as in 人, 热) is made with the tongue tip curled back and some friction, and with spread lips. English /r/ has no friction, and the lips are rounded. Starting from English /w/ and moving the tongue tip up a little is a good way to find it.
- **Glides.** Pinyin *yi*, *ying*, *wu* have no separate glide, which is why *year* can sound like *ear* and *wood* like *ood*.
:::

::: history
The letters *ng* once spelled two sounds everywhere, /ŋɡ/, as they still do in *finger*; the /ɡ/ was lost at the ends of words in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries (see [[#rule-ng]]). The pronunciation of *-ing* as /ɪn/ is also old: in the eighteenth century *-in'* was common among fashionable speakers in England, and only later did the spelling pronunciation /ɪŋ/ become the prestige form. The loss of /r/ after vowels in south-eastern England, which produced non-rhotic GB, took place mainly in the eighteenth century; American English, and the accents of Scotland, Ireland and the south-west of England, kept it.
:::

## Where this leads

You now have all 24 consonants and all the vowels. [[sounds-of-english/syllables-clusters]] shows how they combine into syllables, including clusters with /l/, /r/, /w/ and /j/ (*play*, *three*, *twelve*, *music*) and endings such as *-nths* and *-lts*. Linking /r/, intrusive /r/ and the loss of sounds in fast speech are taught in [[connected-speech/linking]]. The specific difficulties of Chinese speakers with /n/, /l/, /r/, /v/ and /w/ are collected in [[sounds-of-english/sounds-for-chinese-speakers]].

::: summary
- The nasals /m n ŋ/ are made at the same places as /p b/, /t d/, /k ɡ/, with the soft palate lowered ([[#def-nasal]]).
- /ŋ/ never begins a word; *ng* at the end of a word (and in *singer*) is /ŋ/ without /ɡ/, but /ŋɡ/ in *finger*, *English*, *longer* ([[#rule-ng]]).
- Keep final /m/, /n/ and /ŋ/ apart: *some – sun*, *thin – thing*.
- /l/ is a lateral approximant ([[#def-lateral]]); in GB it is clear before vowels and dark at the end of words and before consonants ([[#rule-dark-l]]). Do not drop final /l/.
- English /r/ is an approximant with no friction, tip raised but not touching, lips rounded ([[#def-r]]). GA pronounces every written *r*; GB only before a vowel, with linking and intrusive /r/ ([[#rule-r]]).
- /l/ and /r/: tip touches for /l/, not for /r/: *light – right*, *collect – correct*.
- The glides /w/ and /j/ start from a closer position than the vowel after them: *wood* is not *ood*, *year* is not *ear* ([[#def-glides]]).
:::

::: exercise Practise in context {level=3}

::: solution
Answers will differ. A model:

> *Hi Lily! The train was really long — nearly eleven hours. I spent the morning reading and the evening looking at the hills. I'm feeling tired, but well. I'll ring you tomorrow after I've slept. Hope everything's all right with you!*

- /ŋ/: *long*, *morning*, *reading*, *evening*, *looking*, *feeling*, *ring*, *everything*.
- Clear /l/ (before a vowel): *Lily* (both), *really*, *eleven*, *looking*, *feeling*, *slept*.
- Dark /l/ (at the end or before a consonant): *hills*, *well*, *I'll*, *all*.
- /r/ in GB (before a vowel): *train*, *really*, *reading*, *ring*, *tomorrow*, *everything*, *right*. In GA also *nearly*, *hours*, *after*, *tired*; GB *tired* is /ˈtaɪəd/, with no /r/.
