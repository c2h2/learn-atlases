Say *photograph*, *photographer* and *photographic* aloud. All three words begin with the same letters, but a different syllable is strong in each: **PHO**tograph, pho**TO**grapher, photo**GRA**phic. And the vowels change with the stress: the first *o* is /əʊ/ in *photograph* but a weak /ə/ in *photographer*. A listener who hears pho**TO**graph will probably still understand — but will have to work harder, and may hear a different word.

In every English word of more than one syllable, one syllable is **stressed**: it stands out from the others. Getting the stress right matters as much as getting the sounds right, because English listeners use stress to recognise words. This chapter explains what stress is, how dictionaries mark it, and the most useful patterns for predicting it: two-syllable nouns and verbs, suffixes that move the stress and suffixes that do not, and compounds. It builds on the syllables of [[sounds-of-english/syllables-clusters]] and the weak vowels of [[sounds-of-english/short-vowels]].

## What is word stress?

::: definition Word stress {#def-stress}
**Word stress** is the greater prominence of one syllable in a word compared with the others. The prominent syllable is the **stressed** syllable; the others are **unstressed**. Some longer words also have a **secondary stress**, weaker than the main (**primary**) stress but stronger than the unstressed syllables: in *photographic* the primary stress is on *-graph-* and a secondary stress on *pho-*.
:::

In this course stress is written in three ways:

- with the IPA marks used in dictionaries: **ˈ** before the syllable with primary stress and **ˌ** before the syllable with secondary stress: /ˈfəʊtəɡrɑːf/, /fəˈtɒɡrəfə/, /ˌfəʊtəˈɡræfɪk/ (GB);
- with capital letters in ordinary spelling, as in **PHO**tograph — a quick informal method;
- with a pattern of large and small circles: *photograph* **O**oo, *photographer* o**O**oo, *photographic* oo**O**o (the secondary stress counts as small here).

Note that the IPA mark goes **before** the stressed syllable, not over its vowel: /əˈbaʊt/ *about*, not /əbˈaʊt/.

::: theorem What makes a syllable stressed {#rule-cues}
A stressed syllable in English differs from an unstressed one in four ways, usually all at once:

1. **Pitch.** The voice moves up or down on it. In a word said on its own, the pitch usually jumps or falls on the stressed syllable.
2. **Length.** It is longer: the vowel is held for more time.
3. **Loudness.** It is somewhat louder.
4. **Vowel quality.** It has a full vowel. Unstressed syllables very often have a weak vowel — /ə/, /i/, /u/ or /ɪ/ — or a syllabic consonant ([[sounds-of-english/short-vowels#rule-reduction]]).

Of these, the fourth is the one most learners forget. The unstressed syllables of English are not just quieter: their vowels **change**. *Photograph* /ˈfəʊtəɡrɑːf/ has a schwa in its second syllable; *photographer* /fəˈtɒɡrəfə/ has schwa in its first, third and fourth.
:::

::: proof
Research on English (beginning with the experiments of Dennis Fry in the 1950s) found that pitch and length are stronger cues to stress for listeners than loudness alone. Vowel quality is equally important in practice: because the unstressed vowels are weak, the stressed syllable stands out against them. This also explains why stress matters so much for recognition. A misplaced stress does not just move a loud beat; it puts a full vowel where a weak one should be, and a weak vowel where a full one should be, so the word's sound shape changes.
:::

## Patterns for two-syllable words

There is no single rule that predicts English stress for every word, but a few tendencies cover a large part of the vocabulary.

::: theorem Two-syllable nouns, adjectives and verbs {#rule-two}
1. Most two-syllable **nouns** and **adjectives** are stressed on the **first** syllable: **TA**ble, **WIN**dow, **MO**ney, **CHI**na, **HAP**py, **CLE**ver, **YEL**low.
2. Many two-syllable **verbs** are stressed on the **second** syllable, especially those that begin with a prefix such as *be-*, *de-*, *re-*, *for-*, *ex-*, *con-*: be**GIN**, de**CIDE**, re**PEAT**, for**GET**, ex**PLAIN**, con**TROL**. But many common verbs are stressed on the first: **O**pen, **LIS**ten, **HAP**pen, **AN**swer, **VI**sit, **STU**dy, **FI**nish.
3. **Exceptions** to (1) are common and must be learned: ho**TEL**, ma**CHINE**, a**DVICE**, mis**TAKE**, Ju**LY**, a**LONE**, cor**RECT**.
:::

::: proof
The tendency for initial stress goes back to the Germanic stress of Old English, which fell on the first syllable of the root (see the history note below). Prefixes in Old English verbs were usually unstressed (*be-*, *for-*, *a-*), so stress fell on the root: *begin*, *forget*, *arise*. Many verbs borrowed from Latin and French have a prefix plus a root in the same way (*de-cide*, *re-peat*, *ex-plain*), and they too stress the root. Nouns borrowed from French sometimes kept their final stress (*hotel*, *machine*), which is why the noun rule has exceptions.
:::

### Noun–verb pairs

A group of common two-syllable words, most of them from Latin through French, can be a noun or a verb, with the same spelling. In many of them the stress tells you which: **noun on the first** syllable, **verb on the second**. The vowel of the unstressed syllable usually weakens.

| word | noun | verb |
|---|---|---|
| *record* | /ˈrekɔːd/ (GB), /ˈrekərd/ (GA) *a world record* | /rɪˈkɔːd/ (GB), /rɪˈkɔːrd/ (GA) *record a song* |
| *present* | /ˈpreznt/ *a birthday present* | /prɪˈzent/ *present an idea* |
| *object* | /ˈɒbdʒekt/ (GB), /ˈɑːbdʒekt/ (GA) *a heavy object* | /əbˈdʒekt/ *object to a plan* |
| *permit* | /ˈpɜːmɪt/ (GB), /ˈpɜːrmɪt/ (GA) *a work permit* | /pəˈmɪt/ (GB), /pərˈmɪt/ (GA) *permit smoking* |
| *increase* | /ˈɪnkriːs/ *a price increase* | /ɪnˈkriːs/ *prices increase* |
| *export* | /ˈekspɔːt/ (GB) *China's exports* | /ɪkˈspɔːt/ (GB) *export cars* |
| *produce* | /ˈprɒdjuːs/ (GB), /ˈproʊduːs/ (GA) *fresh produce* | /prəˈdjuːs/ (GB), /prəˈduːs/ (GA) *produce a film* |
| *contrast* | /ˈkɒntrɑːst/ (GB), /ˈkɑːntræst/ (GA) *a strong contrast* | /kənˈtrɑːst/ (GB), /kənˈtræst/ (GA) *contrast two ideas* |

Not all noun–verb words work like this. *Visit*, *answer*, *promise* and *travel* are stressed on the first syllable as both noun and verb; *control*, *report*, *respect*, *surprise* and *advice*/*advise* on the second as both. Some words also have both patterns for the noun: *research* is /rɪˈsɜːtʃ/ or /ˈriːsɜːtʃ/ in GB, and LPD lists both; *address* (noun) is /əˈdres/ in GB, often /ˈædres/ in GA.

::: example Noun or verb? {#ex-noun-verb}
Mark the stress of the word in bold and say whether it is a noun or a verb.

1. *Farmers **produce** most of the country's food.*
2. *They sell local **produce** at the market.*
3. *Did anyone **object** to the new rules?*
4. *There was a strange **object** in the sky.*
5. *We need a **permit** to park here.*
::: solution
1. pro**DUCE** /prəˈdjuːs/ (GB) — a verb (after the subject *Farmers*).
2. **PRO**duce /ˈprɒdjuːs/ (GB) — a noun ('fruit and vegetables'), after *local*.
3. ob**JECT** /əbˈdʒekt/ — a verb, after *Did anyone*.
4. **OB**ject /ˈɒbdʒekt/ (GB) — a noun, after *a strange*.
5. **PER**mit /ˈpɜːmɪt/ (GB) — a noun, after *a*.

Notice how the vowel of the unstressed syllable becomes weak: *object* the noun has a full /e/ in its second syllable (/ˈɒbdʒekt/), but the verb has a weak /ə/ in its first (/əbˈdʒekt/).
:::
:::

## Suffixes and stress

Many long English words are built from a shorter word plus a **suffix** (an ending): *photograph* + *-ic*, *able* + *-ity*, *happy* + *-ness*. Some suffixes move the stress of the word; others leave it where it was. This is the most useful set of stress rules for learners, because it lets you predict the stress of thousands of words.

::: theorem Suffixes that fix the stress {#rule-suffix}
1. **Stress on the syllable just before the suffix** with *-ion* (*-tion*, *-sion*), *-ic*, *-ical*, *-ity*, *-ial*, *-ian*, *-ious*, *-ient*:
   - **e**ducate → edu**CA**tion; de**CIDE** → de**CI**sion; **E**conomy → eco**NO**mic; **PO**litics → po**LI**tical; **A**ble → a**BI**lity; e**LEC**tric → elec**TRI**city; **MU**sic → mu**SI**cian; **MYS**tery → mys**TE**rious.
2. **Stress on the suffix itself** with *-ee*, *-eer*, *-ese*, *-ique*, *-ette*: employ**EE**, engi**NEER**, Chi**NESE**, u**NIQUE**, ciga**RETTE** (GB).
3. Common exceptions with *-ic*: **A**rabic, **CA**tholic, **LU**natic, **PO**litics, **RHE**toric, **A**rithmetic (noun).
:::

::: theorem Suffixes that do not move the stress {#rule-neutral}
Many suffixes are **stress-neutral**: they add a syllable, but the stress stays where it was in the base word. These include *-ness*, *-ment*, *-ful*, *-less*, *-ly*, *-er*, *-ing*, *-ed*, *-able* (usually), *-ship*, *-hood*, *-ish*:

- **HAP**py → **HAP**piness; em**PLOY** → em**PLOY**ment; **BEAU**ty → **BEAU**tiful; **CARE** → **CARE**less; **SUD**den → **SUD**denly; de**VE**lop → de**VE**loper, de**VE**loping, de**VE**loped; **COM**fort → **COM**fortable; **FRIEND** → **FRIEND**ship; **CHILD** → **CHILD**hood.
:::

::: proof
The difference comes from the history of the suffixes. The stress-fixing ones (*-ion*, *-ic*, *-ity*) came into English with Latin and French words, which brought their own stress rules with them: Latin stressed the second-last syllable if it was heavy and the third-last if it was not, and the forms *-ic-*, *-it-*, *-i-* in these endings made a short, light syllable, so the stress landed just before them. The neutral suffixes are mostly native English (*-ness*, *-ful*, *-less*, *-ly*, *-ship*, *-hood*), and they were simply added to a word with its stress already in place. (*-ment* and *-able* came from French but behave like native suffixes.) In practice this means that once you know the stress of the base word, the neutral suffixes cost nothing; the stress-fixing ones need the rule.
:::

::: example A word family {#ex-family}
Mark the primary stress in each word of these families, and say which suffix rule applies.

1. *photograph*, *photography*, *photographic*
2. *economy*, *economic*, *economist*
3. *public*, *publicity*, *publicly*
::: solution
1. **PHO**tograph /ˈfəʊtəɡrɑːf/ — learned with the base. pho**TO**graphy /fəˈtɒɡrəfi/ — *-y* here belongs with the Greek *-graphy*, stressed on the syllable before *-graph-*: learn it as a pattern with *geography*, *biography* (*-ography* is always stressed *-OG-*). photo**GRA**phic /ˌfəʊtəˈɡræfɪk/ — *-ic*: stress on the syllable before ([[#rule-suffix]]).
2. e**CO**nomy /ɪˈkɒnəmi/ (GB) — learned with the base; eco**NO**mic /ˌiːkəˈnɒmɪk/ or /ˌekəˈnɒmɪk/ — *-ic*; e**CO**nomist /ɪˈkɒnəmɪst/ — *-ist* does not move the stress here (it follows *economy*).
3. **PUB**lic /ˈpʌblɪk/; pub**LI**city /pʌbˈlɪsəti/ — *-ity*: stress on the syllable before; **PUB**licly /ˈpʌblɪkli/ — *-ly* is neutral ([[#rule-neutral]]).

In families 1 and 2, notice how the vowels change: /əʊ/ → /ə/ → /əʊ/ in the first syllable of the *photo-* words, and /ɒ/ → /ə/ → /ɒ/ in the second syllable of the *econom-* words.
:::
:::

## Compounds

A **compound** is a word made of two words: *blackboard*, *bus stop*, *mobile phone*. Compound nouns are usually stressed on the **first** part, while an adjective + noun phrase is usually stressed on the **second**:

| compound noun (first part stressed) | phrase (second part stressed) |
|---|---|
| a **BLACK**bird ('a kind of bird') | a black **BIRD** ('any bird that is black') |
| a **GREEN**house ('a glass house for plants') | a green **HOUSE** ('a house painted green') |
| a **HOT** dog ('a sausage in bread') | a hot **DOG** ('a dog that feels hot') |
| a **BUS** stop, a **BOOK**shop, a **CAR** park | a big **SHOP**, a new **CAR** |

There are many exceptions — compounds whose first part is a material or a place often stress the second part (*apple **PIE***, *town **HALL***) — and they are studied in [[connected-speech/word-stress-patterns]].

::: example Compound or phrase? {#ex-compound}
Mark the stress in the words in bold, and explain the difference in meaning.

1. *We grow tomatoes in a **greenhouse**.* / *They live in a **green house** near the station.*
2. *I'd like a **hot dog** with onions.* / *Give the **hot dog** some water.*
3. *She teaches at the **English school** in town.* / *She's an **English teacher**.*
::: solution
1. **GREEN**house /ˈɡriːnhaʊs/ — a compound noun: a glass building for plants. green **HOUSE** — a phrase: a house that is green.
2. **HOT** dog — a compound: a sausage in a bread roll. hot **DOG** — a phrase: a dog that is too warm.
3. *English school* and *English teacher* can each be either. **ENG**lish teacher (a compound) is 'a person who teaches English'; English **TEA**cher (a phrase) is 'a teacher who is English'. In the same way, **ENG**lish school is 'a school where English is taught', and English **SCHOOL** is 'a school in England'.

In speech, the stress alone carries the difference — the spelling may be the same (*hot dog*), or different only by a space (*greenhouse*, *green house*).
:::
:::

::: example Finding the stress in a short text {#ex-text}
Underline (or write in capitals) the primary stress of every word of more than one syllable: *My sister is a photographer. Last year she visited Japan and took beautiful pictures of the mountains.*
::: solution
*My **SIS**ter is a pho**TO**grapher. Last year she **VI**sited Ja**PAN** and took **BEAU**tiful **PIC**tures of the **MOUN**tains.*

- *sister*, *pictures*, *mountains*: two-syllable nouns, first syllable ([[#rule-two]]).
- *photographer*: learned (*-ography*/*-ographer* family).
- *visited*: *visit* has first-syllable stress; *-ed* is neutral ([[#rule-neutral]]).
- *Japan* /dʒəˈpæn/: an exception to the noun rule (but *Japanese* is Japa**NESE**, with *-ese*).
- *beautiful*: *beauty* + neutral *-ful*.

Note that the stress of *Japan* moves in *Japanese*: /ˌdʒæpəˈniːz/. The first vowel is weak /ə/ in *Japan* and full /æ/ in *Japanese*.
:::
:::

## British and American differences

A small number of common words are stressed differently in GB and GA. Both pronunciations are correct; choose one and be consistent.

| word | GB | GA |
|---|---|---|
| *garage* | /ˈɡærɑːʒ/ or /ˈɡærɪdʒ/ | /ɡəˈrɑːʒ/ |
| *ballet* | /ˈbæleɪ/ | /bæˈleɪ/ |
| *café* | /ˈkæfeɪ/ | /kæˈfeɪ/ |
| *adult* | /ˈædʌlt/ (also /əˈdʌlt/) | /əˈdʌlt/ or /ˈædʌlt/ |
| *laboratory* | /ləˈbɒrətri/ | /ˈlæbrətɔːri/ |
| *cigarette* | /ˌsɪɡəˈret/ | /ˈsɪɡəret/ or /ˌsɪɡəˈret/ |

Most of these are words borrowed from French: GA has often kept the final stress of French (*ballet*, *café*, *garage*), while GB has moved it to the first syllable, following the native pattern ([[#rule-two]]).

## A short dialogue

**Receptionist:** *Good afternoon. Welcome to the Riverside Hotel.*

**Wang Lei:** *Hello. I have a reservation for two nights. My name is Wang.*

**Receptionist:** *Let me check. Yes — a double room, with breakfast. Can I see your passport, please?*

**Wang Lei:** *Here you are. Is there a restaurant in the hotel?*

**Receptionist:** *Yes, on the ground floor. And there's a computer in the lobby if you need one.*

Stresses to notice: after**NOON** (a compound stressed on its second part), **WEL**come, **RI**verside, ho**TEL**, reser**VA**tion (*-tion*), **DOU**ble, **BREAK**fast (compound), **PASS**port (compound), **RES**taurant, com**PU**ter, **LOB**by. Compare *Hotel* and *Riverside*: a two-syllable noun stressed on the second syllable (an exception), and a three-syllable compound stressed on the first.

::: note English and Chinese
- **Tone and stress.** In Mandarin every syllable carries a tone, and full syllables are roughly equal in length. Learners therefore tend to give every English syllable a full vowel and the same length, which flattens the difference between stressed and unstressed syllables. The closest Mandarin feature to an unstressed English syllable is the **neutral tone** (轻声): the second syllable of *bàba* (爸爸), *xièxie* (谢谢) or *dōngxi* (东西) is short, light and weak, like the unstressed syllables of *father* or *thank you*.
- **Pitch is not tone.** The pitch movement on an English stressed syllable is not a lexical tone: *photograph* can be said with a rising or a falling pitch on *PHO-*, and it is the same word. What must not change is **which** syllable carries the movement.
- **Chinese loanwords.** English versions of Chinese words often move the stress: *Beijing* is usually /ˌbeɪˈdʒɪŋ/, with stress on the second syllable, and *kung fu* /ˌkʌŋ ˈfuː/ likewise. English speakers apply their own patterns, just as Chinese speakers apply Mandarin patterns to English words.
- **Typical errors.** Words that Chinese learners often stress wrongly include *interesting* (**IN**teresting), *comfortable* (**COM**fortable), *vegetable* (**VEG**etable), *hotel* (ho**TEL**), *develop* (de**VE**lop), *event* (e**VENT**) and *Chinese* (Chi**NESE**, in isolation).
:::

::: history
Old English, like the other early Germanic languages, stressed words on the first syllable of the root: *Ēngland*, *wīfmann* (later *woman*), with prefixes usually unstressed (*be-*, *for-*). After 1066 thousands of French words entered English with the stress near the end of the word (French *nature*, *honour*, *village*). In the verse of Chaucer (late fourteenth century) such words can still be stressed on either syllable, as the metre needs; over the following centuries most of them moved to the first syllable under the influence of the native pattern (**NA**ture, **HON**our, **VIL**lage). Later borrowings, directly from Latin in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, followed a different system — the Latin rule that also explains the stress before *-ic* and *-ity* ([[#rule-suffix]]) — which is why English now has two layers of stress patterns, a native one and a Latin one. The noun–verb pattern of *record*, *present* and *object* goes back at least to the sixteenth century, and it has spread to more words since then. Dictionaries began to mark stress in the eighteenth century: John Walker's *Critical Pronouncing Dictionary* (1791) marked it on almost every entry.
:::

::: summary
- **Word stress** is the prominence of one syllable in a word; long words may also have a secondary stress ([[#def-stress]]).
- Dictionaries mark primary stress with ˈ and secondary stress with ˌ, **before** the syllable: /fəˈtɒɡrəfə/.
- A stressed syllable has pitch movement, greater length and loudness, and a full vowel; unstressed syllables often have weak vowels ([[#rule-cues]]).

1. *possible* + *-ity* 2. *nation* + *-al* 3. *govern* + *-ment* 4. *history* + *-ical* 5. *care* + *-ful* + *-ly* 6. *politics* → *politician*
::: solution
1. **POS**sible → possi**BI**lity — moves (*-ity*).
2. **NA**tion → **NA**tional — does not move; the vowel changes from /eɪ/ to /æ/: /ˈnæʃnəl/.
3. **GO**vern → **GO**vernment — does not move (*-ment* is neutral).
4. **HIS**tory → his**TO**rical — moves (*-ical*).
5. **CARE** → **CARE**fully — does not move (*-ful*, *-ly* are neutral).
6. **PO**litics → poli**TI**cian — moves (*-ian*).
:::

::: exercise GB or GA? {level=2}
For each word, give the stressed syllable in GB and in GA: *ballet*, *garage*, *laboratory*, *café*.
::: solution
*ballet*: GB **BAL**let, GA bal**LET**. *garage*: GB **GA**rage, GA ga**RAGE**. *laboratory*: GB la**BO**ratory /ləˈbɒrətri/, GA **LA**boratory /ˈlæbrətɔːri/. *café*: GB **CA**fé, GA ca**FÉ**.

::: exercise Diagnose the mistake {level=3}
A learner says: *I think this is a very inTERESTing deVElopment. The comPAny will proDUCE more vegeTAbles.* Find each wrong stress, give the correct one, and explain which pattern of this chapter would have helped.
::: solution
1. *in**TER**esting* ✗ → **IN**teresting /ˈɪntrəstɪŋ/ ✓. The base *interest* is stressed on the first syllable, and *-ing* is neutral ([[#rule-neutral]]); in normal speech the word has three syllables.
2. *de**VE**lopment* ✓ — correct: *de**VE**lop* + neutral *-ment*.
3. *com**PA**ny* ✗ → **COM**pany /ˈkʌmpəni/ ✓. A noun, stressed on the first syllable ([[#rule-two]]); the second vowel is weak /ə/.
4. *pro**DUCE*** ✓ — correct as a verb (noun **PRO**duce).
5. *vege**TA**bles* ✗ → **VEG**etables /ˈvedʒtəblz/ ✓. A noun stressed on the first syllable; in normal speech it has three syllables, not four, because the second vowel disappears.

Two of the five stresses were correct. The errors all put the stress on a later syllable; in each case the noun pattern or the neutral suffix rule gives the right answer.
:::

::: exercise Say the dialogue {level=3}
Read the dialogue in the section "A short dialogue" aloud with a partner, recording yourself. Before reading, mark the stress of every word of two or more syllables. Afterwards, listen: are the unstressed syllables short and weak? Choose three words where you think your stress was unclear, and practise each one with the method below.

Method: (1) find the stressed syllable in the dictionary; (2) say the word slowly, clapping only on the stressed syllable; (3) make the unstressed vowels weak; (4) say the word at normal speed in a sentence.
::: solution
Answers vary. Words that learners often find difficult in the dialogue: *reservation* /ˌrezəˈveɪʃn/ (stress on *-va-*, secondary on *res-*, with weak vowels in *-er-* and *-tion*); *restaurant* /ˈrestrɒnt/ or /ˈrestərɒnt/ (GB), /ˈrestərɑːnt/ (GA) — first syllable, often two syllables in GB; *computer* /kəmˈpjuːtə/ (weak /ə/ in the first syllable); *afternoon* /ˌɑːftəˈnuːn/ (GB) with the stress at the end. A good recording has a clear peak of pitch and length on each stressed syllable, and short, weak unstressed syllables between them.
:::
