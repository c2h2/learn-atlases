Every learner of English carries the sound system of their first language into the new one. A speaker of Mandarin hears English through Mandarin categories: two English vowels that Mandarin does not separate may sound like one, and an English syllable that ends in a consonant Mandarin does not allow there may be heard, or said, with a vowel after it. This is not a sign of a "bad ear"; it is how all human speech perception works. It also means that the difficulties of Chinese speakers are to a large extent **predictable** — and what can be predicted can be practised.

This last chapter of the course brings together the points from earlier chapters where Chinese speakers most often need extra work: long and short vowels, the *th* sounds, final consonants, and — for speakers from some regions — *l* and *n* or *v* and *w*. It explains why Pinyin can mislead, and it gives a method for practising with minimal pairs. The sounds themselves were taught in [[sounds-of-english/short-vowels]], [[sounds-of-english/long-vowels-diphthongs]], [[sounds-of-english/stops-fricatives]] and [[sounds-of-english/nasals-approximants]]; here we focus on what to practise first and how.

## What to practise first

Not every difference from a native accent matters equally. Many speakers of English worldwide have accents, and they are understood perfectly well. What matters is **intelligibility**: whether listeners can recognise your words easily.

::: definition Intelligibility and priority {#def-priority}
**Intelligibility** is how easily a listener can recognise the words a speaker intends. A pronunciation feature has **high priority** for a learner when getting it wrong often causes listeners to hear a different word or no word at all; it has **lower priority** when listeners usually understand anyway. A contrast between two phonemes that separates many common words (such as /ɪ/ – /iː/: *live – leave*, *fill – feel*, *ship – sheep*) is said to have a high **functional load**; a contrast that separates few words (such as /θ/ – /ð/: *thigh – thy*) has a low one.
:::

::: theorem Priorities for Chinese speakers {#rule-priorities}
For most Chinese speakers, the following give the largest gain in intelligibility, roughly in this order:

1. **Final consonants**: say them, do not add a vowel after them, and keep clusters intact (*asked*, *months*) — [[#rule-finals]].
2. **Word stress**: put it on the right syllable and make the other syllables weak ([[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics]]).
3. **Vowel contrasts**: /ɪ/ – /iː/, /ʊ/ – /uː/, /æ/ – /e/, /æ/ – /ʌ/ – /ɑː/ ([[#rule-vowels]]).
4. **Regional consonant contrasts**, if they affect you: /n/ – /l/, /v/ – /w/, /f/ – /h/, /s/ – /ʃ/ ([[#rule-regional]]).
5. **The *th* sounds** /θ/ and /ð/: worth learning, but listeners usually understand *I sink so* in context.

This order is based on research on English as an international language — in particular Jennifer Jenkins's *The Phonology of English as an International Language* (2000), which found that consonant sounds, vowel length, initial consonant clusters and the placing of the main stress in a phrase mattered most for understanding between non-native speakers, while *th* rarely did — and on the general experience of teachers. Individual learners differ; a recording of your own speech is the best guide to your own priorities.
:::

::: proof
The order reflects two facts. First, English listeners recognise words largely by their stressed syllables and their consonants; a missing final consonant or a misplaced stress changes the shape of the whole word, while a slightly different vowel quality often does not. Second, contrasts with a high functional load matter more, because a mistake with them is more likely to produce a different real word (*I want to leave* / *I want to live*). The *th* sounds have a low functional load and are replaced by other sounds in several native varieties of English (for example, /f/ in *think* in parts of London, /t/ and /d/ in Irish English), so listeners are used to hearing them replaced.
:::

## Vowels

Mandarin has fewer vowel phonemes than English, and vowel length does not separate words in Mandarin. So the English pairs that differ in length and quality are often heard as one sound.

::: theorem The key vowel contrasts {#rule-vowels}
| contrast | examples | what to do |
|---|---|---|
| /ɪ/ – /iː/ | *ship – sheep*, *live – leave*, *bit – beat*, *fill – feel* | /ɪ/ is short and more relaxed, a little lower and further back; /iː/ is long, with spread lips |
| /ʊ/ – /uː/ | *full – fool*, *pull – pool*, *look – Luke* | /ʊ/ is short and relaxed with lips only slightly rounded; /uː/ is long |
| /æ/ – /e/ | *bad – bed*, *man – men*, *sat – set* | /æ/ is more open: drop the jaw; /e/ is half-open |
| /æ/ – /ʌ/ – /ɑː/ (GB) | *cat – cut – cart*, *hat – hut – heart* | /æ/ front and open, /ʌ/ central and short, /ɑː/ back and long |
| /ɒ/ – /ɔː/ (GB) | *cot – caught*, *not – nought* | /ɒ/ short, /ɔː/ long with more lip rounding |
| /ɜː/ | *bird*, *work*, *learn* (GB) | a long central vowel, not *e* + *r*; in GA, /ɜːr/ is a single r-coloured vowel |

The short vowels are not just shorter versions of the long ones; their quality is different too ([[sounds-of-english/long-vowels-diphthongs#def-long-vowel]]).
:::

::: example Diagnosing vowel errors {#ex-vowels}
A learner says *I want to sit down* so that it sounds like *I want to seat down*, and *Can I have a pen?* so that it sounds like *Can I have a pan?* Explain each error and how to fix it.
::: solution
1. *sit* /sɪt/ → heard as *seat* /siːt/: the learner used a long, tense [iː] for /ɪ/. Mandarin *i* (as in *xī*) is close to /iː/, and it is the nearest Mandarin sound to /ɪ/, so it replaces it. Fix: keep the vowel very short and relaxed, with the tongue a little lower and further back; practise *sit – seat*, *bit – beat*, *hill – heel* ([[#rule-practice]]).
2. *pen* /pen/ → heard as *pan* /pæn/: the vowel was too open. In Mandarin *ian* (as in *tiān*) the vowel is a front vowel between [e] and [æ], and some learners use a sound near [æ] for English /e/; others do the opposite and say *bed* for *bad*. Fix: for /e/, open the mouth only halfway; for /æ/, drop the jaw clearly; practise *pen – pan*, *bed – bad*, *men – man*.
:::
:::

## The *th* sounds

English /θ/ (*think*) and /ð/ (*this*) are made with the tongue tip between or just behind the upper teeth ([[sounds-of-english/stops-fricatives#def-fricative]]). Mandarin has neither, and Chinese speakers usually replace them with the nearest Mandarin sounds:

- /θ/ → /s/ (*think* → *sink*) or /f/ (*three* → *free*);
- /ð/ → /d/ (*they* → *day*) or /z/ (*this* → *zis*), sometimes /l/.

Fix: put the tongue tip lightly against the back of the upper teeth (or just between the teeth), and blow, without stopping the air. For /ð/ add voice. Check in a mirror that the tongue tip is visible or almost visible.

## Final consonants

::: theorem Final consonants {#rule-finals}
Mandarin syllables can end only in a vowel, *-n*, *-ng* (or *-r*), but English words end in almost any consonant or cluster. Three things go wrong, and each has a fix:

1. **Adding a vowel** after the consonant: *bag* → *bag-e*, *desk* → *desk-a*, *good* → *good-a*. Fix: end the word with the consonant; hold the closure of a final plosive rather than releasing it with a vowel ([[sounds-of-english/syllables-clusters]]).
2. **Dropping** the consonant, especially in clusters and grammatical endings: *ask* for *asked*, *want* for *wants*, *wor* for *world*. Fix: practise from the vowel outwards ([[sounds-of-english/syllables-clusters#rule-practice]]).
3. **Losing the voicing contrast**: *bag* and *back*, *bed* and *bet*, *prize* and *price* said the same. Fix: make the vowel **longer** before a voiced final consonant (*bag*, *bed*, *prize*) and **shorter** before a voiceless one (*back*, *bet*, *price*) — this length difference is the main cue English listeners use ([[sounds-of-english/long-vowels-diphthongs#rule-clipping]]).

Also distinguish final /n/ and /ŋ/ (*thin – thing*, *ran – rang*), and do not add a /ɡ/ or /k/ after /ŋ/ (*sing* is /sɪŋ/; [[sounds-of-english/nasals-approximants#rule-ng]]).
:::

::: proof
Mandarin plosives /p t k/ and fricatives such as /s/ never end a syllable, so a final consonant feels incomplete to a Mandarin speaker, and adding a short vowel turns it into a familiar syllable. Dropping the consonant reaches the same familiar shape from the other direction. The voicing problem has a different cause: Mandarin *b*, *d*, *g* are voiceless (they differ from *p*, *t*, *k* by aspiration, not voicing), and since they never occur at the end of a syllable, the English final contrast between /ɡ/ and /k/ is new. English listeners rely mainly on the length of the vowel before it — so lengthening the vowel works even when the final consonant itself is not fully voiced.
:::

::: example Bag or back? {#ex-finals}
Say these sentences so that the word in bold is clear, and explain what you change between the two members of each pair.

1. *Put it in the **bag**.* / *Put it in the **back**.*
2. *Go to **bed**.* / *Place your **bet**.*
3. *She won a **prize**.* / *What's the **price**?*
::: solution
In each pair the first word ends in a voiced consonant (/ɡ/, /d/, /z/) and the second in a voiceless one (/k/, /t/, /s/):

1. *bag* /bæɡ/: hold the vowel /æ/ longer, end with a soft closure for /ɡ/ and no vowel after it. *back* /bæk/: a short /æ/, cut off sharply by /k/.
2. *bed* /bed/: longer /e/; *bet* /bet/: short /e/ cut off by /t/ (in GB often with a glottal stop, [beʔt]).
3. *prize* /praɪz/: a long diphthong /aɪ/, then a buzzing /z/; *price* /praɪs/: a shorter /aɪ/ and a hissing /s/.

What changes most is the **length of the vowel**. No vowel is added after any of the final consonants.
:::
:::

## Regional differences

Chinese speakers do not all have the same difficulties. The dialect or regional Mandarin you grew up with adds its own.

::: theorem Regional contrasts {#rule-regional}
1. **/n/ and /l/.** In many varieties of Chinese, *n* and *l* at the start of a syllable are not distinguished, or are used interchangeably: in the Southwestern Mandarin of Sichuan and Chongqing, in Hunan and Hubei, in parts of Jiangxi, Anhui and Jiangsu, and in the Cantonese of many Hong Kong speakers (where *n* is often replaced by *l*). Speakers from these areas may confuse *night – light*, *know – low*, *knee – Lee*, *snow – slow*. Fix: for /n/, the air goes through the nose (pinch your nose: /n/ stops); for /l/, the air goes over the sides of the tongue and the soft palate is raised.
2. **/v/ and /w/.** Standard Mandarin has no /v/, and many northern speakers say Pinyin *w* with the lips close to the upper teeth, a sound between [w] and [v]. As a result *wine – vine*, *west – vest*, *wet – vet* may be confused. Speakers of Wu dialects (Shanghai, Suzhou, Zhejiang) have /v/ in their own dialect, so /v/ itself is usually easy for them. Fix: /v/ is made with the upper teeth on the lower lip, with buzzing; /w/ with rounded lips that do not touch the teeth.
3. **/f/ and /h/.** Some speakers of Min dialects (Fujian, Taiwan, Chaoshan), which lack /f/, may say *h* for /f/ or the reverse: *fat – hat*, *fold – hold*.
4. **/s/ – /ʃ/** and **/ts/ – /tʃ/.** Speakers from areas where Pinyin *s* and *sh*, *z* and *zh* are not distinguished (much of southern China and Taiwan) may confuse *sip – ship*, *sea – she*, *sort – short*.
5. **/r/ and /l/.** Some southern speakers, including many Cantonese speakers, may use /l/ for /r/: *right – light*, *road – load*, *pray – play*.
:::

::: quiz
A learner from Chongqing says *I like the knight* when she means *I like the light*. Which contrast should she practise?
- [ ] /ɪ/ – /iː/
- [x] /n/ – /l/
- [ ] /v/ – /w/
- [ ] /θ/ – /s/
::: solution
*Knight* /naɪt/ and *light* /laɪt/ differ only in /n/ – /l/. In Chongqing (Southwestern Mandarin), *n* and *l* are not distinguished, so this is a typical regional difficulty ([[#rule-regional]]).
:::
:::

## Pinyin and English spelling

Pinyin uses Latin letters, so it is natural to read English letters with their Pinyin values. In several cases this leads to errors.

| letter | in Pinyin | in English | risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| *b d g* | voiceless, unaspirated: *bā* [pa] | voiced or partly voiced /b d ɡ/ | sounds close to English *p t k* after *s* (*spa*); fine at the start of words, but voicing must be shown by vowel length at the end |
| *p t k* | strongly aspirated | aspirated at the start of a stressed syllable | mostly fine ([[sounds-of-english/stops-fricatives#rule-aspiration]]) |
| *c* | [tsʰ]: *cài* | /k/ or /s/ | ✗ reading *city* or *cat* with [tsʰ] |
| *q* | [tɕʰ]: *qī* | /kw/ (*queen*) | ✗ reading *quick* with Pinyin *q* |
| *x* | [ɕ]: *xī* | /ks/, /ɡz/ | ✗ reading *taxi* as *ta-xi* |
| *z*, *zh* | [ts], [ʈʂ] | /z/ (*zoo*); *zh* is rare | ✗ *zoo* said as [tsu] — English /z/ is a buzzing fricative |
| *r* | [ʐ] or [ɻ] | /r/, a glide without friction | ✗ a buzzing *r* in *red* |
| *e* | [ɤ]: *hē* | /e/, /iː/ or silent | ✗ *he* read as Pinyin *hē*; final *e* is usually silent ([[sounds-of-english/spelling-and-sound#rule-silent-e]]) |
| *i* after *z c s* | a special "buzzing" vowel: *sì* | /ɪ/ or /aɪ/ | ✗ *sit* said with the vowel of *sì* |
| *ou* | [oʊ]: *dōu* | usually /aʊ/ (*out*) | ✗ *house* said as *hose* |
| *ian* | [jɛn]: *tiān* | — | can push English /æ/ towards /e/ |

::: warning Do not write English pronunciation in Chinese characters or Pinyin
✗ Remembering *thank you* as 三克油 (*sān kè yóu*), *bus* as 巴士 (*bā shì*) or *dog* as 道格 (*dào gé*).
✓ *thank you* /ˈθæŋk juː/, *bus* /bʌs/, *dog* /dɒɡ/ (GB), /dɑːɡ/ (GA) — learn the phonemic transcription.

Characters and Pinyin can only write Chinese syllables. They add vowels after final consonants (*bā shì* for /bʌs/), they have no symbol for /θ/, /ð/, /v/ or /æ/, and they do not show stress. Memory aids of this kind fix exactly the errors this chapter tries to remove. The phonemic alphabet of [[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet]] is made for this job.
:::

## Practising with minimal pairs

A **minimal pair** ([[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet#def-minimal-pair]]) is two words that differ in a single sound: *ship – sheep*, *light – night*. They are the best tool for practising a contrast, because you hear and say the difference and nothing else. The figure shows a small network of minimal pairs around *ship*.

::: widget graph
nodes: ship@-2,1; sheep@2,1; chip@-2,3; cheap@2,3; sip@-2,-1; seep@2,-1; lip@-4,-2; leap@0,-2.8; tip@-4,0
edges: ship-sheep; chip-cheap; sip-seep; lip-leap; ship-chip; sheep-cheap; ship-sip; sheep-seep; sip-tip; sip-lip; tip-lip; tip-chip; seep-leap
caption: A network of minimal pairs. Each line joins two words that differ in one sound: across, the vowel /ɪ/ – /iː/ (*ship – sheep*, *chip – cheap*, *sip – seep*, *lip – leap*); up and down, the first consonant (/ʃ/ – /tʃ/ in *ship – chip*, /ʃ/ – /s/ in *ship – sip*, /s/ – /t/ – /l/ in *sip – tip – lip*). A learner can practise one contrast along each line. You can drag the points.
:::

::: theorem The minimal-pair method {#rule-practice}
Practise one contrast at a time, in four stages:

1. **Hear.** Listen to recordings of minimal pairs (dictionaries with audio, or a partner) and decide which word you hear: *ship* or *sheep*? Continue until you are almost always right. If you cannot hear a difference, you cannot reliably make it.
2. **Say in isolation.** Say the pairs slowly, using the description of each sound (tongue position, lips, length). Record yourself and compare with a model; ask a partner which word you said.
3. **Say in sentences.** Use the words in short sentences where only the contrast tells the meaning: *Don't **sit** on that **seat**.* *He **left** the **light** on.* Then in longer texts.
4. **Use freely.** Pay attention to the sound in ordinary speaking for a few minutes a day. Old habits return when you think about meaning; a few weeks of short, regular practice is better than one long session.
:::

::: proof
Stage 1 comes first because perception and production are linked: adults who learn to hear a new contrast usually improve in producing it too, and an accurate model in the mind is needed to judge your own speech. Minimal pairs isolate the contrast, so attention goes only to it. Sentences and free speech are needed because a sound practised only in isolated words often fails to appear when attention moves to meaning; the new habit must be made automatic, which takes repetition over time.
:::

::: example A practice plan for /n/ and /l/ {#ex-plan}
A learner from Hunan finds that her English listeners confuse *night* and *light*. Write a one-week practice plan for her using [[#rule-practice]].
::: solution
**Day 1–2 — hear.** Listen to minimal pairs from a dictionary with audio: *night – light*, *know – low*, *knee – Lee*, *nap – lap*, *snow – slow*, *need – lead*. A partner says one word of each pair; she writes *n* or *l*. Aim: 9 out of 10 correct.

**Day 3–4 — say in isolation.** Feel the difference: for /n/, the tongue tip on the alveolar ridge and air through the nose; pinch the nose and the sound stops. For /l/, the same tongue tip but air over the sides of the tongue; pinching the nose does not stop it. Say each pair five times, recording and checking.

**Day 5–6 — sentences.** *Turn on the light at night.* *I know the snow is slow to melt.* *The lake is near the lane.* Read them, record, check that each /n/ and /l/ is clear.

**Day 7 — free speech.** Tell a partner about her week for two minutes, recording; listen for /n/ and /l/ and note any errors for further practice.

A plan like this is then repeated for a few weeks with new words, and the learner checks progress by recording the same sentences every week.
:::
:::

::: example Diagnosing a learner's speech {#ex-diagnose}
A learner from Shanghai reads *I think the west wind is very cold this week*, and a listener writes down *I sink the vest wind is wery coal-e dis week.* Identify each problem, say which rule it belongs to, and decide what to practise first.
::: solution
1. *think* → *sink*: /θ/ → /s/ (the *th* sounds; low priority).
2. *west* → *vest*, *very* → *wery*: /w/ and /v/ swapped ([[#rule-regional]]). For a Wu speaker, /v/ exists in the dialect, so the problem is choosing the right sound for the right word, not making /v/.
3. *cold* → *coal-e*: the final cluster /ld/ lost its /d/ and gained a vowel ([[#rule-finals]]).
4. *this* → *dis*: /ð/ → /d/ (low priority).

Priorities ([[#rule-priorities]]): first the final consonants (*cold*), because a wrong word shape is the most damaging; then /v/ – /w/, which produced a different real word (*vest*) and a non-word (*wery*); last the *th* sounds, which listeners understood in context.
:::
:::

## A short dialogue

**Teacher:** *Did you practise your sounds this week?*

**Zhang Min:** *Yes. I worked on ship and sheep every evening. I recorded myself and listened back.*

**Teacher:** *Good. What did you notice?*

**Zhang Min:** *My long vowels were fine, but I often forgot the endings — I said "ask" instead of "asked".*

**Teacher:** *That's very common. Try saying the sentence slowly first, and finish each word before you start the next.*

Sounds to notice: /ɪ/ – /iː/ in *ship* – *sheep* and *this* – *each*; final clusters in *worked* /kt/, *recorded* /dɪd/, *listened* /nd/, *asked* /skt/, *sounds* /ndz/, *vowels* /lz/; the *th* in *this*, *That's* /ð/; /v/ in *every*, *evening*, *vowels*; /w/ in *week*, *worked*, *were*.

::: note English and Chinese
- **Cantonese speakers** have some advantages: Cantonese has final /p t k/ (unreleased) and /m/, and a contrast between long and short *a*, so final plosives and vowel length can be easier. On the other hand, Cantonese has no /θ ð v z ʃ ʒ r/, and many Hong Kong speakers merge /n/ and /l/.
- **Taiwanese and Southern Min speakers** often find /f/, /ʃ/ and /r/ difficult, and may not distinguish /n/ – /l/ consistently.
- **Mandarin tones** do not cause errors in English sounds, but they can make learners put a tone on every syllable. English uses pitch for stress and intonation, not for word meaning ([[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics#rule-cues]]; [[connected-speech/tones-and-intonation]]).
- **Aim.** The goal is not to sound like a native speaker of a particular accent; it is to be easily understood by many kinds of listeners. A clear, consistent accent with Chinese features is a perfectly good goal ([[listening-and-conversation/accents]]).
:::

::: history
Minimal pairs as a teaching tool come from mid-twentieth-century structural linguistics, which defined the phoneme by contrast. In 1957 Robert Lado's *Linguistics Across Cultures* proposed **contrastive analysis**: compare the learner's first language with the target language, and the differences will predict the difficulties. Later research showed that the prediction is only partly right: some differences cause no trouble, some errors come from other sources, and sounds that are *similar but not identical* to a first-language sound (like English /ɪ/ and Mandarin *i*) can be harder than completely new ones. Popular minimal-pair books followed, such as Ann Baker's *Ship or Sheep?* (first published in 1977). Since the 1990s, research on English as a lingua franca and on intelligibility, notably Jennifer Jenkins's *Lingua Franca Core* (2000), has moved the aim of teaching away from a native accent and towards intelligibility, which is the approach of this chapter.
:::

## Where this leads

This chapter closes the course on individual sounds and words. The next step is connected speech — how words change in phrases and sentences: [[connected-speech/word-stress-patterns]], [[connected-speech/weak-forms]], [[connected-speech/rhythm]], [[connected-speech/linking]] and [[connected-speech/elision-assimilation]]. For listening to real, fast speech, see [[connected-speech/hearing-fast-speech]] and [[listening-and-conversation/how-listening-works]].

::: summary
- Pronunciation is about **intelligibility**: being understood easily. Contrasts that separate many common words have the highest priority ([[#def-priority]]).
- For most Chinese speakers the priorities are: final consonants, word stress, vowel contrasts, regional consonant contrasts, then *th* ([[#rule-priorities]]).
- Key vowel contrasts: /ɪ/ – /iː/, /ʊ/ – /uː/, /æ/ – /e/, /æ/ – /ʌ/ – /ɑː/ ([[#rule-vowels]]).
- Say final consonants without an added vowel, keep clusters, and show voicing by vowel length (*bag – back*) ([[#rule-finals]]).
- Depending on region: /n/ – /l/ (Sichuan, Chongqing, Hunan, Hubei, Hong Kong Cantonese and others), /v/ – /w/, /f/ – /h/, /s/ – /ʃ/, /r/ – /l/ ([[#rule-regional]]).
- Pinyin letters do not have their English values (*c*, *q*, *x*, *e*, *ou*); do not write English pronunciation in characters or Pinyin.
- Practise one contrast at a time: hear, say in isolation, say in sentences, use freely ([[#rule-practice]]).
:::

::: exercise Your own priorities {level=1}
Using [[#rule-regional]], list the regional contrasts that may affect a speaker from (a) Chengdu, (b) Shanghai, (c) Hong Kong, (d) Xiamen.
::: solution
(a) Chengdu (Southwestern Mandarin): /n/ – /l/; also /s/ – /ʃ/, since many Sichuan speakers do not distinguish *s* and *sh*. (b) Shanghai (Wu): /v/ – /w/ in choosing the right sound (the /v/ itself exists in Shanghainese); /s/ – /ʃ/ for many speakers. (c) Hong Kong (Cantonese): /n/ – /l/ for many speakers, /r/ – /l/, /θ/ and /v/ absent from Cantonese. (d) Xiamen (Southern Min): /f/ – /h/, /r/ – /l/, /s/ – /ʃ/, and for some speakers /n/ – /l/. In every case the general priorities (final consonants, stress, vowels) also apply.

How many of these pairs differ only in the vowel /ɪ/ – /iː/? *ship – sheep*, *fill – feel*, *bit – bet*, *hit – heat*, *sit – set*, *chip – cheap*, *pull – pool*
::: solution
/ɪ/ – /iː/: *ship – sheep*, *fill – feel*, *hit – heat*, *chip – cheap* — four pairs. *bit – bet* and *sit – set* are /ɪ/ – /e/; *pull – pool* is /ʊ/ – /uː/. The answer is 4.
:::
:::

::: exercise Bag or back? {level=2}
For each word, say whether the vowel is longer or shorter, and why: *bag*, *back*, *feed*, *feet*, *rise*, *rice*, *card*, *cart*.
::: solution
Longer (before a voiced final consonant): *bag* (/ɡ/), *feed* (/d/), *rise* (/z/), *card* (/d/). Shorter (before a voiceless final consonant): *back* (/k/), *feet* (/t/), *rice* (/s/), *cart* (/t/). This is pre-fortis clipping ([[sounds-of-english/long-vowels-diphthongs#rule-clipping]]), and it is the main cue for the final voicing contrast ([[#rule-finals]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Sentences for a contrast {level=2}
Write two sentences for practising /v/ – /w/ in which both sounds appear and a mistake would change the meaning.
::: solution
Possible answers: *The **vet** said the dog was **wet**.* *We drank **wine** under the **vine**.* *He wore a **vest** in the **west**.* In each, swapping /v/ and /w/ produces another real word, so a listener would notice the mistake.
:::
:::

::: exercise Diagnose and plan {level=3}
A learner from Guangzhou reads: *I like to ride my bike to the lake on Sunday.* A listener hears *I right to light my bai to the late on Sunday.* Identify each problem, name the rule it belongs to, and write a short practice plan for the two most important problems.
::: solution
Problems:
1. *like* → *right*: /l/ → /r/, an overcorrection of the /r/ – /l/ contrast ([[#rule-regional]]).
2. *ride* → *light*: /r/ → /l/ (the same contrast, in the expected direction), and the final /d/ → /t/, so the vowel was too short — voicing ([[#rule-finals]]).
3. *bike* → *bai*: the final /k/ was dropped ([[#rule-finals]]). Cantonese has final [k], but it is unreleased; the learner may have made it so weak that it was not heard.
4. *lake* → *late*: /k/ → /t/ at the end — a final consonant at the wrong place ([[#rule-finals]]).

Plan ([[#rule-practice]]): first final consonants, then /r/ – /l/.
- **Finals:** hear and say *bike – buy*, *lake – late – lay*, *ride – right*; make the closure at the right place (back of the tongue for /k/, tip for /t/ and /d/), lengthen the vowel before /d/; in sentences: *I ride my bike to the lake.*
- **/r/ – /l/:** hear *right – light*, *ride – lied*, *road – load*; for /r/ keep the tongue tip off the roof of the mouth and round the lips; for /l/ touch the alveolar ridge with the tip. Record the sentence each day for two weeks.
:::
:::

::: exercise A spoken self-assessment {level=3}
Record yourself reading this text, then listen and fill in a list of your own priorities using [[#rule-priorities]].

> *Last week I visited my grandparents in the village. They live near a river, and every evening we walked along the bank. My grandfather thinks the fish were bigger when he was young, but I think his stories have grown with the years.*
::: solution
Answers vary. Check in particular:
- **finals and clusters:** *Last* /st/, *week*, *visited* /ɪd/, *grandparents* /nts/, *walked* /kt/, *bank* /ŋk/, *thinks* /ŋks/, *fish* /ʃ/, *stories* /z/, *grown* /n/, *years* /z/;
- **stress:** **VI**sited, **GRAND**parents, **VIL**lage, **RI**ver, **EVE**ning, **GRAND**father, **BIG**ger, **STO**ries;
- **vowels:** /ɪ/ in *visited*, *village*, *river*, *his*, *fish*, *bigger*; /iː/ in *week*, *evening*; /æ/ in *grandparents*, *bank*; /ʌ/ in *young*;
- **regional consonants:** /v/ in *visited*, *village*, *river*, *every*, *evening*; /w/ in *week*, *we*, *walked*, *were*, *when*, *with*; /n/ and /l/ in *near*, *live*, *last*, *along*, *village*;
- **th:** *They*, *the*, *thinks*, *think*, *with*, *grandfather*.

Then list your three most frequent problems in the order of [[#rule-priorities]], and choose the first one for a week of minimal-pair practice.
