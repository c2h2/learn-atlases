Look at four common words: *though*, *through*, *tough*, *cough*. They all end in the letters *-ough*, but they rhyme with *go*, *too*, *stuff* and *off*. Now look at *see*, *sea*, *me*, *key*, *ski*, *people* and *receive*: seven different spellings of the same vowel. English spelling cannot tell you reliably how to say a word, so every learner's dictionary adds a second spelling between slashes: *though* /ðəʊ/, *through* /θruː/, *tough* /tʌf/, *cough* /kɒf/.

This second spelling is a **phonemic transcription**. In it, one symbol always means one sound, and one sound always has the same symbol. Once you can read it, you can find the pronunciation of any word in a dictionary, even a word you have never heard.

This chapter sets out the symbols used in the whole of this atlas. They are the symbols of the *Longman Pronunciation Dictionary* (LPD) by John Wells, which are also, with small differences, the symbols of the Oxford, Cambridge and Collins learner's dictionaries. The chapter also gives the two accents we use as models, the keywords that name the vowels, and a table that compares our symbols with the "DJ" and "KK" symbols many Chinese learners met at school. Later chapters and courses use this system without explaining it again, so come back here whenever a symbol is unclear.

## Phonemes

English speakers say the *t* in *top*, *stop*, *water* and *football* in different ways (you will see how below). But they hear all of them as "the same *t*". In the same way, Mandarin speakers say the *a* of *tiān* (天) and *tā* (他) differently, but hear one *a*. Each language groups many slightly different sounds into a small number of units that matter. These units are called phonemes.

::: definition Phoneme {#def-phoneme}
A **phoneme** is one of the smallest units of sound that can make a difference of meaning in a language. If you change one phoneme in a word, you can get a different word: *pin* → *bin* → *bun* → *bud*. A phonemic transcription writes each phoneme of a word with one symbol between slashes: /pɪn/, /bɪn/, /bʌn/, /bʌd/.
:::

::: definition Minimal pair {#def-minimal-pair}
A **minimal pair** is two words that differ in only one phoneme, in the same place: *ship* /ʃɪp/ and *sheep* /ʃiːp/, *light* /laɪt/ and *right* /raɪt/, *bag* /bæɡ/ and *back* /bæk/. Minimal pairs prove that two sounds are different phonemes, and they are the best material for practice.
:::

Note that a minimal pair is about **sounds**, not spelling. *Bear* and *pear* are a minimal pair (/beə/, /peə/ in GB). *Knight* and *night* are not: they look different, but they sound the same, /naɪt/. Words that sound the same but are spelled differently are **homophones**.

The different ways of saying one phoneme are called its **allophones**. They are written in square brackets, because they show more detail than a phonemic transcription: [tʰ] means a /t/ with a puff of air. A transcription in square brackets is a **phonetic transcription**.

::: widget graph
nodes: /t/@0,2; [tʰ] top@-3,0; [t] stop@-1,0; [ɾ] water GA@1,0; [ʔ] football@3,0
edges: /t/>[tʰ] top; /t/>[t] stop; /t/>[ɾ] water GA; /t/>[ʔ] football
caption: One phoneme, several allophones. The phoneme /t/ (top) is said with a puff of air at the start of *top*, without one after /s/ in *stop*, as a quick tap like a short *d* between vowels in General American *water*, and often as a "glottal stop" (a short closing of the vocal folds) before another consonant in *football*. English speakers hear all four as "*t*". You can drag the circles; the arrows show "is said as".
:::

::: theorem One symbol, one phoneme {#rule-one-symbol}
In a phonemic transcription:

1. each symbol stands for exactly one phoneme, and each phoneme has exactly one symbol;
2. letters that are not pronounced are not written: *knee* /niː/, *lamb* /læm/;
3. sounds that the spelling does not show are written: *use* /juːz/ (verb) begins with /j/;
4. the transcription is put between slashes, and stress is marked before the stressed syllable: *about* /əˈbaʊt/.
:::

::: proof
The rule follows from the purpose of a transcription: to show exactly which phonemes a word contains, whatever its spelling. It stops at phonemes. A phonemic transcription does not show allophones, because a speaker of English adds them automatically: you do not need to write the puff of air in *top*, because every /t/ at the start of a stressed syllable has it. When a lesson needs that detail, it uses square brackets.
:::

::: quiz
Which pair is a **minimal pair**?
- [ ] *write* – *right*
- [x] *thin* – *tin*
- [ ] *cat* – *dogs*
- [ ] *know* – *no*
::: solution
*Thin* /θɪn/ and *tin* /tɪn/ differ in exactly one phoneme (/θ/ and /t/), in the same place. *Write* and *right* are both /raɪt/, and *know* and *no* are both /nəʊ/ (GB), /noʊ/ (GA): they are homophones, with no difference of sound. *Cat* and *dogs* differ in almost every sound.
:::
:::

## Two models: General British and General American

English is spoken with many accents, and all of them are real English. A learner still needs a model: a clear, widely understood accent to learn from and to find in dictionaries. This atlas uses two.

::: definition GB and GA {#def-gb-ga}
**General British (GB)** is the accent of English most often taught as a British model and shown first in British dictionaries. It is the modern form of the accent long called Received Pronunciation (RP); Alan Cruttenden's *Gimson's Pronunciation of English* (2014) uses the name General British for it. **General American (GA)** is the accent of English heard across much of the United States, without strong features of one region; it is the model of American dictionaries. Both are spoken by millions of people, and neither is more "correct" than the other or than any other accent.
:::

The biggest single difference is the letter *r*:

::: theorem Where GB and GA differ most {#rule-rhotic}
1. **R after a vowel.** GA pronounces /r/ wherever the spelling has *r*: *car* /kɑːr/, *hard* /hɑːrd/. GB pronounces /r/ only before a vowel: *car* /kɑː/, *hard* /hɑːd/, but *carry* /ˈkæri/ and *car alarm* /kɑːr əˈlɑːm/. Accents like GA are called **rhotic**; accents like GB are **non-rhotic**.
2. **Some vowels.** GA has no vowel /ɒ/: *hot* is /hɒt/ in GB and /hɑːt/ in GA. The vowel of *go* is /əʊ/ in GB and /oʊ/ in GA. Some words with /ɑː/ in GB have /æ/ in GA: *bath*, *grass*, *can't*.
3. **T between vowels.** In GA a /t/ between vowels, after a stressed syllable, is usually a quick tap: *water*, *city*, *better*.
:::

::: proof
These are the differences that learners hear first; there are more, described in [[sounds-of-english/long-vowels-diphthongs]] and [[world-englishes/british-american]]. Both accents come from the English of the seventeenth century, when *r* was still pronounced after vowels everywhere. In south-east England it was lost after vowels during the eighteenth century, while American English, like Scottish and Irish English, kept it. The *t*-tap is a later American (and Australian) development. Neither accent is "older" or "purer" in every respect: each has kept some old features and changed others.
:::

In this atlas, when GB and GA differ, we give both, GB first: *car* /kɑː/ (GB), /kɑːr/ (GA). When they do not differ, we give one transcription.

- /j/ is the sound of the letter *y* in *yes*, not the sound of *j* in *jam*. *Jam* begins with /dʒ/.

- /r/ is used for the English *r* sound. Phoneticians write this sound [ɹ] when they compare languages, because in the IPA [r] means a rolled *r* as in Spanish *perro*. Dictionaries write /r/ because English has only one *r* phoneme.

*Vision* /ˈvɪʒn/ has no vowel symbol in its second syllable: the /n/ itself forms the syllable. You will meet this **syllabic consonant** again in [[sounds-of-english/syllables-clusters]]; some dictionaries write /ˈvɪʒən/.

## The vowel symbols and the lexical sets

Vowels are where English accents differ most. A word like *bath* has /ɑː/ in GB and /æ/ in GA; *hot* has /ɒ/ in one and /ɑː/ in the other. To talk about "the vowel of *bath*" in any accent, John Wells (1982) gave each group of words a **keyword**, written in capitals.

::: definition Lexical set {#def-lexical-set}
A **lexical set** is a group of words that have the same vowel in the reference accents, named by a keyword in capitals: the KIT set (*kit*, *ship*, *bridge*, *milk* …), the FLEECE set (*fleece*, *sheep*, *see*, *key* …), and so on. The keyword names the vowel whatever accent you speak: "the BATH vowel" means the vowel of *bath*, *grass*, *ask*, whether it is /ɑː/ (GB) or /æ/ (GA).
:::

The keywords were chosen so that they cannot easily be confused with other words. Here are Wells's 24 lexical sets with our symbols for GB and GA, and three extra keywords for unstressed vowels.

| keyword | GB | GA | more words |

| DRESS | e | e | *bed, head, said, many* |
| TRAP | æ | æ | *cat, bad, hand, apple* |
| LOT | ɒ | ɑː | *hot, stop, watch, got* |
| STRUT | ʌ | ʌ | *cup, love, money, blood* |
| FOOT | ʊ | ʊ | *book, put, good, could* |
| BATH | ɑː | æ | *bath, grass, ask, dance* |
| CLOTH | ɒ | ɔː | *cloth, off, long, cough* |
| NURSE | ɜː | ɜːr | *bird, work, learn, her* |
| FLEECE | iː | iː | *sheep, see, eat, key* |
| FACE | eɪ | eɪ | *day, make, rain, eight* |
| PALM | ɑː | ɑː | *father, calm, spa* |
| THOUGHT | ɔː | ɔː | *saw, caught, talk, law* |
| GOAT | əʊ | oʊ | *go, home, road, know* |
| GOOSE | uː | uː | *food, blue, true, shoe* |
| PRICE | aɪ | aɪ | *my, time, high, buy* |
| CHOICE | ɔɪ | ɔɪ | *boy, coin, noise* |
| MOUTH | aʊ | aʊ | *now, house, out, town* |
| NEAR | ɪə | ɪr | *here, beer, ear, idea* |
| SQUARE | eə | er | *hair, care, there, wear* |

| NORTH | ɔː | ɔːr | *for, horse, short* |
| FORCE | ɔː | ɔːr | *four, more, door, board* |
| CURE | ʊə, ɔː | ʊr | *pure, tour, sure, poor* |
| happY | i | i | the last vowel of *happy, city, coffee* |
| lettER | ə | ər | the last vowel of *letter, doctor, colour* |
| commA | ə | ə | the last vowel of *comma, sofa*; the first of *about* |

Some notes on the table:

- **Long and short.** The mark ː means "long". In GB, the vowels without ː (ɪ e æ ʌ ɒ ʊ ə) are the **short vowels**; those with ː are the **long vowels**; those written with two symbols are **diphthongs**, vowels that glide from one position to another. GA keeps the same symbols, but its /ɑː/ in LOT shows that GA vowels do not fall into exactly the same groups.
- **R-coloured vowels in GA.** We write GA NURSE as /ɜːr/ and lettER as /ər/. Some dictionaries, and the KK system below, write them [ɝ] and [ɚ]: the symbols are different, the sounds are the same.
- **The THOUGHT vowel in GA.** Many Americans, especially in the West and in Canada, say LOT and THOUGHT the same: *cot* and *caught* are homophones for them. Dictionaries keep the two vowels apart.
- **CURE in GB.** Many GB speakers now say *sure*, *poor* and *your* with /ɔː/, the vowel of *saw*. LPD gives both.
- **Weak /i/ and /u/.** The happY vowel /i/ is used only in unstressed syllables; it is between /ɪ/ and /iː/. In the same way, /u/ appears in unstressed syllables such as the last vowel of *thank you* /ˈθæŋk ju/ and the middle of *situation* /ˌsɪtʃuˈeɪʃn/.

::: warning Symbols are not Pinyin letters
Many symbols look like Pinyin letters but stand for different sounds.

- /j/ is the *y* of *yes*. It is not Pinyin *j* (as in *jiā* 家): reading /jes/ with Pinyin *j* gives a word that does not exist.
- /e/ is the vowel of *bed*. It is not Pinyin *e* (as in *hē* 喝), which is a different, back vowel.
- /ʌ/ (*cup*) and /ɑː/ (*car*) are two different English vowels, but Pinyin has only one *a* for both kinds of sound.
- /r/ is the English *r*, made with no friction and with rounded lips. It is not Pinyin *r* (as in *rè* 热).
- /i/, /iː/ and /ɪ/ are three different symbols. Pinyin *i* has yet another value in *zi* and *shi*, where it stands for a vowel English does not have.

Always read a symbol by its keyword, never by its Pinyin value.
:::

::: example Reading a transcription {#ex-read}
What words are these? /ˈmʌni/, /θɔːt/, /ˈwɪndəʊ/ (GB), /ʃʊd/, /jʌŋ/.
::: solution
Read each symbol by its keyword:

1. /ˈmʌni/: /m/ + the STRUT vowel + /n/ + the happY vowel, stressed on the first syllable: *money*.
2. /θɔːt/: /θ/ (*thin*) + the THOUGHT vowel + /t/: *thought*.
3. /ˈwɪndəʊ/: /w/ + KIT + /n/ + /d/ + GOAT: *window*.
4. /ʃʊd/: /ʃ/ (*shoe*) + FOOT + /d/: *should* (the *l* is silent).
5. /jʌŋ/: /j/ (*yes*) + STRUT + /ŋ/ (*sing*): *young*.
:::
:::

::: example Writing a transcription {#ex-write}
Write these words in phonemic symbols, GB and GA: *cheese*, *bird*, *thank*, *door*.
::: solution
Work sound by sound, not letter by letter.

1. *cheese*: the *ch* is one sound, /tʃ/; *ee* is FLEECE /iː/; the *s* is pronounced /z/ and the final *e* is silent: /tʃiːz/ (GB and GA).
2. *bird*: /b/ + the NURSE vowel + /d/. GB /bɜːd/, GA /bɜːrd/.
3. *thank*: /θ/ + TRAP + /ŋ/ + /k/. The *n* is said /ŋ/ before /k/: /θæŋk/.
4. *door*: /d/ + the FORCE vowel. GB /dɔː/, GA /dɔːr/.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which word is /ʃuː/?
- [ ] *sue*
- [x] *shoe*
- [ ] *show*
- [ ] *shy*
::: solution
/ʃ/ is the *sh* sound and /uː/ is the GOOSE vowel: *shoe*. *Sue* is /suː/, *show* is /ʃəʊ/ (GB) or /ʃoʊ/ (GA), and *shy* is /ʃaɪ/.
:::
:::

## Stress and length marks

Words of two or more syllables have one syllable that is **stressed**: louder, longer and higher (or lower) in pitch than the others. A small vertical mark ˈ goes **before** the stressed syllable: *about* /əˈbaʊt/, *teacher* /ˈtiːtʃə/ (GB), /ˈtiːtʃər/ (GA). Long words can also have a **secondary stress**, shown by a low mark ˌ: *understand* /ˌʌndəˈstænd/ (GB). [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics]] explains how stress works.

::: warning The stress mark goes before the syllable
✗ /ˈəbaʊt/ — this marks the first syllable as stressed.
✓ /əˈbaʊt/ — the mark comes **before** the stressed syllable *bout*, not on the vowel.

Do not confuse the stress mark ˈ with an apostrophe, and do not confuse the length mark ː with a colon. Many dictionaries print ː as two small triangles; some old books print a colon.
:::

## DJ, KK and our symbols

Many Chinese learners first met English transcription in one of two older systems.

- **DJ** is the notation of Daniel Jones's *English Pronouncing Dictionary* in the editions before 1977. It was used in many older Chinese textbooks and dictionaries. In 1977 the 14th edition, edited by A. C. Gimson, changed several vowel symbols; Chinese textbooks today use symbols close to the new ones, which are also ours.
- **KK** is the notation of John Kenyon and Thomas Knott's *A Pronouncing Dictionary of American English* (1944). It describes American English and is still common in Taiwan and in some American-English materials in mainland China.

::: theorem Correspondence of DJ, KK and this atlas {#rule-dj-kk}
The consonant symbols of the three systems are the same. The vowels correspond as follows:

| keyword | this atlas: GB | DJ (before 1977) | this atlas: GA | KK |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FLEECE | iː | iː | iː | i |
| KIT | ɪ | i | ɪ | ɪ |
| DRESS | e | e | e | ɛ |
| TRAP | æ | æ | æ | æ |
| STRUT | ʌ | ʌ | ʌ | ʌ |
| LOT | ɒ | ɔ | ɑː | ɑ |
| PALM, START | ɑː | ɑː | ɑː, ɑːr | ɑ, ɑr |
| THOUGHT, NORTH | ɔː | ɔː | ɔː, ɔːr | ɔ, ɔr |
| FOOT | ʊ | u | ʊ | ʊ |
| GOOSE | uː | uː | uː | u |
| NURSE | ɜː | əː | ɜːr | ɝ |
| commA, lettER | ə | ə | ə, ər | ə, ɚ |
| FACE | eɪ | ei | eɪ | e |
| PRICE | aɪ | ai | aɪ | aɪ |
| CHOICE | ɔɪ | ɔi | ɔɪ | ɔɪ |
| GOAT | əʊ | əu | oʊ | o |
| MOUTH | aʊ | au | aʊ | aʊ |
| NEAR | ɪə | iə | ɪr | ɪr |
| SQUARE | eə | ɛə | er | ɛr |
| CURE | ʊə | uə | ʊr | ʊr |

Three differences explain most of the table: DJ shows the KIT–FLEECE and FOOT–GOOSE differences by length alone ([i]–[iː], [u]–[uː]); KK shows them by quality alone ([ɪ]–[i], [ʊ]–[u]) and never uses a length mark; our system uses both, because the vowels of each pair differ in quality **and** in length.
:::

::: proof
The vowel of *ship* is not just a short version of the vowel of *sheep*: the tongue is lower and further back, and the muscles are more relaxed. A learner who reads DJ [i] as "a short [iː]" often makes *ship* sound like a quick *sheep*. Gimson's new symbols of 1977 (ɪ, ʊ, ɒ, ɜː) were chosen to show this difference of quality. KK shows quality but not length, which suits American English, where length is less important for telling vowels apart than in GB. Our system shows both, so that the same symbols can serve GB and GA. The table gives each system's **usual** symbols; individual dictionaries and textbooks have small differences (some older DJ printings write GOAT as [ou]; some KK charts write FACE and GOAT as [eɪ] and [oʊ]).
:::

::: example From DJ to our symbols {#ex-dj}
An old textbook gives *ship* [ʃip], *good* [gud] and *hot* [hɔt]. Write them in our system.
::: solution
Use the table, keyword by keyword:

1. [ʃip]: DJ [i] is KIT, our /ɪ/: /ʃɪp/.
2. [gud]: DJ [u] is FOOT, our /ʊ/; DJ [g] is our /ɡ/ (the same letter in a different typeface): /ɡʊd/.
3. [hɔt]: DJ [ɔ] is LOT, our /ɒ/ (GB). In GA the LOT vowel is /ɑː/: /hɒt/ (GB), /hɑːt/ (GA).
:::
:::

::: example From KK to our symbols {#ex-kk}
A KK dictionary gives *name* [nem], *bird* [bɝd] and *better* [ˈbɛtɚ]. Write them in our system for GA.
::: solution
1. [nem]: KK [e] is the FACE vowel, our /eɪ/: /neɪm/.
2. [bɝd]: KK [ɝ] is the NURSE vowel, our GA /ɜːr/: /bɜːrd/.
3. [ˈbɛtɚ]: KK [ɛ] is DRESS, our /e/; KK [ɚ] is lettER, our GA /ər/: /ˈbetər/.

The GB forms are /neɪm/, /bɜːd/, /ˈbetə/.
:::
:::

::: quiz
In KK a word is written [hom]. What is it in our GB transcription?
- [ ] /hɒm/
- [ ] /huːm/
- [x] /həʊm/
- [ ] /hɔːm/
::: solution
KK [o] is the GOAT vowel, which is /əʊ/ in GB and /oʊ/ in GA. The word is *home*: /həʊm/ (GB), /hoʊm/ (GA).
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **How many sounds?** Chinese school textbooks often say that English has "48 phonemes" (they call them 音素): 20 vowels and 28 consonants. Our list has 24 consonants. The extra four in the textbooks are /ts/, /dz/, /tr/ and /dr/, as in *cats*, *beds*, *tree* and *dream*. In the standard description of English these are **clusters** of two phonemes, not single phonemes, because the two parts can be separated and appear apart (*cat* + *s*). They are still worth practising, because they sound like single sounds.
- **Pinyin is not IPA.** Pinyin was designed to write Mandarin with Latin letters, not to show sounds across languages. Its *b, d, g* are voiceless; its *j, q, x* are sounds English does not have; its *r*, *e* and *i* are different from the English /r/, /e/ and /ɪ/. Read every English symbol by its keyword.
- **Writing Chinese names.** A Chinese name written in Pinyin, such as *Zhou Xin*, will be read by English speakers with English values for the letters. It is helpful to know how they are likely to say it, and to tell people how you prefer it said.
:::

::: history
The International Phonetic Alphabet was first published in 1888 by the association founded in 1886 (see [[sounds-of-english/how-speech-is-made]]). Daniel Jones used it for his *English Pronouncing Dictionary* (1917), and for most of the twentieth century his symbols were the standard way of writing British English — the "DJ" of Chinese classrooms. John Kenyon and Thomas Knott applied IPA symbols to American English in 1944 ("KK"). In 1977 A. C. Gimson, the new editor of Jones's dictionary, introduced symbols that show differences of vowel quality, and most learner's dictionaries followed him. John Wells's *Accents of English* (1982) introduced the lexical sets, and his *Longman Pronunciation Dictionary* (1990; 3rd edition 2008) gave GB and GA pronunciations side by side with the symbols used in this atlas.
:::

- The biggest GB–GA difference is *r* after a vowel: GA pronounces it, GB does not ([[#rule-rhotic]]).
- Our symbols are those of the *Longman Pronunciation Dictionary*: 24 consonants; vowels named by Wells's **lexical sets** (KIT, FLEECE, GOAT …), which name a vowel in any accent ([[#def-lexical-set]]).
- The stress mark ˈ goes **before** the stressed syllable; ː means long.
- DJ shows vowel pairs by length only, KK by quality only; our system shows both ([[#rule-dj-kk]]).
- Pinyin letters are not IPA symbols: read every symbol by its keyword.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Read the words {level=1}
Write the ordinary spelling of each word: /bʊk/, /tʃɪp/, /ðeɪ/, /ˈhæpi/, /jɑːd/ (GB), /saʊθ/.
::: solution
/bʊk/ *book*; /tʃɪp/ *chip*; /ðeɪ/ *they*; /ˈhæpi/ *happy*; /jɑːd/ *yard* (GA /jɑːrd/); /saʊθ/ *south*.

Which consonant symbol stands for the sound written by the underlined letters? (The letters are given after each word.)

1. *sing* — *ng*  2. *yellow* — *y*  3. *shop* — *sh*  4. *jump* — *j*  5. *this* — *th*  6. *thick* — *th*

::: exercise GB and GA {level=2}
Give the GB and the GA transcription of each word: *car*, *hot*, *go*, *bath*, *water*.
::: solution
- *car*: GB /kɑː/, GA /kɑːr/.
- *hot*: GB /hɒt/, GA /hɑːt/.
- *go*: GB /ɡəʊ/, GA /ɡoʊ/.
- *bath*: GB /bɑːθ/, GA /bæθ/.
- *water*: GB /ˈwɔːtə/, GA /ˈwɔːtər/ (often heard with a tap, [ˈwɔːɾər] or, for speakers who merge LOT and THOUGHT, [ˈwɑːɾər]).
:::

::: exercise Minimal pairs {level=2}
For each word, write a word that forms a minimal pair with it by changing the sound shown, and give both transcriptions.

1. *fan*: change the first sound to /v/.
2. *sheep*: change the vowel to /ɪ/.
3. *thing*: change the last sound to /n/.
4. *light*: change the first sound to /r/.
::: solution
1. *fan* /fæn/ – *van* /væn/
2. *sheep* /ʃiːp/ – *ship* /ʃɪp/
3. *thing* /θɪŋ/ – *thin* /θɪn/
4. *light* /laɪt/ – *right* /raɪt/ (*write* and *rite* are also correct: they are pronounced the same way)

::: exercise Count the phonemes {level=2 check="3"}
How many phonemes are there in *thought*?
::: hint
Write the word in phonemic symbols first. Silent letters do not count.
:::
::: solution
*thought* is /θɔːt/ in GB and GA. The symbols are /θ/, /ɔː/ and /t/ — three phonemes, although the word has seven letters: *th* stands for one sound, *ou* for one vowel, and *gh* is silent. The answer is 3.
:::
:::

::: exercise Explain the table {level=3}
A classmate learned DJ symbols at school and says: "*ship* and *sheep* have the same vowel; one is just shorter." Using the table in [[#rule-dj-kk]] and what you know about vowels, write three or four sentences to explain why this is not quite right, and what she should practise.
::: hint
Think about the position of the tongue, not only about time.
:::
::: solution
A model answer: "In DJ symbols *ship* is [ʃip] and *sheep* is [ʃiːp], so the only visible difference is the length mark. But the two vowels are different in quality too: for *ship* the tongue is a little lower and further back, and the mouth is more relaxed, which is why our system writes /ɪ/ and KK writes [ɪ]. If you just say *sheep* quickly, English speakers may still hear *sheep*. Practise the position: say *sheep*, then let the tongue drop a little and relax for *ship*, and use minimal pairs like *ship*–*sheep*, *sit*–*seat*, *live*–*leave*."
:::
:::

::: exercise Your own name {level=3}
Write your name (or a Chinese name such as *Zhou Xin* or *Xu Qing*) as most English speakers would probably read it from the Pinyin, in phonemic symbols. Then write a short note (two or three sentences in English) telling a new colleague how to say it.
::: solution
Answers will differ. For *Zhou Xin*: English speakers who do not know Pinyin often read *Zh* as /z/ or /ʒ/, *ou* as /aʊ/ (as in *out*), *X* as /z/ or /ks/ and *in* as /ɪn/, giving something like /zaʊ zɪn/. A helpful note: "My family name is Zhou — it sounds a bit like *Joe*. My given name, Xin, sounds close to *shin*. In English I put my given name first: Xin Zhou." Any note that gives a clear English model for each syllable, and says which name is the family name, is a good answer.
:::
