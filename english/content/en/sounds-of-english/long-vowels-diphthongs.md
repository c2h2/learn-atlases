Chen Yu tells a new colleague, "I usually walk at home on Fridays." The colleague imagines her walking around her flat, and says, "That sounds relaxing." Chen Yu meant *work*. The two words differ in only one vowel: *walk* has the vowel of *saw*, /ɔː/, and *work* has the vowel of *bird*, /ɜː/ (GA /ɜːr/). Many learners never practise this difference, because both vowels are long, and both are spelled with *o*.

In this chapter we meet the long vowels — the vowels of *sheep*, *car*, *bird*, *door* and *food* — and the diphthongs, which glide from one position to another: the vowels of *face*, *price*, *choice*, *goat* and *mouth*, and in GB also those of *near*, *square* and *cure*. Here GB and GA differ more than anywhere else, mainly because GA pronounces *r* after a vowel. We give both, as always.

You will need the symbols of [[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet]] and the short vowels of [[sounds-of-english/short-vowels]], because most long vowels are best learned next to a short neighbour.

## The long vowels

::: definition Long vowels {#def-long-vowel}
The **long vowels** of GB are /iː/ (FLEECE), /ɑː/ (PALM, START, BATH), /ɔː/ (THOUGHT, NORTH, FORCE), /uː/ (GOOSE) and /ɜː/ (NURSE). They are single vowels — the tongue stays in one position — and they are longer than the short vowels in the same position. Unlike the short vowels, they can end a word: *see*, *car*, *saw*, *two*, *her*. GA has the same long vowels, followed by /r/ in the START, NORTH, FORCE and NURSE words (*car* /kɑːr/), and uses /ɑː/ also for LOT (*hot* /hɑːt/).
:::

### FLEECE /iː/

**How to make it:** the front of the tongue is high and tense, close to the hard palate; the lips are spread, as in a smile. Many speakers start it slightly lower and glide up a little ([ɪi]), but it is written as one vowel.

**Spellings:** *ee* (*see*, *green*), *ea* (*eat*, *teacher*), *e* (*he*, *these*), *ie* (*field*, *piece*), *ei* after *c* (*receive*), *i* in some borrowed words (*police*, *machine*), *ey* (*key*), *eo* (*people*).

**Pairs with KIT:** *sheep – ship*, *leave – live*, *seat – sit*, *feel – fill*.

- *Please take a seat.*
- *The green one is cheaper.*

### GOOSE /uː/

**How to make it:** the tongue is high, the lips are rounded and pushed forward. In modern GB, and in much of the United States, the tongue is quite far forward, more central than back: the vowel of *food* is not the same as Pinyin *u* in *wǔ* (五), which is fully back.

**Spellings:** *oo* (*food*, *school*), *u* (*rule*, *June*), *ue* (*blue*, *true*), *ew* (*new*, *flew*), *o* (*do*, *who*, *move*, *lose*), *ou* (*soup*, *group*, *you*), *ui* (*fruit*, *juice*).

A /j/ often comes before the vowel: *music* /ˈmjuːzɪk/, *few* /fjuː/, *cute* /kjuːt/. After /t/, /d/ and /n/, GB keeps this /j/ and GA usually drops it: *new* GB /njuː/, GA /nuː/; *student* GB /ˈstjuːdnt/, GA /ˈstuːdnt/; *duty* GB /ˈdjuːti/, GA /ˈduːti/.

**Pairs with FOOT:** *fool – full*, *pool – pull*, *Luke – look*.

### PALM, START, BATH /ɑː/

**How to make it:** the jaw is open, the tongue is low and back, the lips are relaxed — the vowel a doctor asks for when you say *ah*.

**Words:** PALM: *father*, *calm*, *spa*, *palm* (with silent *l*). START: *car*, *park*, *heart*, *large* — GB /kɑː/, GA /kɑːr/. BATH: *bath*, *fast*, *ask*, *grass*, *after*, *dance*, *can't*, *half* — /ɑː/ in GB but /æ/ in GA.

**Pairs:** with STRUT, *heart – hut*, *calm – come*, *cart – cut* (GB); with TRAP in GB, *cart – cat*, *heart – hat*.

- *Can you park the car here?*
- *I can't come; I have to work late.*

### THOUGHT, NORTH, FORCE /ɔː/

**How to make it:** the back of the tongue is raised to between mid and close (GB) or a little lower (GA); the lips are firmly rounded. In GB this vowel is quite close and strongly rounded.

**Words:** THOUGHT: *saw*, *law*, *caught*, *taught*, *talk*, *walk*, *all*, *ball*, *water*. NORTH and FORCE: *for*, *horse*, *short*, *more*, *door*, *four*, *board* — GB /ɔː/, GA /ɔːr/.

In GB, therefore, *saw*, *sore* and *soar* are homophones (/sɔː/); in GA *saw* /sɔː/ differs from *sore* /sɔːr/. And many Americans say THOUGHT words with the vowel of LOT, so that *caught* and *cot* sound the same (see [[#rule-gb-ga-vowels]]).

**Pairs with NURSE:** *walk – work*, *ward – word*, *born – burn*, *short – shirt*, *torn – turn*, GB *four – fur*.

### NURSE /ɜː/ (GB), /ɜːr/ (GA)

**How to make it:** the tongue is in the middle of the mouth, at middle height — like a long, tense schwa; the lips are **not** rounded, and the jaw is only a little open. In GA the tongue tip is also raised and pulled back, or the tongue is bunched, giving the vowel an *r* colour throughout.

**Spellings:** always with a written *r* — *er* (*her*, *person*), *ir* (*bird*, *first*), *ur* (*turn*, *nurse*), *or* after *w* (*work*, *word*, *world*, *worse*), *ear* (*learn*, *early*, *heard*), *our* (*journey*).

- *Which word did you hear first?*
- *The early bird catches the worm.* (a proverb: people who start early succeed)

::: warning *Work* is not *walk*
✗ *I walk in a bank.* (when the speaker means her job)
✓ *I work in a bank.* /wɜːk/ (GB), /wɜːrk/ (GA)

For /ɜː/, keep the lips **flat** and the tongue in the middle; for /ɔː/ in *walk*, round the lips and pull the tongue back. A useful check in a mirror: if your lips are rounded on *work*, you are probably saying *walk*. The same contrast separates *word* and *ward*, *burn* and *born*, *shirt* and *short*.
:::

::: theorem Long vowels are shortened before voiceless consonants {#rule-clipping}
A long vowel (or a diphthong) is noticeably shorter before a voiceless consonant in the same syllable than before a voiced consonant or at the end of a word. Compare:

| longest | shorter |
|---|---|
| *bee*, *bead* /biːd/ | *beat* /biːt/ |
| *sore*, *sword* | *sort* |
| *lose* /luːz/ | *loose* /luːs/ |
| *ride* | *right* |

The vowel of *beat* can be as short as the vowel of *bid*, but it keeps its FLEECE quality.
:::

::: proof
This shortening, called **pre-fortis clipping**, is described in all the standard accounts of English pronunciation (for example Roach 2009; Cruttenden 2014), and was measured for American English by Peterson and Lehiste (1960). It is the main clue listeners use to tell *bead* from *beat* or *lose* from *loose*, because the final consonant itself is often hard to hear: a final /d/ or /z/ is only partly voiced. That is why the length of a long vowel is not a fixed property — it depends on what follows — and why quality, not length alone, separates the vowels of *beat* and *bit*. [[sounds-of-english/stops-fricatives]] uses this rule again for consonant pairs.
:::

::: quiz
In which word is the vowel **longest**?
- [ ] *seat*
- [x] *seed*
- [ ] *sit*
- [ ] *sick*
::: solution
*Seed* has a long vowel (/iː/) followed by a voiced consonant, so the vowel is not shortened. In *seat* the same vowel is clipped by the voiceless /t/. *Sit* and *sick* have the short vowel /ɪ/, also before voiceless consonants.
:::
:::

## The diphthongs

Say *my*. Your jaw starts quite open and closes as you finish; your tongue moves from low to high. The vowel is not one position but a movement. Such a vowel is a **diphthong**.

::: definition Diphthong {#def-diphthong}
A **diphthong** is a vowel in which the tongue (and often the lips) glides from one position to another within one syllable. It is written with two symbols, the start and the target: /aɪ/ in *my*. The first part is longer and louder; the second part shows the direction of the glide and is often not fully reached. A diphthong counts as **one** vowel and fills one syllable: *my* has one syllable, not two.
:::

English diphthongs fall into three groups by where they glide **to**:

::: theorem The diphthongs of GB and GA {#rule-diphthongs}
1. **Closing to /ɪ/** (front, high): FACE /eɪ/ *day*, PRICE /aɪ/ *my*, CHOICE /ɔɪ/ *boy*. The same in GB and GA.
2. **Closing to /ʊ/** (back, high): GOAT /əʊ/ in GB, /oʊ/ in GA, *go*; MOUTH /aʊ/ *now*.
3. **Centring to /ə/** (GB only): NEAR /ɪə/ *here*, SQUARE /eə/ *hair*, CURE /ʊə/ *pure*. GA has no centring diphthongs: it has a vowel followed by /r/ — *here* /hɪr/, *hair* /her/, *pure* /pjʊr/.

GB therefore has eight diphthongs and GA five.
:::

::: proof
The centring diphthongs of GB come from the loss of *r* after vowels (see the history note below). Where GA kept the /r/, the vowel before it stayed a simple vowel, and the /r/ supplies the glide. Modern GB is changing too: many younger speakers say SQUARE as a long pure vowel [ɛː] (*hair* [hɛː]), many say CURE words with /ɔː/ (*sure* /ʃɔː/, *poor* /pɔː/), and NEAR is sometimes a long [ɪː]. LPD and other dictionaries list these variants; learners can use either.
:::

### Closing diphthongs

| keyword | GB | GA | spellings | examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FACE | eɪ | eɪ | *a-e, ai, ay, ei, ey, ea* | *make, rain, day, eight, they, great* |
| PRICE | aɪ | aɪ | *i-e, y, igh, ie, uy* | *time, my, high, pie, buy* |
| CHOICE | ɔɪ | ɔɪ | *oi, oy* | *coin, noise, boy, enjoy* |
| GOAT | əʊ | oʊ | *o-e, oa, ow, o* | *home, road, know, go, old* |
| MOUTH | aʊ | aʊ | *ou, ow* | *house, out, now, town* |

**Useful pairs:** FACE–DRESS: *late – let*, *wait – wet*, *pain – pen*, *sale – sell*. GOAT–THOUGHT: *coat – caught*, *low – law*, *so – saw*, *woke – walk*. GOAT–MOUTH (GB): *know – now*, *tone – town*, *phoned – found*.

- *Sorry I'm late. Let me explain.*
- *Wait — the paint is still wet.*
- *I phoned the office and found the address.*

::: example Following the glide {#ex-glide}
Describe what the tongue and lips do in the vowel of each word, and use the description to explain how the words in each pair differ: *late – let*, *coat – caught*, *know – now*.
::: solution
1. *late* /leɪt/: the tongue starts at the DRESS position (front, mid) and glides up towards KIT; the lips stay spread. *let* /let/: the tongue stays at the DRESS position. So *late* = *let* + an upward glide (and a longer vowel).
2. *coat* GB /kəʊt/: the tongue starts in the centre (as for schwa) with flat lips, and glides back and up while the lips round; GA /koʊt/ starts back and already rounded. *caught* /kɔːt/: one position, back, firmly rounded, with no glide. So the difference is movement against stillness.
3. *know* GB /nəʊ/: from the centre to back-high, with rounding at the end. *now* /naʊ/: from a wide-open jaw, low front, to back-high with rounding. Both glide to the same place; they start in very different places, and *now* has the bigger movement of the jaw.

When you practise a diphthong, watch the movement in a mirror: a diphthong you cannot see moving is probably too short or not gliding.
:::
:::

### Centring diphthongs (GB)

| keyword | GB | GA | spellings | examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NEAR | ɪə | ɪr | *ear, eer, ere, ier* | *ear, hear, beer, here, pier, idea* |
| SQUARE | eə | er | *air, are, ear, ere, eir* | *hair, care, bear, where, their* |
| CURE | ʊə, ɔː | ʊr | *ure, our, oor* | *pure, tour, poor* |

**Useful pairs:** NEAR–SQUARE: *beer – bear*, *here – hair*, *fear – fair*, *cheer – chair*.

- *Is there a chair over here?*
- *Their hair is very fair.*

::: example Walk or work? {#ex-walk-work}
Read these sentences, choosing *walk* or *work*, *born* or *burn*. Write the vowel you need.

1. *I ___ to school; it's only ten minutes.*
2. *She has to ___ late tonight.*
3. *He was ___ in 2004.*
4. *Be careful — don't ___ your hand.*
::: solution
1. *walk* /wɔːk/ — THOUGHT, rounded lips. (*To school* tells you it is about movement.)
2. *work* GB /wɜːk/, GA /wɜːrk/ — NURSE, flat lips.
3. *born* GB /bɔːn/, GA /bɔːrn/ — NORTH, rounded lips.
4. *burn* GB /bɜːn/, GA /bɜːrn/ — NURSE, flat lips.

In GB the pairs differ only in the vowel. In GA both words have /r/, so the vowel alone again makes the difference: rounded /ɔː/ against central /ɜː/.
:::
:::

::: example Spelling *ea* {#ex-ea}
The spelling *ea* appears in *meat*, *bread*, *great*, *heart*, *learn*, *bear* and *near*. Which vowel does it stand for in each?
::: solution
1. *meat* /miːt/ — FLEECE, the most common value of *ea*.
2. *bread* /bred/ — DRESS, a short vowel (also *head*, *dead*, *weather*).
3. *great* /ɡreɪt/ — FACE (also *break*, *steak*).
4. *heart* GB /hɑːt/, GA /hɑːrt/ — START (also *hearth*).
5. *learn* GB /lɜːn/, GA /lɜːrn/ — NURSE (also *early*, *heard*, *search*).
6. *bear* GB /beə/, GA /ber/ — SQUARE (also *wear*, *pear*).
7. *near* GB /nɪə/, GA /nɪr/ — NEAR (also *hear*, *clear*).

Seven words, seven different vowels. Before *r*, the vowel letters usually change their value; this is one of the patterns of [[sounds-of-english/spelling-and-sound]].
:::
:::

## How GB and GA vowels differ

::: theorem The main vowel differences between GB and GA {#rule-gb-ga-vowels}
| keyword | GB | GA | examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| LOT | ɒ | ɑː | *hot, stop, job* |
| CLOTH | ɒ | ɔː | *off, long, cough* |
| BATH | ɑː | æ | *bath, ask, can't, half* |
| GOAT | əʊ | oʊ | *go, home, phone* |
| NURSE | ɜː | ɜːr | *bird, work* |
| START | ɑː | ɑːr | *car, hard* |
| NORTH, FORCE | ɔː | ɔːr | *door, short* |
| NEAR | ɪə | ɪr | *here, beer* |
| SQUARE | eə | er | *hair, care* |
| CURE | ʊə, ɔː | ʊr | *pure, tour* |
| lettER | ə | ər | *teacher, doctor* |

In addition: (1) many GA speakers say THOUGHT with the LOT vowel (*caught* = *cot*); (2) GA usually drops /j/ after /t, d, n/ (*new* /nuː/); (3) in GA, *Mary*, *marry* and *merry* are often all pronounced /ˈmeri/, while GB keeps three different vowels (/ˈmeəri/, /ˈmæri/, /ˈmeri/).
:::

::: proof
Most of the table follows from two facts. First, GA is rhotic: it kept /r/ after vowels (see [[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet#rule-rhotic]]), so it has no centring diphthongs and no "lost-r" long vowels. Second, a few changes happened on one side of the Atlantic only. The BATH vowel was lengthened and backed in southern England in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and this spread to GB but not to GA. In America, LOT was unrounded and lengthened, and in the West and in Canada it later merged with THOUGHT. These are tendencies of the two **models**: regional accents in both countries differ from them in many ways ([[world-englishes/british-american]] and [[listening-and-conversation/accents]] describe more).
:::

::: example One sentence, two accents {#ex-gb-ga}
Transcribe the stressed words of this sentence in GB and in GA: *Our first car was a small old Ford.*
::: solution
Work word by word, checking the table in [[#rule-gb-ga-vowels]]:

| word | GB | GA | difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| *our* | /aʊə/ or /ɑː/ | /aʊr/ | GB has no /r/; many GB speakers smooth it to /ɑː/ |
| *first* | /fɜːst/ | /fɜːrst/ | NURSE + /r/ in GA |
| *car* | /kɑː/ | /kɑːr/ | START + /r/ in GA |
| *small* | /smɔːl/ | /smɔːl/ | the same (some GA speakers /smɑːl/) |
| *old* | /əʊld/ | /oʊld/ | GOAT |
| *Ford* | /fɔːd/ | /fɔːrd/ | NORTH + /r/ in GA |

Four of the six words differ only because of *r*; *old* differs in the start of the GOAT diphthong.
:::
:::

## Asking the way: a dialogue

**Tourist:** *Excuse me. Is the art museum near here?* (FLEECE *-seum*; START *art*; NEAR *near*, *here*)

**Ahmed:** *It's not far. Go down this road and turn right at the square.* (START *far*; GOAT *go*, *road*; NURSE *turn*; PRICE *right*; SQUARE *square*)

**Tourist:** *Can I walk, or should I take a bus?* (THOUGHT *walk*; FACE *take*)

**Ahmed:** *You can walk. It takes about four minutes. It's the large white building with blue doors.* (FACE *takes*; MOUTH *about*; FORCE *four*, *doors*; START *large*; PRICE *white*; GOOSE *blue*)

**Tourist:** *Great, thanks. Is it open today?* (FACE *great*, *today*; GOAT *open*)

**Ahmed:** *I think so — it's open every day except Monday.* (GOAT *so*, *open*; FACE *day*)

::: note English and Chinese
- **Diphthongs.** Mandarin has diphthongs too: Pinyin *ai*, *ei*, *ao* and *ou* (as in 爱, 黑, 好, 走) glide in the same directions as PRICE, FACE, MOUTH and GOAT. They are a good starting point. But the English diphthongs are longer, and the first part is held a little before the glide; a Mandarin *ei* used for FACE can sound too short, especially before a consonant (*make*, *late*).
- **GOAT.** Pinyin *ou* is close to GA /oʊ/; the GB vowel /əʊ/ starts from a central, unrounded position, like schwa.
- **NURSE.** Mandarin *er* (儿, 二) is an *r*-coloured central vowel, close to GA /ɜːr/ in *her* and to GA lettER. Speakers who use *er* comfortably have a good basis for GA NURSE. For the GB vowel, start from schwa, keep the lips flat, and hold it long without curling the tongue.
- **No length contrast.** As with the short vowels, Mandarin does not use length to separate words, so practise the long vowels next to their short neighbours (*sheep – ship*, *fool – full*) and remember the clipping rule ([[#rule-clipping]]): a long vowel before *t*, *p*, *k*, *s* is short but keeps its quality.
- **Final r.** Learners who follow a GA model sometimes drop the final /r/; learners who follow GB sometimes add a strong /r/ because of the spelling. Either model is fine — but choose one and keep it consistent within a word.
:::

::: history
Many long vowels and diphthongs of today come from the **Great Vowel Shift**, a series of changes between about 1400 and 1700 in which the long vowels of Middle English were raised, and the highest ones (/iː/ and /uː/) became diphthongs. The word *bite* once had the vowel of *beet* today; *house* had the vowel of *goose*; *name* had a vowel like the *a* of *father*. Because English spelling was becoming fixed during the same period, especially after printing came to England in 1476, the spellings often show the older pronunciation. The order and the causes of the shift are still debated; [[history-of-english/great-vowel-shift]] tells the story. The GB centring diphthongs are younger: they arose in the eighteenth century, when *r* after vowels was lost in the south of England.
:::

## Where this leads

The consonants come next: [[sounds-of-english/stops-fricatives]] and [[sounds-of-english/nasals-approximants]]. The clipping rule ([[#rule-clipping]]) is essential there. How long vowels and diphthongs are spelled (*a-e*, *ai*, *ay* …) is the subject of [[sounds-of-english/spelling-and-sound]], and more British–American differences are described in [[world-englishes/british-american]].

::: summary
- The long vowels are FLEECE /iː/, GOOSE /uː/, PALM–START–BATH /ɑː/, THOUGHT–NORTH–FORCE /ɔː/ and NURSE /ɜː/; in GA, START, NORTH, FORCE and NURSE have /r/ ([[#def-long-vowel]]).
- *Work* (NURSE: flat lips, central tongue) is not *walk* (THOUGHT: rounded lips, back tongue); the same contrast separates *word – ward*, *burn – born*, *shirt – short*.
- Long vowels and diphthongs are shortened before voiceless consonants: *seed* has a longer vowel than *seat* ([[#rule-clipping]]).
- A **diphthong** glides from one position to another in one syllable; the first part is stronger ([[#def-diphthong]]).
- GB has eight diphthongs — FACE, PRICE, CHOICE, GOAT, MOUTH and the centring NEAR, SQUARE, CURE; GA has five, with a vowel + /r/ instead of the centring ones ([[#rule-diphthongs]]).
- The main GB–GA differences are *r* after vowels, LOT, CLOTH, BATH and GOAT ([[#rule-gb-ga-vowels]]).
- Many spellings of today show pronunciations from before the Great Vowel Shift, which is why *bite* and *house* are spelled as they are.
:::

::: exercise Which vowel is longer? {level=2}
In each pair, which word has the **longer** vowel? Explain with [[#rule-clipping]].

1. *rice* – *rise*  2. *place* – *plays*  3. *goat* – *goad*  4. *heart* – *hard*
::: solution
1. *rise* /raɪz/ — before voiced /z/; in *rice* /raɪs/ the vowel is clipped by voiceless /s/.
2. *plays* /pleɪz/ — before voiced /z/.
3. *goad* /ɡəʊd/ — before voiced /d/.
4. *hard* /hɑːd/ (GB) — before voiced /d/.

In every pair the vowel is the same; only its length changes, and that length tells the listener which consonant follows.
:::

::: exercise Count the diphthongs {level=2 check="3"}
How many diphthongs are there in the sentence *My boyfriend phoned.*? Type one number.
::: hint
Transcribe each word first (GB or GA — the answer is the same).
:::
::: solution
*My* /maɪ/ has PRICE; *boyfriend* /ˈbɔɪfrend/ has CHOICE in the first syllable and the short DRESS vowel in the second; *phoned* /fəʊnd/ (GB), /foʊnd/ (GA) has GOAT. That makes three diphthongs. The answer is 3.
:::

::: exercise Transcribe a sentence {level=2}
Transcribe the stressed words in GB and in GA: *We bought four new shirts.*
::: solution
- *bought*: /bɔːt/ (GB and GA; some GA speakers /bɑːt/).
- *four*: GB /fɔː/, GA /fɔːr/.
- *new*: GB /njuː/, GA /nuː/.
- *shirts*: GB /ʃɜːts/, GA /ʃɜːrts/.

*We* is often unstressed (/wi/).
:::

::: exercise Explain a misunderstanding {level=3}
A learner says *I need a cot* in a shop, and is shown a bed for a baby. She wanted a *coat*. Using what you know about GOAT and LOT, explain what happened and how she can avoid it. Would the same thing happen with a GA listener?
::: hint
Think about how many "parts" the GOAT vowel has, and about the GA LOT vowel.
:::
::: solution
*Coat* has the diphthong GOAT, GB /kəʊt/, GA /koʊt/: the tongue glides from a central (GB) or back-mid (GA) position towards /ʊ/, and the lips round as the glide happens. *Cot* has a short single vowel in GB, /kɒt/. If the learner says a single short rounded vowel without the glide, a GB listener hears /ɒ/ and understands *cot*. To avoid this, she should make the vowel longer and let it glide clearly — start like the first sound of *about* (GB) and finish with rounded lips. With a GA listener the confusion is a little different: GA *cot* is /kɑːt/, open and unrounded, so a short rounded vowel may sound more like *caught* /kɔːt/ — still not *coat*. In both accents the glide is the key.
:::
:::

::: exercise A short description {level=3}
Write four or five sentences about a place you like, using at least six words with long vowels or diphthongs. Then read your text aloud and record it. Mark the long vowels and diphthongs, name their lexical sets, and check two things in the recording: are the NURSE words different from the THOUGHT words, and do the diphthongs glide?
::: solution
Answers will differ. A model:

> *I love the old town near my home. On warm days I walk by the river and sit under a tree. Small cafés line the square, where people read and talk for hours.*

Long vowels and diphthongs: *old* GOAT; *town* MOUTH; *near* NEAR; *my* PRICE; *home* GOAT; *warm* NORTH; *days* FACE; *I* PRICE; *walk* THOUGHT; *tree* FLEECE; *small* THOUGHT; *cafés* FACE; *line* PRICE; *square* SQUARE; *where* SQUARE; *people* FLEECE; *read* FLEECE; *talk* THOUGHT; *hours* MOUTH. (*river*, *sit*, *under* have short vowels.) This text has no NURSE word; add one — for example *I go there first on Saturday mornings* — to check the NURSE–THOUGHT contrast against *walk* and *talk*.
