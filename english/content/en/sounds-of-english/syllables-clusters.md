How many syllables are there in *strengths*? Most English speakers say one: /streŋθs/ (some say /streŋkθs/). The word has three consonants before its vowel and three or four after it, but only one vowel, and so only one beat. A learner who says *si-te-reng-ke-si* has turned one syllable into five. Every sound in it may be correct, and the listener may still not recognise the word, because the **shape** is wrong.

Mandarin syllables are short and simple: at most one consonant before the vowel, and only *-n*, *-ng* (or *-r*) after it. English allows up to three consonants before the vowel and four after it. This chapter explains what a syllable is, which groups of consonants English allows, how to count syllables, and how to say clusters such as *str-*, *-sts* and *-ksθs* without adding vowels or dropping sounds — which matters for grammar too, because the English endings *-s* and *-ed* constantly create new clusters.

## What is a syllable?

Say *banana* and clap at the same time. You clap three times: *ba-na-na*. Each clap marks a **syllable**, a beat of speech built around a vowel.

::: definition Syllable, onset, nucleus, coda {#def-syllable}
A **syllable** is a unit of speech with one vowel sound (or, sometimes, a syllabic consonant) at its centre. The centre is the **nucleus**. Any consonants before it form the **onset**; any consonants after it form the **coda**. The nucleus and coda together are called the **rhyme**, because words rhyme when their last rhymes match (*cat – hat*, *light – night*). Only the nucleus is required: *I* /aɪ/ is a syllable with no onset and no coda.
:::

The figure shows the structure of the one-syllable word *strengths*.

The rhyme is everything from the vowel to the end. A Mandarin syllable has room for at most one consonant in the onset and one nasal (or *r*) in the coda. You can drag the points.

### Counting syllables

::: theorem Counting syllables {#rule-count}
The number of syllables in a word is the number of **vowel sounds** in it (plus any syllabic consonants), not the number of vowel letters:

- *cake* /keɪk/ — 1 (the final *e* is silent); *changed* /tʃeɪndʒd/ — 1; *strengths* — 1.
- *wanted* /ˈwɒntɪd/ (GB) — 2: after /t/ or /d/, the ending *-ed* adds a syllable; *watched* /wɒtʃt/ — 1: after other consonants it does not.
- *every* /ˈevri/ — 2; *Wednesday* /ˈwenzdeɪ/ — 2; *business* /ˈbɪznəs/ — 2; *chocolate* /ˈtʃɒklət/ (GB) — 2. In these words a written vowel is not pronounced.
- *button* /ˈbʌtn/ — 2; *little* /ˈlɪtl/ — 2: the second syllable is a syllabic /n/ or /l/.

Some words have two common forms: *family* /ˈfæməli/ or /ˈfæmli/, *different* /ˈdɪfrənt/ or /ˈdɪfərənt/, *comfortable* /ˈkʌmftəbl/ or /ˈkʌmfətəbl/. Dictionaries such as LPD show both.
:::

::: proof
Syllables are built around the most open, loudest sounds — the vowels — because those are what the ear hears as beats. Consonants cluster around them. When a vowel is weak and short, as schwa often is, it can disappear completely in normal speech, and the consonants on each side join: *chocolate*, *every*, *family*. Where the remaining consonant is a nasal or /l/, it can carry the beat itself (*button*, *little*). So the count depends on the pronunciation, not the spelling, and can vary between careful and relaxed speech.
:::

::: quiz
How many syllables does *asked* /ɑːskt/ (GB), /æskt/ (GA) have?
- [x] 1
- [ ] 2
- [ ] 3
::: solution
One vowel sound, so one syllable, with a coda of three consonants /skt/. The ending *-ed* adds a syllable only after /t/ or /d/ (*wanted*, *needed*). Saying /ˈɑːskɪd/ or /ˈɑːskə tə/ adds syllables that the word does not have.
:::
:::

::: example Counting the beats in a sentence {#ex-count}
How many syllables are there in *Every Wednesday my family has chocolate cake.*? Count in normal speech, then compare with the number of written vowel letters.
::: solution
Go word by word and count vowel **sounds** ([[#rule-count]]):

| word | transcription (GB) | syllables |
|---|---|---|
| *Every* | /ˈevri/ | 2 |
| *Wednesday* | /ˈwenzdeɪ/ | 2 |
| *my* | /maɪ/ | 1 |
| *family* | /ˈfæmli/ or /ˈfæməli/ | 2 or 3 |
| *has* | /hæz/ (weak /həz/) | 1 |
| *chocolate* | /ˈtʃɒklət/ | 2 |
| *cake* | /keɪk/ | 1 |

Total: 11 in relaxed speech, 12 if *family* has three syllables. The written words contain 18 vowel letters (counting *y* when it is a vowel), so counting letters would give a very wrong rhythm. The extra letters are silent (*cake*, *Wednesday*) or stand for vowels that are usually not pronounced (*every*, *chocolate*).
:::
:::

## Which clusters does English allow?

A group of two or more consonants in the same onset or coda is a **consonant cluster**. English allows many clusters, but not every combination: *blick* could be an English word (it just happens not to exist), but *bnick* could not.

::: definition Consonant cluster {#def-cluster}
A **consonant cluster** is a sequence of two or more consonants with no vowel between them in the onset or the coda of one syllable: *pl* in *play*, *str* in *street*, *nts* in *wants*. In a phonemic transcription a cluster is a series of consonant symbols: /pleɪ/, /striːt/, /wɒnts/ (GB). A spelling such as *sh* or *ng* is not a cluster, because it stands for one sound.
:::

::: theorem Onset clusters {#rule-onsets}
1. **Two consonants.** The usual patterns are:
   - a plosive or /f/, /θ/, /ʃ/ followed by /l/, /r/, /w/ or /j/: *play*, *bring*, *twin*, *cute* /kjuːt/, *fly*, *three*, *shrink*;
   - /s/ followed by a voiceless plosive, a nasal, /l/ or /w/: *spot*, *stop*, *sky*, *small*, *snow*, *slow*, *swim*.
   Not allowed in English onsets: /tl/, /dl/ (*tl-*, *dl-*), /sr/, /pw/ (except in some names), and any onset with /ŋ/, /ʒ/ (except in loanwords) or /h/ + consonant other than /j/ (*huge* /hjuːdʒ/).
2. **Three consonants.** A three-consonant onset is always /s/ + a voiceless plosive /p t k/ + an approximant: *spring* /spr/, *split* /spl/, *street* /str/, *scream* /skr/, *square* /skw/, *spew* /spj/, *skewer* /skj/, and in GB *stew* /stj/.
3. An onset never has more than three consonants.
:::

::: theorem Coda clusters {#rule-codas}
English codas can have up to four consonants. Common two-consonant codas include a nasal or /l/ + a consonant (*hand*, *think*, *help*, *milk*), /s/ + a plosive (*fast*, *ask*, *lisp*) and a plosive + /s/ or /t/ (*box* /ks/, *act* /kt/). Longer codas are usually created by grammatical endings: *-s* (plural, third person, possessive), *-ed* (past) and *-th* (ordinal numbers and nouns):

| coda | example | built from |
|---|---|---|
| /sts/ | *texts* /teksts/ | *text* + *-s* |
| /mpt/ | *jumped* /dʒʌmpt/ | *jump* + *-ed* |
| /lpt/ | *helped* /helpt/ | *help* + *-ed* |
| /nθs/ | *months* /mʌnθs/ | *month* + *-s* |
| /ksθ/ | *sixth* /sɪksθ/ | *six* + *-th* |
| /ksθs/ | *sixths* /sɪksθs/ | *sixth* + *-s* |
| /mpst/ | *glimpsed* /ɡlɪmpst/ | *glimpse* + *-ed* |
:::

::: proof
The patterns in [[#rule-onsets]] and [[#rule-codas]] follow a general tendency described for many languages: within a syllable, sounds tend to rise in loudness ("sonority") from the onset to the vowel and fall again to the end of the coda — plosives and fricatives are the least sonorous, then nasals, then /l/ and /r/, then the glides, then the vowels. *Play* rises (/p/ → /l/ → vowel); *lpay* would fall and rise again, and English does not allow it. /s/ is a well-known exception at the edge of the syllable: *stop* and *texts* have /s/ outside the expected order, which is why /s/ can begin three-consonant onsets and end long codas. These are descriptions of English in general; LPD and the standard phonology textbooks (Roach 2009; Cruttenden 2014) give the full lists.
:::

::: example Possible or impossible? {#ex-possible}
Which of these made-up words could be English words? *blick*, *bnick*, *strop*, *sprill*, *ngat*, *tlop*, *frisk* (a real word), *stwem*.
::: solution
Check each onset against [[#rule-onsets]]:

1. *blick* /blɪk/: /bl/ — plosive + /l/ — possible. (It is the classic example of a possible but non-existent word.)
2. *bnick* /bnɪk/: /bn/ — plosive + nasal — not allowed.
3. *strop* /strɒp/: /str/ — /s/ + voiceless plosive + approximant — possible; it is in fact a rare word.
4. *sprill* /sprɪl/: /spr/ — possible.
5. *ngat*: an onset /ŋ/ — not allowed.
6. *tlop*: /tl/ — not allowed at the start of an English word.
7. *frisk* /frɪsk/: onset /fr/ and coda /sk/ — both allowed (it is a real word).
8. *stwem* /stwem/: /stw/ — /s/ + voiceless plosive + /w/ is not on the list of three-consonant onsets — not allowed (though *sw-* and *tw-* each exist).

Knowing what is possible helps with listening: when you hear a cluster you did not expect, you have probably misheard a vowel or a consonant.
:::
:::

## Saying clusters

### What goes wrong

Learners whose first language has simpler syllables usually make a cluster fit their own pattern in one of two ways.

::: warning Do not add vowels to clusters
✗ /səˈtriːt/ or /sɪˈtriːt/ for *street* — two syllables instead of one.
✗ /ˈdeskə/ for *desk*, /ˈmɪlkə/ for *milk*.
✓ *street* /striːt/, *desk* /desk/, *milk* /mɪlk/ — one syllable each.

An added vowel can change the word (*sport* → *support* /səˈpɔːt/; *blow* → *below* /bɪˈləʊ/) or make it hard to recognise. Keep the consonants **together**: begin the second consonant before the first one has finished.
:::

::: warning Do not drop the endings
✗ *Yesterday I ask him.* ✗ *She want two book.* ✗ *Three month ago.*
✓ *Yesterday I asked him.* /ɑːskt/ ✓ *She wants two books.* /wɒnts/, /bʊks/ ✓ *Three months ago.* /mʌnθs/

Many clusters at the end of English words are **grammar**: the /t/ of *asked* marks the past, the /s/ of *wants* marks the third person, the /s/ of *books* and *months* marks the plural. Dropping the last consonant of the cluster drops the grammar. Native speakers do simplify some clusters in fast speech (*next week* /neks wiːk/; see [[connected-speech/elision-assimilation]]), but they rarely drop a grammatical ending completely, and listeners use these endings to understand who did what and when.
:::

### How to practise

::: theorem Practising a cluster {#rule-practice}
1. **Start from the vowel.** Practise the rhyme first and add consonants one by one: for *texts*, say *ex* → *ext* → *exts* → *texts*. For an onset, add consonants towards the vowel: *reet* → *treet* → *street*.
2. **Overlap the consonants.** In a cluster such as *act* or *jumped*, the first plosive is usually not released: the tongue moves to the next closure before the first one opens. Do not let a puff of air or a vowel escape between them.
3. **Keep the syllable count.** Clap the beats: *texts* is one clap, *wanted* two, *jumped* one.
4. **Slow, then natural.** Say the word slowly with every consonant, then at normal speed. Natural speed often makes clusters easier, because the movements overlap.
:::

::: example Building a cluster {#ex-build}
Practise *months* /mʌnθs/ and *asked* /ɑːskt/ (GB) using [[#rule-practice]].
::: solution
*months*:
1. Start from the vowel and the nasal: *un* /ʌn/ → *mun* /mʌn/.
2. Add /θ/: keep the tongue tip on the ridge after /n/, then slide it forward to the teeth: *munth* /mʌnθ/.
3. Add /s/: pull the tongue tip back from the teeth to just behind them: *months* /mʌnθs/. (Many native speakers simplify this to /mʌns/ or /mʌnts/ in fast speech; a clear /θ/ is still the model.)

*asked*:
1. *ass* /ɑːs/ → *ask* /ɑːsk/: keep the hiss of /s/ until the back of the tongue closes for /k/.
2. Add /t/ **without releasing /k/**: while the back of the tongue still closes, raise the tip to the ridge, then release only /t/: /ɑːskt/.
3. Clap once. If you clap twice, you have added a vowel.
:::
:::

::: example Repairing a learner's syllables {#ex-repair}
A learner reads *She wants to buy six desks.* as *she want-e-su tu bai si-ku-su de-su-ku-su*. Count the syllables in the learner's version and in the model, and say what went wrong.
::: solution
Model (GB): /ʃi ˈwɒnts tə ˈbaɪ ˈsɪks ˈdesks/ — *She* (1) *wants* (1) *to* (1) *buy* (1) *six* (1) *desks* (1) = **6 syllables**.

The learner's version: *she* (1) *want-e-su* (3) *tu* (1) *bai* (1) *si-ku-su* (3) *de-su-ku-su* (4) = **13 syllables**.

What went wrong:
1. Vowels were added inside and after the clusters /nts/, /ks/ and /sks/, more than doubling the number of beats.
2. The sentence lost its rhythm: the stressed syllables *wants*, *buy*, *six*, *desks* should be the beats; the extra vowels hide them.

Repair: practise the codas from the vowel outward — *ants* → *wants*; *iks* → *six*; *esk* → *desk* → *desks* — and clap six beats for the whole sentence.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which word has a **three-consonant** onset?
- [ ] *stay*
- [x] *splash*
- [ ] *flash*
- [ ] *shrimp*
::: solution
*Splash* /splæʃ/ begins with /spl/: /s/ + voiceless plosive + approximant. *Stay* and *flash* have two-consonant onsets (/st/, /fl/). *Shrimp* /ʃrɪmp/ also has two: /ʃ/ is one sound, so *shr* is /ʃr/.
:::
:::

## A short dialogue

**Mohammed:** *What are your plans for the next six months?*

**Li Na:** *First, I've got my driving test in March. Then I start a new job in the spring.*

**Mohammed:** *Congratulations! Where's the job?*

**Li Na:** *At a sports club in the west of the city. I helped there last summer, and they asked me to come back.*

**Mohammed:** *That's great. It sounds like the next six months will be busy.*

Clusters to listen for — **onsets**: *plans* /pl/, *driving* /dr/, *spring* /spr/, *sports* /sp/, *club* /kl/; **codas**: *plans* /nz/, *next* /kst/, *six* /ks/, *months* /nθs/, *first* /st/, *test* /st/, *helped* /lpt/, *last* /st/, *asked* /skt/, *sports* /ts/, *west* /st/, *sounds* /ndz/.

::: note English and Chinese
- **The Mandarin syllable.** A standard Mandarin syllable has the shape (C)(G)V(N): at most one consonant at the start (an affricate such as *zh* or *q* counts as one), an optional glide (*i*, *u*, *ü* before the main vowel, as in *jiā*, *guó*), a vowel, and an optional final *-n* or *-ng* (or the *-r* of 儿化). There are no consonant clusters, and no plosives or fricatives at the end. English syllables such as *strengths* are therefore far outside the Mandarin pattern.
- **One character, one syllable.** Chinese writing gives every syllable its own character, so Chinese readers are used to seeing syllables. English spelling does not: *strengths* has nine letters and one syllable, *idea* has four letters and three syllables. Count vowel sounds, not letters.
- **Added vowels.** The added vowel is usually short and central, like Mandarin *e* in *de* (的): *desk* becomes something like *desk-e*. Speakers of Cantonese and southern Min, whose home languages have final /p t k/, often add fewer vowels after final plosives, but may still find /s/ + plosive clusters and grammatical endings hard.
- **Grammar and sound together.** Because Chinese has no inflections for tense or number ([[core-grammar/past-simple]], [[core-grammar/nouns-pronouns]]), a learner who drops the final /t/ of *asked* or the /s/ of *books* may also be forgetting the grammar. Practising the clusters is practising the grammar.
:::

::: history
English once had even more clusters at the start of words. In Old and Middle English the *k* of *knee*, *know* and *knight*, the *g* of *gnaw*, and the *w* of *write* and *wrong* were pronounced: *knight* began with /kn/, as its German cousin *Knecht* still does. These clusters were simplified between about the fifteenth and the eighteenth centuries (/kn/ was among the last to go, in the seventeenth). The spelling had already been fixed by then, which is why English is full of "silent letters" at the start of words — a subject of [[sounds-of-english/spelling-and-sound]].
:::

## Where this leads

Spelling patterns, silent letters and the pronunciation of the endings *-s* and *-ed* are in [[sounds-of-english/spelling-and-sound]]. Which syllable of a word is stressed is the subject of [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics]]. In fast speech, clusters are simplified across word boundaries (*next week*, *last time*): see [[connected-speech/elision-assimilation]] and [[connected-speech/rhythm]].

::: summary
- A **syllable** has a nucleus (usually a vowel), an optional onset before it and an optional coda after it ([[#def-syllable]]).
- Count syllables by **vowel sounds**, not letters: *changed* has 1, *wanted* 2, *every* 2, *button* 2 ([[#rule-count]]).
- A **consonant cluster** is two or more consonants together in an onset or coda ([[#def-cluster]]).
- English onsets have at most three consonants; three-consonant onsets are /s/ + /p t k/ + an approximant (*spring*, *street*, *square*) ([[#rule-onsets]]).
- Codas can have up to four consonants, mostly because of the endings *-s*, *-ed* and *-th* (*texts*, *helped*, *sixths*) ([[#rule-codas]]).
- Do not add vowels to clusters (*street* is one syllable), and do not drop final consonants that carry grammar (*asked*, *wants*, *months*).
- Practise from the vowel outwards, overlap the consonants, and clap the beats ([[#rule-practice]]).
:::
