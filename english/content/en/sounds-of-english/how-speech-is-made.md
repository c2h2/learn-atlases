Put two fingers lightly on the front of your throat. Now say a long *ssssss*, like a snake. Then, without stopping, change it to a long *zzzzzz*, like a bee. Under your fingers you will feel a buzz start when *s* becomes *z*, and stop again when you go back to *s*. Your tongue did not move. Your lips did not move. Only one thing changed, deep in your throat, and that one change turns *Sue* into *zoo*, *bus* into *buzz* and *place* into *plays*.

This chapter is about the machine that makes speech: the lungs, the throat, the tongue, the lips and the nose. You do not need to know biology to use it. You need a few names, so that later chapters can say "put the tip of your tongue on the ridge behind your top teeth" in a short and exact way. You also need three simple questions that describe almost every consonant in English, and three that describe every vowel. With these, a new sound is no longer a mystery: you can find out how it is made and practise it on purpose.

Slashes, as in /s/ and /z/, show sounds, not letters. The next chapter, [[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet]], explains all the symbols. In this chapter we use only a few, and each one is given with a common word.

## The organs of speech

Almost all speech in English (and in Chinese) is made with air that comes **out** of the lungs. On its way out, the air passes through the throat, the mouth and sometimes the nose. Different parts of this path can open, close or narrow. Each change gives a different sound.

| part | where it is | what it does in speech |
|---|---|---|
| lungs | in the chest | push the air out |
| larynx (voice box) | in the throat, behind the "Adam's apple" | holds the vocal folds |
| vocal folds | two small bands of muscle inside the larynx | vibrate for voiced sounds, stay apart for voiceless ones |
| pharynx | the space at the back of the throat | part of the "tube" that shapes vowels |
| soft palate (velum) | the soft back part of the roof of the mouth | goes up to close the nose, or down to open it |
| hard palate | the hard middle part of the roof of the mouth | the tongue comes near it for /j/ in *yes* |
| alveolar ridge | the hard bump just behind the top front teeth | the tongue touches it for /t/, /d/, /n/, /l/ |
| teeth | top and bottom | the lip or tongue touches them for /f/, /v/ and the two *th* sounds |
| tongue | tip, blade (just behind the tip), front, back | the most important mover: makes most vowels and consonants |
| lips | | close for /p/, /b/, /m/; round for /uː/ in *food* and /w/ |
| nasal cavity | the space behind the nose | air goes through it for /m/, /n/ and /ŋ/ |

Try to find each part yourself. Run the tip of your tongue back from your top teeth: first you feel the teeth, then a small bump (the alveolar ridge), then the hard palate rises up, and far back it becomes soft (the soft palate). You can see the soft palate in a mirror if you open your mouth wide and say *ah*.

::: definition Articulators {#def-articulator}
The **articulators** are the parts of the mouth and throat that move or are touched to make a sound. The **active articulators** move: the lips, the tongue and the soft palate. The **passive articulators** stay still while something moves towards them: the top teeth, the alveolar ridge and the hard palate.
:::

When we say that /t/ is made "at the alveolar ridge", we mean that the tongue (the active articulator) touches the alveolar ridge (the passive one). The place where they meet is called the **place of articulation**.

## Voice: the buzz in the throat

The vocal folds are two small, soft bands inside the larynx. They can be held apart, so that air passes freely, or brought together. When they are together and air pushes between them, they open and close very fast — roughly 100 to 250 times a second in adult speech. This fast opening and closing makes a buzz, which we call **voice**.

::: definition Voiced and voiceless sounds {#def-voicing}
A sound is **voiced** if the vocal folds vibrate while it is made, and **voiceless** if they do not. In normal speech all English vowels are voiced. Consonants can be either: /z/, /v/, /m/ and /l/ are voiced; /s/, /f/ and /h/ are voiceless.
:::

You can test any sound in three ways:

1. Put your fingers on your throat, as in the opening of this chapter, and feel for the buzz.
2. Put your hands over your ears and say the sound. A voiced sound is loud and buzzing inside your head; a voiceless one is a quiet hiss.
3. Try to sing a tune on the sound. You can sing on *mmmm* or *zzzz*, but not on *ssss* or *ffff*.

The figure below shows why voice can carry a tune. The vocal folds produce a wave that repeats again and again. A repeating wave is made of many simple waves together — a lowest one and its "harmonics" — and the mouth then makes some of them stronger and others weaker. That is how one buzz can become *ee*, *ah* or *oo*.

Many English consonants come in **pairs**: two sounds made in the same place and in the same way, one voiceless and one voiced.

::: theorem Voiceless–voiced pairs {#rule-pairs}
These English consonants form pairs that differ (mainly) in voice:

| voiceless | voiced | example words |
|---|---|---|
| /p/ | /b/ | *pie* – *buy* |
| /t/ | /d/ | *ten* – *den* |
| /k/ | /ɡ/ | *coat* – *goat* |
| /f/ | /v/ | *fan* – *van* |
| /θ/ | /ð/ | *thigh* – *thy* |
| /s/ | /z/ | *sip* – *zip* |
| /ʃ/ | /ʒ/ | *Confucian* – *confusion* |
| /tʃ/ | /dʒ/ | *cheap* – *jeep* |

Changing only the voice of the consonant can change the word: *fan* and *van* mean different things.
:::

::: proof
The pairs are made with the same articulators in the same way; only the vocal folds do something different. In real speech, English speakers do not use voice alone to keep the pairs apart. At the start of a word, /p/, /t/ and /k/ also have a puff of air (aspiration), and at the end of a word the vowel before /b/, /d/, /ɡ/ is longer than before /p/, /t/, /k/. These extra differences are explained in [[sounds-of-english/stops-fricatives]]. They are the reason the table says "mainly". The pair /ʃ/–/ʒ/ is unusual: /ʒ/ is the rarest consonant of English and almost never begins a word, so the example has to use two long words (*Confucian* /kənˈfjuːʃən/, *confusion* /kənˈfjuːʒən/).
:::

::: quiz
Which word **begins** with a voiced sound?
- [ ] *sea*
- [ ] *fine*
- [x] *very*
- [ ] *hat*
::: solution
*Very* begins with /v/: the lower lip touches the top teeth and the vocal folds vibrate, so you can feel a buzz. /s/ in *sea*, /f/ in *fine* and /h/ in *hat* are voiceless — only air, no buzz.
:::
:::

::: example Feeling the difference {#ex-feel-voicing}
Say these words slowly with your fingers on your throat: *fast*, *vast*, *sink*, *zinc*. Where does the buzz begin in each word?
::: solution
1. *fast* /fɑːst/ (GB), /fæst/ (GA): /f/ is voiceless. The buzz begins only with the vowel.
2. *vast*: /v/ is voiced. The buzz begins at once, with the first sound.
3. *sink*: /s/ is voiceless. The buzz begins with the vowel /ɪ/.
4. *zinc*: /z/ is voiced. The buzz begins at once.

In each pair the mouth does the same thing; only the start of the buzz moves. Notice also that the buzz **stops** before the end of every word: the last sounds /st/ and /ŋk/ include voiceless consonants (/ŋ/ itself is voiced, /k/ is not).
:::
:::

## Vowels and consonants

In school you learned that *a, e, i, o, u* are "vowels". Those are vowel **letters**. In this course a vowel is a **sound**, and the two ideas do not always match.

::: definition Vowel and consonant {#def-vowel-consonant}
A **vowel** is a sound made with the air flowing freely through the middle of the mouth, without any closing or narrowing that makes noise. A **consonant** is a sound made by closing the way for the air or making it narrow somewhere in the mouth or throat. In English, a vowel is normally the centre of a syllable; consonants come before and after it.
:::

Two English sounds sit between the two groups: /j/ (the first sound of *yes*) and /w/ (the first sound of *wet*). The mouth is almost as open as for a vowel, but they do not form the centre of a syllable, so they are counted as consonants. They are often called **semivowels**.

::: warning Letters are not sounds
✗ *an university*, ✗ *an European country* — the learner looked at the first **letter**, *u* or *e*.
✓ *a university*, *a European country*, *a one-way street* — these words begin with the **consonant** sounds /j/ and /w/, so they take *a*.
✓ *an hour*, *an honest man* — these begin with a **vowel** sound, because the *h* is silent.

The choice between *a* and *an* depends on the first **sound** of the next word, not the first letter. The same is true of *the*, which many speakers say as /ðə/ before a consonant sound and /ði/ or /ðiː/ before a vowel sound (*the apple*, *the hour*).
:::

### Describing a consonant

Every consonant can be described with three questions. Phoneticians call the answers **voice**, **place** and **manner**.

::: theorem Three questions for a consonant {#rule-voice-place-manner}
To describe a consonant, answer:

1. **Voice:** do the vocal folds vibrate? (voiced or voiceless)
2. **Place:** where is the air stopped or narrowed? (which articulators meet)
3. **Manner:** how is the air stopped or narrowed? (closed completely, made narrow, sent through the nose, and so on)

Two consonants of English that have the same three answers are the same sound.
:::

The **places** used for English consonants, from the front of the mouth to the back:

| place | what touches what | English examples |
|---|---|---|
| bilabial | both lips | /p/ *pen*, /b/ *bed*, /m/ *man* |
| labiodental | lower lip and top teeth | /f/ *fish*, /v/ *voice* |
| dental | tongue tip and top teeth | /θ/ *think*, /ð/ *this* |
| alveolar | tongue tip and alveolar ridge | /t/ *top*, /d/ *dog*, /s/ *sun*, /z/ *zoo*, /n/ *no*, /l/ *leg* |
| postalveolar | tongue blade and the area just behind the ridge | /ʃ/ *shoe*, /ʒ/ *measure*, /tʃ/ *chair*, /dʒ/ *jam*, /r/ *red* |
| palatal | front of the tongue and hard palate | /j/ *yes* |
| velar | back of the tongue and soft palate | /k/ *cat*, /ɡ/ *go*, /ŋ/ *sing* |
| glottal | between the vocal folds | /h/ *hat* |

/w/ (*wet*) uses two places at once: the lips are rounded and the back of the tongue rises towards the soft palate. It is called **labial-velar**.

The **manners** used in English:

| manner | what happens to the air | English examples |
|---|---|---|
| plosive (stop) | completely stopped, then let go suddenly | /p b t d k ɡ/ |
| fricative | forced through a narrow gap, which makes a hissing noise | /f v θ ð s z ʃ ʒ h/ |
| affricate | stopped, then let go slowly through a narrow gap | /tʃ dʒ/ |
| nasal | stopped in the mouth, but the soft palate is down and air goes out through the nose | /m n ŋ/ |
| approximant | the articulators come close, but not close enough to make noise | /l r w j/ |

/l/ is a special approximant: the tongue tip touches the alveolar ridge, but air goes out over the **sides** of the tongue. It is called a **lateral** ("side") approximant.

::: example Describing a consonant {#ex-describe-consonant}
Describe the first sound of *sun*, of *man* and of *go*.
::: solution
- *sun* /s/: the vocal folds do not vibrate (voiceless); the tongue tip is near the alveolar ridge (alveolar); the air is pushed through a narrow gap and hisses (fricative). So /s/ is a **voiceless alveolar fricative**.
- *man* /m/: you can hum on it (voiced); both lips are closed (bilabial); the air goes out through the nose (nasal). So /m/ is a **voiced bilabial nasal**.
- *go* /ɡ/: voiced; the back of the tongue touches the soft palate (velar); the air is stopped completely and then let go (plosive). So /ɡ/ is a **voiced velar plosive**.

Phoneticians give the three answers in this order: voice – place – manner.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sound is a **voiceless labiodental fricative**?
- [ ] /v/ as in *van*
- [x] /f/ as in *fan*
- [ ] /p/ as in *pan*
- [ ] /θ/ as in *thank*
::: solution
Labiodental means lower lip and top teeth: that is /f/ or /v/. Fricative means the air hisses through a narrow gap. Voiceless means no buzz: /f/. /v/ is the voiced partner; /p/ is bilabial and a plosive; /θ/ is dental (tongue and teeth).
:::
:::

### Describing a vowel

For a vowel, nothing touches anything. The tongue changes the shape of the space in the mouth, and the lips change the shape of its opening. Three questions describe a vowel.

::: theorem Three questions for a vowel {#rule-vowel-questions}
To describe a vowel, answer:

1. **Height:** how high is the tongue? A **close** vowel has the tongue high and the mouth nearly closed, as in *see*; an **open** vowel has the tongue low and the mouth open, as in *father*. Between them are **mid** vowels, as in *bed*.
2. **Front or back:** which part of the tongue is highest? For a **front** vowel it is the front (*see*, *bed*, *cat*); for a **back** vowel it is the back (*food*, *saw*, *hot*); for a **central** vowel the middle (the first sound of *about*).
3. **Lips:** are the lips **rounded** (pushed forward into a circle, as in *food* and *saw*) or **unrounded** (spread or relaxed, as in *see* and *cat*)?

Some vowels also change during the vowel (*my*, *now*, *boy*) and some are longer than others. These are the subjects of [[sounds-of-english/short-vowels]] and [[sounds-of-english/long-vowels-diphthongs]].
:::

::: proof
Height and front–back describe the highest point of the tongue, because that point decides the shape of the two main spaces in the mouth: the space in front of it and the space behind it. Each shape makes some harmonics of the voice stronger (see the figure above), and our ears hear the result as a particular vowel. Rounding the lips makes the front space longer, which changes the sound again. Real tongues do not move to fixed points: the labels describe areas, and every speaker's vowels are a little different. That is why a vowel chart is a guide, not an exact map.
:::

::: example Describing a vowel {#ex-describe-vowel}
Say *see*, then *saw*, then *sat*, and watch your mouth in a mirror. Describe the vowel in each word.
::: solution
- *see* /siː/: the mouth is nearly closed, the front of the tongue is high, and the lips are spread (like a smile). The vowel is **close, front, unrounded**.
- *saw* /sɔː/: the back of the tongue is raised a little, and the lips are rounded. The vowel is **mid, back, rounded** (in GB it is a little higher than mid; in GA a little lower).
- *sat* /sæt/: the jaw drops, the tongue is low and at the front, the lips are not rounded. The vowel is **open (or nearly open), front, unrounded**.

Going from *see* to *sat*, you can feel the jaw move down. Going from *see* to *saw*, you can feel the tongue pull back and the lips go round.
:::
:::

::: intuition The tongue as a hill
Think of the tongue as a hill inside the mouth. For *see* the top of the hill is high and near the front teeth. For *food* it is high but at the back. For *father* the whole hill is low and flat. Every vowel is a different position of the top of the hill, plus round or flat lips.
:::

## The nose and the soft palate

The soft palate is a door. When it goes **up**, it closes the way into the nose, and all the air goes out through the mouth. This is its position for almost all English sounds. When it goes **down**, air can go out through the nose.

For /m/, /n/ and /ŋ/ the mouth is closed at some point (at the lips for /m/, at the ridge for /n/, at the soft palate for /ŋ/), so the air can only go out through the nose. Try this: say a long *mmmm* and pinch your nose closed. The sound stops, because the air has nowhere to go. Now say a long *ssss* and pinch your nose. Nothing happens, because the air was going out through the mouth anyway.

::: example Finding the difference {#ex-find-difference}
What is the one difference between the first sounds of the words in each pair? (Use the three questions.)

1. *bat* – *mat*
2. *tie* – *sigh*
3. *sea* – *she*
::: solution
1. /b/ and /m/ are both voiced and bilabial. For /b/ the air is stopped and then let go (plosive); for /m/ it goes through the nose (nasal). The difference is **manner**: the soft palate is up for /b/ and down for /m/.
2. /t/ and /s/ are both voiceless and alveolar. For /t/ the tongue closes the way completely; for /s/ it leaves a narrow gap. The difference is **manner**: plosive against fricative.
3. /s/ and /ʃ/ are both voiceless fricatives. For /s/ the narrow gap is at the alveolar ridge; for /ʃ/ it is a little further back, the tongue is wider, and the lips are usually rounded. The difference is **place**: alveolar against postalveolar.
:::
:::

## A dialogue in class

A teacher and two students are practising the *v* sound. Read it aloud and feel each sound as you say it.

**Ms Okafor:** *Put your top teeth on your lower lip. Now blow.*

**Zhang Hao:** *Ffffff. Like this?*

**Ms Okafor:** *Good. That's /f/. Now do the same, but add your voice.*

**Zhang Hao:** *Vvvvvv. Oh, my lip is buzzing!*

**Ms Okafor:** *Exactly. Your vocal folds are vibrating. That's /v/. Now say "very".*

**Maria:** *Very. Very good!*

**Ms Okafor:** *Now try "wet" and "vet". For "wet", don't touch your teeth. Just round your lips.*

**Zhang Hao:** *Wet … vet. They feel completely different.*

**Ms Okafor:** *They are. One uses the lips and the teeth; the other uses round lips and no teeth at all.*

::: note English and Chinese
Mandarin uses the same organs of speech, and many sounds are made in the same places. But Mandarin and English use **voice** in different ways.

- In Mandarin, almost all consonants at the start of a syllable are voiceless. The voiced ones are *m*, *n*, *l* and *r* (and *ng* at the end of a syllable). Pinyin *b*, *d*, *g* are **not** voiced: they are voiceless sounds without a puff of air, and Pinyin *p*, *t*, *k* are voiceless with a puff of air. Mandarin keeps these pairs apart by the puff of air (aspiration), not by voice.
- English has voiced fricatives — /v/, /ð/, /z/, /ʒ/ — that standard Mandarin does not have as separate sounds. For a Mandarin speaker, the buzz of /z/ in *zoo* is new work for the vocal folds.
- Chinese speakers come from many language backgrounds. Speakers of Wu varieties, such as Shanghainese, already have voiced sounds such as /v/ and /z/ in their home language and often find *very* and *zoo* easy.

So the finger-on-the-throat test of this chapter is worth doing often. It lets you check, with your own body, whether you are making the buzz that English needs.
:::

::: history
People have described how speech is made for more than two thousand years: the ancient Indian grammarians of Sanskrit classified sounds by place and manner long before European scholars did. For English, a modern science of phonetics grew in the nineteenth century. In 1867 Alexander Melville Bell published *Visible Speech*, a set of symbols that drew the position of the speech organs. In 1877 Henry Sweet published *A Handbook of Phonetics*, which set out a careful system for describing sounds. In 1886 a group of language teachers led by Paul Passy founded the association that became the **International Phonetic Association**; its alphabet (the IPA), first published in 1888, is the source of the symbols you will learn in the next chapter.
:::

## Where this leads

The three questions for consonants (voice, place, manner) and for vowels (height, front–back, lips) are used in every chapter of this course. [[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet]] gives a symbol to each English sound. [[sounds-of-english/short-vowels]] and [[sounds-of-english/long-vowels-diphthongs]] go through the vowels; [[sounds-of-english/stops-fricatives]] and [[sounds-of-english/nasals-approximants]] go through the consonants. Readers who want the full science, including sounds of other languages, will find it in [[linguistics/phonetics-phonology]].

::: summary
- Speech is made with air from the lungs, shaped by the **articulators**: lips, tongue and soft palate move; teeth, alveolar ridge and hard palate stay still ([[#def-articulator]]).
- A sound is **voiced** if the vocal folds vibrate and **voiceless** if they do not; you can feel the difference with your fingers on your throat ([[#def-voicing]]).
- Many English consonants come in voiceless–voiced pairs: /p b/, /t d/, /k ɡ/, /f v/, /θ ð/, /s z/, /ʃ ʒ/, /tʃ dʒ/ ([[#rule-pairs]]).
- Vowels and consonants are **sounds**, not letters: *university* begins with the consonant sound /j/, so we say *a university*.
- A consonant is described by **voice, place and manner** ([[#rule-voice-place-manner]]); a vowel by **height, front–back and lip rounding** ([[#rule-vowel-questions]]).
- When the soft palate goes down, air goes through the nose: /m/, /n/ and /ŋ/ are the nasal sounds of English.
- Mandarin keeps *b*/*p*, *d*/*t*, *g*/*k* apart by a puff of air, not by voice, and has no /v/, /ð/, /z/ or /ʒ/ as separate sounds; for many Chinese speakers the buzz of English voiced consonants needs practice.
:::

::: exercise Explain a mistake {level=3}
A learner says *wine* when she wants to say *vine*, and *west* when she wants to say *vest*. Using the three questions for consonants, explain the difference between /w/ and /v/, and write three short instructions she can follow to make /v/.
::: hint

:::
::: solution
Both /w/ and /v/ are voiced, so voice is not the problem. They differ in **place** and **manner**:
- /w/ is labial-velar: the lips are rounded and do not touch the teeth; the back of the tongue rises. It is an approximant, so there is no noise of friction.
- /v/ is labiodental: the top teeth touch the lower lip. It is a fricative, so air is pushed through the narrow gap and makes a buzzing noise.

Instructions (any similar set is correct):
1. Put your top teeth lightly on the inside of your lower lip. Do not round your lips.
2. Blow, to make /f/ (*ffff*).
3. Keep blowing and add your voice, so that your lip tickles: *vvvv*. Then open into the vowel: *vvv-ine*.

She can check herself in a mirror: for /v/ the top teeth should be visible on the lip; for /w/ the lips form a small circle.

::: exercise Speaking task: a sound diary {level=3}
Choose five English words that you find hard to say. For each, write the word, find the hardest sound in it, and describe that sound with the three questions (or, for a vowel, with height, front–back and lips). Then say each word slowly five times, checking one thing each time (for example, "Is my lip touching my teeth?").
::: solution
Answers will differ. A model entry:

- *clothes* — hardest sound: /ð/ before the final /z/. /ð/ is a voiced dental fricative: the tongue tip is lightly between or behind the top teeth, air passes through a narrow gap, and the vocal folds vibrate. Check 1: is my tongue touching my teeth? Check 2: can I feel the buzz? Check 3: is the buzz still there for the /z/ at the end?

A good entry names the sound correctly, gives all three labels, and turns each label into something you can check in a mirror or with your fingers.
