Wang Lei is in a café in London. He asks for "a cap of coffee", and the waiter looks confused for a moment before he understands: *cup*, not *cap*. The next day a classmate tells him she has a new "bad" in her room, and he wonders why anyone would buy a bad thing — she meant *bed*. Neither speaker made a grammar mistake. Each changed one short vowel, and changed the word.

English has more vowels than most languages: in GB, twelve single vowels and eight diphthongs. Six of the single vowels are short and appear only before a consonant: the vowels of *ship*, *bed*, *cat*, *cup*, *hot* and *book*. A seventh short vowel, schwa, is the most common vowel of all, but it is never stressed. This chapter teaches all seven: how to make them, how they are spelled, how they differ in GB and GA, and which neighbours they are most often confused with.

The symbols and keywords are those of [[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet]]; the words for describing vowels (close, open, front, back, rounded) come from [[sounds-of-english/how-speech-is-made]].

## What makes a vowel short

Say *bit* and then *beat*. The vowel of *beat* takes longer. But listen and feel more carefully: the two vowels are also made in different places. For *beat* the tongue is high and tense, close to the hard palate. For *bit* it is a little lower, a little further back, and relaxed. If you say *beat* very fast, it still sounds like *beat*, not like *bit*.

::: definition Short vowels {#def-short-vowel}
The **short vowels** of English are the vowels of the KIT, DRESS, TRAP, STRUT, LOT and FOOT sets, plus schwa: in GB /ɪ e æ ʌ ɒ ʊ ə/. They are shorter than the long vowels and diphthongs in the same position, and they are also made with the tongue in a different, usually more central and more relaxed, position. In GA the LOT vowel is the long /ɑː/, so GA has five stressed short vowels: /ɪ e æ ʌ ʊ/.
:::

The six stressed short vowels share a surprising rule.

::: theorem Short vowels need a consonant after them {#rule-checked}
In English words, the stressed short vowels /ɪ e æ ʌ ɒ ʊ/ are followed by a consonant. No English word ends in a stressed /ɪ/, /e/, /æ/, /ʌ/, /ɒ/ or /ʊ/. Long vowels and diphthongs can end a word: *see*, *car*, *saw*, *two*, *her*, *day*, *go*, *my*.
:::

::: proof
This is a fact about the patterns of English, not about the mouth: there is nothing in the tongue that stops a short vowel at the end of a word, and other languages end words with similar vowels. Phonologists call the short vowels **checked** (they must be "checked", closed off, by a consonant) and the others **free**. The rule explains a spelling habit too: a single vowel letter at the end of a short word is usually long (*he*, *go*, *my*), while the short vowels appear in closed spellings (*hit*, *hot*, *hut*). Schwa is not covered by the rule, because it is never stressed; it ends many words (*sofa*, *China*, GB *teacher*).
:::

## The six stressed short vowels

### KIT /ɪ/

**How to make it:** the front of the tongue is high, but lower than for FLEECE /iː/; the lips are relaxed, not spread; the jaw is a little open. It is short and relaxed.

**Spellings:** usually *i* (*sit*, *bridge*, *milk*); also *y* (*gym*, *myth*), and some single words: *women* /ˈwɪmɪn/, *busy* /ˈbɪzi/, *build* /bɪld/, *pretty* /ˈprɪti/, *English* /ˈɪŋɡlɪʃ/.

**Minimal pairs with FLEECE:** *ship – sheep*, *bit – beat*, *live – leave*, *fill – feel*, *hit – heat*, *sit – seat*.

- *Can you fill this bottle for me?*
- *I feel much better today.*
- *We live near the station.*
- *We leave at six.*

### DRESS /e/

**How to make it:** the front of the tongue is at middle height; the jaw is more open than for KIT; the lips are relaxed.

**Spellings:** usually *e* (*bed*, *red*, *went*); also *ea* (*head*, *bread*, *weather*), and *said* /sed/, *says* /sez/, *any* /ˈeni/, *many* /ˈmeni/, *friend* /frend/, *leisure* GB /ˈleʒə/.

**Minimal pairs with TRAP:** *bed – bad*, *men – man*, *pen – pan*, *said – sad*, *set – sat*, *send – sand*.

- *There are three men in the photo.*
- *There's one man at the door.*
- *She said nothing.*
- *She looked sad.*

### TRAP /æ/

**How to make it:** the jaw is open, the front of the tongue is low, the lips are spread a little. In modern GB the vowel is quite open, close to the *a* of many European languages; in GA it is often a little higher, and before /m/ and /n/ (*man*, *hand*) many Americans glide it towards [ɛə].

**Spellings:** almost always *a* (*cat*, *hand*, *apple*, *family*); *plaid* /plæd/ is a rare exception.

**Minimal pairs with STRUT:** *cat – cut*, *hat – hut*, *bag – bug*, *cap – cup*, *ran – run*, *match – much*.

- *I ran to the bus stop.*
- *I run every morning.*
- *There's a bug in my bag.*

### STRUT /ʌ/

**How to make it:** the tongue is low-to-mid and central — between TRAP and LOT; the lips are relaxed and not rounded; the jaw is a little open. In GB it is close to the open central vowel; in GA it is a little further back.

**Spellings:** *u* (*cup*, *bus*, *sun*); *o* in many common words (*love*, *son*, *come*, *some*, *mother*, *money*, *month*, *other*, *wonder*); *ou* (*young*, *country*, *touch*, *double*, *enough*); and *blood*, *flood*, *does*.

**Minimal pairs with TRAP and LOT:** *cup – cap*, *much – match*; *cut – cot*, *luck – lock* (GB), *nut – not*.

- *Could I have a cup of tea?*
- *She's wearing a red cap.*
- *Don't forget to lock the door.*
- *Good luck with your exam!*

### LOT /ɒ/ (GB), /ɑː/ (GA)

**How to make it (GB):** the back of the tongue is low; the lips are slightly rounded; the jaw is open. **GA:** the vowel is long and unrounded, the same as in *father*: *hot* /hɑːt/, *stop* /stɑːp/.

**Spellings:** usually *o* (*hot*, *stop*, *dog*, *box*); *a* after *w* or *qu* (*want*, *watch*, *wash*, *swan*, *quality*); and *knowledge* /ˈnɒlɪdʒ/, *because* (GB /bɪˈkɒz/, often also /bɪˈkəz/).

**Minimal pairs with STRUT (GB):** *not – nut*, *cot – cut*, *lock – luck*, *shot – shut*, *boss – bus*.

- *It's not hot today.*
- *Please shut the door.*
- *My boss takes the bus to work.*

### FOOT /ʊ/

**How to make it:** the back of the tongue is high, but lower and more relaxed than for GOOSE /uː/; the lips are only a little rounded.

**Spellings:** *oo* (*book*, *good*, *foot*, *look*, *wood*); *u* (*put*, *push*, *pull*, *full*, *sugar*, *butcher*); *ou* (*could*, *would*, *should*); *woman* /ˈwʊmən/, *wolf*.

**Minimal pairs with GOOSE:** *full – fool*, *pull – pool*, *look – Luke*, *should – shoed* (rare), *wood – wooed* (rare). The pair *full – fool* is the most useful.

- *The bus is full.*
- *Don't be a fool.*
- *Pull the door; don't push it.*
- *We swam in the pool.*

::: warning *-oo-* is not one sound
✗ Reading every *oo* as /uː/: *book* /buːk/, *good* /ɡuːd/, *foot* /fuːt/.
✓ *book* /bʊk/, *good* /ɡʊd/, *foot* /fʊt/, *look* /lʊk/, *cook* /kʊk/, *wood* /wʊd/, *wool* /wʊl/ — but *food* /fuːd/, *moon* /muːn/, *school* /skuːl/, *room* /ruːm/, *too* /tuː/.

There is no reliable spelling rule. Many words with *-ook* have /ʊ/; the others must be learned word by word. When in doubt, check a dictionary. (A few words, such as *room* and *roof*, are said with either vowel by different speakers.)
:::

::: example One letter, many vowels {#ex-letter-o}
The letter *o* is the stressed vowel letter in *son*, *hot*, *go*, *move*, *woman* and *women*. Which vowel does it stand for in each word?
::: solution
Check each word in a dictionary, or compare it with a word you are sure of:

1. *son* /sʌn/: STRUT — it sounds exactly like *sun*.
2. *hot* /hɒt/ (GB), /hɑːt/ (GA): LOT.
3. *go* /ɡəʊ/ (GB), /ɡoʊ/ (GA): GOAT, a diphthong; *o* at the end of a word is never short ([[#rule-checked]]).
4. *move* /muːv/: GOOSE, a long vowel.
5. *woman* /ˈwʊmən/: FOOT in the first syllable.
6. *women* /ˈwɪmɪn/: KIT in the first syllable — the plural changes the vowel, although the spelling changes only in the second syllable.

One letter gives six different vowels. This is why learners need transcriptions and why minimal pairs are better practice than spelling rules alone.
:::
:::

::: example Finding the short vowels in a sentence {#ex-identify}
Find each stressed short vowel in the sentence and name its lexical set: *My mother put the box on the bed.*
::: solution
Go word by word and ignore the spelling:

- *My* /maɪ/: PRICE — a diphthong, not short.
- *mother* /ˈmʌðə/ (GB), /ˈmʌðər/ (GA): the stressed vowel is STRUT /ʌ/, spelled *o*.
- *put* /pʊt/: FOOT /ʊ/, spelled *u*.
- *the*: an unstressed function word with schwa.
- *box* /bɒks/ (GB), /bɑːks/ (GA): LOT; short /ɒ/ in GB, long /ɑː/ in GA.
- *on* /ɒn/ (GB), /ɑːn/ (GA): LOT again.
- *bed* /bed/: DRESS /e/.

So the stressed short vowels are STRUT (*mother*), FOOT (*put*), LOT (*box*, *on* — short in GB only) and DRESS (*bed*). The spellings *o* in *mother* and *o* in *box* stand for different vowels.
:::
:::

::: example Choosing the right word {#ex-choose}
Which word fits each sentence? Give its vowel.

1. *She gave me a ___ of tea.* (cap / cup)
2. *Turn ___ at the next corner.* (left / laughed)
3. *I need a new ___ for my bike.* (lock / luck)
4. *The ___ were all asleep.* (men / man)
::: solution
1. *cup* /kʌp/, STRUT. A *cap* /kæp/ (TRAP) is a hat.
2. *left* /left/, DRESS. *Laughed* is GB /lɑːft/, GA /læft/: in GA it is a near minimal pair with *left*, differing in DRESS and TRAP.
3. *lock* /lɒk/ (GB), LOT. *Luck* /lʌk/ (STRUT) is good or bad fortune.
4. *men* /men/, DRESS: the plural is needed after *were all*. *Man* /mæn/ (TRAP) is singular.

In every item the grammar or the meaning tells you which word it is — but a listener needs the vowel to know which word **you** said.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which word has the **same** stressed vowel as *love*?
- [ ] *move*
- [x] *come*
- [ ] *cove*
- [ ] *stove*
::: solution
*Love* /lʌv/ has the STRUT vowel, and so does *come* /kʌm/. *Move* /muːv/ has GOOSE, and *cove* and *stove* have GOAT (/kəʊv/, /stəʊv/ in GB). The spelling *-ove* is the same in all five words; the vowel is not.
:::
:::

## Schwa: the weak vowel

Say *banana* slowly, as you would say it in a sentence. You will probably hear three different vowels, not three of the same: /bəˈnɑːnə/ (GB), /bəˈnænə/ (GA). The first and last vowels are short, quiet and central. This is **schwa**, written /ə/.

::: definition Schwa {#def-schwa}
**Schwa** /ə/ is a short, central, relaxed vowel: the tongue is in the middle of the mouth, neither high nor low, neither front nor back, and the lips are relaxed. It is the most common vowel in English. It appears only in **unstressed** syllables, and it can be spelled with any vowel letter: *about* /əˈbaʊt/, *taken* /ˈteɪkən/, *pencil* /ˈpensl/ or /ˈpensəl/, *lemon* /ˈlemən/, *support* /səˈpɔːt/ (GB).
:::

::: theorem Unstressed syllables are often reduced {#rule-reduction}
In many unstressed syllables, English speakers do not say the "full" vowel that the spelling suggests. They say schwa, or (especially before a consonant at the end of a syllable) the short /ɪ/ of KIT. This is the normal pronunciation, not a careless one:

- *a*: *about* /əˈbaʊt/, *banana* /bəˈnɑːnə/ (GB)
- *e*: *problem* /ˈprɒbləm/ (GB), *moment* /ˈməʊmənt/ (GB)
- *o*: *police* /pəˈliːs/, *second* /ˈsekənd/
- *u*: *support* /səˈpɔːt/ (GB), *success* /səkˈses/
- *er*, *or*, *ar* at the end of a word: *teacher* GB /ˈtiːtʃə/, GA /ˈtiːtʃər/; *doctor*, *sugar*

Many short **function words** — *a*, *the*, *to*, *of*, *and*, *for*, *can*, *was* — also have weak forms with schwa when they are not stressed: *a cup of tea* /ə ˈkʌp əv ˈtiː/.
:::

::: proof
English is a stress language: the stressed syllables are long, loud and clear, and the unstressed ones are short and quiet. A short, quiet syllable has no time for the tongue to reach the edges of the mouth, so its vowel moves towards the centre — and the vowel of the centre is schwa. Dictionaries agree on the main cases, but they differ in detail: some unstressed vowels can be /ə/ or /ɪ/ (*pocket* /ˈpɒkɪt/, also /ˈpɒkət/ in GA), and slow, careful speech sometimes uses a fuller vowel. The weak forms of function words are taught in [[connected-speech/weak-forms]].
:::

::: example Finding schwas {#ex-schwa}
Mark the stressed syllable and find every schwa: *photograph*, *photographer*, *Canada*, *computer*.
::: solution
Look up (or work out) the stress first, then reduce the unstressed vowels.

1. *photograph* /ˈfəʊtəɡrɑːf/ (GB), /ˈfoʊtəɡræf/ (GA): stress on *pho*; schwa in *-to-*. The last syllable keeps a full vowel.
2. *photographer* /fəˈtɒɡrəfə/ (GB), /fəˈtɑːɡrəfər/ (GA): stress on *-tog-*; schwas in *pho-*, *-ra-* and (GB) *-pher*. The stress has moved, so the first syllable, a full vowel in *photograph*, is now schwa.
3. *Canada* /ˈkænədə/: stress on *Ca-*; two schwas.
4. *computer* /kəmˈpjuːtə/ (GB), /kəmˈpjuːtər/ (GA): stress on *-pu-*; schwa in *com-* and (GB) *-ter*.

In these four words, schwa is spelled *o*, *a*, *e* and *er*: the letter tells you nothing; the stress tells you a lot.
:::
:::

::: warning Do not give every syllable a full vowel
✗ /bɑːˈnɑːnɑː/ — three full vowels make *banana* sound like three separate stressed words.
✓ /bəˈnɑːnə/ (GB), /bəˈnænə/ (GA).

Reading each letter with its "full" value is one of the strongest signs of a foreign accent, and it makes the stressed syllable harder to hear, because every syllable sounds equally important. Make the unstressed vowels short and quiet, and let the stressed one stand out.
:::

## A dialogue at the café

Read it aloud, making the short vowels clearly different. The stressed short vowels are listed after each line.

**Waiter:** *Hi. What can I get you?* (LOT in *what* in GB, often STRUT in GA; DRESS in *get*; *can* is unstressed and weak, /kən/)

**Wang Lei:** *Could I have a cup of coffee, please?* (FOOT in *could*; STRUT in *cup*; LOT in *coffee*, GB)

**Waiter:** *Sure. Milk?* (KIT in *milk*)

**Wang Lei:** *Just a bit, thanks.* (STRUT in *just*; KIT in *bit*; TRAP in *thanks*)

**Waiter:** *Anything to eat? The lemon cake is good.* (DRESS in *any-*; DRESS in *lemon*, with schwa in *-on*; FOOT in *good*)

**Wang Lei:** *Then I'll have a lemon cake as well.* (DRESS in *then*, *lemon*, *well*)

::: note English and Chinese
- **No length contrast in Mandarin.** Mandarin vowels are not long or short in a way that changes meaning, so Mandarin speakers often hear KIT and FLEECE, or FOOT and GOOSE, as one vowel. Do not rely on length alone: practise the different tongue positions ([[#def-short-vowel]]).
- **Pinyin *i* and *u*.** The Pinyin *i* of *yī* (一) is close to FLEECE /iː/, not KIT; the Pinyin *u* of *wǔ* (五) is close to GOOSE /uː/, not FOOT. Learners who map English *i* and *u* onto Pinyin say *ship* like *sheep* and *full* like *fool*.
- **TRAP, DRESS and STRUT.** Mandarin has one low vowel phoneme, written *a*, so the three-way contrast of *bad*, *bed* and *bud* is new for many speakers. DRESS is close to the vowel of Pinyin *ye* (耶) or *yan* (烟) in many speakers' Mandarin; TRAP needs a wider-open jaw; STRUT is shorter and more central than Pinyin *a*.
- **Pinyin *e* is not DRESS.** The *e* of *hē* (喝) and *è* (饿) is a back, unrounded vowel. It is nearer to an English schwa held for a long time than to the vowel of *bed*.
- **Schwa** is easy to make — Mandarin has a schwa-like vowel in syllables such as *en* (恩) — but hard to **use**, because Mandarin does not reduce the vowels of unstressed syllables in the same way. Most syllables in Mandarin keep their full vowel and tone, except the neutral-tone syllables, so it can feel strange to "weaken" a syllable on purpose.

Learners whose first language is Cantonese or another southern variety meet a different set of problems, because those varieties have their own vowel contrasts; the final chapter, [[sounds-of-english/sounds-for-chinese-speakers]], looks at them together.
:::

::: history
In the English of Chaucer's time (the late fourteenth century), *cut* and *put* had the same short vowel, like the vowel of *put* today. In the south of England during the seventeenth century this vowel split in two: in most words it became the new vowel of STRUT (*cut*, *sun*, *love*), while some words, mostly after lip sounds such as /p/, /b/, /f/ and /w/, kept the old vowel (*put*, *pull*, *full*, *wolf*). This is called the **FOOT–STRUT split**. It did not happen in the north of England, where many speakers still say *cup* and *put* with the same vowel today — a reminder that GB and GA are two models among many accents. The name *schwa* comes from the Hebrew grammatical term *shva*, the name of a vowel sign.
:::

## Where this leads

The long vowels and the diphthongs, the "free" partners of the short vowels, are the subject of [[sounds-of-english/long-vowels-diphthongs]]. Schwa returns in [[sounds-of-english/word-stress-basics]], because unstressed vowels are reduced, and it is central to [[connected-speech/weak-forms]] and [[connected-speech/rhythm]]. How the short vowels are spelled, and why, is part of [[sounds-of-english/spelling-and-sound]].

::: summary
- The short vowels are KIT /ɪ/, DRESS /e/, TRAP /æ/, STRUT /ʌ/, LOT /ɒ/ (GB; GA /ɑː/), FOOT /ʊ/ and schwa /ə/ ([[#def-short-vowel]]).
- Short vowels differ from their long neighbours in **position** as well as length: KIT is lower and more relaxed than FLEECE, FOOT than GOOSE.
- A stressed short vowel is always followed by a consonant; no English word ends in a stressed short vowel ([[#rule-checked]]).
- The most useful minimal pairs: *ship – sheep*, *bed – bad*, *cat – cut*, *cut – cot* (GB), *full – fool*.
- Spelling is a poor guide: *o* can be STRUT (*love*), LOT (*hot*) or GOAT (*go*); *oo* can be FOOT (*book*) or GOOSE (*food*).
- **Schwa** is the most common vowel in English; it appears only in unstressed syllables and can be spelled with any vowel letter ([[#def-schwa]], [[#rule-reduction]]).
- Mandarin has no vowel-length contrast and only one low vowel phoneme, so KIT–FLEECE, FOOT–GOOSE and TRAP–DRESS–STRUT need deliberate practice.
:::

::: exercise Write minimal-pair sentences {level=3}
Write four pairs of short sentences that are the same except for one word, where the two words form a minimal pair with different short vowels (for example *I need a pen.* / *I need a pan.*). Use four different pairs of vowels. Then read each pair aloud to a partner, in a random order, and ask them which sentence they heard.
::: solution
Answers will differ. A model set, with four different pairs of vowels:

1. KIT–FLEECE: *She bit it.* / *She beat it.*
2. DRESS–TRAP: *I need a pen.* / *I need a pan.*
3. TRAP–STRUT: *Look at that cat.* / *Look at that cut.*
4. FOOT–GOOSE: *Look, it's here!* / *Luke, it's here!*

Check your own pairs: only **one** sound may change, and both sentences should make sense. (*The men are here.* / *The man is here.* does not work, because *are* and *is* change too.) If your partner chooses correctly at least four times out of five for a pair, that contrast is clear; if not, it is the one to practise.
:::
:::

::: exercise A short text {level=3}
Read this text aloud. Then copy it and underline every stressed short vowel, writing its lexical set above it.

> *Ben and his sister Lin run a small shop in the middle of the town. It sells good books, cheap cups and maps. On Sundays it is shut, and they go to the hills.*
::: solution
Stressed short vowels (one possible reading; *and*, *his*, *a*, *of*, *the*, *it*, *is*, *to* are usually unstressed and weak):

- *Ben* DRESS; *sister* KIT; *Lin* KIT; *run* STRUT; *shop* LOT (GB); *sells* DRESS; *middle* KIT; *good* FOOT; *books* FOOT; *cups* STRUT; *maps* TRAP; *Sundays* STRUT; *shut* STRUT; *hills* KIT.
- Stressed but not short: *small* (THOUGHT), *town* (MOUTH), *cheap* (FLEECE), *they* (FACE), *go* (GOAT).

Learners who underlined *cheap* or *small* have confused short and long vowels; compare them with *chip* and *shop*.
