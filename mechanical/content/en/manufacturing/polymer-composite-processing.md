Polymers are the largest-volume engineering materials on the planet and the ones mechanical engineers understand least, because everything they were taught about metals stops being true at the first sentence of polymer physics. **A polymer part is not cast, forged or machined: it is poured, stretched, blown or squeezed into a shape at a pressure of 1000 atmospheres and a temperature 200 degrees above anything a metal sees, and — this is the part that breaks the intuition — the properties of the finished part are not the properties of the material. They are the properties of the material *as left by the process*.** Two injection mouldings of the same polypropylene, one moulded at a mould temperature of $2 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ and one at $7 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ , differ by 15 % in stiffness, 40 % in impact strength, 30 % in shrinkage and completely in surface gloss, and both are "in specification" for the resin. A polyethylene bottle that was stretched 3.5 times one way and 2.5 the other is ten times tougher than the same preform that was not stretched at all. A carbon-fibre wing skin with a 5° wrinkle in one ply has half the compression strength of the identical skin without the wrinkle, and nothing about its weight, its dimensions or its ultrasonic trace tells you which one you have.

That is the theme of this lesson. **Metal processing changes the shape; polymer and composite processing changes the shape *and* the microstructure *and* the fibre architecture *and* the residual stress, all in the same thirty seconds, and all of them are controlled by the same handful of temperatures, pressures and times that the machine operator can see.** The consequence for a designer is that a moulded part must be designed *with* its mould, and a laminated part must be designed *with* its ply book, its tooling and its inspection method — and that the drawing of a moulded part is a legal document about a process, not just about a geometry.

::: warning Nine ways polymer and composite processing goes wrong
**Treating a polymer melt as a liquid with a viscosity.** A polymer melt is *shear thinning* (its viscosity falls by a factor of 30-100 between the barrel and the gate), *elastic* (it remembers its shape and swells out of the die), *time-dependent* (its relaxation time is 0.01-10 s, comparable to every process time, so its Deborah number is never much below 1) and *extremely temperature sensitive* (a 20 °C change moves the viscosity by a factor of two). **A single "viscosity" number on a datasheet — the melt flow index — is one point on that surface, measured at one shear rate, and it is a quality-control number, not a design number.** **Designing a moulding with a non-uniform wall.** The thick bit is where the sink mark, the void, the warp and the eight-second cycle come from. **Uniform wall thickness, and where that is impossible, a gradual transition no steeper than 3:1, is the first and most valuable rule of moulding design.** **Ignoring the shrinkage difference between "with the flow" and "across it".** An unreinforced amorphous part shrinks 0.4-0.7 %; a semi-crystalline one 1.5-3 %; a 30 % glass-reinforced nylon 0.3 % along the flow and 0.9 % across it, and **that anisotropy is what makes a 200 mm reinforced rib curl out of flat by 2 mm.** **Putting a metal insert in without thinking about the polymer's thermal expansion.** Polyamide expands at $8 0 \times 1 0 ^ { - 6 } \ \mathrm { K ^ { - 1 } }$ , brass at 19. A moulded-in brass bush in a nylon housing that is cycled from $- 3 0$ to $+ 9 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ has an 11 % change in its grip and, at the cold end, a loose bush. **Designing a composite part as a metal part with plies.** A laminate is not "plastic with fibre in it": it is a **stack of anisotropic layers whose in-plane properties you choose and whose through-thickness properties you do not control**, and 80 % of composite failures are **delaminations and fibre wrinkles**, not fibre breaks. **Assuming the hand lay-up's 30 % fibre fraction is the same material as the prepreg's 60 %.** It is not: the stiffness in the fibre direction is roughly proportional to the fibre volume, so the hand lay-up is at half the stiffness and a third of the compression strength, at twice the weight. **Infusing fast to save cycle time.** The resin runs down the paths of least resistance — the 1000-times-more-permeable gaps *between* the tows — and the resin *inside* each tow, whose permeability is $1 0 ^ { - 1 5 } \ \mathrm { m ^ { 2 } }$ , never fills. **You get a part that is 100 % wetted on the flow front and 2 % void inside every bundle, and it fails in compression at half the allowables.** **Curing a thermoset to a schedule instead of to a state of cure.** The degree of cure is what sets the glass transition, and the glass transition is what sets the maximum service temperature and the chemical resistance; a "5 minute" cure at a mould 15 °C cold is a part that fails hot-water immersion. **Trying to make a composite part cheap by making it big and hand-laid.** The labour is 60-70 % of a low-volume composite part's cost, and the only ways out are fewer parts, larger tools, faster resin, automated placement and — the one that actually works at volume — a shorter cure.
:::

## 1. What a polymer is when it is being processed

::: definition The three families, and the two temperatures that decide everything {#def-families}
Every polymer process is a negotiation between two temperatures and one pressure. **$T _ { \mathrm { g } }$ , the glass transition**, is where the amorphous chains stop being able to slide past each other cooperatively: below it the material is a rigid, brittle glass with a modulus of 2-3.5 GPa; 20 °C above it, a rubbery, leathery, tough solid at 10-100 MPa. **$T _ { \mathrm { m } }$ , the melting point**, exists only for the chains regular enough to pack into crystals, and it is where those crystals give up: polyethylene at $1 3 5 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ , polypropylene at $1 6 5 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ , nylon 6,6 at $2 6 5 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ , PEEK at $3 4 3 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ . **Processing happens at $T _ { \mathrm { m } } + 3 0$ to $+ 8 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ for a semi-crystalline polymer and at $T _ { \mathrm { g } } + 1 0 0$ to $+ 1 5 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ for an amorphous one, and the whole of the process window is between "flows" and "degrades" — which for polyoxymethylene or plasticised PVC is a window of 15 °C, and is the reason those two materials are difficult.**

$$
\begin{array} { l c c c l l }
& T _ { \mathrm { g } } \ [ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ] & T _ { \mathrm { m } } \ [ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ] & \text{Melt} \ [ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ] & \text{Decomposes} \ [ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ] & \text{Window} \\ \hline
\text{LDPE} & - 1 1 0 & 1 1 0 & 1 5 0 { - } 2 6 0 & 3 0 0 & \text{wide} \\
\text{PP (homo)} & 0 & 1 6 5 & 2 0 0 { - } 2 7 0 & 3 0 0 & \text{wide} \\
\text{ABS} & 1 0 5 & - & 2 2 0 { - } 2 6 0 & 2 8 0 & \text{medium} \\
\text{PA 6} & 5 0 & 2 2 0 & 2 5 0 { - } 2 9 0 & 3 1 0 & \text{medium} \\
\text{PC} & 1 4 7 & - & 2 9 0 { - } 3 3 0 & 3 6 0 & \text{medium} \\
\text{POM (acetal)} & - 5 0 & 1 7 5 & 1 8 5 { - } 2 0 5 & 2 1 5 & \text{narrow} \\
\text{PVC (rigid)} & 8 2 & - & 1 6 0 { - } 1 9 0 & 2 0 0 & \text{narrow} \\
\text{PEEK} & 1 4 3 & 3 4 3 & 3 7 0 { - } 4 0 0 & 5 0 0 & \text{wide, expensive}
\end{array}
$$

The three families sort themselves by what they do when you heat them, and the family decides the process. **Thermoplastics** (85 % of everything moulded: PP, PE, PS, PVC, PA, PC, POM, PET, and the engineering ones PBT, PPS, PAI, PEEK) are linear or branched chains held together by secondary bonds only, so they melt, flow and solidify reversibly — and can be re-melted, which is what makes them recyclable and what limits their maximum service temperature to something well below $T _ { \mathrm { g } }$ . **Thermosets** (epoxy, phenolic, unsaturated polyester, melamine, polyurethane, silicone) start as a low-viscosity pre-polymer and *react* into an infinite network: they cannot be re-melted, they char instead, the reaction is usually irreversible and gives off heat and sometimes volatiles, and — the crucial processing fact — **the viscosity of a thermoset falls as it heats and then rises for ever as it gels, so its processing window is a race against its own chemistry.** **Elastomers** are lightly crosslinked (1-3 mol % of the chains) so that they can be stretched 300-700 % and spring back; they are shaped uncrosslinked and crosslinked afterwards, which is why rubber processing is a story about *scorch* (an accidental early cure) and *undercure*.

**The microstructure a polymer process leaves behind is a four-layer record of its own thermal history**, and it is not what the datasheet describes: a **frozen skin** of oriented or glassy material, a few tens of microns thick, made in the first half-second against a $4 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ steel wall and having the properties of a material nobody specified; a **shear-oriented layer** where the fountain flow laid the chains and the fibres along the flow direction; a **transverse-core** where the material was stationary, cool and slow, with fibres standing on end; and, in a semi-crystalline part, a **spherulitic structure whose size and whose degree of crystallinity were set by the cooling rate** — $5 { - } 3 0 \ \ \%$ crystallinity quenched against a cold mould against $4 5 { - } 5 5 \ \ \%$ cooled slowly, with 10 % more shrinkage, 30 % more stiffness, worse impact and a completely different chemical resistance in between. **This is why "moulding grade" and "extrusion grade" of the same resin are different products, why a moulding annealed before use changes its dimensions, and why a semi-crystalline part must never be machined from a slowly-cooled bar and expected to stay flat.**
:::

::: definition Why a melt is nothing like a liquid {#def-melt}
A polymer melt is a metre-long spaghetti strand, ten thousand monomers long, entangled with every neighbour, and the entanglements are temporary knots that slip free in a time of $1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ to 10 s. Four consequences dominate every process:

**Shear thinning.** Above the entanglement molecular weight the viscosity goes as $M _ { w } ^ { \, 3 . 4 }$ , and under shear the coils align and the knots slip out, so the viscosity falls with shear rate as a **power law**,

$$
\tau = K \, { \dot { \gamma } } ^ { n } \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \eta _ { \mathrm { app } } = K \, { \dot { \gamma } } ^ { \, n - 1 } , \qquad n = 0 . 2 { - } 0 . 8
$$

with $n = 1$ being the water that never happens. **A polypropylene at $2 3 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ has an apparent viscosity of $1 0 0 0 \ \mathrm { P a \, s }$ at the screw's $1 0 0 \ \mathrm { s ^ { - 1 } }$ and $2 2 \ \mathrm { P a \, s }$ at the gate's $1 0 ^ { 5 } \ \mathrm { s ^ { - 1 } }$ — a factor of 45.** **This is the single most useful fact in polymer processing: the melt thins exactly where you need it to flow fastest, which is why a part with a 0.5 mm wall can be filled at all, and it is also why the pressure-drop calculation you learned for water is wrong by two orders of magnitude.** Long-chain branching makes $n$ smaller (a "broad" melt that flows easily in the gate but holds its melt strength in a parison); narrow distribution and low molecular weight make it larger.

**Temperature dependence.** Flow is thermally activated, with an activation energy of $2 0 { - } 8 0 \ \mathrm { k J / m o l }$ , so $\eta \propto \exp ( E _ { \eta } / R T )$ and

$$
{ \frac { \eta ( T _ { 1 } ) } { \eta ( T _ { 2 } ) } } \; = \; \exp \! \left[ { \frac { E _ { \eta } } { R } } \left( { \frac { 1 } { T _ { 1 } } } - { \frac { 1 } { T _ { 2 } } } \right) \right] \; \approx \; 2 \quad \text{for a } 2 0 { - } 3 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C } \text{ change}
$$

**which means barrel temperature is a viscosity control before it is a temperature control, and it is why "the melt is too cold" and "the shot is short" are the same sentence.** It is also why a polymer's processing window is so narrow: the viscosity you need at the end of the flow path is set by geometry, and if the melt cools 20 °C more than you thought, it doubles the viscosity and freezes the last 20 mm of the part.

**Elastic memory.** The same entanglements that thin the melt store energy, and they release it when the stress goes. A melt extruded from a die **swells** to 1.2-2.0 times the die diameter; a melt in a corner pushes back; a melt stirred by a rod climbs the rod (the Weissenberg effect); and a moulded part that was filled under $8 0 \ \mathrm { M P a }$ in half a second contains a frozen-in stress of several MPa that will spend the next week pulling the part out of shape. **The dimensionless group that decides whether any of this matters is the Deborah number $\mathrm { D e } = \lambda / t _ { \mathrm { process } }$ , the relaxation time over the process time — and for polymers it is between 0.01 and 10 for essentially every process ever invented.** **Metals and water have a Deborah number of $1 0 ^ { - 1 2 }$ ; polymers live at $\mathrm { D e } \approx 1$ , which is the deep reason polymer processing is an art: the material is simultaneously a viscous liquid that flows and an elastic solid that springs back, in the same operation, and the ratio is a knob the process engineer turns.**

**Extensional hardening.** Stretch a melt and its viscosity *rises*, often by 10-100 times, because the chains align and cannot slip past each other. Long-chain branched melts (LDPE, and the specially-made grades for thermoforming and blow moulding) harden strongly, which is what lets a 300 mm-long molten **parison** hang for six seconds without necking and breaking. **This is why "the same" polyethylene bought for extrusion coating fails in blow moulding, and why a thermoforming grade is sold by its 'melt strength' — a number that has nothing to do with any property of the solid part.**
:::

::: example How much does the gate thin the melt? {#ex-shear level=2 check="22"}
A polypropylene has $\eta = 1 0 0 0 \ \mathrm { P a \, s }$ at a shear rate of $1 0 0 \ \mathrm { s ^ { - 1 } }$ and a power-law index $n = 0 . 4 5$ . What is its apparent viscosity at the $1 0 ^ { 5 } \ \mathrm { s ^ { - 1 } }$ of a $0 . 4 \ \mathrm { m m }$ gate?

$$
\eta \; = \; 1 0 0 0 \left( { \frac { 1 0 ^ { 5 } } { 1 0 0 } } \right) ^ { 0 . 4 5 - 1 } \; = \; 1 0 0 0 \times 1 0 ^ { - 1 . 6 5 } \; = \; { \bf 2 2 \ P a \, s }
$$

**Forty-five times thinner.** The practical consequence is that the filling of a mould is dominated by the *gate*, not by the cavity: the gate's 22 Pa·s over a 0.4 mm gap generates more pressure drop per millimetre than the 1000 Pa·s melt does anywhere in the 3 mm cavity, and once the gate freezes (which it does in one to three seconds) the packing pressure is cut off from the part and the shrinkage is fixed. **This is why "gate freeze time" is the number a moulder sets the hold time by, and why a part that sinks at the boss is nearly always a gate that froze too early, not a machine that packed too weakly.**
:::

## 2. Injection moulding: thirty seconds of four different processes

::: definition The machine, the cycle, and where the money goes {#def-irm}
An injection moulding machine is a **screw pump with a heater** and a **press with cold fingers**. The screw turns at $3 0 { - } 3 0 0 \ \mathrm { r p m }$ against a back pressure of $5 { - } 2 0$ bar, conveying, compressing, shearing and melting the granulate in three zones and metering a precise **shot** at its tip; then it stops turning and becomes a ram that pushes that shot into a closed mould at $5 0 { - } 5 0 0 \ \mathrm { c m ^ { 3 } / s }$ and $2 0 { - } 1 5 0 \ \mathrm { M P a }$ of injection pressure; then it holds pressure while the part shrinks onto its core; then it cools; then it opens and the half-mould ejects the part. The four phases are **fill** (0.5-5 s, and the fountain flow lays down the skin-core structure), **pack** (1-10 s at 40-80 % of the injection pressure, forcing in the extra material that thermal contraction demands), **hold and cool** (5-60 s, doing nothing but conducting heat), and **open-eject-close** (3-10 s).

**The economics are entirely in the tool and the cycle.** A production mould for a technical part costs £30,000-150,000 of tool steel (P20 or 1.2344 pre-hardened at 30 HRC for a 500,000-shot life; hardened 420 stainless at 50 HRC and polished to N1 for an optical surface with a 2 million-shot life), takes 6-16 weeks to cut and try, and must be amortised. The machine costs £25-80 an hour depending on tonnage. The material costs £1-8/kg, and the part in the hand usually contains 1.2-1.4 times its weight in purchased resin, because the sprue and runners are either reground (which shortens the fibres and drops the impact strength 10-20 % per regrind cycle) or scrapped. **The three levers of the process, in order of their effect on cost, are: the number of cavities (a 4-cavity tool quarters the machine time per part and adds 40 % to the tool), the cooling time (60-70 % of the cycle, and the only part of it that physics sets), and the cycle's dry time (the open-eject-close and the robot, which a good cell has down to 3 s).**

The machine must be sized on two numbers, and getting either wrong is a common and expensive error. **Shot size:** the part plus runner should be 30-80 % of the screw's maximum shot, because a shot that is too small leaves the melt sitting in the barrel for minutes (which degrades colour and glass-reinforced nylon) and gives a screw that cannot meter accurately. **Clamp force:** the pressure inside the cavity tries to blow the mould open with a force equal to the *average cavity pressure times the projected area*, and the machine's tonnage must exceed it with a margin:

$$
F _ { \mathrm { clamp } } \; \geq \; 1 . 1 \times { \bar { p } } \, A _ { \mathrm { proj } } \qquad \left[ 1 \ \mathrm { tonne } \approx 9 . 8 \ \mathrm { k N } \right]
$$

with $\bar { p } = 3 0 { - } 6 0 \ \mathrm { M P a }$ for a thin, long-flow or glass-filled part. **Under-clamping is self-limiting and obvious (the mould flashes, the part is heavy and the shots vary); over-clamping is silent and expensive (the mould's vents are crushed shut, so the air cannot get out, and the part burns at the end of the flow — "diesel effect" scorching, which is a compression ignition of the trapped air and looks exactly like burnt resin, because it is).**
:::

::: definition Cooling: the one part of the cycle that physics decides {#def-cool}
Everything about an injection moulding cycle except the cooling time can be argued with: fill faster, pack longer, open sooner, put a robot in. **The cooling time is set by conduction, and it is the whole of the cycle's floor.** A part of full thickness $s$ , cooled on both faces by a mould whose surface sits at $T _ { w }$ , must get its *centreline* below the ejection temperature $T _ { e }$ (which is the heat deflection temperature at the part's own stiffness, not a temperature you choose). The one-term Fourier solution of the slab problem gives

$$
t _ { \mathrm { cool } } \; = \; { \frac { s ^ { 2 } } { \pi ^ { 2 } \alpha } } \; \ln \! \left[ { \frac { 8 } { \pi ^ { 2 } } } \, { \frac { T _ { \mathrm { melt } } - T _ { w } } { T _ { e } - T _ { w } } } \right] \qquad \alpha = { \frac { k } { \rho c _ { p } } } \approx ( 0 . 0 7 { - } 0 . 1 3 ) \ \mathrm { m m ^ { 2 } / s }
$$

**and the two things to read off it are that the time goes as the square of the wall thickness and that it is almost independent of the melt temperature.** Doubling the wall quadruples the cycle: this is why "uniform, and as thin as the stiffness allows" is the whole of moulding design, and why a rib (which buys bending stiffness as $t ^ { 3 }$ for almost no extra cooling time, since its heat escapes into the adjacent wall) is worth more than a thickening. The thermal diffusivity of every commercial plastic is within a factor of two of $0 . 1 \ \mathrm { m m ^ { 2 } / s }$ — polymers are all good insulators — so **no material change will ever make a part cool much faster.** What does work, in order of value:

$$
\begin{array} { l l l }
\textbf{Measure} & \textbf{Effect on cooling time} & \textbf{Cost} \\ \hline
\text{Thinner, uniform wall} & \propto s ^ { 2 } \ ( \text{quadratic!} ) & \text{design, i.e. free} \\
\text{Conformal cooling channels} & - 2 0 \text{ to } - 4 0 \ \% & \text{additive tool inserts} \\
\text{Higher water flow, turbulent} & - 1 0 \text{ to } - 2 0 \ \% & \text{£500 of plumbing} \\
\text{Beryllium-copper or Al inserts} & - 1 0 \text{ to } - 3 0 \ \% \text{ locally} & \text{£2-10k per insert} \\
\text{Cold mould, hot part} & - 3 0 \ \% \text{ (and worse surface)} & \text{free, and a bad idea} \\
\text{Vacuum or "cold-skin" cycle} & - 4 0 \ \% & \text{a special machine}
\end{array}
$$

**Conformal cooling is the one genuinely new idea in moulding in thirty years:** a laser-printed beryllium-copper or maraging-steel insert with a cooling channel that follows the part's surface at a constant 6 mm, instead of a drilled straight hole that must miss the core, is worth 20-40 % of the cycle and — more importantly — removes the *temperature difference across the part* that causes half the warpage. It costs three to five times a drilled insert and is now standard on high-volume optical and automotive tools.

Second, the toolmaker's: the same part with a mould at $6 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ needs 14.4 s of cooling, so the choice of a glossy, low-warp, well-welded part versus a cheap one is a decision about a *water temperature*, and it should be made on the drawing, not on the shop floor at two in the morning.**

A four-cavity moulding has a total projected area of $2 5 { , } 0 0 0 \ \mathrm { m m } ^ { 2 }$ and an average cavity pressure of $4 5 \ \mathrm { M P a }$ during packing. What clamp force is needed?

$$
F = 1 . 1 \times 4 5 \times 2 5 { , } 0 0 0 = { \bf 1 } . 2 4 \times 1 0 ^ { 6 } \ \mathrm { N } \quad ( \bar { p } A = 4 5 \times 2 5 { , } 0 0 0 = { \bf 1 } 1 2 5 \ \mathrm { k N } = 1 1 5 \ \mathrm { t } )
$$

**A 130 tonne machine, then, and the reason the exercise asked for the raw $1 1 2 5 \ \mathrm { k N }$ is that this is the number on the machine's plate and the number the moulder argues with.** **Two things the arithmetic hides. The cavity pressure is not uniform: it is $8 0 \ \mathrm { M P a }$ at the gate and near zero at the end of flow, so a long thin part has a much lower average than a squat one and can be run on a smaller machine than the projected area suggests. And the tonnage is also what limits the number of cavities: a 16-cavity tool for the same part needs twice the clamp, twice the platen, and a much more expensive balanced runner system — which is the crossover at which "more cavities" stops buying cheaper parts.**

::: definition Filling a cavity: what the melt does, and what it leaves behind {#def-fill}
Molten polymer does not push along a pipe like water; it **fountains**. The melt arrives at the flow front, spreads over the surface of the growing solid skin, and turns back on itself — so the material at the front is *always fresh melt*, and the material at the wall is *material that arrived first and has been stretched flat against a cold surface*. Three things follow, and they are the reason a moulding is anisotropic.

**The skin-core structure.** The fountain flow plus the enormous shear at the wall stretches the chains (and any fibres) flat against the flow direction, and the $4 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ steel freezes them there before they can relax. The result is a **highly oriented skin of 20-100 μm, a transition layer, and a core of relaxed, transverse-oriented material** — with a modulus 30-50 % higher in the flow direction than across it in a 30 % glass-reinforced part, and a **shrinkage of 0.2-0.4 % along the flow against 0.8-1.2 % across it**. That anisotropy, integrated over a part with ribs and bosses, is what makes a moulding warp, and it is why a moulding's shrinkage is quoted as two numbers and why a mould is cut with a "shrink rule" that is a compromise between them.

**Weld (knit) lines.** Where two flow fronts meet — around a hole, from a second gate, or at the end of a core pin — they meet at a shallow angle, and the chains must diffuse across the interface to bond. **Diffusion takes seconds; the interface freezes in a fraction of a second.** So a weld line is a real discontinuity: **50-70 % of the bulk strength in an unfilled amorphous polymer, 20-40 % in a semi-crystalline one, and 10-30 % with 30 % glass, because the fibres lie parallel to the interface and bridge nothing.** The cures are all about temperature and pressure: hotter mould and melt, thicker section at the weld, a **cold slug well** to catch the material that touched the runner wall, a **vent** of $0 . 0 2 \ \mathrm { m m }$ deep at the weld (so the air can escape before it compresses and burns), an **oversized or relocated gate** so the two fronts meet at a high angle, or a **bump** in the land that forces fresh melt across the joint. **And the designer's cure, which is better than all of them: move the hole.**

**Flow length and the "flow ratio".** A melt will flow roughly **100-150 times its own wall thickness** in a normal part and 300 in a thin-wall one with a hot runner and a vacuum-assisted mould; beyond that the pressure required exceeds the machine. The **flow ratio** $L / t$ (with $L$ the distance from the gate to the *farthest point*, not the longest path) is the first thing a mould flow analysis reports, and a part with $L / t > 2 0 0$ needs more gates, thicker walls, or a different part. **The single most common cause of a "short shot" on a new tool is not the machine but a vent that is 0.05 mm too shallow, and the second is a wall 15 % thinner than the model assumed.**

**The PVT behaviour and the sink.** A polymer melt is enormously compressible — 10-20 % denser at $1 5 0 \ \mathrm { M P a }$ than at zero — and it contracts by a further 5-15 % as it cools, plus 5-10 % more if it crystallises. **Packing exists to replace that contraction, and packing only works while there is a path of unmolten-skin material from the screw to the last-to-freeze point (the "last point to freeze", which is always the thickest bit).** Once the gate freezes the part is hydraulically isolated and shrinks freely, which is why **hold time is set to the gate freeze time and no longer** — extra hold time buys nothing but cycle. Where the last point to freeze is a boss or a rib behind a nominal wall, it shrinks and pulls the visible surface in with it: **a sink mark, which is not a defect of the process but a geometric consequence of a thick section, and is cured by making the boss hollow with a 60 % rib-to-wall ratio, not by packing harder.**
:::

::: example Shear heating in a gate {#ex-sheat level=3 check="35"}
A $0 . 4 \ \mathrm { m m }$ deep, $4 \ \mathrm { m m }$ wide edge gate passes the melt at a shear rate of $5 0 0 0 \ \mathrm { s ^ { - 1 } }$ for 5 ms, with an apparent viscosity of $5 0 0 \ \mathrm { P a \, s }$ . How much heat does the melt generate in that time, and what does its temperature do? Take $\rho c _ { p } = 1 . 8 \times 1 0 ^ { 6 } \ \mathrm { J / m ^ { 3 } K }$ .

$$
\dot { q } = \eta \, { \dot { \gamma } } ^ { 2 } = 5 0 0 \times 5 0 0 0 ^ { 2 } = 1 . 2 5 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 0 } \ \mathrm { W / m ^ { 3 } }
$$

$$
\Delta T = { \frac { { \dot { q } } \, \Delta t } { \rho c _ { p } } } = { \frac { 1 . 2 5 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 0 } \times 0 . 0 0 5 } { 1 . 8 \times 1 0 ^ { 6 } } } = { \bf 3 5 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C } }
$$

**The melt arrives at the cavity thirty-five degrees hotter than it left the nozzle, and there is no heat source in the machine that put it there.** **This is why the material in a cold-runner gate is measurably hotter than the melt in the barrel, why a very small gate can be used to "shear-heat" a viscous engineering resin into a thin part, and — on the other side — why an over-sized gate on a glass-filled nylon scorches: the same dissipation concentrated at a fibre-rich, low-conductivity skin is how a gate burns without the barrel ever being too hot.** **It is also the mechanism behind the "flow-induced crystallisation" skin of a semi-crystalline part, and behind the transcrystalline layer that grows from a glass fibre and is the reason a reinforced part's shrinkage is not the neat resin's.**
:::

::: definition Designing the part, the gate and the tool {#def-design-irm}
**Part rules, and the reason for each.** Uniform wall, 1-4 mm for most parts and 0.5-1.5 mm for thin-wall packaging, with transitions no steeper than 3:1 and never a sudden thickening. **Every internal corner radiused to 0.5 times the wall and every external corner to 1.5 times the wall** — for the mould (a sharp internal corner is a stress raiser in the tool that cracks, and a sharp external corner is a moulding that cannot be ejected without marring), for the melt (a sharp corner is where the flow stalls and the weld line forms), and for the part (a sharp corner in a glass-filled nylon at $- 3 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ is a crack that has not happened yet). **Draft of 1-2° on every vertical face, 3-5° on a textured one**, because a moulding shrinks *onto* its cores and *away from* its cavity walls, and ejecting a zero-draft core scratches it lengthwise. **No undercuts if you can avoid them; if you cannot, put them all on the same parting line or the same side**, because every undercut is a **lift** (a cam, a lifter, a unscrew) and each one is £3,000-15,000 of tool, a maintenance item, and a source of flash and of a witness mark. **Ribs at 50-60 % of the nominal wall, spaced at 2-3 times the wall, and never opposite each other on two walls.** **Bosses cored and blended with fillets, never solid.** **Snap fits designed for a strain of 2-4 % for an unfilled nylon, 1-1.5 % for a polycarbonate and 0.5 % for a plasticised one, and for a *strain*, not a stress, because a moulding's creep means a snap fit held deflected for a year at $6 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ loses half its holding force.**

**Gating.** The gate is a restriction whose job is to freeze at the right moment, and there are five families worth knowing. **Edge and tab gates** (the workhorse, full-depth or part-depth, with a witness mark on the parting line). **Submarine (tunnel) gates**, which cut under the parting line and shear off at ejection, leaving no mark on the visible face and giving automatic de-gating. **Pin-point gates** for a cosmetic part, 0.5-1.5 mm, which leave a small witness and are the only choice for a three-plate tool. **Film and flash gates** for a thin, wide, flat part, where the whole parting line feeds and the weld lines and orientation are minimised — at the cost of a de-flash operation. **Fan and annular gates** for a tube, so the flow front is symmetric and there is no weld line opposite the gate. **Runner systems are either "naturally balanced" (equal paths, equal times, which is impossible for an asymmetric part set) or "fully balanced" (equal path lengths by construction, the 1:2:4 rule), and the moulder adjusts the difference with a hand-held pyrometer and a stopwatch.** **Hot runners** delete the sprue and the cold runners — 20-40 % of the material and 1-3 s of cycle — at a cost of £5,000-30,000 per tool, a control box, and a whole new class of defects (a cold spot, a burnt tip, a colour change that takes four hours).

**The tool.** Hardened and polished steel for optics and 2 million-shot life; **P20 at 30 HRC** for a technical part; **beryllium-copper** where a hot spot must be cooled; **aluminium (2244/7075)** for a 50,000-shot bridge tool or a low-volume part, which machines in a third of the time and polishes to a decent surface but must be nickel-plated against the abrasion of glass-filled resin and the corrosion of flame-retardant ones. **Vents are 0.02-0.03 mm deep and 5-10 mm wide, cut in the parting line or in a vent land, and they are the most under-designed feature on every tool in the world.** **Cooling is drilled 8-10 mm channels at 3-5 times the wall thickness apart, at a constant distance from the surface, in a baffle or bubbler around a core, and it should be turbulent (Re > 10,000) — which for a 8 mm channel means 1.5 m/s and 12 litres a minute, and most moulds are run at a third of that because somebody turned the valve down to stop a leak.**
:::

## 3. Extrusion: the continuous moulding

::: definition The screw is three machines in one {#def-extrude}
A 90 mm, 24-diameter-length single-screw extruder turns at $2 0 { - } 2 0 0 \ \mathrm { r p m }$ and does three physically different jobs in one barrel, and knowing which zone you are in is how you diagnose an extrusion problem.

**Zone 1, solids conveying** (the feed throat, 4-6 diameters long and three times as deep as the metering section): the granulate is dragged forward by friction against a *cooled* barrel wall and pushed along the screw's flight like a cork in a bottle. The physics is the same as a powder bin's: the **friction coefficient between the plug and the steel** (0.2-0.4, and it depends on whether the barrel is water-cooled or not) sets how much material is carried, which is why **a feed throat that runs too hot starves the extruder and why every extruder has a water-cooled, deliberately roughened, dimensionally generous feed section.** A starved extruder surges, and a surging extruder makes a wavy product.

**Zone 2, melting** (the transition, 8-12 diameters): a melt film is sheared off the barrel wall and squeezed into the channel between the solid plug and the flight, forming a **melt pool** that grows until the solid bed is squeezed against the opposite flight and disappears. **The "melting point" — where the last of the solid goes — should be 1-2 diameters before the metering section starts; if it is later, unmelted fines reach the die and the product has "gels" (the little transparent fish-eyes that ruin a film and a coating).** A **barrier (Maddock) screw** puts a second flight across the channel and forces every bit of solid through a thin shear gap, which melts it in half the length, halves the temperature variation and is standard on everything except a few heat-sensitive compounds.

**Zone 3, melt pumping** (the metering, 8-10 diameters, depth $0 . 0 7 D$ ): now it is a pump, and its output is the classic drag-minus-pressure-flow result,

$$
Q \; = \; \underbrace { { \tfrac { 1 } { 2 } } \pi ^ { 2 } D ^ { 2 } h N \sin \theta \cos \theta } _ { \text{drag flow } \propto N } \; - \; \underbrace { { \frac { \pi D h ^ { 3 } \sin ^ { 2 } \theta } { 1 2 \mu } } { \frac { \mathrm { d } p } { \mathrm { d } z } } } _ { \text{pressure flow } \propto \Delta p / \mu }
$$

**which is a straight line on a $Q$ versus $\Delta p$ plot, and the die's own characteristic $Q = K _ { \mathrm { d i e } } \, \Delta p / \mu$ is another straight line through the origin. Their intersection is the operating point** — and this little diagram explains four things that otherwise seem arbitrary: why a hotter barrel (lower $\mu$ ) makes the die *more* productive but the screw *less* in control; why a worn screw (a deeper $h$ ) loses output and makes the extruder sensitive to a die-pressure change; why a **melt pump** or a **gear pump** at the die entry is fitted to anything that must hold a thickness (a wire coating, a sheet, a medical tube), because it decouples the die pressure from the screw; and why the answer to "the line rate keeps drifting" is nearly always the feed-section temperature.

**The die is a pressure drop with a shape.** A sheet die has a **restricting bar** and a **flexing bolt** along its 1-3 m width so the operator can trim the flow to $\pm 0 . 0 5 \ \mathrm { m m }$ ; a pipe die is an annular **crosshead** with a torpedo and a mandrel; a profile die is hand-polished internally by a craftsperson over weeks, because the melt must arrive at every point of the section at the same velocity or the profile twists, sinks and has frozen-in stress. **The design rule for any die is uniform exit velocity, and the tool for achieving it is a variable land length — long where the section is thin and the flow is fast, short where it is thick.** **The limits are set by the melt: above a wall shear stress of $0 . 1 { - } 0 . 4 \ \mathrm { M P a }$ the surface goes dull (**sharkskin**), and above about $0 . 4 \ \mathrm { M P a }$ or a shear rate of $1 0 ^ { 4 } \ \mathrm { s ^ { - 1 } }$ the whole extrudate goes wavy, spiralled or branched (**gross melt fracture**), which is a wall-slip instability and not a temperature problem. The cures are a lower output, a hotter die, a **streamline (rheology-enhancing) die** with a long converging entry, a **die-body filter** of 100-200 mesh, or 5-10 % of a fluoropolymer **processing aid** that makes the melt slip at the wall.**

**And the melt swells on leaving the die** by 20-100 %, because the elastic energy stored in the converging flow is released. **So the die is drawn to the *inverse* of the wanted section: swell for the diameter, draw down for the wall, and the two are set by the haul-off speed.** The **draw-down ratio** $\mathrm { DDR } = A _ { \mathrm { die } } / A _ { \mathrm { product } }$ is the extruder's version of the stretch ratio, and it is the reason a 100 mm pipe line runs at 1-3 m/min while the melt leaves the die at 10 m/min.
:::

::: example The energy bill of an extruder {#ex-energy level=3 check="30"}
A compounding extruder makes $2 0 0 \ \mathrm { k g / h }$ of 30 % glass-reinforced nylon 6,6 at a specific mechanical energy of $0 . 1 5 \ \mathrm { k W h / k g }$ . What shaft power does that need, and how does it compare with the heat the melt actually requires?

$$
P = 0 . 1 5 \times 2 0 0 = { \bf 3 0 \ k W }
$$

**The thermodynamic minimum is the sensible heat alone: nylon from $2 5 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ to $2 8 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ at $c _ { p } \approx 2 . 0 \ \mathrm { k J / k g \, K }$ is $5 1 0 \ \mathrm { k J / k g } = 0 . 1 4 \ \mathrm { k W h / k g }$ , plus $7 0 \ \mathrm { k J / k g }$ of latent heat, giving $0 . 1 7 \ \mathrm { k W h / k g }$ — which is *more* than the mechanical energy supplied.** **That is not a contradiction, it is the design point: a modern extruder gets 40-60 % of its heat from the barrel heaters at idle and *removes* the rest with the cooling water, because at 400 rpm and $0 . 3 \ \mathrm { k W h / k g }$ the screw's shear heating alone exceeds what melting needs and the machine must be cooled.** **This is why "the specific energy" is the number to ask for when comparing a twin-screw to a single-screw, why a barrier screw with a deep channel saves 20 % of the drive power, and why an extrusion line's cooling-water circuit is bigger than its heater circuit.**
:::

::: definition Blow moulding, thermoforming, and the other ways of inflating a polymer {#def-blow}
**Extrusion blow moulding** makes every hollow thing that is not stretch-blown: the fuel tank, the detergent bottle, the ducting, the 200 litre drum. A molten **parison** — a tube of melt, up to 3 m long and hanging under its own weight — is extruded downwards, a mould closes on it, and air at 20-40 bar blows it out against the cold walls. **The whole technology is the parison's own weight.** A melt that is too thin-walled sags and leaves the top of the bottle thin; a melt that is too thick wastes resin and takes forever to cool. The answer is the **axial programme**: a hydraulic or electric servo moves a mandrel that changes the die gap twenty times during the six seconds the parison hangs, so the wall is thick at the top and thin at the bottom, exactly cancelling the sag. A **hoop programme** does the same with the mould's own stretch. **HDPE is the material because it has the long-chain branching that gives melt strength** (a linear metallocene or a PP will sag and cannot be used without a long-chain-branched grade), and the **multilayer** version co-extrudes six or nine layers for a fuel tank: HDPE / tie / EVOH barrier / tie / recycled HDPE / tie / HDPE, with the EVOH at 5 % of the wall doing 99 % of the petrol-vapour blocking.

**Injection stretch blow moulding** makes every carbonated-drink and water bottle in the world, and it is the most beautiful example in this whole lesson of *the process making the material*. A preform (an injection-moulded test tube, 10 g for a 500 ml bottle) is reheated in an infrared oven to $1 0 5 { - } 1 1 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ — **just above the glass transition of $7 8 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ , and no higher, or the PET crystallises and goes opaque** — then a stretch rod drives down into it at 0.3 s while 30-40 bar of air blows it out. **The stretch ratios are 2.5-4.0 axially and 3.5-4.0 in the hoop, and they do two things at once: they orient the amorphous chains, and they set off *strain-induced crystallisation*, which takes the PET from 5 % to 25-30 % crystalline.** The result, against an unstretched bottle, is **roughly 10 times the impact strength, 2 times the stiffness, 6 times the gas barrier and 4 times the stress-crack resistance** — and it is why you can squeeze a PET bottle and see it whiten, and why a bottle that has been overheated in the oven is brittle and purple at the base. **The two-stage process (mould, cool, reheat in an oven, stretch-blow) makes the carbonated bottles that must hold 6 bar; the one-stage (mould, keep hot, stretch-blow in the same machine) makes the water bottles and the 300 ml pharmaceutical round, and is cheaper because it does not reheat.**

**Thermoforming** takes a **sheet** (extruded or rolled, 0.2-6 mm), clamps it, heats it by radiant ceramic elements to $1 0 { - } 3 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ above its forming temperature until the surface sags, and forms it over or into a mould with vacuum, compressed air, or a mechanical plug. **The forming window of an amorphous sheet (HIPS, PET-G, ABS, PVC) is 30-40 °C wide and it is easy; that of a semi-crystalline one (PP, PE, PA) is 5-10 °C wide and it is an art, because at $T _ { \mathrm { m } } - 2 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ the sheet runs like butter and at $T _ { \mathrm { m } } - 4 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ it is elastic and springs back and whitens.** **The wall thickness is not designed, it is inherited: a dome thins according to its draw, and the classic estimate for a spherical cap is**

$$
{ \frac { t } { t _ { 0 } } } \; \approx \; { \frac { 1 } { \sqrt { D } } } \qquad D = \text{local draw ratio}
$$

**so a cup four times deeper than its wall-thickness scale thins to half at the corner, and the corner is where the part is loaded.** The cures are a **plug assist** (a slow mechanical or air-driven billow that stretches the sheet into the corner *before* the mould closes, worth 30-50 % of the corner thickness), a matched mechanical counter-mould (**pressure forming** at 5-8 bar, which will hold a 2 mm texture on a 1 mm wall), and **gate trimming in the same station** so the part is finished off the machine. **The economics: the sheet is 60-80 % of the part cost, so thermoforming wins when the part is big, thin and shallow (a bath, a trunk liner, a blister pack, a yoghurt pot) and loses when the draw is deep or the corners matter.** A **roll-fed line** makes 40-100 pots a minute; a **sheet-fed machine** with a 1.2 × 1.2 m platen makes 4-10 an hour and the tool is a piece of machined aluminium or even MDF.

**Rotational moulding** makes the big, seamless, one-metre-plus hollow things — the water tank, the kayak, the road barrier, the fuel tank of a tractor — by charging a **powdered polyethylene** (100 mesh, 1-2 % of the mould's volume) into a sealed mould and cooking it while it rotates on two axes at 2-8 rpm. **The wall is uniform because it is made by the same powder sticking to the same wall everywhere; there is no pressure anywhere in the process, so the mould carries no clamp load and costs a tenth of an injection tool.** The cycle is slow (20-60 minutes, because the powder insulates the air inside it), the tolerances are $\pm 1 \ \mathrm { m m }$ at best, and the parts are 5-500 kg. **The failure mode is "bridging"** — powder that fails to reach a deep corner or an internal radius — and the cure is a **rivet, a bead or a welded-on rib** at the corner, since a rotational moulding cannot have an insert unless it is welded in afterwards.
:::

::: example The bottle's two stretch ratios {#ex-bottle level=3 check="8.75"}
A 500 ml PET bottle is stretch-blown at 2.5 times axially and 3.5 times in the hoop. What is the total area draw, and what does it do to the wall?

$$
{ \frac { A _ { \mathrm { preform } } } { A _ { \mathrm { bottle } } } } \times A _ { \mathrm { wall } } \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \text{area ratio } = 2 . 5 \times 3 . 5 = { \bf 8 . 7 5 }
$$

**The wall is thinned by 8.75 times and the molecules in it are stretched 2.5 times one way and 3.5 the other, which is what "biaxially oriented" means and what makes a 12 μm-thin wall hold 6 bar of carbon dioxide for nine months.** **Two numbers to keep from this. The wall of a 500 ml bottle is 25-30 μm at the body — thinner than a cling film — and it is the strongest thing you will ever hold, because orientation is free strength. And the 8.75 area ratio is why the preform's base is 2.5 mm thick: after stretching 8.75 times in area it must come out at 0.28 mm, which is what a bottle base needs to stand on.** **The designer's takeaway is that a stretch-blown part cannot have a sharp corner, a deep boss or a moulded thread on a small radius, because the material cannot get there; and that a "one-piece" bottle with a handle, a hinge and a cap is not one piece and never will be.**
:::

## 4. Thermosets and rubbers: racing the clock

::: definition The gel point, the glass transition, and why a cure is a state not a time {#def-cure}
A thermoset is processed as a liquid and finished as a solid by a chemical reaction, and the reaction has a **gel point**: the moment the first infinite molecule appears, the material stops being a liquid that flows and becomes a rubber that creeps. **Before the gel point you can pour, inject, impregnate or mould it; after it you can only heat it and wait.** The viscosity therefore falls with temperature as the molecules get short, reaches a minimum of $0 . 1 { - } 0 . 5 \ \mathrm { P a \, s }$ , and then climbs steeply for ever — and the whole of a thermoset process is designed to **do the shaping at the viscosity minimum and get the part out of the tool before the network locks in the shape's defects**.

The reaction's extent is the **degree of cure** $\alpha$ (0 = unreacted, 1 = fully reacted), measured by the heat given off in a differential scanning calorimeter, and it follows an autocatalytic law

$$
{ \frac { \mathrm { d } \alpha } { \mathrm { d } t } } \; = \; \left( k _ { 1 } + k _ { 2 } \, \alpha ^ { m } \right) ( 1 - \alpha ) ^ { n } , \qquad k _ { i } = A _ { i } \exp \! \left( - { \frac { E } { R T } } \right)
$$

with $m \approx 0 . 6 { - } 1$ , $n \approx 2 { - } 2 . 5$ and $E = 5 0 { - } 7 0 \ \mathrm { k J / m o l }$ . **Three things a process engineer must know about that equation.** **It is autocatalytic**, so the rate is slowest at the start, peaks at $\alpha \approx 0 . 3$ , and dies away as the reactants run out — which is why a cure has an "induction" and why a mould charged with cold resin and closed too early makes a part with a bubble trail. **It is exponentially temperature dependent**, so with $E = 6 0 \ \mathrm { k J / m o l }$ the rate **doubles for every $2 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$** and a mould running $1 5 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ cold costs you twice the cycle. **And it is exothermic**: an epoxy gives $5 0 0 { - } 6 0 0 \ \mathrm { J / g }$ , which with no heat loss would raise its own temperature by $4 5 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ . **A thick casting, a fast infusion or a badly designed compression moulding can run away, char, and set fire to the shop; the cure schedule's ramp rate exists to keep the part within $1 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ of the tool.**

**The number that decides whether the part is any good is not the time but the glass transition, and the glass transition follows the degree of cure** by the DiBenedetto relation,

$$
{ \frac { T _ { g } - T _ { g 0 } } { T _ { g \infty } - T _ { g 0 } } } \; = \; { \frac { \lambda \, \alpha } { 1 - ( 1 - \lambda ) \, \alpha } } \qquad \lambda \approx 0 . 4 { - } 0 . 6
$$

**and this single line explains more thermoset-process failures than any other: as the reaction proceeds, $T _ { g }$ rises, and the moment $T _ { g }$ reaches the cure temperature the material vitrifies — the chains stop moving, the reaction rate collapses by four orders of magnitude, and the cure effectively stops.** **So a part cured at $1 2 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ can never reach a glass transition above $1 2 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ , no matter how long it stays there, and a resin system with a $T _ { g \infty }$ of $1 9 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ needs a post-cure above $1 7 5 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ to get anywhere near it.** **A "cure schedule" is therefore a temperature trajectory designed to stay just ahead of vitrification, and a part that fails a hot-water soak, a solvent wipe or a hot-gas test has almost always been cured to a time, not to a state.**

**Rubber is the same story with a different chemistry.** Natural rubber, SBR, BR, EPDM, nitrile, silicone and fluoroelastomer are supplied **uncrosslinked** (or barely so), compounded with carbon black or silica, zinc, sulfur and accelerators, and shaped at $8 0 { - } 1 1 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ by extrusion, calendering, injection moulding or press-forming into a **"green"** part that is sticky, weak and useless. **Vulcanisation** — sulfur at 1-3 phr with a zinc/accelerator system, at $1 5 0 { - } 1 8 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ — then throws in the 1-3 mol % of crosslinks that turn it into an elastomer. **The two numbers on every rubber compound's data sheet are the scorch time $t _ { \mathrm { s } } 2$ (when the cure starts, and it must be long enough to survive the injection moulding's hot runner) and $t _ { \mathrm { c } } 9 0$ (when it is 90 % cured, typically 3-8 minutes at $1 7 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ )**, both measured on an oscillating-disc rheometer that reports the rising torque as the network forms. **Under-cure and the part is tacky, weak and will bloom; over-cure and the sulfur crosslinks become short, stiff and brittle and the fatigue life collapses; scorch in the mould and the runner is blocked and the tool is stripped and re-chromed.** A tyre is cured at $1 6 5 { - } 1 7 5 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ for 12-18 minutes at 25-35 bar in a two-plate press, against a **curing bag** that is inflated with nitrogen (which is preheated, so the bag does not steal the heat, and which diffuses through rubber 4 times more slowly than air, so it leaks less and the tyre comes out sharper); the press opens, the tyre is off in 90 seconds, and the whole plant's throughput is set by that 15 minutes.
:::

::: example How far can a 120 °C cure go? {#ex-cure level=3 check="77"}
An epoxy has $T _ { g 0 } = 5 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ , $T _ { g \infty } = 1 9 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ and $\lambda = 0 . 5$ . It is cured isothermally at $1 2 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ . What is the highest degree of cure it can reach?

Vitrification stops the reaction when $T _ { g }$ reaches $1 2 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ , so set the DiBenedetto left-hand side to $( 1 2 0 - 5 ) / ( 1 9 0 - 5 ) = 0 . 6 2 2$ and solve

$$
{ \frac { 0 . 5 \, \alpha } { 1 - 0 . 5 \, \alpha } } = 0 . 6 2 2 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad 0 . 5 \alpha = 0 . 6 2 2 - 0 . 3 1 1 \alpha \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \alpha = { \frac { 0 . 6 2 2 } { 0 . 8 1 1 } } = { \bf 0 . 7 7 }
$$

**Seventy-seven per cent, and there it stays for ever: the part is 23 % unreacted, permanently, and it will keep giving off solvent and plasticiser for its whole life.** **This is why every aerospace prepreg's cure cycle has a ramp to $1 8 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ and a two-hour dwell, why a "two-part epoxy bonded joint cured at $2 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ " is specified with a $T _ { g }$ of $5 5 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ and a service limit of $4 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ , and why the fastest way to ruin a thermoset part is to save energy on the post-cure.**
:::

::: definition Compression moulding, transfer moulding, RIM and the thermoset processes {#def-thermoset}
**Compression moulding** is the oldest and still the highest-volume thermoset process: a pre-measured charge (a tablet of phenolic powder, a BMC pellet, a 25-30 % glass SMC sheet cut to shape and pre-heated to $8 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ ) is dropped into a matched steel die at $1 4 0 { - } 1 8 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ , the press closes at $1 0 { - } 4 0 \ \mathrm { M P a }$ , the charge flows and cures, and the part is ejected after **30-60 seconds per millimetre of thickness**. **It is a low-velocity, low-pressure, low-orientation process, which is exactly what a brittle, fibre-filled, electrically insulating part wants**: no fibre attrition (the glass stays long, so the strength is 2-3 times an injection moulding's), no weld lines, near-net-shape, and a tool that costs a fifth of an injection mould. **The costs are the flash (5-15 % of the material, trimmed off), the manual charge, and the cure time.** **Sheet moulding compound** made this way is the electrical enclosure, the automotive bonnet of the 1990s Corvette, the bathroom suite and the transformer bushing; **bulk moulding compound** injection-moulded at the same time is the connector, the switchgear part and the under-bonnet bracket.

**Transfer moulding** puts the charge in a pot, squeezes it through a runner into a closed multi-cavity mould, and gets the injection-like filling without the screw's fibre damage. **It is the process that encapsulated the semiconductor industry for forty years** — 200 million packages a year of a $1 7 5 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ , 7 MPa, 90-second epoxy cure, in which the compound must flow 100 mm around a 0.3 mm gold bondwire without moving it, which is a story about viscosity, not about strength.

**Reaction injection moulding (RIM)** mixes two low-viscosity liquids (the polyol and the isocyanate for a polyurethane, at 150-200 bar in a 0.5 mm impingement chamber, 1-2 ms of mixing, then a mould that fills in 3 seconds) and cures in the part. **The clamp pressure needed is $0 . 5 { - } 3 \ \mathrm { M P a }$ of projected area, so a car bumper needs 1-3 MN against an injection moulding machine's 100 MN — which is why RIM tools are cast aluminium or even epoxy, cost a tenth as much, and can be 3 m long.** The demould time is 2-5 minutes, the part is a 4 mm skin with a 30 % glass-fibre reinforcement (the "HR-RIM" or "glass mat reinforced reaction injection moulding"), and the applications are the bumper, the dashboard skin, the truck fender and the agricultural fairing. **The whole business case of RIM is that it is the only way to make a large, tough, Class-A, low-volume part without a 10,000 tonne press.**

**Liquid silicone rubber injection moulding** is the same trick with a platinum-cure silicone: a 30,000 mPa·s liquid, a cold-runner mould at $1 7 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ , a 30-60 second cure, no post-cure needed for many grades, and a part that is food-contact, biocompatible, and usable from $- 6 0$ to $2 0 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ . **It is why a baby bottle teat, a phone's waterproof seal and a medical catheter manifold are all cheap.**
:::

## 5. Composites: the process *is* the part

::: definition The two ways to make a composite part, and everything that follows {#def-composite}
A composite part is **fibres, put where the load wants them, held in a resin that transfers load between them and protects them from each other**. Everything about the process is a fight to get the fibres at the right **volume fraction** (the target is 55-65 %, and the strength and stiffness in the fibre direction are very nearly proportional to it), in the right **orientation**, with the resin **everywhere including inside each tow**, and with **no voids, no wrinkles and no resin-rich pockets**. There are only two families of process and the choice between them is the biggest single decision in composite design.

**Wet processes: the resin goes in as a liquid, into dry fibre.** Hand lay-up and spray-up (a shop worker with a brush makes 1-2 m²/h at 25-30 % fibre, and the styrene emissions made it a regulated occupation), the **resin transfer moulding** (dry fibre in a matched, clamped steel tool, resin injected at 2-10 bar in 5-30 minutes, a 10,000-cycle tool for £300-800k, and the process of choice anywhere between a 3-minute automotive part and a 30-minute aerospace one), the **vacuum-assisted resin transfer moulding** (a dry fabric stack on a one-sided tool, a peel ply, a breather, a bag and a vacuum, resin drawn in at 1 bar over 2-8 hours, and the process that makes a 60 m wind-turbine blade shell and a boat hull), and the **film-infusion** variants. **The physics of all of them is Darcy's law through a fibrous bed,**

$$
\mathbf { v } \; = \; - \, { \frac { \mathsf { \mathbf { K } } } { \phi \, \mu } } \, \nabla p \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad L \; = \; { \sqrt { \frac { 2 \, K \, \Delta p \, t } { \phi \, \mu } } } \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad t \; = \; { \frac { \phi \, \mu \, L ^ { 2 } } { 2 \, K \, \Delta p } }
$$

**with the permeability $\mathsf { K }$ spanning fourteen orders of magnitude across a real preform: $1 0 ^ { - 1 5 } \ \mathrm { m ^ { 2 } }$ inside a tow, $1 0 ^ { - 1 2 } \ \mathrm { m ^ { 2 } }$ across a woven fabric's tows, $1 0 ^ { - 9 } \ \mathrm { m ^ { 2 } }$ along an aligned preform, and $1 0 ^ { - 7 } \ \mathrm { m ^ { 2 } }$ down a purpose-made flow medium.** **The flow front therefore goes as the square root of time, not as time, and it goes 1000 times faster down the flow medium than across the fabric — which is why every infusion has a distribution medium, why the flow front is an ellipse, and why the last thing to fill is always a corner you did not put a vent on.**

**Dry processes: the resin is already in.** **Prepreg** — continuous fibre pre-impregnated at the factory with a B-stage epoxy to 30-35 % by weight, chilled to $- 1 8 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ , with a shelf life of six months and an "out time" of 10-30 days at room temperature — is laid up by hand or by machine into a tool, covered with peel ply, bleeder, breather and bag, and cured in an **autoclave** at 6 bar of nitrogen and $1 2 0 { - } 1 8 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ for 1-2 hours. **It gives 60 % fibre, 0.5-1 % voids, a 1.5 mm-thick plate with a 3 % tolerance on thickness, and a repeatable structural property — which is why every primary aircraft structure since 1985 has been made this way, and why it is expensive: the material costs three times the wet resin and fibre together, the autoclave is £5-30 M and uses 3-5 kWh per kilogram of part, and one load per day is a good week.** **Out-of-autoclave prepreg** does the same in a one-bar vacuum bag in an oven, and its whole technical case rests on **consolidating the laminate before the resin gels** — a window of 30-60 minutes in which the bag must squeeze out every void while the viscosity is still falling — at a void content of 1.5-2.5 % against the autoclave's 0.5-1 %, which costs about 10 % of the interlaminar shear strength per 1 % of voids.

**The wet process's central, unforgiving problem is that the two flows happen at once and at different speeds.** The resin races down the **inter**-tow channels (permeability $1 0 ^ { - 9 } \ \mathrm { m ^ { 2 } }$ ) while the **intra**-tow pores ($1 0 ^ { - 1 5 } \ \mathrm { m ^ { 2 } }$ ) can only be filled by the capillary pressure of the fibre bundle itself, which is a couple of bar at best. **Get the macro flow front to the vacuum port in 30 minutes and the tows are still 2 % full of air, and no amount of vacuum afterwards will remove it once the gel starts.** The cures are all about the ratio of the two flows: a resin at $1 0 0 { - } 2 0 0 \ \mathrm { m P a \, s }$ rather than 400, a fibre **sizing** that makes the fibre surface wettable, a **soak** of 30-60 minutes under vacuum after the mould is full, and **back-pressurising the mould to 2-6 bar for the last hour**, which is what a modern blade-root infusion cycle actually does.
:::

::: example The tows that never filled {#ex-infuse level=4 check="78"}
A carbon tow bundle is 5 mm across. Its **intra**-tow permeability is $1 0 ^ { - 1 5 } \ \mathrm { m ^ { 2 } }$ , the resin viscosity is $0 . 3 \ \mathrm { P a \, s }$ , and the capillary pressure available at the fibre bundle's surface is 2 bar. How long does the bundle take to impregnate?

$$
t \; = \; { \frac { \mu \, L ^ { 2 } } { 2 \, K \, \Delta p } } \; = \; { \frac { 0 . 3 \times 0 . 0 0 2 5 ^ { 2 } } { 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 1 5 } \times 2 \times 1 0 ^ { 5 } } } \; = \; { \frac { 1 . 8 8 \times 1 0 ^ { - 6 } } { 4 \times 1 0 ^ { - 1 0 } } } \; = \; 4 6 9 0 \ \mathrm { s } \; = \; { \bf 7 8 \ min }
$$

**Seventy-eight minutes for a 5 mm bundle to fill by capillary action — and the mould's flow front reached the vacuum port in 35 minutes.** **This is the whole of the "dry tow" problem in one line, and it explains four things about infusion practice. Why a fast infusion gives a part that passes every visual and dimensional check and fails compression after impact at 60 % of the allowables. Why the cycle has a "soak" dwell of another hour after the mould is full. Why the resin's viscosity is the single most argued-about property on the technical data sheet, and why the infusion resins are formulated to 100-200 mPa·s and no lower, because below that the resin drains down the flow medium and leaves the fabric behind. And why the aerospace industry, which cannot accept a 2 % intra-tow void population in a primary structure, still pays for the autoclave.**
:::

::: definition Placing the fibre: hand, machine, and the ply book {#def-placement}
**The ply book is the composite part's source code.** A laminate is specified as a stack of ply orientations and thicknesses — `[148/45/-45/0₂/90/0₂/-45/45/148₁₆]` for a wing skin — and the rules are as rigid as any tolerance: **balance** (for every $+ 4 5 ^ { \circ } , \ - 4 5 ^ { \circ }$ , or the part twists when it is loaded in tension), **symmetry about the mid-plane** (or it curls when it cures, which is the same thermal-residual-stress argument as an unsymmetric weld), **no more than four plies of the same orientation adjacent** (or the delamination toughness collapses and the buckling wave has nothing to stop it), **a $1 0 \ \ \%$ surface ply at $\pm 4 5 ^ { \circ }$ minimum** (to hold the surface down against a compressive buckle), and **a 20:1 taper on every ply drop** (a step change in thickness is a delamination starter, and aerospace will not accept less than 20 plies of runout per dropped ply; automotive, with its 3-minute cycle, accepts 6:1 and pays for it in fatigue).

**Hand lay-up of a dry fabric** is 1-2 m² per person-hour, at 25-35 % fibre, with a strength that depends on the operator and a void content nobody measures. **Automated tape laying (ATL)** lays 75-300 mm-wide pre-impregnated tape at 10-15 m/min with hot-roll in-situ consolidation, and it is what makes an aeroplane fuselage barrel: a 787 forward fuselage is four ATL machines running continuously for weeks, laying 300 mm tape onto a 6 m-diameter mandrel. **Automated fibre placement (AFP)** lays up to 32 tows, each 3.2-6 mm wide, simultaneously and each independently angled, at 1-7 m/min depending on the curvature, onto a 5-axis mandrel or cell. **Its unique capability is the steered fibre: a tow whose angle changes by 1° every 25 mm, so the load path can be followed around a hole or a door cut-out instead of being cut by it, which is worth 10-20 % of the weight of a wing.** Its characteristic defects are equally unique: **gap and overlap** between adjacent tows, **starvation** (a resin-rich triangle where a steered tow lifts off the tool on a concave curve), and **bridging** over a corner. **A tow gap wide enough to leave a 1 mm resin streak costs 10-20 % of the local compression strength, and a resin-rich pocket under a lifted tow is a crack starter.**

**Dry preforming** is what makes infusion economical: **non-crimp fabric** (layers of unidirectional 0°, 90° and ±45° stitched together with a thread, at 300-1200 gsm, which is 20 % cheaper than a woven fabric of the same properties and 10 % better in compression because the fibres are not crimped), **2.5-D weaving** (the warp threads loop down through the thickness, giving a fabric that holds together when it is cut and can be draped over a 300 mm radius), **3-D weaving and braiding** (a real through-thickness fibre, which multiplies the mode-I fracture toughness three to five times and is the only way to make a composite part you can drill), and **thermoplastic net binders** (a 3 % polyethylene powder dusted between the plies and melted to hold the stack together). **Every one of these costs strength in the fibre direction and buys it in damage tolerance, which is exactly the trade a designer should be making consciously.**

**The processes that are not lay-up at all:** **filament winding** (a saturated tow is wound onto a rotating mandrel at a computed angle, at 1-3 m/min, and it makes every pressure vessel, rocket motor case and pipe in existence, because it is the only process that puts the fibre exactly where a hoop stress is); **pultrusion** (the fibre is pulled continuously through a resin bath, a preforming die and a 5-10 m heated die at $0 . 5 { - } 5 \ \mathrm { m / m i n }$ , and out comes a **60-70 % fibre** constant-section profile with the highest specific strength and stiffness of any structural process — the fibre direction properties of a pultruded carbon plate are within 10 % of an autoclave prepreg at a fifth of the cost, and its weakness is that everything transverse to the fibres is a third as strong and the part has to be a straight prism); **braiding** (the fibre carriers rotate around a moving mandrel, giving a tube or a spar with ±(20-70)° and no seams); and **the thermoplastic composite**, which is the industry's current obsession, because a PEEK-, PEKK- or PP-carbon tape has **no shelf life, no freezer, no cure reaction and no autoclave**, can be **welded** instead of bolted or co-cured, can be **formed in a 5-minute cycle** and can in principle be **re-melted and recycled** — at the cost of a melt viscosity so high that impregnating a fibre bundle takes minutes rather than seconds and a processing temperature of $4 0 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ .
:::

## 6. What goes wrong, how you find it, and what it costs

::: definition The defect list, and the three that actually kill structures {#def-defects}
A composite or moulded part can contain almost anything, and the industry has learned — expensively, and mostly from aircraft — that most defects are benign and a few are not.

**Benign, mostly:** porosity below 1 %; a resin-rich band; a colour or gloss variation; a witness mark; a sink mark 0.1 mm deep on a non-visible face; a fibre-end at the surface; a slight thickness variation from a bridged ply. **Serious:** a **dry spot** (an un-impregnated region, which is a delamination that has not started yet); a **through-thickness wrinkle**; a **void above 2 %**; a **gap or overlap** in a placed laminate; a **foreign object** (a bleeder-fibre, a scrap of release film, a glove — the classic cause of a fuselage skin rejection); a **kissing bond** (a face-to-face contact that transmits no tension and shows nothing on a through-transmission ultrasonic scan).

**The three that kill structures are the wrinkle, the delamination and the impact.**

**The wrinkle.** A 5° out-of-plane fibre wrinkle in a compression flange — from a bridged ply over a corner radius, a mis-registered tape, or a preform that was draped rather than cut — **reduces the compressive strength by 40-60 %**, and it does so by making the fibres buckle locally at a strain of 0.2 % while the rest of the part is at 0.1 %. **It is nearly invisible to the eye, it is nearly invisible to a through-transmission ultrasonic scan, and it is the single largest cause of composite structural test failures in development.** The controls are geometric (a corner radius of at least 5 times the laminate thickness, and a ply-drop taper), procedural (a **full-side** lay-up against a template rather than a **tool-side** one, a **trellis** or a **slit** to let a fabric shear without buckling), and inspectographic (a **digital-image-correlation** strain map during a proof load, in which a wrinkle shows as a strain anomaly long before it cracks).

**The barely-visible impact damage.** A dropped wrench, a tooling drop, a hailstorm, a runway stone. **The design requirement for primary structure is that a 6.5 J impact on a 2 mm panel (3.25 J/mm) — which leaves a dent you have to feel for with your fingernail — must not reduce the compression strength by more than half.** It usually does: the damage is a **subsurface delamination 15-25 mm across** under a 3 mm dent, with a **transverse fibre crack** at the impact point, and the compression-after-impact strength falls from 500 MPa to 240-300 MPa. **This is why every composite airframe has a documented inspection task with a tap test and an ultrasonic scan, why composite structures are designed with a "no-growth" or a "safe-life" philosophy rather than the metal structure's "crack growth" one, and why the biggest single technical argument about the 787 was not the weight but the inspection interval.**

**The delamination** is the composite's own failure mode, because there is nothing through the thickness except resin. Its resistance is $G _ { \mathrm { I C } } = 3 0 0 { - } 5 0 0 \ \mathrm { J / m ^ { 2 } }$ for a toughened aerospace epoxy (and 100 for a brittle one, which is why the 1970s boron and the early carbon laminates were such disappointing structures), and it is raised by toughening the resin with thermoplastic particles, by co-curing rather than secondary-bonding, by through-thickness fibres, and by **z-pinning** — driving 0.2 mm pins through the thickness at 5 mm pitch, which costs 3 % of the compression strength and doubles the delamination growth life.

**And the environment.** A composite's allowables are quoted **"hot-wet"**: 1 % absorbed moisture and $9 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ , at which the matrix is near its glass transition, the compression strength is 30-50 % below the dry value and the open-hole strength 25 % below. **A laminate that has been picked at with a screwdriver, sanded with the wrong disc, or left in a bilge is a different structure. And carbon fibre against aluminium is a galvanic couple with 0.6 V of driving force in salt water: the separator is a glass-ply, a sealant bead, and anodising plus primer on the metal — the single most-repeated maintenance instruction on every composite airframe.**
:::

::: definition Inspection and cost: where the argument actually is {#def-econ}
**Inspection.** The workhorse is **through-transmission ultrasonics**: a 1-5 MHz pair of immersion transducers either side of the part, water-coupled, scanned at 0.5-1 m²/min, giving a **C-scan** in which a delamination is a black blob and a dry spot is a black region with a fuzzy edge. It finds anything that reflects or absorbs sound, which is everything except a **kissing bond** and a **planar wrinkle parallel to the surface** — the two defects that have driven twenty years of research into nonlinear ultrasound, thermography and, in production, **in-situ monitoring** (a dielectric or ultrasonic sensor in the tool that watches the resin flow and the cure in real time, and which is now standard on a blade-root infusion line). **The tap test** — a coin, or a calibrated hammer with an accelerometer — finds a 20 mm delamination under 2 mm of surface in the hands of a trained inspector and is the only inspection most of the world's composite structures ever get. **Proof testing** (load to 1.2 times limit load with strain gauges and acoustic emission monitoring) catches the gross mis-manufacture, and **the residual-strength test on a sacrificial panel from every autoclave load** is what ties the parts to the process.

**Cost.** The honest numbers, per kilogram of finished structure: **steel £1.5-2, aluminium £6-10, a machined titanium part £150-400, a wet-wound carbon tube £25-50, an infusion-moulded carbon panel £60-120, an autoclave-cured prepreg primary structure £150-300.** **The fibre is 50-70 % of the material cost** (a standard-modulus aerospace carbon tow at £20-30/kg, an automotive intermediate-modulus at £12-20, a wind-turbine tow at £8-12, an epoxy resin at £6-10, a dry glass fabric at £2-3), and **at low volume the labour is 60-70 % of everything**. Which produces the crossover that decides every composite business case:

$$
c ( N ) \; = \; { \frac { F _ { \mathrm { tool } } } { N } } + c _ { \mathrm { unit } } \qquad \text{with} \qquad F _ { \mathrm { tool } } ^ { \mathrm { steel } } \approx 1 . 5 \times 1 0 ^ { 6 } , \quad c _ { \mathrm { unit } } ^ { \mathrm { steel } } \approx \pounds 4 5
$$

**A stamped steel car outer panel costs £45 a part with a £1.5 M press tool; the composite equivalent costs £180 with a £400 k autoclave tool. The composite tooling is cheaper at any volume and the composite part is four times the cost — so composites live in the low-volume, high-value corner of every market, and the only way out of that corner is a shorter cure, not a cheaper fibre.** **This is the real content of the "why is there no carbon-fibre car" question: not the £20/kg fibre, but the 15-minute cure against the 4-second press stroke, the 20,000-per-year line against the 300,000-per-year line, and the body shop that cannot be repaired with a hammer, a spot welder and a filler.**

A nylon 6,6 melt has a power-law index $n = 0 . 3 0$ . By what factor is its apparent viscosity lower at $1 0 { , } 0 0 0 \ \mathrm { s ^ { - 1 } }$ than at $1 0 \ \mathrm { s ^ { - 1 } } \mathrm { ? }$ (Enter the factor to the nearest whole number.)
::: solution
$\eta ( 1 0 ^ { 4 } ) / \eta ( 1 0 ) = ( 1 0 ^ { 3 } ) ^ { \, n - 1 } = 1 0 ^ { 3 \times ( - 0 . 7 0 ) } = 1 0 ^ { - 2 . 1 } = 1 / 1 2 6$ , so the factor is ${ \bf 1 2 6 }$ . **Two and a half decades of shear rate buy two and a half decades of viscosity — which is why a moulding machine can push a 100 Pa·s melt through a 0.4 mm gate without needing a hydraulic press, and why the "viscosity" in a mould-flow analysis is a field with 40 values in it, not a number.**
:::

::: exercise The wall-thickness decision {#ex-wall level=2 check="8.8"}
A part moulds with an 18 s cooling time at a $4 \ \mathrm { m m }$ wall. Redesigning it with ribs lets the wall go to $2 . 8 \ \mathrm { m m }$ at the same stiffness. What is the new cooling time? (Enter seconds to one decimal place.)
::: solution
$t \propto s ^ { 2 }$ , so $t = 1 8 \times ( 2 . 8 / 4 ) ^ { 2 } = 1 8 \times 0 . 4 9 = { \bf 8 . 8 \ s }$ . **Nine seconds saved on a 26 s cycle is a 35 % higher output, bought with a rib pattern and no new tooling steel — which is why the mould-flow review of a part is nearly always a conversation about ribs, and why "make it uniform and make it thin" is the first sentence of every moulding design guide.**
:::

::: exercise Blown film orientation {#ex-bur level=3 check="24"}
A film die has a $6 0 \ \mathrm { m m }$ flat, the bubble is collapsed to $1 8 0 \ \mathrm { m m }$ flat, and the take-up draws the film to one eighth of the die gap. Find the blow-up ratio, the take-up ratio and the total area draw.
::: solution
$\mathrm { B U R } = 1 8 0 / 6 0 = 3$ (the hoop orientation), $\mathrm { T U R } = 8$ (the machine-direction orientation), and the area draw is $3 \times 8 = { \bf 2 4 }$ . **Twenty-four times, split 3:8 between the two directions — which is why a carrier bag tears along the machine direction and not across it, why a "balanced" film needs BUR × TUR to be split as nearly as the application allows, and why the two ratios are the film maker's only two property controls.**
:::

::: exercise Extruder energy {#ex-se level=2 check="0.2"}
A single-screw extruder with a 30 kW drive runs at $1 5 0 \ \mathrm { k g / h }$ . What is its specific mechanical energy in kWh/kg?
::: solution
$\mathrm { SME } = 3 0 / 1 5 0 = { \bf 0 . 2 \ k W h / k g }$ . **Against a thermodynamic minimum of about $0 . 1 7 \ \mathrm { k W h / k g }$ for a nylon, that is a well-pumped extruder; against $0 . 0 5 { - } 0 . 0 8 \ \mathrm { k W h / k g }$ for a polyethylene (whose melting enthalpy is small and whose $c _ { p } \Delta T$ is short) it is a wasteful one. Ask for this number before you ask for the output: a barrier screw, a deeper channel and a cooled barrel typically take 15-25 % off it.**

A plug assist, a mechanical counter-mould, or a deeper radius that lets the sheet slide before it touches will buy back 30-50 % of it; a designer who specifies a 150 mm-deep box from a 3 mm sheet without asking this question has specified a part that will crack at the corner.**

(Enter the factor.)
::: solution
Every term in the logarithm is a temperature difference and none of them scales with the part, so the whole bracket is constant for geometrically similar parts at the same temperatures, and $t \propto s ^ { 2 } / \alpha$ . Halving the wall gives a factor of ${ \bf 4 }$ . **The same argument, applied to a diffusion-limited process of any kind, is why a 25 mm wall is not six times the cycle of a 4 mm one but forty times, and why the answer to "the part is too expensive" is nearly always on the drawing.**
:::
:::

::: exercise Derive the infusion law {#ex-derive-darcy level=4 check="4"}
Integrate Darcy's law for a one-dimensional, constant-pressure impregnation of a preform of porosity $\phi$ and state the infusion time's dependence on $\mu$ , $L$ , $K$ and $\Delta p$ . By what factor does the time fall if the resin's viscosity goes from $0 . 4$ to $0 . 1 \ \mathrm { P a \, s ? }$
::: solution
Darcy's velocity is $v = - ( K / \phi \mu ) \, \mathrm { d } p / \mathrm { d } x$ ; with a fixed pressure drop $\Delta p$ across a front at $L$ , the gradient is $\Delta p / L$ , so $\phi \, \mathrm { d } L / \mathrm { d } t = K \Delta p / ( \phi \mu L ) \times \phi$ , i.e. $L \, \mathrm { d } L = ( K \Delta p / \phi \mu ) \, \mathrm { d } t$ , and

$$
L ^ { 2 } = { \frac { 2 K \Delta p } { \phi \mu } } \, t \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad t = { \frac { \phi \, \mu \, L ^ { 2 } } { 2 K \, \Delta p } }
$$

**The time is linear in viscosity, quadratic in distance, and inversely proportional to the driving pressure.** The factor is $0 . 4 / 0 . 1 = { \bf 4 }$ . **Which is why the resin's viscosity at the infusion temperature, measured after 30 minutes at that temperature, is the number that decides whether a mould can be filled at all — and why doubling the vacuum from 0.5 to 1 bar buys only a factor of two, while heating the resin by $2 5 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ can buy a factor of four.**
:::
:::

::: exercise Vitrification {#ex-vitr level=3 check="80"}
A different epoxy has $T _ { g 0 } = 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ , $T _ { g \infty } = 2 1 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ and $\lambda = 0 . 5$ , and is cured at $1 4 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ . What maximum degree of cure can it reach? (Enter the percentage.)
::: solution
Setting $T _ { g } = 1 4 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ gives $1 4 0 / 2 1 0 = 0 . 6 6 7 = 0 . 5 \alpha / ( 1 - 0 . 5 \alpha )$ , so $0 . 5 \alpha = 0 . 6 6 7 - 0 . 3 3 3 \alpha$ and $\alpha = 0 . 6 6 7 / 0 . 8 3 3 = { \bf 0 . 8 0 }$ , i.e. ${ \bf 8 0 \ \% }$ . **The remaining 20 % never reacts at that temperature. A $1 9 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ post-cure takes the same part to $\alpha = 0 . 9 5$ and a $T _ { g }$ of $1 9 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ , and the difference between the two states is 40 % of the hot-wet compression strength and the whole of the solvent resistance.**
:::
:::

::: quiz A moulding has a 0.3 mm sink mark over an internal boss. Which change fixes it at source? {#quiz-sink}
- [ ] Raise the hold pressure by 20 %
- [ ] Extend the hold time by 3 s
- [x] Hollow the boss and drop its rib to 60 % of the wall thickness
- [ ] Raise the melt temperature by 15 °C
::: solution
**A sink mark is a thick section shrinking with nothing behind it to feed it, so the fix is geometric. More packing helps only while the gate is frozen open, and once the gate has frozen (which is why the hold time is set to the gate freeze time) extra pressure just makes the part heavier, the stresses higher and the demould harder. A hotter melt shrinks more, not less. Hollowing the boss removes the reservoir of shrinking material that caused the sink in the first place — and it is free, because it is a drawing change.**
:::
:::

::: quiz An infusion moulding passes every visual check but fails compression after impact at 60 % of the allowables. What is the most likely cause? {#quiz-void}
- [ ] The tool temperature was too low
- [ ] The fibre volume fraction was too high
- [x] Dry cores inside the fibre tows from an infusion that ran too fast
- [ ] The resin was over-cured
::: solution
**The macro flow front reached every gate, so the part looks wet; but the intra-tow permeability is a million times lower than the inter-tow, so at a fast front the bundles never filled. Intra-tow voids are the classic cause of a compression and compression-after-impact shortfall with everything else nominal. A low tool temperature slows the cure but does not leave voids; a high fibre fraction raises the strength; an over-cure is a different failure (brittle, and visible as a low elongation).** **The diagnosis is a metallographic section of a witness panel, polished and etched, in which a dry tow shows as a white ring of unfilled filaments — and the cure is a slower front, a lower viscosity, a soak dwell and back-pressure.**
:::
:::

## Where this lesson leads

- **[[materials-science/material-families|Material families]]** is where the polymer families, their $T _ { \mathrm { g } }$ and $T _ { \mathrm { m } }$ , and their chemical resistance come from; this lesson is what happens to them at $2 5 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ and 1000 bar.
- **[[heat-transfer/transient-conduction|Transient conduction]]** is the mathematics behind every cooling-time, cycle-time and cure-ramp number in this lesson — the Fourier number is the whole of injection moulding economics.
- **[[fluid-mechanics/fluid-properties|Fluid properties]]** and **[[fluid-mechanics/pipe-flow|Pipe flow]]** supply the pressure-drop machinery that a power-law melt breaks; the die design of §3 is Hagen-Poiseuille with $\mu$ replaced by $K { \dot { \gamma } } ^ { n - 1 }$ .
- **[[materials-science/phase-transformations|Phase transformations]]** is the Avrami kinetics of a polymer's crystallisation, which is why a mould's cooling rate decides the shrinkage, the haze and the chemical resistance of a semi-crystalline part.
- **[[solid-mechanics/composites|Composites]]** is the mechanics that the ply book is written against — classical lamination theory, the $A B D$ matrix, the Tsai-Wu and Puck criteria, and the energy-release rates behind the delamination of §6.
- **[[materials-science/failure|Failure]]** covers the creep, the stress cracking and the fatigue of a thermoplastic part, and the sub-critical delamination growth of a laminate.
- **[[manufacturing/additive-manufacturing|Additive manufacturing]]** is where the conformal cooling inserts of §2 are printed, and where the same thermoplastic and thermoset chemistry appears as a filament, a photopolymer or a powder bed.
- **[[manufacturing/metrology-quality|Metrology, quality and automation]]** is where the C-scan, the in-line vision and the statistical process control of a 2-million-shot tool live.
- **[[materials-science/corrosion|Corrosion]]** covers the galvanic pair of carbon against aluminium and the moisture that plasticises every epoxy in service.

::: history
**1839-1844.** **Charles Goodyear**, after a decade in and out of debtors' prison, finds that natural rubber mixed with sulfur and heated turns into a material that stays elastic when hot and will not go brittle when cold; the 1844 patent makes **vulcanisation** a process and creates an industry still arguing about cure schedules 180 years later. **1862.** **Alexander Parkes** shows "Parkesine" at the London International Exhibition — a nitrocellulose lacquer squeezed through a die, and the first **extrusion** of a polymer in history. **1870.** Hyatt's **celluloid** brings **compression moulding** of a thermoplastic at 120-150 °C and 20 MPa, and makes the billiard ball, the comb and, twenty years later, photographic film. **1907-1912.** **Leo Baekeland's Bakelite**: a phenol-formaldehyde resin driven to a **B-stage** and then cured in a heated mould under pressure — the first wholly synthetic thermoset, the first material that could not be melted, and the birth of the phrase "moulding powder". **1920-1935.** **Staudinger** persuades chemistry that a polymer is a **molecule** ten thousand atoms long rather than a colloid, which is the discovery every sentence of this lesson rests on; **Carothers at DuPont** runs the work that yields **neoprene** (1931) and **nylon 6,6** (1935), and *whisk, extrude and spin* become industrial verbs. **1933-1940.** ICI's **Gibson and Fawcett** make **polyethylene** by accident at 1400 bar and 170 °C (1933); **radar cable insulation** for the Royal Air Force makes it a commodity by 1940, and the blown film and the plastic bag follow within a decade. **1941-1953.** **Whinfield and Dickson** patent polyethylene terephthalate at the Calico Printers' Association (1941); **DuPont** buys the licence, spends a decade on the dyeing problem and launches **Dacron** (1953), turning a brittle bottle resin into the most-produced fibre on earth. **1953-1955.** **Karl Ziegler** finds the organometallic catalysts that make linear polyethylene and **Giulio Natta** turns the same chemistry into isotactic polypropylene; the two share the 1963 Nobel Prize and, between them, now account for half of all plastic made. **1946.** **James Watson Hendry** builds the first **reciprocating-screw injection moulding machine**: for the first time one screw melts, mixes and meters the material, the melt comes out homogeneous, thin wall and glass-filled moulding become possible, and injection moulding becomes the process it is today. **The reciprocating screw is to plastics what the covered electrode was to welding.** **1940s-1950s.** **Fibre glass goes structural**: the wartime radar radome, the glass-boat boom of the 1950s, and the **filament-wound** Polaris motor case (1959) — the first structure designed outward from the fibre rather than machined inward from the solid. **1960s.** **The aerospace prepreg system** is invented around the commercially failed **boron fibre**: the **autoclave cure, the peel ply, the bleeder, the vacuum bag, the C-scan and the allowable-property system** all date from it, and every structural composite in service still uses them. **1961-1972.** **Roger Bacon** at Union Carbide makes high-modulus carbon fibre by stretching a rayon precursor (1961); **Osamu Suzuki's** team at **Toray** industrialises the **PAN** route in Japan (1969-71), and in 1972 Boeing buys Toray fibre for the 737's passenger-floor panels — the beginning of the 230 GPa standard-modulus PAN fibre on which the 787 is built. **1969.** **The Boeing 747** flies with boron-reinforced stringers and well under 1 % composite by weight, and the industry concludes that composites are a niche, low-rate, expensive material; it defends that conclusion for twenty years. **1966-1988.** **The moulding revolution**: hot runners, **H. M. Outwater's** computer simulation of mould filling at Xerox (1966) and the **Wang and Kennedy** models that became the industry's mould-flow software, the valve gate, the **structural-foam** and **gas-assisted** processes, and the robot at the mould's front door, which took the operator out of the cycle and the cycle time down by a third. **1983-1988.** **The Airbus A310-300's carbon-fibre tail fin** (1983) and then the **A320's** horizontal stabiliser: the first primary structures in carbon prepreg on an airliner, and the start of the "50 % by weight" trajectory. **1988-1995.** **The closed mould moves ashore**: resin-transfer moulding of the Corvette body (1972 onwards), the **SCRIMP** and **VARTM** patents of the late 1980s, and the emissions rules that finally killed the open lay-up — driven as much by styrene in the air as by the part in the hand. **1990s-2000s.** **The wind turbine blade** grows from a 20 m hand lay-up to a 60 m infused shell with a single-shot resin path and a six-hour cure: the largest composite structures ever made by volume, and the industry whose demand for cheap tow made carbon fibre affordable for aerospace. **2008.** **The Boeing 787**: 50 % composites by weight, fuselage barrels laid and wound in four automated cells and cured in the largest autoclaves ever built, and the first airframe certified against a **barely-visible-damage** requirement instead of a crack-arrest one. **2013.** **The BMW i3** puts a 4-minute wet compression moulding and a 24-hour structure on sale, and fails commercially — the most expensive lesson ever taught about the difference between a material that can be made quickly and a factory that can. **2013-2015.** **The Airbus A350 XWB** flies and enters service at 53 % composites, with a wing cover 7 m wide and 50 m long, and the question moves from "can it be done" to "can it be done at sixty aeroplanes a month". **2000s-2020s.** **The thermoplastic turn and the reckoning**: out-of-autoclave prepreg cures the wind industry's blade cost, laser- and ultrasonic-welded thermoplastic airframes leave the demonstrator stage, and 400 million tonnes a year of polymer are produced against a cumulative recycling rate of 9 % — the discovery that a material designed to be shaped once at 250 °C is very hard to shape again.

::: summary
**A polymer melt is shear thinning, elastic, temperature sensitive and extensionally hardening, and its Deborah number is never far below 1.** $\eta = K { \dot { \gamma } } ^ { \, n - 1 }$ with $n = 0 . 2 { - } 0 . 8$ , so a melt thins by 30-100 times between the barrel and the gate; $\eta \propto \exp ( E _ { \eta } / R T )$ , so $2 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ is a factor of two; and the melt swells, climbs the rod and remembers. **There is no single viscosity.**
**Injection moulding is four processes in thirty seconds, and the cooling is 60-70 % of it.** $t _ { \mathrm { cool } } = ( s ^ { 2 } / \pi ^ { 2 } \alpha ) \ln [ { \frac { 8 } { \pi ^ { 2 } } } ( T _ { \mathrm { melt } } - T _ { w } ) / ( T _ { e } - T _ { w } ) ]$ , every plastic has $\alpha \approx 0 . 1 \ \mathrm { m m ^ { 2 } / s }$ , and the wall thickness is a **square** on the cycle. Uniform wall, ribs not thickening, and a mould temperature chosen on the drawing.
**The fountain flow writes the part's anisotropy into it.** A frozen skin, an oriented shear layer and a transverse core; a shrinkage of 0.3 % along the flow against 0.9 % across it; a weld line at 20-40 % of the bulk strength with glass in it; and a sink mark wherever the last point to freeze is. **Move the thick section, then move the gate, then argue with the machine.**
**Extrusion is a screw that is three machines and a die that is a pressure drop.** Drag minus pressure flow against $Q \propto \Delta p / \mu$ , an operating point that moves with viscosity, a melt that swells 20-100 % out of the die and must be drawn down to compensate, and a wall shear stress of $0 . 1 \ \mathrm { M P a }$ beyond which the surface fails.
**A thermoset is a race with its own gel point, and it is won or lost on the glass transition.** $\mathrm { d } \alpha / \mathrm { d } t = ( k _ { 1 } + k _ { 2 } \alpha ^ { m } ) ( 1 - \alpha ) ^ { n }$ doubles every $2 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ , gives $5 5 0 \ \mathrm { J / g }$ , and **stops when $T _ { g }$ reaches the cure temperature** — so a $1 2 0 \ \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ cure can never exceed 77 % and a part cured to a time rather than a state fails hot.
**Composites are made by getting resin inside the tows, and the two flows are a million times apart.** Darcy gives $L \propto { \sqrt { K \Delta p t / \phi \mu } }$ , the inter-tow permeability is $1 0 ^ { - 9 } \ \mathrm { m ^ { 2 } }$ and the intra-tow $1 0 ^ { - 1 5 } \ \mathrm { m ^ { 2 } }$ , so a fast front makes a wet-looking part with dry bundles; and the cure is a soak, a low viscosity and a back-pressure.
**Three defects kill structures: the wrinkle, the delamination and the impact.** A 5° wrinkle costs half the compression strength and shows on nothing; a 6.5 J drop makes a 20 mm delamination you cannot see and halves the compression-after-impact; and the allowables are quoted hot and wet for a reason.
**The cost of a composite is the cure, not the fibre.** £20/kg for the tow, £150-300/kg for the cured autoclave part, 60-70 % of the cost in labour at low volume, and a crossover against stamped steel at 8,000 a year that no cheaper fibre has ever moved.
:::
