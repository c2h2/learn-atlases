::: warning A gear drives both ways
A cam can push and cannot pull, and everything in [[mechanisms/cams]] is an argument about what happens when that asymmetry is pushed hard enough. A gear has no such problem: **its teeth can drive in both directions**, so a gear pair transmits torque through a reversal, through a stall, through an oscillation, with no spring, no gravity and no second mechanism holding the contact. That, together with an exactly constant speed ratio, is why the gear is one of the most widely used machine elements, and why its failure — a spalled tooth flank or a cracked tooth root — takes a machine out of service completely.

The price is a chain of constraints that cams do not have to obey. **Two gears must have the same module and the same pressure angle**, which in practice means they are generated by the same basic rack. Their centre distance must be held closely: opening it does not change the speed ratio of an involute pair, but it adds backlash and shortens the path of contact. And their tooth count is bounded below by interference, which is why the smallest gear in almost every gearbox is the one that decides the module.
:::

## What you already know

- **Rolling without slipping** — the pitch circles of a gear pair are the two circles that roll on each other, exactly as the wheels of [[mechanisms/mechanisms-mobility]]'s friction-drive discussion did, with the difference that the teeth make the rolling exact rather than dependent on friction.
- **Transmission angle and pressure angle** — the cam's $\tan \psi = s' / \left( r_p + s \right)$ in [[mechanisms/cams]] is a special case of the gear's pressure angle, and the gear's is *constant* while the cam's wanders through the lift.
- **Hertz contact stress** — $p_0 = \sqrt{ F E^{*} / ( \pi b R ) }$ from [[mechanisms/cams]] governs gear pitting exactly as it governed cam wear, with one crucial difference: the relative radius of curvature $R$ at a gear contact *changes continuously* through the mesh, and is smallest near the start of contact, low on the pinion flank.
- **Instant centres** — the pitch point of a gear pair is the instant centre of the two links, and Kennedy's theorem in [[mechanisms/velocity-analysis]] is what forces it to stay put.
- **Equivalent inertia and torque** — $T_{\mathrm{eq}} = T / \left( \eta i \right)$ and $J_{\mathrm{eq}} = J / i^{2}$ from [[mechanisms/acceleration-analysis]], which is why a gearbox is chosen as much for the inertia it hides as for the speed it produces.

## The law of gearing: what makes a tooth profile work

::: definition The law of gearing {#def-law}
Two tooth profiles are **conjugate** — they transmit a constant angular velocity ratio — if and only if **the common normal to the two surfaces at every point of contact passes through a fixed point on the line of centres**. That fixed point is the **pitch point** $P$, and the two circles through $P$ centred on the two axes are the pitch circles.

Equivalently, with $O_1 P = r_1$, $O_2 P = r_2$ and $N_1$, $N_2$ the feet of the perpendiculars from the two centres to the common normal,

$$
\boxed{ \; \frac{\omega_1}{\omega_2} = \frac{O_2 P}{O_1 P} = \frac{r_2}{r_1} = \frac{O_2 N_2}{O_1 N_1} \; }
$$

(the last equality is similar triangles $O_1 N_1 P$ and $O_2 N_2 P$). The ratio is constant only if $P$ does not move, and the simplest way to guarantee that is to make $N_1$ and $N_2$ *fixed points* — that is, to make the common normal a **fixed straight line tangent to two fixed circles**. Those circles are the base circles, and the profiles that do it are involutes.
:::

::: exercise Derivation: the involute as the profile with a straight line of action {#deriv-involute level=3}
::: solution
Take a common normal that is a fixed line $\ell$ tangent to a circle of radius $r_{b1}$ about $O_1$ and to a circle of radius $r_{b2}$ about $O_2$. The contact point may be anywhere along $\ell$, so the ratio $O_1 N_1 / O_2 N_2 = r_{b1} / r_{b2}$ is constant by construction (the line $\ell$ cuts the line of centres at the fixed point dividing $O_1 O_2$ in the ratio $r_{b1} : r_{b2}$), and the law of gearing is satisfied at every contact point along $\ell$.

Now ask what profile on link 1 has the property that its normal at the contact point is always $\ell$. Seen from link 1, the line $\ell$ rolls without slipping on the base circle, and the contact point is a point fixed on that rolling line; its path relative to link 1 is the profile. **Unwrap a taut string from a circle of radius $r_b$: the free end traces the involute**, and the parametric form falls straight out of the construction, with $t$ the unwound angle,

$$
x = r_b \left( \cos t + t \sin t \right) , \qquad y = r_b \left( \sin t - t \cos t \right)
$$

so that the radius and the local pressure angle at parameter $t$ are

$$
r = r_b \sqrt{ 1 + t^{2} } , \qquad \cos \phi = \frac{r_b}{r} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{ 1 + t^{2} } } , \qquad t = \tan \phi
$$

and the **involute function**, the polar angle of the involute point measured from the start of the involute on the base circle, is

$$
\operatorname{inv} \phi = \tan \phi - \phi
$$

Two facts follow and both matter in practice. **The involute of a circle depends on one parameter only, $r_b$** — and as $r_b \to \infty$ it becomes a straight line, so the conjugate of every involute gear of a given module and pressure angle is the same straight-sided rack. That is what lets a single rack-type tool generate gears of every tooth count. **And the involute exists only outside its base circle**, which is the geometric root of every one of the limits in this lesson: minimum teeth, undercutting, and the fact that a gear with too few teeth is not merely weak but undercut.
:::
:::

## Why the involute won

Two families of conjugate profiles have been used in quantity, and the reasons one displaced the other are worth knowing because both are still made.

**The cycloidal tooth** has an epicycloid above the pitch circle (the curve traced by a point on a circle rolling *outside* the pitch circle) and a hypocycloid below it (a circle rolling *inside*). Gears whose profiles are generated by the same rolling circle are conjugate, so a set can be made interchangeable, and the convex addendum always works against a concave dedendum, which keeps the contact stress low. It was the dominant form in millwork and machinery well into the nineteenth century and is still the usual form in clock and watch trains. Its weaknesses are practical: the profile is a compound curve that needs a different form for every tooth count, the pressure angle varies through the mesh, and **the velocity ratio is exact only at the theoretical centre distance**.

**The involute** has none of those weaknesses. Its line of action is a fixed straight line, so the pressure angle is constant; and because the ratio is fixed by the base circles alone ($\omega_1 / \omega_2 = r_{b2} / r_{b1}$), **an involute pair keeps an exactly constant velocity ratio when the centre distance changes** — only backlash and contact ratio change. Most important for manufacture, the involute rack has **straight-sided teeth**, so a rack cutter, a hob (a rack wrapped helically round a cylinder) or a grinding wheel with straight flanks generates every tooth count of a given module and pressure angle. That generating property, more than any advantage in strength, is why the involute became the standard form for power gearing by the end of the nineteenth century.

The consequences you pay for: the involute's relative radius of curvature is small near the start of contact, the tooth is narrow at the root of a small pinion, and the tip of a mating gear can run off the end of the involute at the base circle — the interference problem of the next sections.

## The geometry of a spur gear

::: definition Standard proportions {#def-proportions}
A spur gear is defined by its **module** $m = d / N$ (millimetres per tooth, the SI replacement for the diametral pitch $P = 25.4 / m$ still used in inch practice), its tooth count $N$ and its pressure angle $\phi$. The standard full-depth proportions, from the standard basic rack (ISO 53 and its AGMA equivalent), are

| Feature | Symbol | Value | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pitch diameter | $d$ | $m N$ | Where the pitch circles roll |
| Base diameter | $d_b$ | $m N \cos \phi$ | The involute's generating circle |
| Addendum | $a$ | $1.0 \, m$ | Pitch circle to tip |
| Dedendum | $b_d$ | $1.25 \, m$ | Pitch circle to root; $0.25 m$ is radial clearance |
| Tip diameter | $d_a$ | $m ( N + 2 )$ | |
| Root diameter | $d_f$ | $m ( N - 2.5 )$ | |
| Circular pitch | $p$ | $\pi m$ | Arc, pitch circle to pitch circle |
| Base pitch | $p_b$ | $\pi m \cos \phi$ | **The pitch that actually matters** |
| Tooth thickness | $s$ | $\pi m / 2$ | On the pitch circle, for zero backlash |
| Centre distance | $C$ | $m ( N_1 + N_2 ) / 2$ | Standard, unshifted |

**The base pitch, not the circular pitch, is the quantity that governs whether two gears mesh.** Two involute gears mesh correctly only if their base pitches are equal, and since $p_b = \pi m \cos \phi$, for standard gears that means equal module *and* equal pressure angle. (Gears with different $m$ and $\phi$ but the same $m \cos \phi$ would in principle mesh, at a non-standard operating pressure angle, but they are not cut by the same standard tool.) A $14.5^{\circ}$ gear and a $20^{\circ}$ gear of the same module will not mesh properly, and a spur gear will not mesh with a helical gear of the same normal module and pressure angle, because the helical gear's transverse pitch is larger.
:::

::: example Geometry of a 24-tooth, module-4 pinion {#ex-geometry}
Compute the diameters, the pitches and the tooth thickness at the base circle for $N = 24$, $m = 4\ \mathrm{mm}$, $\phi = 20^{\circ}$.

**Answer.** $d = 96$, $d_a = 104$, $d_f = 86\ \mathrm{mm}$; $p = 12.566$, $p_b = 11.808\ \mathrm{mm}$; base-circle tooth thickness $s_b = 7.25\ \mathrm{mm}$. With a $72$-tooth wheel, $C = 192\ \mathrm{mm}$.

::: solution
$$
d_b = 96 \cos 20^{\circ} = 90.21\ \mathrm{mm} , \qquad p = \pi \times 4 = 12.566\ \mathrm{mm} , \qquad p_b = 12.566 \times 0.93969 = 11.808\ \mathrm{mm}
$$

The tooth thickness at any radius $r_x$ follows from the involute function: the half-angle that the tooth subtends at the centre is $s / d + \operatorname{inv} \phi$ at the base circle and decreases by $\operatorname{inv} \phi_x$ as one moves out to radius $r_x$, so

$$
s_x = 2 r_x \left[ \frac{s}{d} + \operatorname{inv} \phi - \operatorname{inv} \phi_x \right] , \qquad \cos \phi_x = \frac{d_b}{d_x}
$$

At the base circle $\phi_x = 0$, $\operatorname{inv} 0 = 0$, and with $s = \pi m / 2 = 6.283\ \mathrm{mm}$, $\operatorname{inv} 20^{\circ} = 0.36397 - 0.34907 = 0.014904$,

$$
s_b = 2 \times 45.105 \times \left[ \frac{6.283}{96} + 0.014904 \right] = 90.21 \times 0.080354 = 7.249\ \mathrm{mm} = 1.812 \, m
$$

**Compare with $1.571 \, m$ at the pitch circle: the involute tooth is 15% thicker at the base circle than at the pitch circle.** Going the other way, at the tip circle $r_a = 52\ \mathrm{mm}$ the local pressure angle is $\phi_a = \arccos ( 45.105 / 52 ) = 29.84^{\circ}$, $\operatorname{inv} \phi_a = 0.05284$, and

$$
s_a = 2 \times 52 \times \left[ 0.06545 + 0.014904 - 0.05284 \right] = 2.86\ \mathrm{mm} = 0.72 \, m
$$

So the tooth tapers from $1.81 \, m$ at the base circle to $0.72 \, m$ at the tip. The tip land shrinks as the tooth count falls or the addendum grows, and a common design rule asks for at least $0.25$–$0.4 \, m$ — which is one of the limits on how far profile shift can be pushed.
:::
:::

## Interference: the tooth count below which the gear is wrong

The involute exists only outside the base circle. Below the base circle the flank is not an involute at all — it is whatever the cutting tool left behind, and the tip of a rack-type cutter sweeps a trochoid relative to the blank. If the *other* gear's tip reaches inside the pinion's base circle — beyond the point where the line of action touches it — it would have to pass through the pinion's tooth. That is **interference**. In a generated gear the cutter, which behaves as such a mating rack, removes the offending material as it cuts: the root is **undercut**, the tooth is weakened at its most stressed section and part of the useful involute is lost. Unlike the other limits in this lesson it is not a matter of degree; it is geometry.

::: proposition Minimum teeth to avoid interference {#thm-minteeth}
For a pinion of $N_1$ teeth driving an external gear of $N_2 = G N_1$ teeth at standard centres, with pressure angle $\phi$ and addendum $a = 1.0 \, m$, interference begins when either tip circle crosses the tangency point of the line of action with the *other* base circle. Writing $r = m N / 2$, $r_a = r + m$, $r_b = r \cos \phi$ and $C = r_1 + r_2$, the condition that the gear's tip stay inside the line of action is

$$
\sqrt{ r_{a2}^{2} - r_{b2}^{2} } \; \le \; C \sin \phi
$$

which reduces, after squaring and cancelling, to

$$
\boxed{ \; N_1 \left( N_1 + 2 N_2 \right) \ \ge \ \frac{4 \left( N_2 + 1 \right)}{\sin^{2} \phi} \; } \qquad \text{and symmetrically} \qquad N_2 \left( N_2 + 2 N_1 \right) \ \ge \ \frac{4 \left( N_1 + 1 \right)}{\sin^{2} \phi}
$$

For a pinion against a **rack** ($G \to \infty$) this collapses to the famous

$$
\boxed{ \; N_{\min} = \frac{2}{\sin^{2} \phi} \; } \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad
\begin{cases} N_{\min} = 31.9 & \phi = 14.5^{\circ} \\[4pt] N_{\min} = 17.1 & \phi = 20^{\circ} \\[4pt] N_{\min} = 11.2 & \phi = 25^{\circ} \end{cases}
$$
:::

::: proof
The line of action is tangent to the pinion's base circle at the interference point $N_1$, a distance $r_1 \sin \phi$ from the pitch point $P$, and to the gear's base circle at $N_2$, a distance $r_2 \sin \phi$ from $P$; so $N_1 N_2 = C \sin \phi$. The gear's tip circle crosses the line of action at a distance $\sqrt{ r_{a2}^{2} - r_{b2}^{2} }$ from $N_2$, and contact stays on the involute of the pinion only if that point lies between $N_2$ and $N_1$, which is the first inequality. Substituting $r_{a2} = m ( N_2 + 2 ) / 2$, $r_{b2} = m N_2 \cos \phi / 2$ and $C = m ( N_1 + N_2 ) / 2$, multiplying by $4 / m^{2}$ and squaring,

$$
( N_2 + 2 )^{2} - N_2^{2} \cos^{2} \phi \le ( N_1 + N_2 )^{2} \sin^{2} \phi \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad 4 ( N_2 + 1 ) \le N_1 ( N_1 + 2 N_2 ) \sin^{2} \phi
$$

using $\cos^{2} \phi = 1 - \sin^{2} \phi$. Exchanging the roles of the two gears gives the second condition. For a rack, divide by $N_2$ and let $N_2 \to \infty$: $4 \le 2 N_1 \sin^{2} \phi$. Assumptions: unshifted gears at the standard centre distance, full-depth addendum $1.0 \, m$, and the sharp tip corner taken as the contacting point (tip rounding relaxes the limit slightly).
:::

caption: The rack case, $N_{\min} = 2 / \sin^{2} \phi$. **The curve is brutally steep at the low-pressure-angle end**: moving from $14.5^{\circ}$ to $20^{\circ}$ throws away fifteen teeth of minimum, which is a large part of why the older $14.5^{\circ}$ system gave way to $20^{\circ}$ as the general standard in the twentieth century. The $20^{\circ}$ value of 17.1 is the origin of the common rule of thumb of not cutting an unshifted pinion below about 17 teeth (a slight undercut at exactly 17 is usually tolerated), and the main reason profile shift exists is to go below it.

Find the smallest unshifted $20^{\circ}$ pinion for ratios $G = 1$, $G = 3$ and $G = \infty$, and check whether a $19 / 57$ pair is safe.

**Answer.** Solving $G N + 1 \le N^{2} ( 1 + 2 G ) \sin^{2} \phi / 4$ for $N$ (this is the first condition of [[#thm-minteeth]] with $N_2 = G N$; it is the binding one for $G \ge 1$):

| Ratio $G$ | Minimum $N_1$ | Binding constraint |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 12.32 → **13** | either, by symmetry |
| 3 | 14.98 → **15** | gear tip into pinion flank |
| $\infty$ (rack) | 17.10 → **18** | rack tip into pinion flank |

::: solution
The $19 / 57$ pair has $G = 3$ and $N_1 = 19 > 15$, so it is safe with four teeth to spare. Checking the geometry directly at module $3$ ($r_1 = 28.5$, $r_2 = 85.5$, $r_{a2} = 88.5$, $r_{b2} = 80.344\ \mathrm{mm}$, $C = 114\ \mathrm{mm}$), the gear's tip must stay short of the pinion's interference point:

$$
\sqrt{ r_{a2}^{2} - r_{b2}^{2} } = \sqrt{ 88.5^{2} - 80.344^{2} } = 37.11\ \mathrm{mm} \; < \; C \sin \phi = 114 \times 0.34202 = 38.99\ \mathrm{mm}
$$

**Two things in the table are worth dwelling on.** First, the minimum pinion tooth count *increases* with the gear ratio, from 13 at 1:1 to 18 against a rack — a small gear meshing with a big one is worse off than two identical ones, because the bigger the mating gear, the further its tip reaches along the line of action towards the pinion's interference point. Second, **the limit is geometric, not a strength limit**: below it the tooth is undercut at the root, which then also costs bending strength and part of the path of contact.
:::
:::

::: warning Profile shift is not a refinement, it is the standard solution
Every limit in the last section assumes the **standard rack**, with addendum exactly $1.0 \, m$ and the pitch circle of the gear tangent to the pitch line of the rack. Slide the rack away from the gear centre by $x m$ — a **profile shift** or **addendum modification** of $x$ — and the tooth you generate is thicker at the base, the tip is narrower, and the interference condition becomes

$$
N_{\min} ( x ) = \frac{ 2 \left( 1.0 - x \right)}{\sin^{2} \phi} \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad x = + 0.3 \ \text{brings} \ N_{\min} \ \text{from} \ 17.1 \ \text{to} \ 12.0
$$

Profile shift (addendum modification) exists largely to buy those five teeth: at module 3 they are the difference between a $51\ \mathrm{mm}$ and a $36\ \mathrm{mm}$ pinion, which can be the difference between a gearbox that fits and one that does not. Shifted gears are still cut with the standard tool, only at a different setting, but **they are no longer interchangeable with catalogue gears, the pinion's tip land gets narrower, and the mate must be designed with it.** A common arrangement is $x_1 = +x$ on the pinion and $x_2 = -x$ on the wheel, summing to zero so that the standard centre distance is kept; a non-zero sum changes the centre distance and the operating pressure angle, and is used deliberately to hit a given centre distance or to balance strength between the two gears.
:::

## Contact ratio: how many teeth are carrying

::: definition Contact ratio {#def-crr}
The **path of contact** is the length of the line of action swept by the contact point, from where the pinion's tip circle crosses it to where the gear's tip circle crosses it,

$$
L = \sqrt{ r_{a1}^{2} - r_{b1}^{2} } + \sqrt{ r_{a2}^{2} - r_{b2}^{2} } - C \sin \phi
$$

The **transverse contact ratio** is that length measured in base pitches,

$$
\boxed{ \; m_p = \frac{L}{p_b} = \frac{ \sqrt{ r_{a1}^{2} - r_{b1}^{2} } + \sqrt{ r_{a2}^{2} - r_{b2}^{2} } - C \sin \phi }{ \pi m \cos \phi } \; }
$$

and it is the average number of tooth pairs in contact. **A contact ratio below 1.0 means that one pair leaves contact before the next pair engages**: for part of each cycle no involute contact exists, the driven gear momentarily loses or gains speed and the next pair engages with an impact. Practical spur gears are designed for $m_p \ge 1.2$, usually $1.4$–$1.8$.
:::

Compute the path of contact and the contact ratio for $N_1 = 20$, $N_2 = 40$, $m = 3\ \mathrm{mm}$, $\phi = 20^{\circ}$, standard proportions and standard centres.

**Answer.** $L = 14.48\ \mathrm{mm}$, $m_p = 1.635$.

::: solution
$$
r_1 = 30 , \quad r_{a1} = 33 , \quad r_{b1} = 28.190 \qquad r_2 = 60 , \quad r_{a2} = 63 , \quad r_{b2} = 56.382 \qquad C = 90
$$

$$
L = \sqrt{ 63^{2} - 56.382^{2} } + \sqrt{ 33^{2} - 28.190^{2} } - 90 \times 0.34202 = 28.109 + 17.155 - 30.782 = 14.48\ \mathrm{mm}
$$

$$

**Read what the 0.635 means.** For 63.5% of the mesh cycle two pairs of teeth share the load and for 36.5% only one pair carries it. At each change from one pair to two the mesh stiffness and the load sharing change, and with tooth errors and deflection this produces a **transmission-error fluctuation at the mesh frequency**, the dominant source of gear whine. Raising the contact ratio is one of the most effective noise treatments, and the two usual ways of doing it are more teeth at a smaller module and a helix angle.

## Centre distance: the tolerance that is tighter than it looks

An unshifted gear pair is designed for $C = m ( N_1 + N_2 ) / 2$. Put it at a slightly larger centre distance and it still turns, at exactly the same velocity ratio — that is the involute's great virtue — but three things happen at once.

1. **Backlash appears.** The tooth space is wider than the tooth by about $j \approx 2 \Delta C \tan \phi$, measured along the pitch circle.
2. **The operating pressure angle rises.** The base circles do not change, so the common tangent through the new centre distance $C' = C + \Delta C$ is steeper:

$$
C' \cos \phi_{\mathrm{op}} = C \cos \phi
$$

3. **The contact ratio falls**, because the subtracted term $C' \sin \phi_{\mathrm{op}}$ in the path of contact grows while both tip-circle terms stay fixed.

::: example Centre-distance sensitivity, 20/40 module-3 {#ex-centre}
Tabulate the operating pressure angle, the backlash and the contact ratio of the $20 / 40$ module-3 pair of [[#ex-crr]] as the centre distance is opened.

::: solution
For each $\Delta C$, $\phi_{\mathrm{op}} = \arccos [ 90 \cos 20^{\circ} / ( 90 + \Delta C ) ]$; the circular backlash is the operating circular pitch minus the two tooth thicknesses on the operating pitch circles (from the tooth-thickness formula of [[#ex-geometry]]); and $m_p = [ 17.155 + 28.109 - C' \sin \phi_{\mathrm{op}} ] / 8.856$.

| $\Delta C$ (mm) | $\phi_{\mathrm{op}}$ | backlash $j$ (mm) | $m_p$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | $20.00^{\circ}$ | 0 | 1.635 |
| +0.05 | $20.09^{\circ}$ | 0.037 | 1.619 |
| +0.10 | $20.17^{\circ}$ | 0.073 | 1.602 |
| +0.20 | $20.35^{\circ}$ | 0.147 | 1.570 |
| +0.45 | $20.77^{\circ}$ | 0.335 | 1.489 |
| +0.90 | $21.50^{\circ}$ | 0.686 | 1.348 |
| +1.80 | $22.89^{\circ}$ | 1.431 | 1.079 |

The backlash column agrees with $2 \Delta C \tan \phi$ to within a few per cent for small $\Delta C$, and the contact ratio falls by about $0.33$ per millimetre of extra centre distance. **A housing bored $0.1\ \mathrm{mm}$ long costs only $0.03$ of contact ratio, but $0.9\ \mathrm{mm}$ ($0.3$ module) costs $0.29$, and $1.8\ \mathrm{mm}$ leaves $m_p = 1.08$** — barely above the limit at which the next pair engages before the last one leaves, with an operating pressure angle three degrees above nominal and a backlash of $1.4\ \mathrm{mm}$.

**Sanity check.** The trend is linear for small $\Delta C$, as it must be: $d ( C' \sin \phi_{\mathrm{op}} ) / dC' = 1 / \sin \phi_{\mathrm{op}}$ at constant $C' \cos \phi_{\mathrm{op}}$, so $\Delta m_p \approx - \Delta C / ( p_b \sin \phi ) = - 1 / ( 8.856 \times 0.342 ) = - 0.33$ per millimetre. Note what makes this dangerous: **nothing looks wrong** — the shafts turn freely and the backlash looks generous — but the pair now spends more of each cycle in single-pair contact, with higher tooth load and more noise.

## Tooth bending: the oldest strength formula in mechanical engineering

::: definition The Lewis equation {#def-lewis}
Model the tooth as a cantilever loaded at the outer corner of the tip, along the line of action. Cut it at the section where an inscribed parabola of uniform strength (with its vertex where the load line crosses the tooth centreline) is tangent to the root fillet — the weakest section — and write bending stress as $\sigma = M c / I$ for a rectangular section of thickness $t$ and unit width:

$$
\sigma_F = \frac{6 W_t a}{b \, t^{2}}
$$

with $W_t$ the transmitted load, $a$ the moment arm from the load to the critical section and $b$ the face width. Writing $t = Y' m$ and $a = k' m$ turns this into the **Lewis equation**,

$$
\boxed{ \; \sigma_F = \frac{ W_t }{ b \, m \, Y } \; } \qquad Y = \frac{ t^{2} }{ 6 a m }
$$

where $Y$ is the **Lewis form factor**, a pure number depending on tooth count and pressure angle alone. The tooth is then checked against a bending endurance $\sigma_{F,\lim}$ with a safety factor, exactly as a shaft is checked against a fatigue limit — because that is what it is: **a tooth root is a bending-fatigue specimen loaded once per revolution**, from zero to maximum on an ordinary gear and fully reversed on an idler, which meshes on both flanks and is therefore derated.
:::

The Lewis equation dates from 1892 and its cantilever model is still at the heart of the AGMA and ISO bending calculations. What it omits is worth listing, because each omission became a correction factor in the modern formulations:

| Omission | Consequence | Code factor | Typical |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stress concentration at the root fillet | Actual root stress 30-50% higher | built into $J$ (AGMA) or $Y_S$ (ISO) | 1.2-1.5 |
| Shear and compression on the section | a few per cent | in the geometry factor | — |
| Load shared between two pairs | lower load where $m_p > 1$ | in $J$ / $Y_\varepsilon$ | — |
| Tooth errors and dynamic overshoot | load rises with speed | $K_v$ | 1.05-1.6 |
| Misalignment and shaft deflection | load crowds one end of the face | $K_m$ (AGMA), $K_{H\beta}$ (ISO) | 1.05-1.4 |
| Pitch and profile errors between pairs | uneven sharing between pairs | $K_{H\alpha}$ (ISO) | 1.0-1.25 |
| Duty cycle and starting peaks | the real peak, not the average | $K_o$ (AGMA), $K_A$ (ISO) | 1.0-2.0 |

::: example Bending stress in a 55 kW industrial pinion {#ex-bend}
A $55\ \mathrm{kW}$, $730\ \mathrm{rev / min}$ blower reducer runs at $i = 94 / 18 = 5.22$ on an $18 / 94$ pair of module $6$, pressure angle $20^{\circ}$, unshifted, in through-hardened 42CrMo4 at $300\ \mathrm{HB}$, hobbed to AGMA quality 8. The wheel's face width $b = 70\ \mathrm{mm}$ governs (the pinion is $75\ \mathrm{mm}$). Take an application factor $K_A = 1.25$, the dynamic factor $K_v$ from the AGMA formula tabulated in the next section, a root-fillet factor $K_F = 1.4$, the standard moment arm $a = 1.2 \, m$ with the tooth taken as a cantilever of thickness $s_b$ (the base-circle thickness), all the load on one pair, and a permissible root stress of $430\ \mathrm{MPa}$.

**Answer.** $\sigma_F = 166\ \mathrm{MPa}$ at the pinion root: $S_F = 2.6$.

::: solution
$$
T_1 = \frac{ 55\,000 }{ 730 \times 2 \pi / 60 } = 719.5\ \mathrm{N \cdot m} , \qquad d_1 = 6 \times 18 = 108\ \mathrm{mm} , \qquad W_t = \frac{ 2 \times 719.5 }{ 0.108 } = 13\,323\ \mathrm{N}
$$

The pitch-line velocity is $V = \pi \times 0.108 \times 730 / 60 = 4.13\ \mathrm{m/s}$, for which the AGMA formula gives $K_v = 1.24$ at quality 8. The critical-section thickness comes from the base-circle formula of [[#ex-geometry]], with $r_b = 54 \cos 20^{\circ} = 50.74\ \mathrm{mm}$:

$$
s_b = 2 r_b \! \left[ \frac{ \pi m }{ 2 d} + \operatorname{inv} \phi \right] = 2 \times 50.74 \times \left[ \frac{ \pi }{ 36 } + 0.014904 \right] = 10.37\ \mathrm{mm} = 1.728 \, m
$$

and with $a = 1.2 \, m = 7.2\ \mathrm{mm}$,

$$
\sigma_F = K_A K_v K_F \frac{ 6 W_t a }{ b \, s_b^{2} } = 1.25 \times 1.24 \times 1.4 \times \frac{ 6 \times 13\,323 \times 7.2 }{ 70 \times 10.37^{2} } = 2.170 \times 76.5 = \boxed{ 166\ \mathrm{MPa} }
$$

so $S_F = 430 / 166 = 2.6$. **The number is unremarkable, and that is the point.** Since $\sigma_F \propto 1 / b$, **the face width at which bending alone would reach the permissible stress is $70 \times 166 / 430 = 27\ \mathrm{mm}$.** The gear has $70\ \mathrm{mm}$ of face width because of something else, and that something is the next section.

**Sanity check.** The nominal bending stress $W_t / ( b m ) = 13\,323 / ( 70 \times 6 ) = 31.7\ \mathrm{MPa}$, and $76.5 / 31.7 = 2.4$ corresponds to an effective form factor $Y = 0.41$ on the base-circle section — the same order as the tabulated Lewis factor for 18 teeth (about $0.31$), and larger because the base-circle section is thicker than the true critical section higher up the fillet.
:::
:::

## Surface durability: why the face width really is 70 mm

::: definition Pitting and the contact safety factor {#def-pitting}
Two gear flanks are two curved bodies in line contact, and the Hertz pressure at the pitch point follows from the same $p_0 = \sqrt{ F E^{*} / ( \pi b R ) }$ that closed [[mechanisms/cams]]. The radii of curvature of the two involutes at the pitch point are their distances to the base-circle tangency points, $r_1 \sin \phi$ and $r_2 \sin \phi$, so

$$
\frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{r_1 \sin \phi} + \frac{1}{r_2 \sin \phi} \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad p_0 = \sqrt{ \frac{ W_n E^{*} }{ \pi b R } } \ , \qquad E^{*} = \frac{ E }{ 2 \left( 1 - \nu^{2} \right) } = 113.7\ \mathrm{GPa}\ \text{(steel on steel)}
$$

with $W_n = W_t / \cos \phi$ the normal tooth load. (Many texts write the result in terms of $W_t$ and absorb the $\cos \phi$ into a geometry factor; this lesson uses $W_t$ for comparison with the rating tables, which is about 3% low — see the sanity check below.)

**Unlike the cam, $R$ is not constant.** It is $\rho_1 \rho_2 / ( \rho_1 + \rho_2 )$ with $\rho_i$ the radius of curvature of each *flank* at the contact point, and $\rho_1$ falls as contact moves down the pinion flank towards its base circle. The worst point is usually the lowest point of single-pair contact on the pinion, where the contact stress is 10-20% above the pitch-point value.
:::

::: example Pitting check of the same pinion {#ex-pit}
For the pair of [[#ex-bend]], find the pitch-point Hertz pressure with the same $K_A$ and $K_v$, and compare it with the AGMA grade-1 allowable contact stress for through-hardened steel, $\sigma_{H,\lim} = 2.22\,\mathrm{HB} + 200 = 866\ \mathrm{MPa}$ at $300\ \mathrm{HB}$.

**Answer.** $p_0 = 830\ \mathrm{MPa}$, $S_H = 1.04$.

::: solution
$$
r_1 = 54 , \quad r_2 = 282\ \mathrm{mm} \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{ 54 \times 0.34202 } + \frac{1}{ 282 \times 0.34202 } = 0.05414 + 0.01037 = 0.06451\ \mathrm{mm^{-1}}
$$

$$
R = 15.50\ \mathrm{mm} \qquad p_0 = \sqrt{ \frac{ 1.25 \times 1.24 \times 13\,323 \times 113\,700 }{ \pi \times 70 \times 15.50 } } = \boxed{ 830\ \mathrm{MPa} }
$$

against $866\ \mathrm{MPa}$ — **a contact safety factor of $1.04$, and with the 10-20% concentration at the lowest point of single-pair contact included, below 1.** The design is pitting-limited: $70\ \mathrm{mm}$ is close to the *minimum* face width, not a comfortable one.

**Now the two numbers side by side.** Since $p_0 \propto 1 / \sqrt{b}$, the face width at which pitting reaches its allowable is $70 \times ( 830 / 866 )^{2} = 64\ \mathrm{mm}$, against $27\ \mathrm{mm}$ for bending. **The gear is about 2.4 times wider than bending alone would need, purely so that its surface survives.** The rule of thumb that falls out of it is worth memorising: **for through-hardened steel gears, pitting usually governs; for case-hardened and ground gears, the much higher allowable contact stress shrinks the gear until bending becomes the competing limit.**

**Sanity check.** Using the normal load $W_n = W_t / \cos 20^{\circ}$ instead of $W_t$ raises $p_0$ by $\sqrt{ 1 / 0.9397 } = 1.032$, to $856\ \mathrm{MPa}$ — the same conclusion, with even less margin.
:::
:::

::: exercise Face width by pitting {level=1 check="21"}
The same $18 / 94$ module-6 pair is rebuilt in case-hardened and ground $20\mathrm{MnCr5}$ with $\sigma_{H,\lim} = 1\,500\ \mathrm{MPa}$ and a permissible root stress of $850\ \mathrm{MPa}$, all other factors unchanged. ::: solution
$p_0 \propto 1 / \sqrt{b}$, and $p_0 = 830\ \mathrm{MPa}$ at $b = 70\ \mathrm{mm}$, so

$$

$$

**a 70% reduction in face width**, which has to be weighed against the much higher cost of carburising and grinding. Checking bending at $b = 21.4$: $\sigma_F = 166 \times 70 / 21.4 = 542\ \mathrm{MPa}$ against $850$, so $S_F = 1.57$ — **bending, which had a margin of 2.6 in the through-hardened design, is now the closer of the two limits once a sensible pitting margin is added.** That is the characteristic signature of a case-hardened gear: the surface allowable rises far more than the root allowable, so the gear shrinks until bending competes again. (In practice a face width this small relative to the diameter would also prompt a smaller module and more teeth.)
:::

::: warning What a pit actually is
Pitting is not "the surface wearing out". In a frictionless Hertzian line contact the maximum **shear** stress lies not at the surface but about $0.78 \, b_h$ below it ($b_h$ the contact half-width), and rolling-contact fatigue cracks can start there. On a gear the picture is complicated by sliding, which is zero at the pitch line and reverses direction across it. On the driving pinion's dedendum (below the pitch line) the friction traction tends to open surface cracks so that the oncoming contact traps lubricant in them and pressurises it — the hydraulic-crack mechanism first proposed by Way in the 1930s. The observed consequence is that **pitting usually starts just below the pitch line on the pinion flank**, and a pit forms when a crack, driven by contact stresses of order $1\ \mathrm{GPa}$ (some ten thousand atmospheres), turns back towards the surface and releases a flake.

Three practical consequences. **Oil viscosity helps:** a thicker elastohydrodynamic film separates the asperities better, which is why a gear that is starting to pit is often helped by a higher viscosity grade before it needs to be made bigger. **A harder surface helps**, and the allowable contact stresses in the codes rise roughly linearly with surface hardness. **And a little initial pitting on a newly run-in gear can be non-progressive**: it stops once the high spots have gone and the flanks conform. The codes' allowable contact stress is tied to a stated life and reliability, not to a no-damage threshold.
:::

## Dynamics: the mesh is a spring whose stiffness keeps changing

Two meshing pairs are two springs in parallel, and as the contact point travels along the line of action the number of pairs in contact alternates between $\lfloor m_p \rfloor$ and $\lceil m_p \rceil$. **The mesh stiffness therefore fluctuates at the mesh frequency.** For steel spur gears the mean mesh stiffness is about $20\ \mathrm{N / ( mm \cdot \mu m )}$ of face width, so the $70\ \mathrm{mm}$ gear above has a mesh stiffness of about $1.4 \times 10^{9}\ \mathrm{N / m}$ and deflects about $13\,323 / 1.4 \times 10^{9} = 9.5\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ under its nominal load.

That number matters because **it is the same size as the manufacturing error.** An AGMA quality-8 gear has pitch and profile errors of order $10\ \mu\mathrm{m}$; a ground gear of high quality has a few micrometres. When the error is comparable with the elastic deflection, the incoming pair must be deflected into place before it carries its share, and the load history over the mesh cycle is dominated by the error rather than by the geometry. That is the physical content of the dynamic factor $K_v$. The AGMA 2001 formula (in SI units, $V$ in m/s, quality number $Q_v$ from 6 to 11) is

$$
K_v = \left( \frac{ A + \sqrt{ 200 V } }{ A } \right)^{B} , \qquad B = 0.25 \left( 12 - Q_v \right)^{2/3} , \qquad A = 50 + 56 \left( 1 - B \right)
$$

valid up to $V_{\max} = [ A + ( Q_v - 3 ) ]^{2} / 200$. Evaluated:

| AGMA quality $Q_v$ | $V = 4\ \mathrm{m/s}$ | $V = 10$ | $V = 20$ | $V = 30$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | 1.38 | 1.59 | — | — |
| 8 | 1.24 | 1.36 | 1.50 | — |
| 10 | 1.12 | 1.19 | 1.25 | 1.30 |
| 11 | 1.07 | 1.10 | 1.14 | 1.16 |

A dash means the speed is beyond $V_{\max}$ for that quality ($19.7\ \mathrm{m/s}$ for $Q_v = 6$, $28.7\ \mathrm{m/s}$ for $Q_v = 8$). The shape of the table is the message: **accuracy buys you speed.** At $4\ \mathrm{m/s}$ the difference between quality 6 and quality 11 is about 29% of tooth load; at $30\ \mathrm{m/s}$ the formula does not even permit the coarser grades.

::: exercise Mesh frequency and resonance {level=1 check="219"}
For the $18$-tooth, $730\ \mathrm{rev / min}$ pinion above, what is the mesh frequency in hertz? If the first torsional mode of the motor-pinion system is measured at $110\ \mathrm{Hz}$, is the design acceptable?

::: solution
$$
f_{\text{mesh}} = \frac{ N_1 n }{ 60 } = \frac{ 18 \times 730 }{ 60 } = \boxed{ 219\ \mathrm{Hz} }
$$

The ratio of mesh frequency to the natural frequency is $219 / 110 = 2.0$, comfortably outside a typical $\pm 20$–$30\%$ exclusion band, so **at the operating speed the mode is not excited by the mesh fundamental**: the system runs well above resonance, where the response to the mesh excitation is reduced. Two things still need checking. First, the mesh frequency passes through $110\ \mathrm{Hz}$ during run-up, at $n = 110 \times 60 / 18 = 367\ \mathrm{rev / min}$; with a motor that accelerates through it in a second or two this is harmless, but a variable-speed drive must not be allowed to dwell there. Second, other excitation orders — the shaft frequency $730 / 60 = 12.2\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and its harmonics from run-out and eccentricity — should also be kept away from $110\ \mathrm{Hz}$ (the ninth shaft order, $109.5\ \mathrm{Hz}$, is close, but high shaft orders are normally weak). The design is acceptable.

| Worm, lead angle $2^{\circ}$, $\mu = 0.05$ | 0.41 | self-locking: $\lambda < \arctan \mu = 2.86^{\circ}$ |

::: solution
The $55\ \mathrm{kW}$ reducer at $\eta = 0.985$ dissipates

$$
P_{\mathrm{loss}} = 55\,000 \times ( 1 - 0.985 ) = 825\ \mathrm{W}
$$

A cast-iron housing in still air, with a combined convection and radiation coefficient of roughly $18\ \mathrm{W / ( m^{2} K )}$, sheds about $720\ \mathrm{W}$ per square metre at a $40\ \mathrm{K}$ rise, so **$825\ \mathrm{W}$ needs about $1.15\ \mathrm{m^{2}}$ of effective surface** — about what a $600 \times 400 \times 400\ \mathrm{mm}$ housing offers ($1.28\ \mathrm{m^{2}}$ in total, not all of it effective). The gearbox is thermally marginal. The standard fixes, in order, are fins, a fan on the shaft, an oil cooler, and a bigger box; and the standard mistake is to size the box from the shaft centres and discover the thermal problem at the acceptance test.

**Now compare the worm.** The same $55\ \mathrm{kW}$ through a $5^{\circ}$-lead worm at $\eta = 0.63$ dissipates $55 \times 0.37 = 20\ \mathrm{kW}$ — **more than a third of the input power and about 25 times the helical gearbox's loss.** That is why worm gears are used at low duty cycle or low power, why they are derated heavily for continuous service, why they need a cooler before they need a stronger gear, and why the design question for a worm gearbox is never "will it break" but "will it boil".

## Helical gears: the same involute, dragged sideways

::: definition Helical geometry {#def-helical}
A helical gear is a spur gear whose tooth trace is a helix of **helix angle** $\psi$ on the cylinder. It is cut by the same rack tool as a spur gear, but the tool works in the plane **normal** to the tooth, so the standard module and pressure angle are normal-plane values, while the gear's involute lies in the **transverse** plane, on a base circle of diameter $d \cos \phi_t$. Writing the subscript $n$ for the normal plane (perpendicular to the tooth) and $t$ for the transverse plane,

$$
m_n = m_t \cos \psi \qquad \tan \phi_t = \frac{ \tan \phi_n }{ \cos \psi } \qquad d = \frac{ m_n N }{ \cos \psi }
$$

and the gear behaves, for both geometry and strength, like a **virtual** or **equivalent spur gear** of

$$
\boxed{ \; N_v = \frac{ N }{ \cos^{3} \psi } \; }
$$

teeth. The cube comes from the ellipse: the plane normal to the tooth cuts the pitch cylinder in an ellipse whose radius of curvature at the tooth is $d / ( 2 \cos^{2} \psi )$, so the virtual pitch diameter is $d / \cos^{2} \psi$, and dividing it by $m_n$ with $d = m_n N / \cos \psi$ supplies the third cosine.
:::

Three consequences, in order of how much they change designs.

**1. The contact ratio gains a second, independent term.** Because the tooth enters contact gradually along a diagonal line rather than abruptly across the whole face, the **face contact ratio** adds to the transverse one,

$$
m_F = \frac{ b \sin \psi }{ \pi m_n } \qquad m_{\text{total}} = m_p + m_F
$$

and unlike $m_p$ it is **unbounded** — you can buy as much of it as the face width and helix angle allow. A spur gear of standard proportions cannot reach 2 (the rack-and-rack limit at $20^{\circ}$ is 1.98, and practical pairs are 1.4–1.8); the $18 / 94$ module-6 pair as a helical gear at $\psi = 20^{\circ}$ with $b = 70\ \mathrm{mm}$ has $m_F = 70 \sin 20^{\circ} / ( 6 \pi ) = 1.27$ and a transverse ratio of 1.55, total 2.82. **That is the main reason helical gears replaced spur gears in almost everything that has to be quiet.**

**2. The undercut limit relaxes.** Since the gear behaves like $N_v$ teeth, the minimum count becomes approximately $N_{\min} = 17.1 \cos^{3} \psi$; the exact rack condition in the transverse plane is $N_{\min} = 2 \cos \psi / \sin^{2} \phi_t$. At $\psi = 20^{\circ}$ both give 15 teeth (14.2 and 14.4), at $25^{\circ}$ 13 to 14 (12.7 and 13.1). **A helical pinion can be a few teeth smaller than a spur one before it is undercut**, and in a gearbox where the pinion diameter sets the centre distance, a few teeth matter.

**3. You have created an axial thrust.** The tooth trace is inclined, so the normal force has a component along the shaft:

$$
F_a = W_t \tan \psi
$$

For the 55 kW pinion at $\psi = 20^{\circ}$ that is $13\,323 \times 0.364 = 4\,850\ \mathrm{N}$ of axial load on a shaft that was previously carrying none — a thrust bearing, a shoulder, a spacer and a shim stack. **This is the tax, and there are only two ways to avoid it: put the helix angle to zero, which is a spur gear, or cut the tooth as two opposite-handed halves on the same blank, which is a double-helical or herringbone gear**, which cancels the thrust internally. A double-helical gear normally has a central gap so that the hob can run out between the two halves; a true herringbone without a gap needs a special shaping machine. Double-helical gears are standard in large turbine and marine reduction gears, where a single-helical thrust would need a very large thrust bearing.

## Bevel, worm, internal and planetary in one paragraph each

**Straight bevel** gears are involute teeth on a cone, and the whole design is done on the **back cone at the large end** because the tooth shrinks to nothing at the apex. The virtual number of teeth is $N_v = N / \cos \delta$ where $\delta$ is the pitch-cone half-angle (Tredgold's back-cone approximation), so a $1 : 1$ pair of 20-tooth bevels behaves like a $28 / 28$ spur pair. Their weakness is that any mounting-distance error moves the contact pattern, which is why bevel sets are assembled to a specified mounting distance with shims and checked by a contact-pattern test, and why a differential that has been re-shimmed without checking the pattern whines.

**Spiral bevel** gears add a spiral angle, which gives them a total contact ratio above 2 and makes them quiet and strong enough for vehicle axles and helicopter transmissions. The **hypoid** gear of a rear axle goes one step further: the pinion axis is offset below the wheel axis, which allows a larger, stronger pinion and a lower driveshaft, at the price of lengthwise sliding that demands an extreme-pressure (EP) oil and costs a few per cent of the power.

**Worm gears** are a screw driving an arc of a worm wheel, and their whole character comes from the sliding velocity between them, which is the whole of the relative motion at the contact; it is why the wheel is usually bronze running on a hardened steel worm, and why worm drives are efficiency-limited and thermally limited. Their genuine advantages are ratios up to about 60:1 or more in one stage, quiet operation and a compact right-angle layout. Their cost, at high ratios and low lead angles, is a third to more than half of the power.

**Internal gears** — a gear inside a ring — have an internal-contact Hertz formula in which the two curvatures *subtract* rather than add, giving a much larger relative radius and therefore a lower contact stress for the same size. They have additional interference limits of their own (the pinion and ring tooth counts must not be too close, or the tips foul), and they cannot be hobbed, only shaper-cut, broached or power-skived, which makes them expensive. They are used wherever the envelope is the constraint: planetary gearboxes, including the planetary sets of automatic transmissions, and wheel-hub drives.

**Planetary gears** split the torque over $n$ planet meshes, which is the classic way to make a gearbox that is small and light and carries a large torque. The cost is a carrier, $n$ planet bearings, and the requirement that all $n$ planets share the load — which they do not do exactly, because of manufacturing errors, unless some member (usually the sun) is allowed to float radially or the structure is compliant enough to equalise the loads. Rating codes account for this with a load-sharing (mesh-load) factor: a three-planet set with a floating member shares almost equally, while sets with more planets need a factor of roughly 1.2–1.4 on the nominal planet load, so a five-planet stage behaves as if it had nearly four equally loaded planets, not five. The details belong to [[mechanisms/gear-trains]].

## Manufacturing, quality numbers and backlash

The AGMA quality number of the older AGMA 2000 system runs from about 3 to 15 (higher is more accurate; the newer AGMA 2015 and ISO 1328 grades run the other way) and is a specification of pitch, profile and lead tolerances, not a vague grade of excellence. Indicative values:

| Quality | Typical process | Pitch error (order) | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4-6 | Cast, milled | tens of $\mu$m | Open gears, mining girth gears |
| 7-8 | Hobbed, shaped | 10-30 $\mu$m | Industrial reducers, agricultural |
| 9-10 | Shaved after hobbing | 5-15 $\mu$m | Automotive transmissions |
| 11-12 | Ground | 3-8 $\mu$m | Aircraft, machine-tool, wind |
| 13-14 | Fine ground | 1-3 $\mu$m | Precision instruments and spindles |

**The process sequence is the design.** A carburised gear distorts in heat treatment by more than its tolerance, so a high-quality case-hardened gear must be ground (or honed) after hardening; shaving, by contrast, is done before hardening. A hobbed through-hardened gear can be put straight into a gearbox.

**Backlash** is the play between the flanks: the arc the pinion turns through with the wheel held, or the circular-thickness deficit on the pitch circle. It is not an error but a specified quantity, needed for thermal expansion, for an oil film and for the tolerance stack of the centre distance. A typical minimum for industrial gears is of order $0.03$–$0.1 \, m$, smaller for instrument gears, and it is normally set by **tooth thickness** — cutting the teeth slightly thinner than nominal — rather than by opening the centre distance, for the reason tabulated in [[#ex-centre]].

::: example Backlash by thickness, not by centre distance {#ex-backlash}
The $20 / 40$ module-3 pair of [[#ex-centre]] needs $0.20\ \mathrm{mm}$ of circular backlash at the pitch circle. Compare achieving it by thinning the teeth and by opening the centre distance.

**Answer.** By thickness: each tooth is cut $0.10\ \mathrm{mm}$ thin, the centre distance is untouched, and the contact ratio stays at $1.635$. By centre distance: $m_p$ falls to $1.545$.

::: solution
By centre distance, $\Delta C \approx j / ( 2 \tan \phi ) = 0.20 / 0.728 = 0.275\ \mathrm{mm}$, and repeating the calculation of [[#ex-centre]] at $C' = 90.275\ \mathrm{mm}$ gives

$$
\phi_{\mathrm{op}} = \arccos \frac{ 90 \cos 20^{\circ} }{ 90.275 } = 20.47^{\circ}, \qquad m_p = 1.545 \quad \text{against} \ 1.635
$$

**a loss of 5.5% of contact ratio for a backlash that costs almost nothing when bought with tooth thickness.** Centre-distance backlash is acceptable only where the centre distance is set by other considerations — a shifted pair designed for a given centre distance, for instance — and even then the shifts and thickness tolerances are chosen to give the required backlash at that centre distance.
:::
:::

## Choosing a gear: the decision tree nobody prints

::: example Selecting the stage {#ex-select}
Specify the gear type for each of these, and say why.

**(a)** A $7.5\ \mathrm{kW}$, $1450\ \mathrm{rev / min}$ to $48\ \mathrm{rev / min}$ conveyor drive, continuous, in a cement plant, budget-constrained.
**(b)** A $250\ \mathrm{W}$ robot wrist joint requiring $180 : 1$ with less than $0.05^{\circ}$ of lost motion.
**(c)** A $2\ \mathrm{MW}$ wind-turbine main drive from $14$ to $1500\ \mathrm{rev / min}$, designed for 20 years without opening.
**(d)** A $0.75\ \mathrm{kW}$ conveyor that must stop and hold on a $15^{\circ}$ incline when the power fails.

**Answer.**

::: solution
**(a)** $i = 1450 / 48 = 30.2$, about $5.5$ per stage over two stages. With integer teeth, $( 90 / 17 ) \times ( 97 / 17 ) = 5.29 \times 5.71 = 30.21$ gives $1450 / 30.21 = 48.0\ \mathrm{rev / min}$. For a parallel-shaft conveyor: **two helical stages, through-hardened, hobbed to about quality 8, splash-lubricated, $\eta \approx 0.985^{2} = 0.97$** (a helical stage followed by a bevel stage if the output must be at right angles). Case-hardening is not justified at 7.5 kW; the gearbox will be housing-limited, not gear-limited.

**(b)** $0.05^{\circ}$ is $3$ arc-minutes ($0.87\ \mathrm{mrad}$) at the output. A $180 : 1$ involute train needs three stages, and the backlash of every stage, referred to the output, adds; reaching 3 arc-minutes that way needs anti-backlash (split, spring-loaded) gears or very tight, expensive tolerances. **The usual answer is a strain-wave (harmonic) drive or a cycloidal (RV-type) reducer**, which give high ratios in one or two compact stages with many teeth in contact and low or zero backlash. The cycloidal reducer is a reminder that non-involute profiles are still the right choice where a fixed ratio and many simultaneous contacts matter more than generating with a straight-sided tool.

**(c)** $i = 107$. No single stage does this. **A common arrangement for this size is one planetary stage at $i \approx 5$ plus two helical stages at $i \approx 4.6$ each** ($5 \times 4.6 \times 4.6 = 106$), with the planetary carrying the huge low-speed torque where splitting it over several planets saves the most size. Twenty years without opening means case-carburised and ground gears (18CrNiMo7-6 is a typical steel), generous contact and bending safety factors, fine oil filtration, and — the part people forget — **"hunting-tooth" tooth counts with no common factor** in each mesh, so that every pinion tooth meets every wheel tooth in turn ($N_1 N_2$ tooth engagements before a pair repeats) and local defects do not keep meeting the same mate.

**(d)** $0.75\ \mathrm{kW}$, hold on an incline. **A worm gear with a lead angle near or below the friction angle** is the traditional choice: at this power its poor efficiency costs little, and while the load is static nothing slides, so holding costs no power and the static friction angle (higher than the running one) works in its favour. **But self-locking is not a substitute for a brake**, because the worm's friction depends on temperature, lubrication and vibration, and a drive that holds in the test can creep in service. The answer is a worm reducer *plus* a spring-applied motor brake, with the self-locking treated as a bonus.
:::
:::

::: exercise Minimum teeth with profile shift {level=1 check="11"}
A $20^{\circ}$ pinion is generated by a standard rack cutter. What is the smallest tooth count that avoids undercutting with a profile shift of $x = + 0.4$?

::: solution
The rack's tip is what does the digging, and shifting the rack away from the gear centre by $x m$ moves the generated root outward by the same amount, so the interference condition becomes

$$
N_{\min} = \frac{ 2 \left( 1 - x \right)}{\sin^{2} \phi} = \frac{ 2 \times 0.6 }{ 0.11698 } = 10.26 \ \Rightarrow \ \boxed{ 11 }
$$

**Six teeth fewer than the unshifted 17, and the price is threefold.** The tooth's tip narrows — at $x = + 0.4$ a 12-tooth pinion with addendum $( 1 + x ) m$ has a tip land of only $0.36 \, m$, close to the usual minimum — the mating gear must be shifted by $- 0.4$ or the centre distance changes, and the pair can no longer be replaced by catalogue gears. **This is why a drawing for a shifted gear states the shift coefficient and a tooth-thickness check such as the span measurement over $k$ teeth or the dimension over pins, not just the pitch diameter.**
:::
:::

::: exercise Contact ratio at the interference limit {level=2 check="1.44"}
Compute the transverse contact ratio of an unshifted $13 / 13$ pair of $20^{\circ}$ gears with standard proportions, to two decimal places. Is the result comfortable?

::: solution
With unit module, $r_1 = r_2 = 6.5$, $r_a = 7.5$, $r_b = 6.108$, $C = 13$:

$$
L = 2 \sqrt{ 7.5^{2} - 6.108^{2} } - 13 \times 0.34202 = 2 \times 4.352 - 4.446 = 4.258\ \mathrm{mm}
$$

$$
p_b = \pi \cos 20^{\circ} = 2.952\ \mathrm{mm} \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad m_p = \frac{ 4.258 }{ 2.952 } = \boxed{ 1.44 }
$$

**against 1.635 for the $20 / 40$ pair of [[#ex-crr]].** It is acceptable (above the usual 1.2 minimum) but not comfortable: the pair spends 56% of each mesh cycle in single-pair contact instead of 36%, so the tooth carries the full load for longer and the mesh stiffness varies more, which means more noise. Positive profile shift, the usual cure for undercutting on small pinions, tends to shorten the path of contact further. When the envelope allows, more teeth at a smaller module is often the better trade than fighting the geometry with a very small tooth count.
:::
:::

::: exercise Virtual tooth count of a helical pinion {level=1 check="15"}
A helical pinion is required to run unshifted against a $\phi_n = 20^{\circ}$ rack at a helix angle of $20^{\circ}$. What is the smallest tooth count that avoids undercutting?

::: solution
The helical gear behaves like a spur gear of $N_v = N / \cos^{3} \psi$ teeth, so undercutting is avoided when $N_v \ge 17.1$:

$$
N \ \ge \ 17.1 \cos^{3} 20^{\circ} = 17.1 \times 0.8298 = 14.19 \ \Rightarrow \ \boxed{ 15 }
$$

The exact transverse-plane condition, $N \ge 2 \cos \psi / \sin^{2} \phi_t$ with $\phi_t = \arctan ( \tan 20^{\circ} / \cos 20^{\circ} ) = 21.17^{\circ}$, gives $14.4$ and the same answer. **Three teeth fewer than the spur gear's 18.** The reason is that in the transverse plane the helical gear has a larger pressure angle, and the pitch circle lies further outside the base circle where the involute ends. Larger helix angles and a $25^{\circ}$ normal pressure angle relax the limit further: with $\psi = 30^{\circ}$ and $\phi_n = 25^{\circ}$ the exact limit is $7.7$, so 8 teeth — but the axial thrust then grows with $\tan 30^{\circ}$.
:::
:::

::: exercise Worm efficiency and the self-locking boundary {level=1 check="0.77"}
A worm drive has a lead angle of $12^{\circ}$ and a coefficient of friction of $0.06$ at operating temperature. What is its forward efficiency (as a decimal), and does it self-lock when cold at $\mu = 0.12$?

::: solution
$$
\phi = \arctan 0.06 = 3.43^{\circ} \qquad \eta = \frac{ \tan 12^{\circ} }{ \tan \left( 12 + 3.43 \right)^{\circ} } = \frac{ 0.2126 }{ 0.2762 } = \boxed{ 0.77 }
$$

Cold, $\phi = \arctan 0.12 = 6.84^{\circ}$ and $\eta = \tan 12^{\circ} / \tan 18.84^{\circ} = 0.62$. A worm drive self-locks (cannot be back-driven) when $\lambda < \phi$; here $12^{\circ}$ exceeds both $3.43^{\circ}$ and $6.84^{\circ}$, so **it does not self-lock hot or cold.** Note the direction of the trade: a worm designed to self-lock when hot ($\lambda < 3.4^{\circ}$) has a running efficiency below 50%, and a worm with $\lambda$ just above the cold friction angle ($\approx 6.9^{\circ}$) runs hot at only $\eta \approx 0.66$. Whatever the lead angle, a worm should not be relied on as the sole means of holding a load.
:::
:::

::: exercise Bevel virtual teeth {level=1 check="28"}
A $1 : 1$ straight-bevel pair has 20 teeth each and a $20^{\circ}$ pressure angle. How many teeth (to the nearest whole tooth) does the equivalent spur gear on the back cone have, and what does that imply for the choice of form cutter?

::: solution
For a $1 : 1$ pair the pitch-cone half-angle is $\delta = 45^{\circ}$, and the virtual count is

$$
N_v = \frac{ N }{ \cos \delta } = \frac{ 20 }{ 0.7071 } = 28.3 \ \Rightarrow \ \boxed{ 28 }
$$

**which is comfortably above the 17-tooth interference limit** — the back cone's radius is $\sqrt{2}$ times the pitch radius, so the tooth behaves like one on a larger gear. The virtual count is also what selects a form cutter when a bevel gear is roughed out with a disc cutter: in the standard set of eight involute form cutters (No. 1 for 135 teeth to a rack down to No. 8 for 12–13 teeth), a 28-tooth virtual gear falls in the range of the **No. 4 cutter (26–34 teeth)**. The cutter set exists because a form cutter reproduces one tooth space exactly, and the involute's shape changes with the tooth count; generating methods, which roll the gear past a straight-sided tool, avoid the problem altogether.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Two gears of the same module, same face width, same material and same heat treatment carry the same load. Gear A has 17 teeth, gear B has 60 teeth, each meshing with an identical 60-tooth wheel. Which statement is correct?

- [ ] Gear A is weaker in bending because its teeth are more highly curved
- [x] Gear A has the higher contact stress and the lower contact ratio, and it is closer to the interference limit — three separate penalties for the same diameter saving
- [ ] Gear B is quieter only because it is larger
- [ ] Both are equally stressed because the module is the same

::: solution
All three penalties land on A. The contact stress goes as $1 / \sqrt{ R }$ and $1 / R = 1 / ( r_1 \sin \phi ) + 1 / ( r_2 \sin \phi )$, so the smaller pinion has the smaller $R$ and the higher $p_0$ — for $17 / 60$ against $60 / 60$ at equal tangential load, $\sqrt{ ( 1/17 + 1/60 ) / ( 2/60 ) } = 1.50$ times higher. The contact ratio is 1.65 against 1.78. And 17 teeth is within two teeth of the interference limit for this ratio (15.3, so 16 teeth) while 60 is nowhere near it. **The reason small pinions are nevertheless used is that everything else in the machine scales with the centre distance: the housing, the shafts, the bearings and the mass. Much of gear design is the negotiation of that trade.**
:::
:::

::: quiz
A gearbox is assembled with a centre distance $0.15\ \mathrm{mm}$ over nominal and the measured backlash is within specification. Should the build be accepted?

- [ ] Yes — backlash is the specified acceptance criterion, and it is met
- [x] No, or at least not without checking the contact pattern, because the backlash may have been bought with centre distance at the cost of contact ratio
- [ ] Yes, provided the oil level is correct
- [ ] No — any positive centre-distance error is a reject regardless of backlash

::: solution
For the $20 / 40$ module-3 pair of [[#ex-centre]], $\Delta C = + 0.15\ \mathrm{mm}$ gives $j \approx 0.11\ \mathrm{mm}$ of backlash and $m_p = 1.586$ against 1.635 — **an in-specification backlash that may come partly from centre distance rather than tooth thickness, with a 3% loss of contact ratio and a $0.26^{\circ}$ rise in operating pressure angle.** The better acceptance criteria are the **contact pattern** (a marking-compound test showing the print well placed on the flank, not crowded to one end or to the tip) and a direct tooth-thickness measurement on the parts (span over $k$ teeth or dimension over pins). **Backlash is a symptom, not a property: the same measured backlash can mean a correctly cut gear at the right centre distance or a thin-cut gear at the wrong one, and only a pattern test or a direct thickness measurement tells them apart.**
:::

## Where this leads

The gear is where this course's two halves meet. The **kinematics** of [[mechanisms/position-analysis]] and [[mechanisms/velocity-analysis]] was about finding where things are and how fast they move; the gear settles that question by construction, permanently, with one integer — the tooth ratio — replacing a trigonometric solution. The **dynamics** of [[mechanisms/acceleration-analysis]] and [[mechanisms/machine-dynamics]] was about the forces that kinematics implies; the gear spends most of its design life on exactly one of them, the Hertz contact pressure.

Three things follow from taking that seriously. **Elastohydrodynamic lubrication** explains why the $830\ \mathrm{MPa}$ of [[#ex-pit]] does not destroy the surface at once: an oil film around a micrometre thick, made almost solid by the pressure, separates the flanks; the fluid properties behind it are introduced in [[fluid-mechanics/fluid-properties]]. **Contact fatigue** explains why the same surface fails after $10^{8}$–$10^{9}$ cycles rather than immediately, and belongs with [[materials-science/failure]]. **Transmission-error dynamics** explains gear noise, which in a car cabin is a design constraint as binding as strength, and belongs with [[vibrations/harmonic-excitation]].

And the failure you will most often be asked to explain is a pitted or spalled flank on a gearbox whose calculation said it was adequate. Very often the contact pattern moved: shafts, bearings and housing deflected more under load than the design assumed, the flank modifications (crowning, helix correction) no longer matched the deflection, the contact crowded towards one end of the face, and the local pressure rose well above the average the rating used. **Gears rarely fail because the average stress was too high. They fail because the stress was not average.**

::: summary
- The **law of gearing** requires the common normal at contact to pass through a fixed pitch point; the involute achieves it by making the common normal a fixed line tangent to the two base circles. It keeps a constant ratio even when the centre distance changes, and its straight-sided rack makes generating cutters simple — which is why it became the standard.
- A gear is defined by module $m$, tooth count $N$ and pressure angle $\phi$. **Two gears mesh properly only if their base pitches $\pi m \cos \phi$ are equal** — module and pressure angle must both match.
- **Minimum teeth against a rack is $N_{\min} = 2 / \sin^{2} \phi$**: 31.9 at $14.5^{\circ}$, 17.1 at $20^{\circ}$, 11.2 at $25^{\circ}$. Below it the tooth is undercut. Profile shift $x$ lowers the limit to $2 ( 1 - x ) / \sin^{2} \phi$ at the cost of interchangeability and a narrower tip.
- **Contact ratio $m_p = L / p_b$** is the average number of pairs in contact; about 1.4-1.8 for practical spur gears, never below about 1.2 by design. Below 1.0 the next pair engages only after the last has left.
- **Opening the centre distance keeps the ratio but costs contact ratio**: about $0.33$ per millimetre for the module-3 example, while adding backlash $\approx 2 \Delta C \tan \phi$. Backlash should be made with tooth thickness, not centre distance.
- **Bending is $\sigma_F = W_t / ( b m Y )$ (times rating factors) and pitting is $p_0 = \sqrt{ W E^{*} / ( \pi b R ) }$** with $E^{*} = E / [ 2 ( 1 - \nu^{2} ) ]$ for steel on steel. For through-hardened industrial gears pitting typically needs two to three times the face width bending needs; for case-hardened gears bending competes again.
- **The dynamic factor $K_v$ is a manufacturing-quality story as much as a speed story.** At $4\ \mathrm{m/s}$ the AGMA formula gives 1.38 for quality 6 and 1.07 for quality 11, and the coarse grades are not permitted at high speed at all.
- **Helical gears add a face contact ratio $m_F = b \sin \psi / ( \pi m_n )$ that grows with face width, relax the undercut limit roughly by $\cos^{3} \psi$, and create an axial thrust $W_t \tan \psi$** that must be carried by a bearing or cancelled with a double-helical gear.
:::
